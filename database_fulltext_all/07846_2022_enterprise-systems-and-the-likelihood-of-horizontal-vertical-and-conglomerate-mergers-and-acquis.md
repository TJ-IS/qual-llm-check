# ENTERPRISE SYSTEMS AND THE LIKELIHOOD OF HORIZONTAL, VERTICAL, AND CONGLOMERATE MERGERS AND ACQUISITIONS<sup>1</sup>

Chengxin Cao Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College New York, NY, U.S.A. {chengxin.cao@baruch.cuny.edu}

Gautam Ray, Mani Subramani, and Alok Gupta Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {gautamr@umn.edu} {subra010@umn.edu} {gupta037@umn.edu}

This paper examines the relationship between enterprise systems (ES) and the likelihood of mergers and acquisitions (M&A). The key argument is that since ES can reduce agency costs associated with internal coordination and the transaction cost of coordinating with external partners, ES may be related to the likelihood of M&A. Using a sample of 3,289 firms headquartered in North America from 2010 to 2018 that made 8,373 M&A deals, the empirical analysis suggests that ES are positively related to horizontal acquisitions and negatively related to conglomerate acquisitions. However, as internal coordination costs increase, ES are becoming associated with more conglomerate M&A, especially when the goal is to introduce new products and enter new markets. Also, in contexts where partners require transactionspecific investments, ES are associated with a relative decrease in the number of horizontal and vertical M&A. These findings suggest that ES create options for managers to engage in ownership as well as information-based coordination, depending on the internal and external coordination costs as well as the goals of the organization.

Keywords: Mergers and acquisitions (M&A), transaction cost economics, agency theory, enterprise systems, information-based coordination, ownership-based coordination

## Introduction

Mergers and acquisitions (M&A) are used by firms to acquire new resources and redeploy existing resources in new contexts. The value-generating potential of M&A explains the number and value of M&A (Cartwright & Schoenberg, 2006). In 2018, 97,709 M&A deals were completed around the world with a total deal value of 5.3 trillion U.S. Dollars (Holley, 2018). Prior research suggests a variety of efficiency-oriented arguments for M&A, such as cost reduction and profit enhancement from using production capacity more effectively (Singh & Montgomery, 1987), sharing knowledge among operating units (Morck & Yeung, 1998), and umbrella branding of products (Wernerfelt, 1988). In contrast to the above efficiency-based logic, prior work also presents alternative motivations for M&A including creating market power, self-serving attempts by acquirer management to overexpand, taking advantage of opportunities for diversification by exploiting internal capital markets, and managing risk for undiversified managers (Andrade et al., 2001).

There is a large body of research in IS that examines how information technology (IT) impacts firm boundaries by reducing internal and external coordination costs (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 1994; Clemons et al., 1993; Dewan et al., 1998; Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Hitt, 1999; Malone et al., 1987). The empirical findings of this literature suggest that IT may reduce internal as well as external coordination costs, as IT is associated with more diversified and less vertically integrated firms (Dewan et al., 1998; Hitt, 1999). There is also research on the impact of IT on M&A performance. This body of work examines how integration and resource redeployment capabilities provided by IT improve post-M&A firm performance (e.g., Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Du, 2015; Benitez et al., 2018). However, prior research has focused on IT capital in general, viewing IT as being undifferentiated. There has been little attention devoted to understanding how the capabilities of enterprise systems (ES) influence the likelihood of M&A. At their core, ES enable the monitoring and coordination of activities within and across organizations. Thus, the internal coordination capabilities provided by ES can reduce the agency and decision information costs associated with operating a larger (i.e., the focal and the acquired) firm and thereby increase the motivation to engage in M&A. However, the external coordination capabilities provided by ES can reduce the transaction cost to monitor and coordinate activities with business partners and reduce the need for costly and risky M&A. Thus, the goal of this paper is to examine how ES might affect firm boundaries by influencing the likelihood of M&A.

This study uses a panel dataset of 3,289 public firms headquartered in North America from 2010 to 2018 that executed 8,373 M&A deals and makes key theoretical and empirical contributions. The theoretical contribution of this paper is the explication of how, by reducing agency and transaction costs, ES may influence the likelihood of M&A. The empirical contribution of this study is the finding that while, in general, ES are positively related to horizontal M&A and negatively related to conglomerate M&A, the impact of ES is consistent with the predictions of agency theory as well as transaction cost economics in nuanced ways. As internal coordination costs in multibusiness firms increase, ES are associated with more conglomerate M&A, as ES can reduce internal coordination costs, especially when the goal is to introduce new products and enter into new markets. Analogously, in contexts where partners require transactionspecific investments that increase external coordination costs, ES are associated with a relative decrease in the number of horizontal and vertical M&A, as ES can reduce external coordination costs. These findings suggest that ES create options for managers to engage in ownership, as well as information-based coordination, to achieve economies of scale, depending on the internal and external coordination costs and the goals of the organization.

## Theory and Hypotheses

ES comprise a centralized database and a modular suite of software applications. The centralized database captures the transactions processed by ES modules and provides a realtime view of core business processes. ES also standardize cross-functional business processes. These cross-functional business process modules facilitate intra- and interorganizational integration (Stratman, 2007). In this way, the transactional and management control features of ES can be used to coordinate internal operations and organize activities with external partners.

The optimal boundary of the firm is influenced by the trade-off between internal and external coordination costs (Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991). On the one hand, when a firm makes an acquisition, it incurs the cost of operating a larger firm (i.e., the focal and the acquired firm). This cost of operating a larger firm is the internal coordination cost highlighted in agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). When internal coordination costs decrease, the firm boundary may extend because the firm can achieve economies of scale through M&A. ES reduce internal coordination costs via the standardization and automation of administrative processes. This minimizes individual discretion by easing the process of recording and reporting to management using a single database across the organization (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2000) and by enabling business process integration, because tightly coupled modules facilitate coordination among subsystems. Therefore, ES enable firms to benefit from the economy of scale and the joint ownership of assets and capabilities. Since ES can reduce the internal coordination costs associated with operating a larger firm, it may encourage firms to make acquisitions and facilitate ownership-based governance i.e., coordinating via ownership and internal hierarchy to avoid interorganizational coordination with external partners.

On the other hand, transaction cost economics posits that there are costs associated with using a market as a coordination mechanism and that the firm represents an alternative mechanism that facilitates economizing on market transaction costs (Coase, 1937). Although market suppliers have lower production costs due to the economy of scale and specialization, using the market involves significant transaction costs (also known as external coordination costs): the costs involved in finding a reliable supplier; contracting, monitoring, and enforcing the contract; and coordinating with the supplier for the duration of the contract (Clemons et al., 1993). Thus, if ES can reduce external coordination costs associated with coordinating with external business partners, ES may decrease the likelihood of M&A activities, since firms can realize the benefits of scale and integration without the commitment and risks associated with M&A (Capron & Pistre, 2002). ES can reduce external coordination costs in interorganizational relationships by integrating complex processes across organizational boundaries through workflow design and process configuration (Malhotra et al., 2005; Tanriverdi et al., 2010). Furthermore, these costs can be reduced by increasing interorganizational visibility and transparency via the monitoring features of ES along with the ability to raise alerts and support communication when contingencies needing intervention arise (Berente et al., 2016).

Consequently, ES reduce external coordination costs and offer a means to realize the benefits of economy of scale and integration without the cost and risk associated with M&A. Therefore, by lowering the cost of external coordination, ES may lead firms to coordinate their activities through information-based governance—i.e., using ES to coordinate with external partners effectively and efficiently, achieving quasi-integration, rather than through ownership-based governance as in M&A. Accordingly, ES may discourage acquisitions. Table 1 describes what internal and external coordination costs comprise and how ES affect these costs.

In summary, our principal theoretical argument is that if ES reduce the cost of operating a larger firm, ES should be related to more M&A; likewise, if ES reduce the cost associated with coordinating activities with external partners, ES should be related to fewer M&A. In this research we also emphasize and test the proposed causal mechanisms—internal and external coordination costs—that drive the influence of ES on M&A. Specifically, we introduce the information asymmetry index and customer- and supplier-industry research and development (R&D) intensities as drivers of internal and external coordination costs. Further, we also examine how ES influence different motivations for M&A, including operational synergy, introducing new products, expanding to new markets, etc.

The information asymmetry index measures the dispersion of the focal firm’s divisions across different industries. It reflects the ex ante costs the firm faces in monitoring and managing the divisions dispersed across different industries (Duchin & Sosyura, 2013). We propose that if internal coordination costs drive the relationship between ES and M&A, the relationship should be positive, and strongest among firms that face high internal coordination costs, i.e., a high information asymmetry index. Similarly, we use customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities as proxies for external coordination costs (Shenoy, 2012) because research-intensive industries tend to involve specialized inputs that require partners to invest in relationshipspecific assets (Allen & Phillips, 2000). Thus, firms that work with customer or supplier industries with high R&D intensity require more specialized inputs and are more likely to face high external coordination costs. We propose that if the ES and M&A relationship is driven by external coordination costs, we would expect to find a significantly more negative relationship when customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities are high.

Further, we study the influence of ES on different motivations of M&A. M&A can be categorized as operational synergy, introducing new products, expanding into new markets, etc. If ES influence M&A because of the reduction in internal coordination costs, ES would be expected to have a positive relationship with operational synergy and with introducing new products and markets related to M&A, especially when internal coordination costs are high. And, if ES influence M&A because of the reduction in external coordination costs, ES would be expected to have a negative relationship with operational synergy and with introducing new products and markets related to M&A, especially when external coordination costs are high.

## Methodology

## Data and Sample

Our sample comprises large public firms headquartered in North America. The unit of analysis is the firm year i.e., the number of M&A a focal firm experienced in a given year. This study has five major data sources. First, we obtained data on ES implementation from the Harte-Hanks CI database. Second, we collected M&A data from the SDC Platinum M&A database. Third, the industry-level control variables were drawn from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and the Census Bureau of United States. Fourth, firm-level control variables were calculated from the COMPUSTAT and Harte-Hanks CI databases. Finally, the instrumental variables of ITemployment and IT-salary were drawn from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. From the SDC Platinum M&A database, we obtained all the M&A completed between 2011 and 2019 (The M&A lag by one year of the ES variables) that were acquired by an entity in North America.<sup>2</sup> We categorized M&A transactions into horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate M&A. Finally, we aggregated the data to the firm-year level. This process resulted in a final sample of 3,289 unique firms<sup>3</sup> across nine years from 2010 to 2018, totaling 10,229 firm-year observations. In total, there were 8,373 M&A deals in our sample.

## Variables and Measurements

## Dependent Variables

The dependent variables in this study include the total number of M&A and the number of horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate M&A. We assessed the M&A deal type based on the primary industries the acquirer and target firms belong to, as well as the input/output flow between these two industries. Using the Input-Output Use table from BEA, we constructed a continuous vertical relatedness index (VRI) for every industry pair. For each industry pair i and j, the VRI is defined as the maximum of the dollar value of input from industry i (j) needed to produce one dollar of industry $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } \left( i ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } \right)$ output. Based on the VRI and the industries that the acquiring and target firms belong to, we define the dependent variables as follows (Fan & Goyal, 2006): A horizontal M&A is an M&A between firms belonging to the same IO industry. A vertical M&A is an M&A between firms belonging to different IO industries that have a VRI higher than 5% (Shenoy, 2012; Fan & Goyal, 2006). A conglomerate M&A is a merger or acquisition between firms in different IO industries that have a VRI of less than 5%. The choice of these three dependent variables has many advantages. First, the sum of the horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate M&A equals the total number of M&A, meaning that double counting is not an issue. Also, since ES influence both internal and external coordination costs, they may affect horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate M&A in different ways. As such, the analysis provides a comprehensive picture of the impact of ES on (horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate) M&A.

## Independent Variables

Our independent variables measure two dimensions of ES: extensiveness and standardization (Du, 2015). ES extensiveness refers to the variety and the organizational breadth of the implementation of different ES modules; ES standardization refers to the use of common ES modules from the same vendor implemented across different units of an organization. In our analysis, we consider five ES modules: accounting/finance (ACC), human resources (HR), workflow (WORKFLOW), customer relationship management (CRM), and supply chain management (SCM).<sup>4</sup> The Harte-Hanks database provides information on the status of the implementation of these five modules (installed or planned) at each firm site. We coded the module availability as 1 if it was listed as “installed” and 0 otherwise.

Harte-Hanks also provides information on which vendor was used for each of these five modules at each site.

ES extensiveness represents the implementation of the five modules/systems; its value equals the average of these five module implementations at the firm. First, we aggregated the five-module availability at the site-level to the firm level, using the proportion of employees at the firm sites as the weight. Second, we used the average of the five-module availability at the firm level as the measure of extensiveness of ES, which can be understood as the average proportion of employees at the focal firm using the five ES modules.

ES standardization is measured by an entropy measure to assess the diversity of the ES module vendors used across the different firm sites (Du, 2015). ## Control Variables

Existing research identifies a number of variables that influence M&A. First, we controlled for the industry conditions faced by the focal firm. Dynamism refers to the volatility and unpredictability of the changes that a firm has to deal with (Keats & Hitt, 1988). We controlled for the concentration of both focal and supplier industries. We used industry concentration to control for the level of competition in the industry that may influence M&A. Munificence refers to the opportunities for growth in an industry that may also influence M&A (Dess & Beard, 1984). Industry conditions such as the total volume of M&A activities in the industry are also known to affect acquirers’ behavior (Bergh & Lawless, 1998). Thus, we controlled for the total value of M&A in the acquirer industry in the current year (aggregated from SDC data). We also controlled for a possible location effect: the total number of M&A completed by firms within each Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA), excluding any M&A from the focal firm.

Other control variables include the size and financial condition of the focal firm. Multiple studies report the effect of the size of the acquirer, where large firms are known to make more acquisitions (Bruner, 2002). Acquirer performance is also an important factor in M&A, as highly successful firms may engage in more M&A (Harford, 1999). Organizational slack has also been linked to M&A (Lang et al., 1991) as firms with more slack may engage in more M&A. We also controlled for other information technology at the firm, including number of PCs and servers per employee. These variables measure the level of IT investment and IT capabilities in the focal firm. Finally, we controlled for vertical integration and diversification. A more vertically integrated firm may have a greater propensity to engage in more vertical M&A. We controlled for both related and unrelated diversification (Fan & Lang, 2000) because firms with a high level of related diversification may engage in more vertical and horizontal M&A and firms with a high level of unrelated diversification may engage in more conglomerate M&A. ## Empirical Strategy

The baseline specification was Poisson regression with instrumental variables (IVs) and firm- and year-fixed effects (with robust standard errors clustered around the firm). Although the firm-fixed effect controls for any timeinvariant omitted variables, it does not eliminate all potential endogeneity problems. Thus, we employed IVs to address the possibility of time-variant omitted variable bias. For the estimation of the Poisson regression with IVs and firm- and year-fixed effects, we used a control function approach (Wooldridge, 2010).

## Instrumental Variables (IVs)

IVs were used to correct for possible omitted variable bias and simultaneity. There could be unobservable variables that correlate with both M&A and the implementation of ES at the firm. For example, a manager who seeks more power may decide to expand the firm through M&A and, at the same time, implement ES to get better access to local information. Similarly, ex ante IT management capability could be positively related to both ES implementation and M&A activities because of the capability to extract more value from ES implementation and M&A. Another possibility is that the acquirer’s historical acquisition experience may correlate with both ES and future M&A because the acquirer’s current ES is the result of combining the acquirer’s and previous targets systems. At the same time, prior acquisition experience and recent acquisition performance are positively related to the likelihood of subsequent acquisition (Haleblian & Kim, 2006).

For our first endogenous variable, ES extensiveness, we used three IV, including total IT employment (IT-employment) and average annual IT salary (IT-salary) in each MSA from the previous year, as well as ES extensiveness in the focal MSA and industry (MSA\_IND\_ES) excluding the focal firm (Wu et al., 2018). IT-employment and IT-salary represent the total employment and mean annual wage of computer-related occupations in each of the 385-399 MSA, respectively. We weighed the mean annual wage of each IT occupation in each MSA by the total IT employment to calculate the IT-salary for each MSA year. Each firm in our sample had multiple sites that were typically in different MSA. Therefore, ITemployment and IT-salary for each firm-year observation represent weighted averages of IT employment and salaries for all MSA with a focal-firm site. MSA\_IND\_ES is calculated as follows: (1) The Harte-Hanks database provides detailed information on 553,172 sites in North America from 2010 to 2018. (2) Each site-year is categorized into a specific MSA (using the zip code of the Harte-Hanks site) industry (using two-digit SIC code). (3) For the focal firm, the variable MSA\_IND\_ES is calculated as the employee-weighted ES score (we constructed the extensiveness of the ES independent variable in the same way) of all the sites within the MSA and industry to which the focal firm’s sites belong, excluding the site of the focal firm.

Similarly, we used MSA\_IND\_STD as an IV for the second independent variable ES standardization. MSA\_IND\_STD was constructed using similar steps as MSA\_IND\_ES. (1) For each site of the focal firm, we calculated the five module standardization scores of the MSA and industry the site belongs to (with the focal site removed). (2) For each module, we used the weighted average of these site MSA\_IND\_STD measures (the weight being the proportion of employees at the site to total firm employees) to generate focal firm MSA\_IND\_STD for each module. (3) We use the average of the five module MSA\_IND\_STD calculated in Step 2 as the IV for ES standardization.

Valid IVs needed to satisfy two conditions: (1) they had to directly influence ES implementation, and (2) they had to be exogenous i.e., they could not affect M&A other than through the ES variables. First, both sets of our instruments directly affected the explanatory variables. IT-employment and ITsalary reflect the availability of local MSA IT labor/skill that was relevant to ES implementation and the cost of using such a labor pool. Therefore, these two IVs affected focal firm ES extensiveness through IT labor demand and supply.

MSA\_IND\_ES and MSA\_IND\_STD influenced the implementation of the focal firm’s ES through local peer influence. A focal firm that is in an MSA industry where extensive or standardized ES implementation is common is more likely to implement such systems and standardize them as well. For example, if peers have already implemented ES, it would exert competitive pressure to establish parity and would lower the firm’s cost of implementing ES because of the ability to learn from prior implementation by peers. We also conducted formal tests of weak IV. The first-stage results show (see more details in Table 4) that these IVs significantly correlated with the endogenous variables. Second, these IVs also met the exclusion restriction condition. For the first set of the IVs IT-employment and IT-salary, we used a one-year lag to remove the possible scenario that the focal firm might have inflated the local IT employment and salary because of a large-scale ES implementation. It is quite unlikely that the MSA IT-employment and salary from the previous year would have directly influenced the focal firm’s decision on an M&A deal.

We argue that given the strategic significance of M&A, it is improbable that the focal firm would forego an otherwise attractive deal simply because of the low availability or high cost of IT skills locally, since there are other options to fill the IT skill gap—for example, through outsourcing. Similarly, it is also quite improbable that the focal firm would have conducted an M&A deal solely because it saw an opportunity to take advantage of an oversupply or low cost of local IT labor. Additionally, MSA and industry peer extensiveness and standardization (i.e., MSA\_IND\_ES and MSA\_IND\_STD) did not directly affect the focal firm’s M&A, with the model controlling for the focal firm’s performance, slack, and product market competition. Results of the Sargan-Hansen test validate the overidentifying restriction. The test shows that when using the total number of M&A as well as horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate M&A as dependent variables, the Sargan-Hansen statistics are 1.971, 0.856, 0.032, and 2.872, respectively. The corresponding p-values are 0.3732, 0.6519 and 0.9839 and 0.2379, respectively. Since the p values are higher than 0.05, we do not reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the overidentifying restriction is valid.

## Results

As shown in Table 3, on average, each firm completed approximately 0.45 M&A in total each year: this breaks down to approximately 0.14 horizontal, 0.03 vertical, and 0.27 conglomerate M&A every year. The descriptive statistics show that firm size (both based on total employment and total assets) positively correlates with M&A. MSA M&A also has a positive correlation with the total number of M&A and all categories of M&A.

The results show that ES standardization has a positive impact on horizontal M&A (at p < 0.01 level; Column 4) and a negative impact on conglomerate M&A (at p < 0.05 level; Column 6).

## Causal Mechanisms

The findings in the baseline model suggest that focal firm ES (especially standardization) is positively related to horizontal M&A (i.e., ownership-based coordination) and negatively related to conglomerate M&A (i.e., information-based coordination). To explore the mechanism underlying these relationships, we examine the moderating effects of variables that drive internal coordination costs (information asymmetry in the firm) and external coordination costs (relationshipspecific investments).

## Internal Coordination Costs: Information Asymmetry as a Moderator

The first moderator is the information asymmetry index, a proxy for internal coordination costs. We combined two measures to calculate the information asymmetry index (Duchin & Sosyura, 2013): (1) dispersion of divisions across industries, measured as the percentage of a firm’s divisions that operate in industries with non-overlapping two-digit SIC codes; and (2) the Herfindahl index of the fraction of divisional sales in a firm’s total sales. The information asymmetry index is the average of the focal firm’s percentile ranking in the sample according to each measure, rescaled to the range of 0 to 1.

Using Compustat segment data, we calculated this after removing all single-segment firms. Firms with more dispersed industry segments face higher agency costs because managers at headquarters need to monitor and manage dispersed businesses across different industries. As the firm becomes more dispersed, the relocation of information from local units to headquarters also increases decision information costs because of poor/delayed information and thus higher internal coordination costs. Note that this information asymmetry measure is an exogenous condition in which the firm functions, regardless of ES. Thus, we used it as an ex ante measure of the internal coordination cost faced by the firm.

If the reduction in internal coordination costs is a mechanism influencing the ES-M&A relationship, it should moderate the positive relationship between ES and M&A. Since a firm with high preexisting internal coordination costs provides more opportunity for ES to exert influence on monitoring and coordinating activities and decisions, the impact of ES on M&A is likely to be more pronounced. On the other hand, ES are not as effective at reducing internal coordination costs when such costs are low to begin with. Therefore, if the reduction in internal coordination costs is a mechanism driving the relationship between ES and M&A, we would expect to find a more positive relationship between ES and M&A when internal coordination costs are high (i.e., the information asymmetry index is high).

Table 5 presents the results. In order to conserve space, we report only the main effects and interactions of the independent variables and the moderator (but the full set of control variables is included). The analysis suggests that the information asymmetry index positively moderates the relationship between ES extensiveness and total and conglomerate M&A (Model 1 and 4). When the information asymmetry index is zero (lowest level of information asymmetry), ES extensiveness is positively related to the firm’s horizontal expansion through M&A. When information asymmetry is high, ES extensiveness is positively related to total and conglomerate M&A. This indicates that ES extensiveness is effective at reducing internal coordination costs when the firm faces high costs in this area and that it encourages M&A.

## External Coordination Costs: Customer- and Supplier-Industry R&D Intensities

The empirical literature on transaction cost economics suggests R&D intensity as a measure of relationship-specific investment (Levy, 1985; Allen & Phillips, 2000; Kale & Shahrur, 2007). Levy (1985) and Allen and Phillips (2000) indicate that research-intensive industries tend to involve specialized inputs that require partners to invest in relationship-specific assets. Thus, firms that work with industries with high R&D intensity require more specialized inputs and are more likely to face high external coordination costs. Therefore, we used customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities to measure the external coordination costs the focal firm faces when coordinating with partners. Similar to the information asymmetry index, customer- and supplierindustry R&D intensities measure the ex ante external coordination costs faced by the firm, regardless of ES. Therefore, if the reduction in external coordination costs is a mechanism driving the relationship between ES and M&A, the negative relationship between ES and M&A should be most pronounced when customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities are high, i.e., when external coordination costs are high. Therefore, we added customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities to our baseline model as moderators (Shenoy, 2012).

Table 6 reports the baseline results with the above two moderators (both moderators are centered). Similar to the baseline models, the main effect of ES standardization on horizontal M&A is positive (Model 2) when customer- and supplier-industry R&D intensities are at their mean. When the focal firm works with customer industries that are R&D intensive, the positive relationship between ES standardization and horizontal M&A is attenuated (Model 2, when customer-industry R&D intensity is at 2 and 1 standard deviations below the mean, the mean, or 1 and 2 standard deviations above the mean, the marginal effects of ES standardization are 1.93, 1.87, 1.81, 1.75, and 1.70, respectively). This suggests that as partner-industry R&D intensity increases, ES are associated with a relative decrease in horizontal M&A. The analysis also suggests that ES extensiveness is negatively associated with vertical M&A as customer-industry R&D intensity increases (Model 3). These moderating effects indicate that when external coordination costs are high, firms use ES to engage in more informationbased coordination with partners rather than ownership-based governance effected through M&A. Although the internal coordination cost reduction by ES encourages the firm to engage in horizontal M&A, the high external coordination costs of working with R&D-intensive external partners offset some of the gains and reduce the number of horizontal (Model 2) and vertical M&A (Model 3).

## Discussion and Conclusion

How IT affects firm boundary choices is an enduring topic of interest in the IS field. Early studies in the literature suggest that the impact of IT on external coordination cost dominates the impact of IT on internal coordination costs and thus that IT is associated with smaller firms (Brynjolfsson et al., 1994).

Similarly, the work of Dewan et al. (1998) and Hitt (1999) using panel data on IT capital investments highlights that IT causes a decrease in vertical integration and an increase in diversification. There is also an emerging literature that suggests that IT enables firms to generate economic value from M&A by helping to integrate acquisitions and redeploy digital resources (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Tafti, 2011; Du, 2015; Benitez et al., 2018). Our research bridges the IT and firm boundary and the IT and M&A performance literatures by examining how IT affects firm boundaries by influencing the likelihood of different types of M&A because firms may undertake M&A in anticipation of performance gains. However, in contrast to the prior work that views IT investments as homogeneous, this research examines how the coordination capabilities provided by one key component of IT capital—enterprise systems—are related to the likelihood of M&A.

This paper makes important theoretical and empirical contributions. The primary theoretical contribution of this study is to explore the dual role of ES: by reducing agency costs involved in operating a larger firm, ES can encourage firms to achieve economies of scale through M&A; and by reducing the transaction cost of coordinating activities with external partners and realizing the benefits of scale through information-based coordination, ES can decrease the propensity of making acquisitions. In other words, agency theory-based arguments suggest that the capabilities provided by ES to integrate internal operations and achieve cross-unit synergies and economies of scale through streamlined information flows are likely to enhance the propensity to engage in M&A. In contrast, logic drawing on transaction cost theory suggests that ES-enabled capabilities to coordinate with external partners and achieve quasi-integration through information sharing with external partners can substitute for M&A. Thus, this paper provides the rationale to link the capabilities provided by ES, agency theory, and transaction cost economics to M&A, and highlights how ES, by reducing internal and external coordination costs, influence M&A.

The analysis, based on a sample of 3,289 unique firms across nine years from 2010 to 2018, provides support for both of these predictions under different contexts. We find that, in general, ES are positively related to horizontal M&A and negatively related to conglomerate M&A. This finding is consistent with the IT and M&A performance literature that finds that IT enables firms to generate economic value from horizontal M&A (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Tafti, 2011; Du, 2015; Benitez et al., 2018). However, as internal coordination costs in multibusiness firms increase, ES are associated with more conglomerate M&A, especially when the goal of the M&A is to introduce new products or expand into new markets. This is based on the expectation that the capabilities of managing complex operations across different divisions of a multibusiness firm are likely to lead to an increase in M&A, particularly when the firm is seeking to introduce new products or expand into new markets. This is consistent with the finding in the IT and firm boundary literature that IT is associated with more diversification (Dewan et al., 1998; Hitt, 1999); it is also consistent with findings that IT can be used to support revenue growth and profits (Mithas et al., 2017) and innovation across global operations (Saldanha et al., 2020). This suggests that information technologies like ES can be used to coordinate activities across different industries as well as across foreign markets.

Analogously, the empirical analysis suggests that in contexts where business partners require transaction-specific investments that increase external coordination costs, ES are associated with a relative decrease in the number of horizontal and vertical M&A, especially when the goal of M&A is operational synergy or new products and markets. The reason for this is that ES enable firms to coordinate with external partners through improved information sharing and to derive value from IT-enabled quasi-integration with external partners, without the need to engage in M&A. Thus, when transactionspecific investments that increase external coordination costs are involved, ES-enabled information-based quasi-integration with external partners can provide the same benefits of operational synergy and new product and market expansion as ownership-based strategy achieves via M&A. This is consistent with the finding in the IT and firm boundary literature that IT is associated with less vertical integration (Dewan et al., 1998; Hitt, 1999). Taken together, these findings suggest that ES create options for managers to engage in ownership as well as information-based coordination, depending on the internal and external coordination costs as well as the goals of the organization.

The findings of this paper have important implications for managers. Many firms have implemented ES to integrate internal operations as well as coordinate activities with external partners (Hitt et al., 2002). The findings of this research suggest that the internal and external coordination capabilities provided by ES also have implications for firm scope through M&A. Conventional logic suggests that M&A are important mechanisms to achieve economies of scale and the benefits of process integration across related activities. We find evidence that ES support this logic for ownership-based resource coordination. For multibusiness firms that face increased internal coordination costs, the capabilities provided by ES can mitigate this cost and encourage M&A, especially when the goal is new product and market expansion. Our results also suggest that information-based coordination capabilities provided by ES can be an alternative route to strengthen and rationalize operations, introduce new products and expand to new markets, and achieve economies of scale and synergy, without engaging in risky M&A, especially when transactionspecific investments increase external coordination costs. That is, since ES can reduce the costs associated with coordinating activities with external partners, ES can help firms achieve economies of scale and synergy through quasi-integration with external partners (Zaheer & Venkatraman, 1994) and obviate the need for costly/risky M&A.

Our analysis has certain limitations that suggest directions for future research. This research examines the relationship between the implementation of ES and M&A activities. Future research could examine the relationship between the actual use of ES and M&A activities. Future research might also examine the economic impact of the use of ES for information and ownership-based coordination with business partners e.g., does the use of ES for information or ownership-based coordination lead to higher performance for the focal firm as well as its partners?

This research focuses on the potential acquirers and the impact of acquirers’ ES on the likelihood of making acquisitions. It would be interesting to examine whether and how the implementation of ES affects a firm’s likelihood of being acquired. The acquirers in our sample are large public firms in North America that are typically much larger than the targets and probably have superior IT capabilities compared to the targets. Thus, the targets’ IT systems are often not a key consideration in the M&A decision (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2015). It will be important to extend this analysis to the more general case where acquirers are not large public firms and where acquirers and targets have comparable IT capabilities.

## About the Authors

Chengxin Cao is an assistant professor in the Paul H. Chook Department of Information Systems and Statistics at Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College. Her research interests are in the area of the impact of information technology (IT) on mergers and acquisitions, firm information provision, data breaches, and economics of IS.

Gautam Ray is a professor in the Information and Decision Science Department at Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. His research interests are in the area of impact of IT on firm scope and structure, and how does IT create value.

Mani Subramani is an associate professor in the Information and Decision Science Department at Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. His research interests include learning and expertise development, knowledge management in firms and the influence of technologies on organizational capabilities.

Alok Gupta is the senior associate dean for faculty, research and administration at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. He is the Curtis L. Carlson Schoolwide Chair in Information Management, and the former chair of the Information and Decision Sciences Department. He was awarded the prestigious NSF CAREER Award for his research on dynamic pricing mechanisms on the internet in 2001. He has won numerous awards including ISS Design Science Award twice (in 2011 and 2012) and AIS Impact Award in 2020. He was named an INFORMS Information Systems Society (ISS) Distinguished Fellow in 2014 and AIS Fellow in 2016. He has served in editorial positions for most major IS journals including senior editor at Information Systems Research and Journal of Management Information Systems, and assistant editor at Management Science. Since 2017, he has served as editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research.
