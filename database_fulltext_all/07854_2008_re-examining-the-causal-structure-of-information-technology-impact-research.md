# Re-examining the causal structure of information technology impact research

## Abstract

About 20 years ago, Markus and Robey noted that most research on IT impacts had been guided by deterministic perspectives and had neglected to use an emergent perspective, which could account for contradictory findings. They further observed that most research in this area had been carried out using variance theories at the expense of process theories. Finally, they suggested that more emphasis on multilevel theory building would likely improve empirical reliability. In this paper, we reiterate the observations and suggestions made by Markus and Robey on the causal structure of IT impact theories and carry out an analysis of empirical research published in four major IS journals, Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), the European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), and Information and Organization (I&O), to assess compliance with those recommendations. Our final sample consisted of 161 theory-driven articles, accounting for approximately 21% of all the empirical articles published in these journals. Our results first reveal that 91% of the studies in MISQ, ISR, and EJIS focused on deterministic theories, while 63% of those in I&O adopted an emergent perspective. Furthermore, 91% of the articles in MISQ, ISR, and EJIS adopted a variance model; this compares with 71% from I&O that applied a process model. Lastly, mixed levels of analysis were found in 14% of all the surveyed articles. Implications of these findings for future research are discussed.

## Introduction

Since IT were first introduced in organizations, researchers have been postulating their consequences or impacts on individuals, groups, and firms as a whole (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 1994; Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1997; Robey & Boudreau, 1999; Chan, 2000; Kohli & Devaraj, 2003). Leavitt & Whisler’s (1958) article represents one of the first attempts to predict how organizational structures would be altered by IT. Yet despite the prominence and importance of IT in organizations, and the volume of research that has tackled the issue since then, we are still unable to reliably generalize on the organizational transformations that result from IT implementation. Indeed, the literature investigating the organizational impact of IT has long been contradictory and inconclusive (Markus & Robey, 1988; Brynjolfsson, 1993; Robey & Boudreau, 1999; Kohli & Devaraj, 2003).

For instance, researchers have found that IT both empowers and oppresses employees, both increases and decreases organizational hierarchy, both downsizes and upsizes staff; it has even been shown that technology can simultaneously enrich some employees while it deskills others in an organization (e.g., Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1997; Robey & Boudreau, 1999). In a similar sense, IT has been shown to have negative or insignificant impacts on firm productivity, profit, and consumer surplus in some studies – known as the ‘productivity paradox’ – while having positive and significant impacts on others, substantiating the ‘value of IT’ (Attewell, 1994; Barua et al., 1995; Kohli & Devaraj, 2003).

Two main causes have been attributed to these inconclusive results. First, several methodological reasons have been pointed out. Among the most salient are a reliance on cross-sectional designs (Chan, 2000), the use of quantitative aggregated firm performance measures (Barua et al., 1995), small sample sizes (Attewell, 1994), and secondary data sources, and the use of productivity measures as the ultimate dependent variable (Kohli & Devaraj, 2003). To solve these methodological issues, researchers have proposed the following approaches: measuring performance via intermediary level variables (Barua et al., 1995), including qualitative performance metrics (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Chan, 2000), using profitability-based data for the dependent variable (Kohli & Devaraj, 2003), and adopting more longitudinal designs (Attewell, 1994; Kohli & Devaraj, 2003).

Second, Markus & Robey (1988) asserted that the lack of consistent findings and the paucity of theories advanced in IT impact research is also a result of researchers’ conceptions of the nature and direction of causality. More specifically, they recommended that IT impact theorists carefully consider the structure of their theories when designing their studies, giving adequate consideration to the causal structure and its three dimensions: causal agency, logical structure, and level of analysis. For instance, they noted that researchers had previously focused on deterministic theories, neglecting process theories and the emergent perspective, and had failed to account for indeterminate organizational changes by omitting the dynamic perspective emanating from the relationships that exist between the actors, technology, and context. Without promoting one causal structure over another, Markus and Robey also pointed out the evident dominance of the technological deterministic view of research that uses a variance theory approach over emergent process theories. In short, Markus & Robey (1988) argued that IS researchers should use multiple and complementary approaches to capture and understand the complex and evolving IT impact phenomena, develop sounder theories, and build a cumulative tradition.

We agree that sound theoretical structure, such as good theoretical substance, is necessary for better IT impact theory. For this reason, we decided to carry out an analysis of IT impact research published in four major IS journals to assess compliance with Markus and Robey’s recommendations. In this light, our work falls within the realm of MIS ‘field maintenance.’ As is the case with other such articles (e.g., Culnan & Swanson, 1986; Boudreau et al., 2001; Vessey et al., 2002; Dube´ & Pare´, 2003; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Richardson & Robinson, 2007), by assessing, and providing standards and direction for current and future IT impact research, our overall purpose is to help the broader MIS research community reflect on its work. In short, the primary question that we attempt to answer is, ‘To what extent has IT impact research published in a subgroup of leading IS journals supported Markus and Robey’s claims about causal structure?’

We also understand that causal structure cannot easily be separated from methodological and ontological issues. For instance, Markus and Robey’s recommendations might be interpreted, rightly or wrongly, as a call for researchers to shift their focus away from the more traditional (positivist) position. While we believe that the structure of theory (i.e., researchers’ conceptions of the nature and direction of causality) should not be mistakenly equated with the ontological assumptions researchers hold (for instance, whether the reality to be investigated is objective and external to the individual or the product of individual consciousness), we feel that a thorough discussion of these complex and somewhat contentious questions requires a depth of treatment that is beyond the scope of this paper. Consequently, our focus on causal structure will leave out several important issues that must also figure prominently in the development and testing of good IT impact theory.

This paper is structured as follows. The next section offers an overview of the notion of causal structure along with observations and recommendations made by Markus and Robey. Subsequently, we detail the selection of journals and articles as well as the coding process. This is followed by a presentation and discussion of the results of our analysis.

## Markus and Robey’s observations

The structure of a theory basically refers to the researchers’ conceptions of the nature and direction of causality. Like Markus and Robey, we believe that sound theoretical structure is a necessary, but not in itself sufficient condition for better theorizing about the impacts of IT in organizations. As mentioned earlier, the causal structure of theories comprises three key dimensions: causal agency, logical structure, and level of analysis. Each dimension will be discussed in turn.

First, causal agency refers to beliefs about the identity of the causal agent and the direction of causal influence. Markus & Robey (1988) argue that ‘much of our thinking about technology’s consequences in organizations has been guided by theories with fairly simple notions of causal agency’ (p. 595). On the one hand, the technological imperative perspective views IT as an ‘external force’ that engenders or determines impacts on individuals and organizations. Those who adopt this perspective believe that social actors have little or no control over the technological options and consequences of IT, whether they are related to the hierarchical structure of organizations, the nature of jobs, or the performance of firms, to name but a few. In short, organizational impacts are thought to be determined solely by the features of the technology. On the other hand, the organizational imperative assumes ‘almost unlimited choice over technological options and almost unlimited control over the consequences’ (Markus & Robey, 1988, p. 587). According to this view, impacts are not driven by the characteristics of the technology itself but rather by the motives and actions of systems designers and managers. These change agents act purposefully to realize anticipated outcomes by evaluating various options and exercising free rational choice.

In stark contrast to these perspectives that claim the presence of a dominant cause of change, the emergent perspective proposed by Markus and Robey holds that, due to the complex and unpredictable interactions between technological features, actors’ intentions, and wider organizational context, one cannot predict IT impacts. According to this view, information systems are conceived as a set of pieces of equipment, applications, and techniques that carry social meanings. This view is supported by the variety of evidence that shows how the application of identical technologies, in very similar organizational contexts, can often result in radically different organizational impacts (e.g., Barley, 1986; Pare´ & Trudel, 2007). Markus and Robey argue that the emergent perspective ‘accounts for conflicting findings about impacts by demonstrating the different meanings that the same technology acquires in different social settings’ (p. 595). In short, they recommend its adoption because of its greater ability to faithfully mirror IT impacts when compared to deterministic perspectives. Hence, our first objective is to investigate whether the observation made by Markus and Robey regarding the pervasive use of the deterministic perspective still holds true, and assess the extent to which their recommendation to adopt an emergent perspective has been followed by IT impact theorists.

The second dimension of Markus and Robey’s framework is called the logical structure. This dimension is concerned with the logical formulation of the theoretical reasoning. Markus and Robey consider two complementary groups of theories with regard to logical structure, namely, variance theories and process theories. Variance theories are concerned with predicting levels of outcome from levels of antecedents, while process theories aim to explain how outcomes develop over time. Variance theories, then, differ from process theories in their assumptions about the relationships between antecedents and outcomes. In variance theories, the antecedents are posited to be necessary and sufficient to explain a specific outcome, while in process theories the antecedents are necessary but not in themselves sufficient to explain a specific outcome. Last, contrary to variance theories, outcomes in process theories are not conceived as variables that can take on a range of values; rather, they are seen as discrete or discontinuous events.

While Markus and Robey do not argue that process models are superior to variance models, they claim that process theories have been unjustly neglected in favor of the more common variance theories. They argue that ‘by accepting a more limited definition of prediction, one in which the analyst is able to say only that the outcome is likely (but not certain) under some conditions and unlikely under others, process theories may be able to accumulate and consolidate findings about the relationship between IT and organizational change’ (1988, p. 593). Hence, they call for the advancement of process theories that will more faithfully depict how and why IT is likely to impact individuals, groups, and organizations. From this, our second objective is to investigate whether IT impact theorists have adopted a more balanced mix of variance and process theories.

The third and final dimension refers to the level of analysis of a theory. This dimension is related to the ‘entities about which the theory poses concepts and relationships’ (Markus & Robey, 1988, p. 583). In the social sciences, levels of analysis have traditionally been divided into ‘macro-level’ (e.g., organizations, populations, societies) and ‘micro-level’ (e.g., individuals, small groups) theories. Defining concepts and collecting data at levels of analysis that are appropriate for the theoretical propositions is crucial.

Markus and Robey concur that technological consequences are a mix of micro- and macro-levels; the introduction of an information system in an organization, for example, is a macro-level event that affects the skills and interactions of the individuals in the organizational setting, and their use and sense-making about the technology become institutionalized (Scott, 1995) at the organizational level. The use of mixed levels of analysis allows researchers to explore the dynamic interplay among individuals. technology. and larger social structures and, hence, is likely to improve empirical reliability. Our third and final objective is to investigate whether or not Markus and Robey’s recommendation to develop and/or test multilevel theories of IT impacts in organizations has been followed.

## Selection of journals and coding process

This section describes how the journals and articles were selected along with the basic characteristics of those articles assembled (see Figure 1). To attain our objective, we reviewed empirical research published in four journals, namely, Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), the European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), and Information and Organization (I&O) over a 15-year period (i.e., 1991–2005). Three factors determined our choice of journals. First, both MISQ and ISR are listed among the leading journals in our field in the 2007 Journal Citation Report, as well as in other recent evaluations of top-ranked journals (e.g., Lowry et al., 2004; Barnes, 2005; Nerur et al., 2005).

Second, we included EJIS which ‘provides a distinctive European perspective on the theory and practice of information systems’ (Paul, 2005, p. 208), in general, and the theory of IT impacts, in particular. The EJIS is also recognized as a top journal in our field appearing among the Association for Information Systems (AIS) Senior Scholars’ Basket of Journals.<sup>1</sup> Finally, we also considered I&O because it accepts research with a wide range of theoretical and epistemological perspectives to underpin the study of several IT phenomena (including IT impacts), and because of its overall openness to multiple paradigms. Although I&O falls outside the standard lists of ‘top journals,’ it is a respected outlet and one of the most prominent journals publishing qualitative research in the IS field (Trauth, 2001).

Each journal review started with an examination of the table of contents and then the articles themselves. Each candidate article was evaluated on the basis of two main inclusion/exclusion criteria. First, we considered only empirical studies: that is, studies where data were actually collected in the field and analyzed (e.g., questionnaire surveys, case studies, experiments, field studies, etc.). Therefore, articles such as research essays, issues and opinions, and editorial notes were excluded from our sample. The initial selection process produced a total of 778 empirical articles from 1991 through 2005.

Second, given the main purpose of our review, we considered only those studies that were specifically concerned with the impacts of IT. More specifically, in order to maintain a spirit similar to Markus and Robey’s work, our focus was on the effects of the technology itself and not on those related to the IT function, IT personnel, IT investments, or IT strategy. Hence, for instance, we did not consider studies that examined the value of technology investments through quantifiable financial measures such as ROI, market share, productivity analysis, and profitability (e.g., Dos Santos et al., 1993; Im et al., 2001; Subramani & Walden, 2001) or those that focused on impacts resulting from the alignment between the IT function and the organization (e.g., Sabherwal & Chan, 2001).

Our initial sample consisted of 189 empirical articles. We can therefore confirm that IT impacts constituted a key topic of interest in MISQ, ISR, EJIS, and I&O during the 1991–2005 period. A preliminary assessment of these articles proved to be interesting and, hence, deserves some attention. All 189 articles were classified according to journal names and publication dates. As shown in Table 1, the proportion of IT impact articles published in EJIS was lower than in the other three journals (F ¼ 3.72, Po0.05). Indeed, 31% of all empirical papers published in ISR between 1991 and 2005 were concerned with IT impacts, as compared to 27% for I&O and MISQ, and 15% for EJIS. Furthermore, although the actual proportion of IT impact articles fluctuated from year to year, it did not increase or decrease during the second half of the covered period (t ¼ -1.0, P ¼ 0.324).

A coding scheme was developed in order to classify the 189 articles in our database. As shown in Appendix A, the scheme was mainly based on Markus & Robey’s (1988) causal structure dimensions. In addition to the three dimensions of causal structure, the overall nature of the study (i.e., descriptive, theory building, or theory testing) as well as its primary methodology were also codified. In total, five attributes were coded for each surveyed article. Each article was coded independently by two of the authors. Despite the small number of codes per article, the process was demanding and required a thorough assessment of each article. While some papers contained explicit statements regarding causal agency, logical structure, and level of analysis, for the majority of the articles we extracted the information based on in-depth reading. An inter-rater agreement rate of 85% was obtained from this process, which is substantial (Landis & Kock, 1977). All disagreements were discussed and reconciled during three team meetings with all the authors present. Only one minor change had to be made to the original coding scheme: the category ‘not applicable’ was added to the logical structure dimension (variance vs process theories) in order to classify descriptive studies. Samples of coded articles from all four journals along with explanations for the coding logic can be found in Appendix B.

## Findings

Table 3 presents the nature of the causal structure in the studies surveyed. Three important points come out of this analysis. First, over 60% of the studies in our sample reflect a technological imperative, while the organizational imperative accounts for about 22% of the sample. The remaining 17% of articles adopted an emergent perspective. Interestingly, the proportion of articles adopting an emergent perspective was higher in I&O than in the other three journals (F ¼ 12.66, Po0.001). Indeed, approximately two-thirds of the IT impact studies published in I&O between 1991 and 2005 adopted an emergent perspective, as compared to 16% for EJIS, 7% for MISQ, and 6% for ISR. A second observation is that almost four studies out of five adopted a variance model, this compares with 21% that applied a process model. Here again, results vary across journals (F ¼ 14.42, Po0.001). Precisely, while the proportion of articles that developed or tested a process model in I&O is 71%, this ratio falls to 23% for EJIS, 11% for MISQ, and 6% for ISR.

Third, the data in Table 3 suggest that the investigators were mainly interested in IT impacts on the organization (26%), the individual (23%), or the group (19%). This finding supports Vessey et al. (2002) and King & He (2005) who demonstrated that, generally speaking, most empirical studies in information systems examine the organization, the individual, and the group. Further, our results confirm that relatively little academic effort has been made to evaluate the impact of IT on society (Bannister & Remenyi, 2003). In this regard, at a recent

ICIS panel session titled ‘Information Systems Research That Really Matters: Beyond the IS Rigor vs Relevance Debate,’ Omar El Sawy and Bob Galliers both suggested that further investigation of broad societal problems is needed in order to remain relevant (Desouza et al., 2005). Of utmost importance, it must be noted that Markus and Robey’s recommendation regarding the use of mixed levels of analysis was adopted in 14% of the surveyed articles. Contrary to the results associated with the causal agency and logical structure presented above, the use of mixed levels of analysis was similar across journals (F ¼ 1.08, P ¼ 0.367).

As expressed by the author herself, this model ‘provides a way of seeing that change may not always be as planned, inevitable, or discontinuous as we imagine. About 5% of the theorybuilding studies adopted a variance logical structure, while this rate was 95% for studies that tested a theory. With the exception of Hess & Kemerer’s (1994), and Macredie & Sandom’s (1999) research, all the studies that had applied a process logical structure had been undertaken to build theory.

We have thus far examined the theoretical structure of empirical research investigating the impacts of IT on organizations. While the majority of this research has adopted a deterministic perspective, using variance theories and single levels of analysis, the question remains regarding the nature of those studies that adopted a process theory, emergent perspective, and multiple levels of analysis. A total of nine articles in our database followed all three recommendations made by Markus and Robey. Of these, four were published in I&O, two were found in both EJIS and ISR, and one in MISQ.

A notable aspect of these nine articles is the theoretical lenses and perspectives on which they were based.

Indeed, seven of the studies used Giddens’s (1984) structuration framework as a theoretical lens (e.g., Orlikowski, 1996; Hayes, 2001; Barrett & Scott, 2004; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004; Vaast & Walsham, 2005). This supports the idea that the substance of theory cannot be easily separated from its causal structure. Gregor (2006), for instance, posits that the aim of structuration theory is to explain outcomes in terms of the reciprocity between human action and the social structure; by its nature therefore, structuration theory is a process-type theory, in which ‘ascriptions of causality [are] made very carefully’ (p. 625).

## Discussion

Overall, our results indicate that although MISQ, ISR and EJIS have repeatedly manifested an interest in accepting alternative research (e.g., Liebenau & Smithson, 1993; Zmud, 1995; Robey, 1996; Lee, 2000; O’Keefe & Paul, 2000; Baskerville, 2004; Weber, 2004), IT impact research published in these journals has largely remained deterministic and variance-oriented. This is in stark contrast with I&O, where approximately two-thirds of IT impact studies adopted a process/emergent perspective. For the sake of clarity, we will discuss the issues of causal agency and logical structure together and then proceed with the third and final dimension, namely, the level of analysis.

## Adoption of an emergent perspective and use of process theories

Our findings reveal that much of IT impact research published in MISQ, ISR, and EJIS is still guided by deterministic imperatives, representing 91% of the surveyed articles in these journals, while 63% of the papers in I&O adopted an emergent perspective. Similarly, a vast majority (88%) of IT impact studies published in MISQ, ISR, and EJIS developed or tested variance theories. This is in stark contrast with I&O, where 71% of the articles applied a process model. Overall, our results concur with Weber’s (2003b) view that many IS researchers still value parsimonious theories that provide a sound level of predictive power. Then the question becomes ‘Why has IT impact research published in leading journals such as MISQ, ISR, and EJIS neglected the emergent/process approach?

One potential explanation might be related to the time-consuming nature of emergent/process research. Under this line of reasoning, ‘prediction requires detailed understanding of dynamic organizational processes in addition to knowledge about the intentions of actors and the features of information technology’ (Markus & Robey, 1988, p. 589). This often entails a need to conduct detailed observations over long periods of time, from several months to a few years (e.g., Orlikowski, 1993, 1996; Majchrzak et al., 2000; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004).

Time and money are luxuries that most researchers do not have due to severe tenure and promotion constraints (Avital. 2000: Ancona et al., 2001: Weber. 2003a)

Throughout the world, academics are under increasing pressure to carry out more research, more teaching, and more administrative duties with fewer resources (Liebenau & Smithson, 1995; Weber, 2003a). The academic environment causes researchers to adopt research models that lend themselves to easy empirical testing (Weber, 2003b): that is, models that are sensitive to time constraints (Avital, 2000). If anything, researchers tend to choose a theory and methodology based on what has been applied in previous work. This is particularly true for young researchers who are trying to get tenure and promotion and who need to publish – quickly – in top journals (Ancona et al., 2001; Weber, 2003a; Dennis et al., 2006).

It has been previously argued that research diversity fosters creativity and expands the foundation upon which knowledge claims in our field are based (Robey, 1996). For instance, Sabherwal & Robey (1995) demonstrated how combining variance and process research within the same study, while maintaining their distinct forms, reveals different but related information about the information system development process. In order to promote diversity in the area of IT impact research, however, some adjustments must be made to the tenure and promotion system (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Indeed, if the tenure and promotion system better incorporated diverse quality standards into its evaluation criteria, it might encourage IT impact theorists to be more committed to conducting research projects that require in-depth, long-term endeavors, as is the case with process/emergent research.

A second explanation refers to the presence and role of ‘gatekeepers’ in the publication process. Kuhn (1962) refers to gatekeepers as those researchers who support only their own paradigm – the one considered to be the dominant one. While past and current editors-in-chief of MISQ, ISR, and EJIS have repeatedly manifested an interest in publishing alternative research and making their journals more ‘daring,’ our findings tend to demonstrate that, in the particular context of IT impact research, it will take more than ‘calls for research diversity’ to actually see what Markus and Robey described as the process/emergent perspective in print. Indeed, unless reviewers, associate editors, and even senior editors accept new ideas, theories, and methodologies that differ from well-established ones or from those for which they are recognized, and see what editors-inchief truly want (i.e., more creative questions requiring alternative methodological approaches), deterministic/ variance models might continue to be the main focus of IT impact theorists.<sup>2</sup>

The difficulty of conducting process/emergent research represents a third barrier. Indeed, qualitative data analysis is a complex and extensive endeavor that does not easily lend itself to analysis tools (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles &

Huberman, 1994; Avital, 2000; Ancona et al., 2001) and often requires several steps and extensive interpretation by researchers (Markus & Robey, 1988; Orlikowski, 1993; Langley, 1999). Like most qualitative studies, process/ emergent research requires specific skills and abilities, such as tolerating ambiguity in the pursuit of complexity (Glesne & Peshkin, 1992). In addition, the demanding requirements of emergent/process research may hinder entry into some organizations. Although emergent/ process research poses particular challenges to researchers, we concur with Markus and Robey that it represents a complementary, yet essential approach to capture and understand the complex nature of IT impact phenomena, develop better theories, and build a cumulative tradition in our field.

To conclude, various exogenous barriers and pressures may prohibit researchers from using an emergent/process approach when investigating IT impact phenomena, in particular, and IT research in general. But the scarcity of process/emergent IT impact research might also be related to endogenous factors, independently of the presence of external pressures. Such an alternative explanation unveils the notion of researchers’ active choice behavior (Oliver, 1991). Burrell & Morgan (1979) contend that organizational researchers embrace certain ontological and epistemological assumptions with which they view and investigate ‘the nature of the social world.’ More recently, Pozzebon (2004) argued that ‘Some researchers might find deterministic explanations through models that deal with a limited number of variables [y]. Other researchers, however, might prefer [y] microscopic and detailed studies of daily interactions without necessarily believing that such things as unidirectional causation and independent and dependent variables make a lot of sense. It is above all an ontological and epistemological matter.’ (p. 252). In the case of IT impact research, it is likely that a group of researchers actively choose to adopt a deterministic perspective using variance models in their works.

## Use of mixed levels of analysis

Like Markus and Robey, we argue that IT impacts in organizations are difficult to confine to a single level of analysis and suggest that researchers should use mixed levels of analysis. Adopting a multilevel perspective would give researchers a complementary viewpoint when studying IT impacts through a focus on the dynamic interplay between individuals, technologies, and larger organizational and social constructs. We have found that mixed levels of analysis were adopted in 22 studies, representing 14% of the surveyed articles. We recognize that it is difficult to perform any style of research, be it multilevel or not, well, but contend that having good methodological textbooks facilitates such undertakings. For this reason, we see the recent developments in the formalization, the foundations, and the analytical techniques associated with multilevel research as an encouraging sign. For instance, in 1999, the Academy of

Management Review devoted a special issue to multilevel theory building (e.g., Klein et al., 1999). For their part, Klein & Kozlowski (2000) edited a book that discusses the theoretical, methodological, and analytical foundations supporting multilevel organizational research and suggests new directions for multilevel theory building and research.

In the same line of thought, although multilevel techniques can be applied when conducting both variance research (e.g., Ang et al., 2002; Short et al., 2005) and process research (e.g., Orlikowski, 1996), we observed that only eight of the 22 articles in this group adopted a variance approach. One explanation may be that traditional statistical techniques – ordinary least squares, for example – have limited researchers from doing multilevel research and estimating ‘nested data structures’ (Ang et al., 2002, p. 1428). According to Klein & Kozlowski (2000), however, the recent development of statistical methods or techniques such as hierarchical linear modeling should encourage quantitative researchers to perform multilevel and cross-level estimations in the future.

Another potential explanation for the limited number of multilevel IT impact research is that IS doctoral students are seldom exposed to multilevel research. Exposing students to relevant publications and teaching multilevel analytical techniques in doctoral seminars will allow future researchers in our field to explore constructs and relationships at various levels of analysis while invoking appropriate theories about them at each level. In this regard, the 2005 MISQ paper of the year by Lapointe & Rivard, although not addressing the topic of IT impacts, represents exemplary multilevel research in the IS field.

## Conclusion

The implementation of IT within organizations almost invariably results in a wide variety of, often very significant, impacts upon the design of business, its economic performance, and the working conditions of employees (e.g., Clegg et al., 1997; Robey & Boudreau, 1999; Rivard et al., 2004). As it is widely acknowledged that the failure rates for IT projects are far too high, the need to understand the nature of organizational impacts, and how best to manage them, has become critical (Doherty & King, 2005).
