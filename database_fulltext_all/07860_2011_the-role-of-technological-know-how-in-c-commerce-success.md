# The role of technological know-how in c-commerce success

Edward Hartono <sup>a,1</sup>, Clyde W. Holsapple <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Haihao Jin <sup>c,2</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

Collaborative commerce (c-commerce), involving the use of technology to enable and facilitate interorganizational processes, is an increasingly important area for technology innovation and investment, and has become a backbone of modern business activity. A vital basis for enhancing the likelihood of c-commerce success is an understanding of the factors that contribute to that success. This research studies one set of such factors: the facets of an organization's technological know-how for performing inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration. It builds on prior research that <sup>fi</sup>nds a link between organizational know-how and successful inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration to develop hypotheses about the relationship between an organization's technological knowhow for performing inter-organizational collaboration and the success of such collaboration. These hypotheses are tested using data collected from survey respondents who have led c-commerce initiatives. We <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the proposition that there are aspects of an organization's technological-collaborative know-how that have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive relationship with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's c-commerce success.

## 1. Introduction

Successful inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration depends on effective information exchange among participants [45]. Computer-based technology has become the backbone of modern inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration [7,39], helping <sup>fi</sup>rms reap greater bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by facilitating more up-to-date (i.e., more real-time), more reliable (i.e., more accurate), and richer (i.e., more variety) exchanges of knowledge [11,55].

Inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration supported by computer-based technology is more succinctly referred to as collaborative commerce (c-commerce) [75]. C-commerce has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “electronically-enabled and facilitated collaborative relationships involving knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow among distributed participants engaged in various supply chain activities to increase value to all supply chain participants…” [1]. Central to c-commerce is the rapid computer-enabled exchange of knowledge among <sup>fi</sup>rms involved in joint activities along a supply chain [33,76]. It is a matter of getting the right knowledge to the right processors in the right <sup>fi</sup>rms at the right times in the right representations at the right cost.

Ample anecdotal and case evidence has suggested that successful implementations of c-commerce improve <sup>fi</sup>rms' operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and competitiveness [9]. A classic example is Dell Computer Corporation. Dell's success in emerging as the market leader in personal computers has been attributed to its single-minded dedication to c-commerce [37]. Dell and its suppliers have worked closely to combine their resources in planning and executing their inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, and services. This collaborative work has been strongly supported by computer-based technology. Dell's successful c-commerce implementation has enabled it to keep signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower levels of inventory (i.e., material and <sup>fi</sup>nished product inventory), to suffer considerably less stock-out incidents, and to provide better customer service than competitors.

On the other hand, ample anecdotal and case evidence has also suggested a high level of dissatisfaction with actual c-commerce outcomes relative to expectations, and a correspondingly high rate of failure [35]. An example is the case of Kmart [40]. Kmart implemented IT-supported supply chain collaboration strategy to match supply chain collaboration standards set by its major competitors, Wal-Mart and Target. However, the implementation was a failure, and has been blamed as a contributor to the bankruptcy of Kmart.

The evidence of successful and unsuccessful c-commerce implementation could well indicate that even when potential synergies are anticipated (or present) to implement c-commerce initiatives, organizations face substantial dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in attaining them [6]. This situation poses an important research question: “How can <sup>fi</sup>rms realize the full potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from c-commerce implementations?” Here, we address this question from the angle of organizational collaborative know-how through an empirical study of <sup>fi</sup>rms engaged in c-commerce.

Research in strategic management has demonstrated a positive link between the success of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and organizational collaborative know-how, which is also known as collaborative capability, alliance know-how, and alliance capability [72]. Based on many years of research tradition, strategic management researchers have developed a vast knowledge based on this subject (see an extensive literature review on this subject in [28]).

However, studies examining this subject within the context of ITsupported inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, or c-commerce, are scarce. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, detailed study of what exactly constitutes organizational collaborative know-how that links to the success of c-commerce is virtually nonexistent. This study aims to identify facets of technological collaborative know-how and evaluate which of them correlate significantly with success of c-commerce initiatives. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne technological collaborative know-how as an organization's level of knowledge and skills for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration processes.

We begin with a brief review of prior research on organizational technological know-how and its role in business process performance. Building on this work, we develop four hypotheses about facets of organizational technological know-how that may be important predictors of c-commerce success. The methodology used to collect data for studying these hypotheses is then described, followed by results of the data analysis. We then discuss results in terms of their contributions to the literature and managerial implications. The paper concludes by pointing out limitations of this study and identifying opportunities for further research.

## 2. Organizational technological know-how and business process success

Organizational technological know-how refers to sets of knowledge and skills that participants have developed and used to guide the acquisition, creation, and operation of computer-based systems that enable or facilitate the performance of business processes. Such knowledge is stored, embedded, or represented in an organization's participants, knowledge artifacts (e.g., policy manuals, patents, and training videos), culture, infrastructure (i.e., roles, relationships, and regulations), and strategy [31].

Mata, Fuerst, and Barney [49] and Ross, Beath, and Goodhue [65] contend that the level of organizational technological know-how links to the success of an IT-supported business process, and this, in turn, contributes to a firm's overall performance.

C-commerce is an IT-supported business process [27]. Central to ccommerce is the computer-enabled exchange of voluminous and rich knowledge among <sup>fi</sup>rms involved in joint activities along a supply chain [33].

Therefore, in the c-commerce context, successful c-commerce should be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively correlated with an organization's level of knowledge and skills for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration processes. Such know-how is referred to as organizational technological collaborative know-how throughout the rest of this paper.

The premise of a link between c-commerce success and this organizational technological collaborative know-how poses the basic research question for this study: “What facets of such know-how are important predictors of c-commerce success?” The next section discusses four facets of such know-how and posits their relationships with c-commerce success in the form of hypotheses.

## 3. Research hypotheses

Simonin [72] contends that an organization develops knowledge and skills about how to collaborate (i.e., collaborative know-how) through prior collaboration experience. However, this prior experience has to be assimilated before it becomes valuable organizational knowledge [30]. Simonin's [72] empirical work shows that this assimilated collaborative know-how is an important determinant of how effectively an organization enters into and manages its inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration initiatives. As an organization more fully develops its collaborative know-how, it can gain both tangible and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., products that are delivered quickly and reliably when and where they are needed, high responsiveness to short lead times, the elimination of the bullwhip effect, and improved <sup>fi</sup>rm performance) from the collaboration episodes it enters and manages [44].

In his empirical study, Simonin [72] shows that success of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the level of organizational knowhow in performing four major collaboration processes: (1) the process of identifying and selecting potential partners, (2) the process of negotiating the terms and structure of a collaborative agreement, (3) the process of managing an ongoing collaboration, and (4) the process of terminating collaboration. Simonin's work gives a structure for developing four hypotheses, as explained below and summarized in the theoretical model in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Partner identification and selection

The <sup>fi</sup>rst process refers to activities involved in searching for and selecting partners with whom there are the best prospects for successfully collaborating. Prior studies suggest that the choice of the right partner is an important variable that in<sup>fl</sup>uences collaboration success.

Harrigan [26] and Killing [38] propose and demonstrate that partner selection in<sup>fl</sup>uences the mix of resources that are available for achieving collaboration objectives. Selecting the right partner helps collaborating participants obtain resources required to accomplish the objective successfully.

The theory of complementarity and similarity in psychology and sociology [50,64,71] also contends that partner choice is associated with the amount of con<sup>fl</sup>ict among collaborating participants. Attempting to collaborate with incompatible partners involves relatively high con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Too much con<sup>fl</sup>ict impedes achievement of collaboration objectives. Therefore, selecting the right partners results in less of the con<sup>fl</sup>ict among participants that can hinder realization of collaboration objectives.

Considering the premise presented in the prior section (i.e., there is a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant link between c-commerce success and organizational technological collaborative know-how), we argue that the degree of an organization's c-commerce success is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively correlated to its know-how for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates the process of identifying and selecting the right partners. As a <sup>fi</sup>rm more fully develops its organizational technological know-how for performing this process, it can gain greater tangible and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from its c-commerce initiatives.

H1. In the c-commerce context, the higher the level of organizational know-how in applying collaborative technology to <sup>fi</sup>nd the right partner, the higher the degree of c-commerce success.

## 3.2. Negotiating objectives, infrastructure, and culture

Simonin's second process refers to activities concerned with negotiating a joint objective that is consistent with the individual goal(s) or purpose(s) of all participants. Collaboration is a cooperative effort to achieve this collective objective [27]. All collaborating participants work to agree on a collective objective as a prelude to cooperating in its achievement. Nevertheless, each participant also has and desires to realize its own individual objectives. Therefore, each participant is motivated to shape the collective objective in a way that contributes substantial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the direction of realizing its own individual objectives. Takala and Uusitalo [74] argue that successful collaboration is mutually rewarding. All collaborating participants must gain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the collaboration. Therefore, successful collaboration requires that all collaborating participants negotiate to create agreed-upon collective objective whose attainment realizes individual objectives of all participants [54].

The second process also encompasses all activities involving negotiation of agreed-upon governing or control mechanisms. These mechanisms include infrastructure and culture that govern, shape, or constrain what happens within the collaboration. Infrastructure refers to formal governance mechanisms including the roles that have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned for collaborating participants to <sup>fi</sup>ll, the relationships among these roles, and regulations that govern the use of roles and relationships [32]. Culture refers to informal governance mechanisms including values, principles, norms, unwritten rules and procedures [31]. Lane and Beamish [41] propose that, after the partners are selected, all collaborating participants establish a formal structure to determine the roles (e.g., task responsibility and decision-making authority) of each participant in the collaboration, the relationships among these roles, and the regulations that govern the use of roles and relationships. Each participant is motivated to shape the formal structure to give the maximum bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the participant. Therefore, all collaborating participants must negotiate to create formal structure that provides suitably high bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to all participants. Moreover, because collaboration is complex and elaborate [27], it is impractical to formalize all rules to govern all possible events in the collaboration. Therefore, all collaborating participants also need to negotiate informal governance mechanisms as the culture of the collaborative endeavor.

Considering the premise presented in the prior section (i.e., there is a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant link between c-commerce success and organizational technological collaborative know-how), we argue that the degree of an organization's c-commerce success is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively correlated to its know-how for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates the process of negotiating an agreed-upon collective objective, infrastructure, and culture. As a <sup>fi</sup>rm more fully develops its organizational technological know-how for performing this process, it can gain greater tangible and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from its c-commerce initiatives.

H2. In the c-commerce context, the higher the level of organizational know-how in applying collaborative technology to negotiate the objective and governance structure of the collaboration, the higher the degree of c-commerce success.

## 3.3. Collaboration management

The third major collaboration process involves activities to direct, evaluate, and enforce the actions of all participants to comply with the agreed-upon collective objective, infrastructure, and culture which have been negotiated to serve as guiding principles or to de<sup>fi</sup>ne courses of action. Successful collaboration becomes less likely when one or more collaborating participants fail to operate in line with the collective objective, infrastructure, and culture. It requires all participants to direct, evaluate, and enforce each other's actions to comply with the collective objective, infrastructure, and culture. This process of directing, evaluating, and enforcing is referred to as the collaboration management process.

Considering the premise presented in the prior section (i.e., there is a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant link between c-commerce success and organizational technological collaborative know-how), we argue that the degree of an organization's c-commerce success is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively correlated to its know-how for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates the process of collaboration management to direct, evaluate, and enforce the actions of all participants to comply with the agreed-upon collective objective, infrastructure, and culture. As a <sup>fi</sup>rm more fully develops its organizational technological know-how to perform this process, it can gain more tangible and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the c-commerce it implements.

H3. In the c-commerce context, the higher the level of organizational know-how in applying collaborative technology to manage the operation of the collaboration, the higher the degree of c-commerce success.

## 3.4. Monitoring progress

Simonin's fourth collaboration process refers to activities concerned with monitoring the progress of the collaboration in achieving the collective objective and how participants' actions conform to the collaboration culture and infrastructure as the basis for adapting and terminating the collaboration. The ability of a participant to decide when to terminate collaboration is an important in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the extent of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts participants realize from collaboration [70]. Terminating collaboration that can still deliver bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to all collaborating participants is not an optimum business decision. On the other hand, extending collaboration that should have been terminated wastes resources provided by collaborating participants to sustain the collaboration.

Considering the premise presented in the prior section (i.e., there is a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant link between c-commerce success and organizational technological collaborative know-how), we argue that the degree of an organization's c-commerce success is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively correlated to its know-how for developing and operating technology that enables or facilitates the process of monitoring collaboration progress in achieving the collective objective and how well participants' actions conform to the collaboration culture and infrastructure as the basis for adapting and terminating the collaboration. As a <sup>fi</sup>rm more fully develops its organizational technological know-how to perform this process, it can gain more tangible and intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the c-commerce it implements.

H4. In the c-commerce context, the higher the level of organizational know-how in applying collaborative technology to monitor the progress and management of the collaboration, the higher the degree of c-commerce success.

## 4. Construct measures

Upon conducting a literature review, we <sup>fi</sup>nd no study that has developed and validated an instrument to measure technological collaborative know-how. Therefore, we developed such an instrument as described below.

4.1. Measure of organizational technological know-how in finding partners (TEC\_ID)

An examination of literature within IS and strategic management reveals four distinct attributes of the “right” collaboration partners (i.e., partners having characteristics that increase the likelihood of collaboration success). Such partners are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as those with: (1) complementary resources (i.e., skills and assets), (2) compatible culture, (3) compatible infrastructure, and (4) compatible objective.

Having a collaborating partner that has complementary resources (i.e., the needed skills and/or assets) is an important factor for successful inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration [20,25]. The main bene<sup>fi</sup>t of collaboration stems primarily from the exchange of resources to compensate for one another's lack of them [42]. In the course of collaboration on a task, each collaborating participant supplies skills and/or assets that are valuable in completing the task, but which no other participant possesses in any high degree [48].

Having a collaborating partner that has compatible culture and infrastructure is critical for successful inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration [36,57,59]. Collaboration is governed by a collective culture (i.e., informal governance mechanisms) and a collective infrastructure (i.e., formal governance mechanisms). Success of collaboration is very much determined by the effectiveness of the collective culture and infrastructure in managing (i.e., facilitating, coordinating, and controlling) what happens within collaboration. The collective culture and infrastructure are adaptations from the collaborating participants' individual cultures and infrastructures. It is easier to create effective collective culture and infrastructure if participants do not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different or con<sup>fl</sup>icting individual cultures and infrastructures [36,57,59].

Having a collaborating partner that has compatible objectives is a necessary condition for inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration success [25,36,57]. In collaboration, participants aim to accomplish a collective objective. This collective objective is a mutual objective that is consistent with individual goal(s) or purpose(s) of all participants. As all participants jointly achieve this collective objective, they also realize their individual goal(s) or express their individual purpose(s) [54].

Therefore, in the context of c-commerce, we gauge organizational technological know-how for <sup>fi</sup>nding the right partners, TEC\_ID, in terms of organizational knowledge and skills that members of an organization have developed and use to guide their creation and operation of computer-based systems that help identify and select other firms having: (1) the complementary resources (i.e., skills and assets), (2) compatible culture, (3) compatible infrastructure, and (4) compatible objective. The measures for the TEC\_ID construct appear in the instrument shown in Appendix A.

4.2. Measure of organizational technological know-how in negotiating (TEC\_NEG)

An examination of literature within IS and strategic management reveals that a major determinant for collaboration success is an effective negotiation in determining the three basic aspects of collaboration:

(1) collective culture, (2) collective infrastructure, and (3) collective objective.

Frey and Schlosser [22] contend that effective contract negotiation (i.e., negotiation to establish collective formal structure or collective infrastructure) is an important factor for successful collaboration. Moreover, Arino and de la Torre [3] assert that effective negotiation to institute formal governance mechanisms (i.e., collective infrastructure) to control and coordinate interactions among participants, and to solve con<sup>fl</sup>icts, determines the value that participants can gain from collaboration. Mattessich and Monsey [52] argue that effective negotiation to reach an agreement about the collective purpose (i.e., collective objective) of the collaboration is essential to collaboration success. Takala and Uusitalo [74] contend that an effective negotiation results in not only agreed-upon procedures or formal structure (i.e., collective infrastructure) to solve con<sup>fl</sup>icts and govern relationships among collaborating participants, and a collective objective that facilitates the realization of participants' individual objectives, but also agreed-upon ethical standards for relationships among participants (i.e., collective culture).

The arguments and their supporting studies cited above indicate that effective negotiation to establish collective culture, infrastructure, and objective should link signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to collaboration success. It follows that, in the c-commerce context, organizational technological know-how for negotiating the terms and structure of a collaborative agreement, TEC\_NEG, can be gauged in terms of organizational knowledge and skills developed and used to guide creation and operation of computer-based systems to help negotiate with partners to establish: (1) collective culture, (2) collective infrastructure, and (3) collective objective. Measures for the TEC\_NEG construct appear in the instrument shown in Appendix A.

4.3. Measure of organizational technological know-how in managing collaboration (TEC\_MAN)

If we accept the proposition that effectively negotiating to establish an appropriate collective culture, infrastructure, and objective can be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributor to collaboration success, it follows that effectively managing these three aspects as collaboration unfolds can also be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributor. Conformance of collaborating participants' actions to agreed-upon standards (i.e., collective culture, infrastructure, and objective) is an important factor in successful collaboration [74]. Accordingly, the ability to effectively manage (i.e., direct, evaluate, and enforce compliance with) the collaboration culture, infrastructure, and objective can improve the likelihood of collaboration success.

It follows that, in the c-commerce context, organizational technological know-how for managing an ongoing collaboration, TEC\_MAN, can be gauged in terms of organizational knowledge and skills developed and used to guide creation and operation of computer-based systems to enable or support the process of managing: (1) the collective culture, (2) collective infrastructure, and (3) collective objective. Measures for the TEC\_MAN construct appear in the instrument shown in Appendix A.

4.4. Measure of organizational technological know-how in monitoring (TEC\_MON)

Knowledge management research suggests that sound measurement of activities gives a basis for controlling and coordinating them [30]. Measurement, in turn, depends on effective monitoring of what is transpiring. Having established a partner and negotiated the collaboration relationship with that partner (e.g., in terms of infrastructure, objective), it follows that monitoring how the collaboration is being managed and is progressing may well in<sup>fl</sup>uence the collaboration outcome. Results of monitoring give a basis for making adjustments to the management process or the negotiated collaboration relationships (e.g., objective).

At the most fundamental level, there are the ongoing management decisions about whether to continue a collaboration relationship. When objectives are not being met, timely termination minimizes undesirable effects of a collaboration relationship. On the other hand, if monitoring reveals that objectives are being met (perhaps, with adjustments along the way), then termination does not occur prematurely. The collaboration continues until it has run its natural course. Thus, we maintain that monitoring is a notable determinant of successful collaboration: (1) monitoring the management of collaboration, and (2) monitoring the progress in achieving the collective objective.

The ability of a participant to decide when to terminate collaboration is important for the participants to acquire bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from collaboration [10,70]. Extending collaboration that should have been terminated wastes resources provided by collaborating participants to sustain the collaboration. Serapio and Cascio [70] identify major reasons for termination of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration: participants' actions fail to conform to the collective culture, infrastructure, and/or objective; collaboration succeeds in achieving its collective objective; and collaboration fails to achieve its collective objective given resources available to support the collaboration. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst situation, prolonged failure to conform to the collective culture, infrastructure, and objective will result in unsuccessful collaboration [74]; hence such collaboration must be ended. Secondly, achieving the collective objective is clearly the end of a collaboration episode; thus, the collaboration is concluded [27], although the participants may later collaborate to accomplish another collective objective. In the third case, when there are insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources to achieve the collective objective, it would be fruitless to attempt continued collaboration.

A participant can justify whether to continue or terminate collaboration by monitoring whether any of the foregoing events has occurred. Various technologies offer monitoring capabilities (e.g., business intelligence dashboards). Hence, it follows that an important factor for collaboration success is the extent to which there is appropriate technology know-how for monitoring the unfolding of collaboration events. In the context of c-commerce, it follows that organizational technological know-how for developing and operating computer-based systems, TEC\_MON, can significantly contribute to ccommerce success if it enables or facilitates the observation and examination of: (1) collaboration management, and (2) progress of collaboration, as a basis for adapting and terminating the collaboration. Measures for the TEC\_MON construct appear in the instrument shown in Appendix A.

## 4.5. Measure of c-commerce success (SUCCESS)

Successful inter-organizational collaboration has been cited as a means for improving <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness [16,18,19]. As c-commerce is a special class of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, it is reasonable to assume that successful c-commerce increases <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness. Therefore, this study uses improved <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness as an indicator of the c-commerce success.

There has not been a universal and exact de<sup>fi</sup>nition for <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness [21]. This study adopts the PAIR (Productivity, Agility, Innovation, and Reputation) model for considering dimensions that contribute to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitiveness: productivity, agility, innovation, and reputation [29,34]. An underlying assumption here is that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's degree of competitiveness can be regarded as a measure of its ccommerce success. In other words, a <sup>fi</sup>rm undertakes a c-commerce arrangement to enhance its competitiveness by being more productive, agile, innovative, and/or reputable — and thereby increase its success.

Productivity refers to the ability to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently use and convert resources into products (i.e., goods and services); it is a ratio of outputs to inputs [23]. Agility refers to the ability of being alert to and able to respond to challenges and opportunities presented by changing circumstances (e.g., a turbulent environment) in a timely and flexible manner [46]. Innovation refers to the ability to generate and implement new ideas that add value to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's portfolio of products (e.g., new products, new processes for producing them) [2]. Reputation refers to the ability to create and maintain the status of being held in high esteem (e.g., via high quality products, social responsibility, brand recognition) [62].

In the context of c-commerce, it follows that successful c-commerce increases firm's: (1) productivity, (2) agility, (3) innovation, and/or (4) reputation. Measures for the SUCCESS construct appear in the instrument shown in Appendix A.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Measure pre-testing

In order to overcome problems and possible biases inherent in the use of a single item to measure a construct, researchers have advocated the use of multiple items for the measurement of a construct [13]. All constructs examined in this study are measured by multiple items. Each item is measured by using a 1-to-5 Likert-type scale (1 being “not at all” and 5 being “great”) [5,53,67]. The initial design of measures incorporated into the survey instrument was pretested by eight scholars (e.g., university professors and researchers) and practitioners (e.g., consultants) knowledgeable in IT-supported inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration. These individuals were asked to provide critiques and suggestions about the survey instrument in terms of its content and format. Their suggestions were addressed, yielding a changed instrument. The <sup>fi</sup>nal version of the questionnaire appears in Appendix A.

Organizational technological know-how for monitoring the collaboration (TEC\_MON) seems somewhat related to organizational technological know-how for managing the collaboration (TEC\_MAN). The reason we include them as two separate constructs is twofold. First, Simonin's framework clearly distinguishes between managing an ongoing collaboration, and monitoring the progress of the collaboration in achieving the collective objective and how participants' actions conform to the collaboration culture and infrastructure as the basis for adapting and terminating the collaboration. Second, the results of the measure validity assessment (see Section 5.4) suggest that TEC\_MON and TEC\_MAN are two separate constructs.

## 5.2. Survey subjects

The hypotheses are tested using cross-sectional survey data. This study focuses on a major class of c-commerce: IT-supported supply chain collaboration.

The subjects for this survey are persons in <sup>fi</sup>rms that either are currently involved, or have been involved, in implementation of ITsupported supply chain collaboration. To anchor the meaning of supply chain collaboration for purposes of the data collection, the survey clearly states that, to be quali<sup>fi</sup>ed to respond to the questionnaire, the person must have participated in his/her <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT-supported supply chain collaboration of any of the following types: (1) collaborative sourcing [24], (2) collaborative product/process design and development [73], (3) collaborative supply/replenishment planning [8], and/or (4) collaborative logistics [24].

To create the list of potential subjects, we <sup>fi</sup>rst identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>rms that have implemented IT-supported supply chain collaboration. This involved visiting websites of major supply chain collaboration support technology vendors. From the vendors' websites, we compiled a list of their client <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Second, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed employees of these <sup>fi</sup>rms who are highly likely to have in-depth knowledge about the level of organizational technological collaborative know-how in their respective <sup>fi</sup>rms. As the survey is related to IT-supported supply chain collaboration, employees appropriate to complete the survey would be supervisors of supply chain activities. It is reasonable to assume that such managers well understand the degree of organizational technological collaborative know-how of their <sup>fi</sup>rms. Hence, this study targeted such managers to complete the survey. To <sup>fi</sup>nd name and contact information for people in top or middle management that might have dealt with supply chain collaboration initiatives in their <sup>fi</sup>rms, we used Hoovers' Online, a database of about 40,000 companies, 180,000 of<sup>fi</sup>cers, and 600 industries. We also visited the websites of some of the <sup>fi</sup>rms on the list to get the information. We found managers who supervise supply chain activities have titles such as senior vice president, vice president, director, or manager of supply chain management, purchasing, procurement, inventory, electronic commerce, electronic business, logistics, inventory, merchandising, sourcing, or demand planning.

This process resulted in a pool of 425 candidate respondents, all of whom received a survey package that included a cover letter and the survey questionnaire. In cases where a recipient of the survey package knows a colleague who is more familiar with supply chain collaboration initiatives in a <sup>fi</sup>rm, the cover letter asks that the person forward the survey to that colleague. The individual completing the questionnaires might be concerned that his/her responses can be known by the <sup>fi</sup>rm and can cause him/her problems. To avoid response bias due to this factor, the cover letter also states clearly that the identity of the respondent will be strictly protected. Attempts to foster a high response rate included the offer of an executive summary of study results, respondent anonymity, reminders to non-respondents that encourage them to participate, and shortness of the survey.

## 5.3. Data collection

Fifty-two subjects returned completed, usable surveys (12% response rate). Investigation revealed several reasons for non-response. There were cases where responses were incomplete (i.e., omission of answers for some questions in the questionnaire). These are treated as non-responses for the purpose of this study. Another reason is that the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed person may not have been an appropriate person and did not forward the questionnaire to someone who was, or the targeted person may have moved and the questionnaire was not forwarded to his/her replacement. Some targeted persons explained that they were in challenging situations that did not allow time to complete the questionnaire or that company policy does not allow participation in external surveys. The response rate (12%) is comparable to much of the published survey research in the information systems literature [e.g., 43,47]. The sample size (52) is typical for published studies about inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration [e.g., 66].

Because of the high percentage of non-responses, there is a potential problem of non-response bias. This problem, if not addressed properly, can lead to conclusions that differ systematically from the actual situation in the population. To assess the existence of non-response bias, the authors compared the responses returned earlier and the ones returned later [4]. Because late respondents require prompting to respond and are therefore apparently less eager, they are likely to be similar to non-respondents. Thus, if late respondents and early respondents do not differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in certain characteristics, it is less likely that non-respondents will differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly from respondents [12]. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the two groups can indicate serious problems caused by non-response bias that can affect the validity of the interpretation of the results.

The early respondents, from 26 different <sup>fi</sup>rms, are those who returned completed surveys within 31 days of the mailing. The late respondents, also representing 26 <sup>fi</sup>rms, returned completed surveys within 32 to 93 days. We tested whether there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in early versus late means for each of the independent and dependent variables. Because we found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the means for early versus late respondents, we conclude that there is no evidence of a non-response bias that could impair the validity of the conclusions derived from the data analysis.

## 5.4. Measure validation

The reliability of the multi-item measure for each construct is assessed by using Cronbach alpha [14]. This method has been widely used in the estimation of internal-consistency reliability [58]. This internal-consistency approach to estimate reliability is based on the notion that the items are homogeneous (i.e., measuring the same phenomenon). As shown in Table 1, the Cronbach alphas of all measures used in this research are above the recommended minimum standard of 0.60 [56].

The validity assessments of multi-item measures usually include convergent and discriminant validity assessments. Convergent validity refers to a convergence among different items designed to measure the same construct [58]. Discriminant validity refers to the distinctiveness of constructs, demonstrated by the divergence of items designed to measure different constructs [58].

One way to assess convergent and discriminant validity of multiitem measures is by comparing the correlations between items measuring the same construct, with the correlations between items measuring different constructs [58,69]. High correlations for items measuring the same construct are expected to demonstrate convergent validity. Low correlations for items measuring different constructs are expected to demonstrate discriminant validity. Table 2 shows that the averages of correlations among items measuring the same construct are always higher than those of among items measuring different constructs. Therefore, on average, the correlations among items within each construct are higher than the ones among items across different constructs. This indicates that items of constructs that theoretically should be related to each other are indeed observed to be related to each other, while items of constructs that theoretically should not be related to each other are indeed observed not to be related to each other. Overall, it suggests a fair amount of convergent and discriminant validity.

Because all constructs are measured by using items in a questionnaire completed by a single respondent, it is necessary to examine whether common method variance is a serious issue. Common method variance refers to the fact that because both independent and dependent variable measures come from the same source, any defect in the source contaminates both measures [58]. Here, the examination is done based on the method suggested by Podsakoff and Organ [60]. One method of investigating whether this condition exists is Harmon's one-factor test [60], which consists of a factor analysis of all relevant variables. If a large degree of common method variance is present, one factor will emerge [60]. In this test, all items measuring the independent and the dependent variables are entered together into a factor analysis. The results of the un-rotated factor solution are examined. If substantial common method variance is present, a single factor would emerge. Results depicted in Table 3 show that there are four factors with Eigenvalues of more than one, explaining less than 72% of the total variance. No general factor is apparent.

## 7. Discussion

Considering the magnitude of c-commerce in current business practice [51], the crucial role of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's technological know-how in ITsupported business endeavors, and the fact that no study has investigated the role of technological know-how in c-commerce success, the results of this study contribute to further development of the cumulative knowledge about c-commerce success factors. Moreover, this study provides a framework for understanding the kinds of organizational technological know-how that may be required for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from its c-commerce initiatives.

This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the role of organizational technological collaborative know-how in c-commerce success. It provides a four-fold characterization in terms of an organization's know-how for applying collaborative technology to <sup>fi</sup>nd the right partner, to negotiate governance structure and collective objective of the collaboration, to manage the operation of the collaboration, and to monitor the progress and the management of the collaboration.

Perhaps it is common knowledge that the organizational technological collaborative know-how in managing and monitoring the collaboration is critical to the success of a collaboration. However, to our knowledge, no empirical study has shown what exactly constitutes this “managing” and “monitoring” construct. Our study demonstrates that in managing the collaboration, it is important to pay attention to managing the collective culture, infrastructure, and objective. Further, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that in monitoring the collaboration, it is crucial to take care to monitor the progress of the collaboration in achieving the collective objective and to monitor how well participants' actions conform to the collaboration culture and infrastructure as the basis for adapting and terminating the collaboration.

Interestingly, these results are consistent with observations in a case study conducted by Dyer et al. [17]. In the case study, the main reason for failure of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration is that the collaborating participants behaviors contradict the collective objective and governance structure. Therefore, Dyer and colleagues propose that, to be successful, collaborating participants must have a mechanism to manage the collaboration operation to make sure that collaborating participants behaviors agree with the collective objective and governance structure, and that every con<sup>fl</sup>icting behavior is quickly addressed and communicated. In c-commerce, this mechanism is facilitated by computerbased technology. Thus, for successful c-commerce, it is crucial that participants understand how to apply collaborative technology to manage the operation of the collaboration.

The case study also concludes that inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration failure is caused by extending the collaboration beyond its useful life [17]. Dyer and colleagues recommend that participants possess a mechanism to evaluate the progress and management of collaboration as the basis for adapting and terminating a collaboration. In c-commerce, this mechanism is facilitated by computer-based technology. Thus, for successful c-commerce, it is crucial that participants understand how to apply collaborative technology to monitor the progress and the management of the collaboration.

It is quite surprising that the organizational technological collabo rative know-how in <sup>fi</sup>nding partners and negotiating the collective objective, infrastructure, and culture lacks signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. There are several possible explanations for these insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships. First, as the Pearson's correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of these two relationships are highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, it may indicate that as individual factors, these two types of organizational technological collaborative know-how are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with the success of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in gaining competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>t from c-commerce. However, relative to the organizational technological know-how in managing collaboration operations and monitoring progress and management of collaboration, these two factors seem to be much less critical for success of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in gaining competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>t from c-commerce. Second, as inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration evolves [15], c-commerce could develop from successful traditional business-tobusiness relationships. Positive assessment of the arm's length relationship could lead the partners to be willing to make further commitments and investments (e.g., in IT) to increase its scope and its stakes (e.g., to engage in c-commerce) [15]. If this is the case, the partners, collective objective, infrastructure, and culture have been there in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms do not need to perform the <sup>fi</sup>rst two activities, namely <sup>fi</sup>nding the right partners and negotiating the collective objective, infrastructure, and culture. This could diminish the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the technological know-how in these two areas in organizations. Another possibility is that, in its current state of development, technology for helping with the <sup>fi</sup>rst two activities is not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently mature to be particularly valuable (that is, technological know-how pertaining to it is moot).

## 8. Theoretical implications

While research in strategic management has demonstrated a positive link between the success of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration and organizational collaborative know-how, none (to our knowledge) studies this subject within the context of IT-supported inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, or c-commerce. This is in spite of today's widespread use and reliance on c-commerce. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, detailed study of what exactly constitutes organizational collaborative know-how that links to the success of c-commerce is virtually nonexistent.

The results of this study demonstrate the association between the degree of each of these types of organizational know-how in a <sup>fi</sup>rm and the level of c-commerce success as measured by improved competitiveness that is gained by the <sup>fi</sup>rm from its c-commerce. The results show that researchers who seek to understand the cause of successful or failed c-commerce initiatives should consider organizational technological collaborative know-how. The results have also shown positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships for the organizational know-how for applying collaborative technology to manage the operation of the collaboration and to monitor the progress and the management of the collaboration. This organizational technological collaborative know-how allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire greater competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from c-commerce, particularly from collaboration managing and monitoring standpoints.

## 9. Managerial implications

The level of organizational technological know-how in applying computers to help manage the inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration is positively associated with the level of competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts achieved via the ccommerce initiative. Thus, it is important for managers to ensure that their organizations acquire and preserve knowledge and skills about how to develop and/or use computer-based systems to help manage inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration. This includes know-how about using technology to direct, evaluate, and enforce participants' behaviors to ensure conformance to the agreed infrastructure, culture, and objective.

Before a c-commerce initiative starts, it is crucial for managers to ensure that their <sup>fi</sup>rms have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient levels of collaborativetechnology know-how. When this is lacking, managers can improve such know-how from external parties by hiring consultants or champions. If these actions are not possible, to compensate for lack of such know-how, managers may attempt to reduce the complexity of managing the collaboration by selecting fewer partners (having more partners complicates the collaboration management), and/or collaborating with old partners (being more familiar with partner's characteristics simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the collaboration management).

It is also important for managers to train personnel to understand how to use the technology to direct, evaluate, and enforce partners to abide by the agreed collaboration infrastructure and culture. After completing inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, it is important for managers to assimilate (to document) the experience of using computer-based systems to manage the collaboration [72]. Such assimilation is a basis for organizational learning that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t future c-commerce initiatives.

The level of organizational technological know-how for applying computer-based systems to help monitor the management of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration is associated with the level of competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts achieved via the collaboration. It thus follows that managers must encourage ownership of a high level of know-how in applying computer-based systems to help ensure that participant behaviors are in accord with the collaboration infrastructure, culture, and objective, and that the progress of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration to achieve the objective is as expected.

Such monitoring is important as a basis to help decide whether to continue the collaboration. This decision is important. Continuing the collaboration while participants ignore the agreed infrastructure, culture, and objective can be detrimental, inef<sup>fi</sup>cient, wasteful, or ineffective. Thus, managers need to have their personnel trained to understand how to use available technology for monitoring the management and progress of the c-commerce. Where such technological know-how has not been generated and assimilated internally, it needs to be acquired from the outside.

An organization's know-how for applying collaborative technology to <sup>fi</sup>nd partners and negotiate governance structure and collective objective does not exhibit as compelling a link with a <sup>fi</sup>rm's success in gaining competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>t from c-commerce. Nevertheless, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of Pearson's correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for these two facets of collaboration indicate that, independently, they do have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant links to realizing competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>t from c-commerce. Therefore, it is important for managers to consider these types of organizational technological collaborative know-how as well, as each may play a key role in a particular c-commerce context.

## 10. Limitations

Like most social science models, this study excludes some potentially important factors contributing to levels of the dependent variable. For instance, non-technological know-how appears to be another factor in explaining the success of IT-supported inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration. Simonin [72] has already studied various facets of nontechnological know-how in the context of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration, albeit not in IT-supported collaborations. Incorporation of such additional factors into the present study, while interesting, would require a substantially longer survey instrument making data collection more problematic. This is left for future research.

Like many social science studies, this research is based on respondents' self-reported data. Hence, there is a possibility of common method variance and respondent bias (e.g., because of hesitance about con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of the information they provide). Although a test for common method variance does not reveal signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indications of this phenomenon, readers should still be aware of its possibility.

Like many surveys, this study draws only on self-reported success for reaching conclusions about c-commerce success. This method could be problematic, as the idea of “success” in the minds of the survey subjects may not be viewed as “success” by other people [68].

This study asks information from one participant involved in collaboration while collaboration always involves two or more participants. While using single informants remains the primary research design in most information systems studies, multiple informants would enhance the validity of the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Using multiple participants from the same collaboration may provide better insight about the collaboration (e.g., examining levels of knowhow possessed by each participant).

The sample size and characteristics available for this study could constrain generalizability of the interpretation of statistical analysis results. While the demographics suggest a diverse sample of persons leading supply chain initiatives, a larger sample would be ideal. A sample of leaders of c-commerce initiatives in applications other than the supply chain sector could also give insight into generalizability of results.

## 11. Conclusion and future research

As stated in the Introduction section, this study aims to identify facets of technological collaborative know-how and evaluate which of them correlate significantly with the success of c-commerce. Achieving this goal, the results of this study suggest that two facets of technological know-how correlate to successful c-commerce: (1) technological know-how in managing collaboration, and (2) technological knowhow in monitoring collaboration. The other two facets that are investigated in the theoretical model are not shown to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly correlate to successful c-commerce.

Future research can be designed and implemented to reduce or eliminate limitations identi<sup>fi</sup>ed above. In addition, future research can strive to deepen an understanding of the hypotheses and why they are supported. Or, it can seek to extend the theory that has been introduced by going beyond the four hypotheses in this study.

Based on the results reported here, it would be interesting to <sup>fi</sup>nd out speci<sup>fi</sup>cally how organizations use collaborative technology to <sup>fi</sup>nd partners, negotiate, manage, and monitor inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm collaboration processes. What speci<sup>fi</sup>c collaboration tools have been used to support each of these facets in collaboration and why? Findings of such a study could yield insights for collaboration technology vendors in providing tools that have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships with collaboration success (i.e., managing and monitoring the collaboration processes) and in devising tools for other facets of collaboration to strengthen their relationships with collaboration success. Findings of such a study could also help collaborating participants understand tools that have been successfully used to support activities having signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships with collaboration success.

Edward Hartono is on the Management Information Systems faculty in the College of Administrative Science at the University of Alabama in Huntsville. His research focuses on IT implementation, supply-chain collaboration, IT-supported collaboration, and e-commerce. Dr. Hartono's research, appearing in such journals as Decision Support Systems, MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, DATABASE for Advances in Information Systems, and Information Systems and e-Business Management, has been cited 450 times.

Clyde Holsapple, Rosenthal Endowed Chair at the University of Kentucky, is a Fellow of the Decision Sciences Institute and is Editor-in-Chief of the Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce. His research focuses on multiparticipant systems, decision support systems, and knowledge management. His books include Foundations of Decision Support Systems, Decision Support Systems — A Knowledge-based Approach, Handbook on Decision Support Systems, and Handbook on Knowledge Management.

Haihao Jin is the John Deere Supply Chain Management Fellow and Assistant Professor of Supply Chain and Operations at the University of Northern Iowa, His research studies involve collaborative decision making in supply chains; evaluating the methods, technologies and outcomes of complex corporate collaborations; global supply chain design and integration; inter-organizational information assurance and security in supply chains; and database integration and data mining in supply chains.
