# COMPARING COMPETING SYSTEMS: AN EXTENSION OF THE INFORMATION SYSTEMS CONTINUANCE MODEL<sup>1</sup>

Julian Lin Singapore Institute of Management, Blk A, Level 6, 461 Clementi Road, SINGAPORE 599491 {lin@u.nus.edu}

Hock Chuan Chan Department of Information Systems and Analytics, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, 13 Computing Drive, SINGAPORE 117417 {chanhc300@gmail.com}

Hock Hai Teo Department of Information Systems and Analytics, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, 13 Computing Drive, SINGAPORE 117417 {teohh@comp.nus.edu.sg}

Although individual adoption and use of a single system has been examined extensively, little is known about how people evaluate and compare competing systems. In this paper, we discuss and test three alternative models underlying user comparison of competing systems: separate, crossover effect, and relative comparison processes. The separate comparison process proposes that users develop separate cognitive, affective, and conative evaluations toward each system, and the between-system comparison only occurs at the point of choosing a preferred system. The crossover effect comparison process posits that users not only perform separate evaluations for each system, but also consider the competitive effects when proceeding across cognitive, affective, and conative evaluation stages. In contrast, the relative comparison process postulates that users directly compare competing systems within each of the cognitive, affective, and conative evaluation stages. Based on the IS continuance model, we tested each of these three models using data collected from 315 users of two competing instant messaging systems. Our results showed that the relative comparison process is the most parsimonious and the best model in terms of explaining the mechanisms underlying the comparison of system use by individuals. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

Keywords: Comparison processes, competing systems, cognitive-affective-conative framework, IS continuance, system use, preferred choice

## Introduction

Extant research has yielded significant insights on individual adoption and use of a single system (see Venkatesh et al., 2003; for a review, see Venkatesh et al., 2016). However, this approach may be unrealistic today, given that users can easily compare many similar systems developed by different software vendors (Xu et al., 2010). A focus on studying single systems may yield misleading results when users are considering competing alternatives at the same time. For instance, when a user is asked to rate only a single browser, such as Safari, a high rating of 6 on a seven-point Likert scale for the intention to use may not truly indicate that the user will use it. The individual might have an even higher intention (e.g., 7) to use the competing browser, Chrome. Indeed, user perceptions toward information technology (IT) applications would differ when making an isolated evaluation versus making a multiple alternatives comparison (Ho et al., 2020; Valacich et al., 2018). When users learn of a competing system that provides an easier user interface and more useful features, they may revise their beliefs and attitudes toward the existing system. More research is thus needed to examine the possible influences from competing systems on user adoption and use.

Researchers consistently found that the explanatory power of dependent variables in studies that compare competing systems is greater than those reported by single-system studies (Muthitcharoen et al., 2011; Schwarz & Schwarz (2014; Sun et al., 2016).<sup>2</sup> For instance, the explained variance in intention in a study examining the use of two competing wiki systems (i.e., PBworks and Google Sites) was higher than the explained variance in those studies examining the use of a single system (Sun et al., 2016). Hence, incorporating the influence of competing systems into existing information system (IS) adoption models has the potential to improve the predictive validity and the generalizability of the models.

Although past IS adoption and use research has been valuable in explaining the use of a single system in isolation (for a review, see Venkatesh et al., 2016), our understanding of how competing systems may affect user behavior and preferred choice is rather limited. To address this gap, we examine how individuals evaluate and compare two competing systems they have previously used and are currently using. Among IS use theories, the IS continuance model is particularly suitable for our purpose because it not only considers the continuance of use, but also assumes that users have already compared their initial expectations of the systems with their hands-on experience, which is a necessary condition for users to compare the two systems.

Drawing upon the IS continuance model and other relevant work, we develop and compare three comparison processes, each of which embodies a different assumption of how users evaluate and compare two competing systems. First, the separate comparison process posits that users develop separate cognitive beliefs, affect, and conation for the two systems. The between-system comparison only takes place when choosing a preferred system. Second, the crossover effect comparison process assumes that users not only perform separate evaluations of the two systems, but also take the aspects of the competing system into consideration when they proceed across the cognitive, affective, and conative stages. Finally, the relative comparison process assumes that users directly compare both systems within each of the cognitive, affective, and conative stages. We assess the efficacy of the three proposed comparison processes to explain system continuance intention and preferred choice. Using data collected from 315 users of two competing instant messaging systems, we found that the relative comparison process provides the best overall description of the mechanisms underlying the comparison of system use by individuals.

Our work makes important contributions to the literature on IS adoption and use by theorizing how competing systems influence users’ system evaluation and preferred choice, a widespread phenomenon that has hitherto been overlooked. Specifically, it extends the IS continuance model and shows that the continued use of a particular system is not only determined by users’ beliefs, attitudes, and conative intentions toward the same system, but also those toward the competing system. It responds to the call for research on context theorization by incorporating IS usage context into theory building (Hong et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2016). Our results suggest that this context may account for some of the inconsistent findings in IS adoption and use research; for example, the negative or nonsignificant path coefficients found between user perceptions and use of a single system (Hong et al., 2014; also see reviews by Deng et al., 2005; King & He (2006) may be explained by an excessive focus on the use of a single system. From a practical perspective, managers can leverage the knowledge gained here to analyze systems in their organization as well as those of their key rivals, which could contribute to a more comprehensive view of user preference in a competitive marketplace.

## Theoretical Background

To examine the mechanisms underlying the comparison of two competing systems, we review the cognitive, affective, and conative (CAC) framework in the attitudinal research domain (Bagozzi, 1992). In the CAC framework, cognition refers to a set of perceptions, evaluations, or beliefs that has a direct effect on affective outcomes such as attitude or satisfaction. Affect, as the emotional interpretation of these perceptions, evaluations, or beliefs, subsequently influences conation. Conation is the intentional component of motivation that drives a person to execute the behavior (i.e., cognition ➔ affect ➔ conation ➔ behavior).

Theoretical models on IS adoption and use, such as the technology acceptance model (TAM, Davis et al., 1989), the decomposed theory of planned behavior (Taylor & Todd, 1995), and the IS continuance model (Bhattacherjee (2001), are in line with the CAC framework. As such, the CAC framework serves as an appropriate theoretical framework for our comparison processes, in which conative intention to use a focal system and preferred choice may be predicted by the affective elements with respect to the focal system and the competing system that are in turn determined by cognitive perceptions and beliefs toward the two systems. Before elaborating the three comparison processes, we will first introduce the IS continuance model and discuss the dependent variable of the three processes (i.e., the preferred choice).

## Information Systems Continuance Model

The IS continuance model (Bhattacherjee (2001) has been widely used to explain an IS user’s continuance intention toward an individual system based on user perceptions of usefulness, confirmation, and satisfaction. Perceived usefulness (PU) is defined as an individual’s cognitive perception of the expected benefits of system use, and confirmation is defined as one’s cognitive perception of the congruence between the expectation of system use and its realized performance during actual use. Satisfaction is defined as one’s affect with the system and feelings of prior system use. The IS continuance model posits that an individual’s continuance intention toward a particular system is determined by his or her perception of its usefulness and use satisfaction. User satisfaction, in turn, is influenced by the two cognitive beliefs: PU and confirmation. Moreover, PU is affected by confirmation because users will try to reduce any dissonance by adjusting their future perceptions of the system when their pre-adoption usefulness expectations are disconfirmed. Figure 1 summarizes the major constructs in the IS continuance model into the cognitive, affective, and conative elements.

We chose the IS continuance model over other IS use theories for the following three reasons. First, the IS continuance model is an established model with a focus on the continued use of systems, rendering it particularly suitable for our research purposes (i.e., comparing the continuance use of two competing systems). Second, the IS continuance model is appropriate for capturing user system preferences because the parent theory of the IS continuance model—the expectation disconfirmation theory (EDT)—has been widely used to examine consumer preference and choice, ranging from video games, electronic goods to sales channels (e.g., Evangelidis & van Osselaer (2018; Goode et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2009). Third, unlike other models, the IS continuance model that presumes users have already compared their pre-acceptance expectations of the system with their hands-on experience to decide whether to continue using the system or not, serves both as an internal (within-system) comparison layer and a necessary condition for users to compare two competing systems. In particular, the three aforementioned comparison processes provide the external (between-system) comparison layer when users compare competing systems.

## Preferred Choice of Information Systems

We define preferred choice as the user preference between two or more competing systems in the same category. If an individual indicates WhatsApp as her preferred choice, it means that she is more likely to choose WhatsApp if she has to make a choice among diverse messaging applications that she is currently using. As people nowadays are often presented with multiple competing systems, preferred choice can more realistically represent individual-level IS use outcome. Indeed, when users are making an isolated system evaluation, their affect and use are primarily determined by their cognitive beliefs toward the same system. However, different beliefs will be accessible when considering alternatives (Ajzen & Kruglanski (2019; Fishbein & Ajzen (2011). Thus, the inclusion of competing systems can provide useful information and add to the prediction of behavioral intention and use (Muthitcharoen et al., 2011; Schwarz & Schwarz (2014). However, research that explicitly examines the mechanisms underlying system comparison and choice is relatively rare (Schwarz & Schwarz (2014; Venkatesh (2006).

According to existing literature, preferred choice can be predicted by affective antecedents in at least three different approaches (see Appendix A for a summary). First, conative intention can be used as a proxy of preferred choice such that a higher choice intention indicates a stronger system preference (e.g., Laroche, 1994; Polites & Karahanna (2012). Second, affective antecedents, such as satisfaction, can have a direct effect on preferred choice; for example, consumers’ share-of-wallet allocation to alternative financial institutions is determined primarily by their satisfaction resulting from prior experience with the institutions (Cooil et al., 2007). Third, affective antecedents can have an indirect effect on preferred choice via conative intention. For example, in a study on travel mode choice, Gardner and Abraham (2010) extended the theory of planned behavior and found that caruse attitudes could predict the preference for car use over other travel options via car-use intentions. In this research, we adopt the last approach because it is consistent with these extended attitudinal theories (Gardner & Abraham (2010) as well as the CAC framework.

## Three Alternative Models of Comparison Process

In this section, we discuss the three alternative models that capture comparison processes underlying the evaluation of two competing systems: separate, crossover effect, and relative comparison processes.

## Separate Comparison Process

The separate comparison process proposes that users separately develop cognitive, affective, and conative evaluations for System A (SysA) and System B (SysB), and that the between-system comparisons materialize only at the point of choosing a preferred system. The relationships between the cognitive, affective, and conative elements of each system follow the CAC framework. Figure 2 summarizes the separate comparison process for the IS continuance model.

The separate comparison process has its roots in attitudinal and marketing literature. Traditional attitudinal research has largely studied the prediction of intentions and behaviors with respect to a single course of action (Fishbein & Ajzen (2011). Acknowledging the exclusion of competing alternatives as a critical limitation, researchers have extended the original attitudinal theories, such as the theory of reasoned action, by incorporating choice among alternative courses of action (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1969; Ajzen & Kruglanski (2019). They argued that individuals would form separate intentions toward each alternative behavior and choose the alternative associated with the strongest behavioral disposition (Ajzen & Kruglanski (2019). For example, real-world transportation decisions are often informed by choices among alternative transportation modes (e.g., walk, bike, bus, and car). Individuals will compare the intention toward each option and opt for the transportation mode associated with the strongest behavioral intention (e.g., intention to drive). In the same vein, market researchers have developed the intention comparison process to model consumer brand choice (Dabholkar, 1994), according to which a consumer formed separate cognitive beliefs, attitudes, and intentions toward each brand, after which choice took place by a direct comparison of intentions across alternatives. Dabholkar further noted that this model is particularly suitable in highly familiar, frequent-use contexts wherein consumers do not need to reevaluate each alternative. This model can thus be extended to the evaluation of two familiar systems that people have used for some time and have decided to continue using. Accordingly, we propose that when comparing two competing systems, users’ cognitive, affective, and conative states toward one specific system are independent of those toward other systems and choice occurs only when comparing conative intentions across alternatives. Next, we draw from IS use research to provide further theoretical support.

An individual’s system adoption and use can be predicted by his or her affect that in turn is influenced by the cognitive perceptions about that system (Bhattacherjee (2001; Davis, 1989). Although these associations are developed in the context of evaluating a single system, they are likely to hold true when evaluating two competing systems because users can develop positive beliefs toward multiple systems. As a result, they may develop positive affect with, and decide to use, more than one system at the same time. For example, those who perceive both WhatsApp and Skype as instrumental in sending text and voice messages are likely to be satisfied with both applications and thus use both applications in their daily mobile communication. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that cognitive beliefs toward each system will have independent and positive effects on the resultant affect with system use that will in turn positively influence the continuance intention toward the corresponding system. A study that examined the adoption of two competing browsers further illustrates these associations (Chan et al., 2004). Salient to this research, users separately evaluated cognitive, affective, and conative elements of Internet Explorer (IE) and Netscape. For example, perceived usefulness of IE was found to be positively related to attitude toward the same browser that in turn had a positive effect on intention to use IE, independent of the cognitive beliefs and attitudes toward Netscape. Taken together, we anticipate that the more positive cognitive beliefs of SysA (SysB), the more positive affect with SysA (SysB), and the higher intention to continue using SysA (SysB). The cognitive beliefs toward a particular system can also directly influence the conative intention to use that system, over and above the positive or negative feelings toward using the system (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2016). The rationale here is that IS use is often viewed as the means to achieve enhanced performance and such instrumental considerations are largely based on cognitive rules and heuristics without necessarily activating the affect component (Davis, 1989). We expect a similar effect in a system choice context because one’s desire to pursue performance gains should persist when evaluating competing systems. For example, people will continue using WhatsApp and Skype simultaneously as long as they believe both systems could help them connect with friends and family, regardless of whether they like or dislike the two systems. Also, empirical evidence suggests that cognitive beliefs can be salient predictors of intention and use when multiple systems are juxtaposed for evaluation. For example, Szajna (1994) examined user choice among four to six competing database management systems and found that the intention to use one specific database and choice behavior could be predicted by the highest rating of cognitive beliefs (i.e., perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use) with an accuracy of 70 percent. In another related study, Venkatesh and Ramesh (2006) asked participants to rate both website (that are accessed through personal computers and laptops) and wireless site (that are accessed through wireless handheld devices). They found differences in evaluation criteria across the two sites such that the effect of perceived ease of use (PEoU) on site use was stronger for wireless sites. These studies suggest that one’s cognitive beliefs toward each alternative can directly predict intentions toward and use of the corresponding alternative, although they do not explicitly focus on the comparison of system use. Thus, we theorize that conative intention to use one specific system will depend on the anticipation of job performance benefits brought by system use when assessing two competing systems. That is, users’ cognitive evaluations toward SysA (SysB) could override affect in motivating them to continue using SysA (SysB). We therefore propose

Proposition 1c: The cognitive element regarding a particular system will have a positive effect on the conative intention toward that particular system.

Finally, conative intentions toward the two competing systems are expected to predict preferred choice. Many single-system studies have found that intention is positively related to adoption and use because “intention is the most proximal influence on behavior and mediates the effect of other determinants on behavior” (Venkatesh & Brown (2001, p. 76). Nonetheless, this may not hold for less preferred systems. For example, a higher conative intention to use WhatsApp may not necessarily translate into preferred choice if people have even higher intentions toward other competing alternatives such as Skype. Indeed, research has shown that when alternative options are capable of attaining goals, people will prefer to choose the alternative associated with the highest intention (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1969; Ajzen & Kruglanski (2019; Sheppard et al., 1988). Applying this logic to our context, all else being equal, people will prefer to use the system associated with a higher continuance intention. Therefore, for the preferred system, continuance intention toward one system will be positively related to user preference. Conversely, for the less preferred system, conative intention will have a negative effect on preferred choice. Thus, we posit

## Crossover Effect Comparison Process

In addition to the separate evaluations of the two competing systems, the crossover effect comparison process posits that users also form cognitive, affective, and conative evaluations with effects crossing over from one system to the other. Figure 3 presents the crossover effect comparison process for the IS continuance model. The crossover effects are derived from the extended competitive vulnerability model (ECVM) in marketing research (Laroche et al., 1994), which proposes that the formation of attitudes and intentions toward one particular brand is unlikely to be independent of the competing brand. In other words, a consumer’s attitude toward a focal brand is not only influenced by his or her cognitive beliefs about that particular brand, but also determined by the perceptions of other competing brands. Similarly, purchase intention is jointly determined by the attitude toward the focal brand and its close competitors. Although few or no IS studies have examined the possible crossover effects among competing systems, the same theoretical arguments should hold because, as suggested by the accessibility–diagnosticity framework (Feldman & Lynch, 1988), perceptions toward SysA will transfer to the competing system SysB when the perceptions of the two competing systems are accessible at the same time. Therefore, we extend the IS continuance model by incorporating crossover effects in the following three links: from affect to conation (proposition 2a), from cognition to affect (proposition 2b), and from cognition to conation (proposition 2c). To refine the mechanisms underlying the crossover effects, we draw from the social judgment theory, the belief perseverance theory, and the balance theory to provide further theoretical explanations.

Users may change their intention to continue using a particular system if their affect toward that system has changed. The social judgment theory seeks to understand the conditions under which the direction of users’ affect toward a system may change (Sherif et al., 1965). The theory maintains that people categorize the affective element into one of three zones: the latitude of acceptance, the latitude of noncommitment, and the latitude of rejection. These latitudes together form the full spectrum of the affective element from satisfaction to dissatisfaction. To illustrate, when users are satisfied with a particular system, their affect toward the system will fall within the latitude of acceptance, and they are more likely to accept and use the system as a result. When users feel neither satisfied nor dissatisfied with a particular system, their affect can be categorized into the latitude of noncommitment. These users are the ones who are most likely to change their attitudes toward the competing system. The theory further suggests that users who are highly involved in a particular system are more likely to exhibit negative evaluations toward the competing system as high involvement is associated with a wider latitude of rejection. As a result, the more extreme stance users adopt toward SysA, the greater their rejection is of SysB. It is also more difficult to persuade them to use SysB. In other words, when users hold positive affect with SysA, their affect toward SysB is more likely to fall within the latitude of rejection. As a result, they will be less likely to accept and use SysB. Also, numerous attitudina and marketing studies have provided empirical support for the negative crossover effects at the intention formation stage (Gardner & Abraham (2010; Posavac et al., 2014; Thompson & Sinha (2008). For example, it is shown that consumers with positive attitudes toward the focal brand tended to hold negative and opposing attitudes toward the competing brand and were less likely to adopt new products under the competing brand (Thompson & Sinha (2008). Similar results can be found in the IS literature. Muthitcharoen et al. (2011) argued that the behavioral intention to adopt the online store was a function of the attitude-based preference, which captured an explicit comparison of attitudes toward competing sales channels. The results showed that attitudes toward the traditional brick-and-mortar store negatively influenced the adoption intention toward the online store, suggesting that the competing alternative is likely used as a reference in intention formation. Based upon the social judgment theory as well as empirical evidence, we propose that users’ affect with a focal system will negatively affect their conative intention to use the competing system. The belief perseverance theory provides a theoretical basis for the crossover effect between cognition and affect. According to this theory, users tend to maintain the validity of their initial beliefs (Ross et al., 1975) and they “will dismiss and discount evidence that contradicts their initial views” (Lord et al., 1979, p. 2099). We extend this logic to the evaluation of two competing systems. Such an extension is reasonable because users’ knowledge about competing systems and associated attributes is often interconnected. If users hold positive beliefs about the benefits of SysA (e.g., PU), they tend to discount features and benefits offered by the competing system (i.e., SysB) and will subsequently lower their affective evaluations of SysB. Conversely, if they regard SysB as a useful system, their affect toward SysA will be negatively influenced as well. Prior research has also shown that consumers’ cognitive perceptions toward brand extensions were negatively related to their attitudes toward the stronger competing brand, providing further support for the negative crossover effects in attitude formation (Kapoor & Hesloop (2009). Thus, we expect that the cognitive beliefs about the focal system will negatively influence the affective element regarding the competing system. We hence propose that

Proposition 2b: The cognitive element regarding a particular system will have a negative effect on the affective element toward the competing system.

When assessing two competing systems, however, it remains unknown what would happen at the conative level when the cognitive and affective levels are dissonant. Would they use the competing system? ## Relative Comparison Process

The relative comparison process proposes that the cognitive, affective, and conative elements of one system could be directly compared with those of the other system. Figure 5 shows the relative comparison process for the IS continuance model.

This comparison model builds on, but differs from Dabholkar’s (1994) models of mental comparison processes in the marketing literature. This model posits that individuals would compare competing alternatives in one of the following four levels: cognitive beliefs, expectancy-value components, attitudes, or intentions. Correspondingly, four alternative choice models were developed to represent different possible mental comparison processes. For instance, the belief comparison model assumes that people would form separate cognitive beliefs within each alternative and subsequently choose one option by comparing beliefs across alternatives. After that, they would form expectancy-value, attitudes, and intentions in a relative context. However, it seems unrealistic to assume that people will develop separate evaluations within each alternative at one stage but assess alternatives in a comparative frame of reference at another stage. Mathur (1998) showed that consumer choice among competing brands could simultaneously involve the comparison of cognitive beliefs and comparison of intentions, indicating that any choice situation may involve mental comparisons at more than one level. In another related study on the choice about the use of the hog futures versus cash market, Pennings et al. (2003) found that an owner-manager’s intention toward hedging services was determined by his relative attitude toward the two alternatives that was in turn influenced by the relative cognitive beliefs such as perceived performance and perceived ease of use. This research further suggests that an individual may form cognition, affect, and conation toward competing alternatives in a comparative reference frame. Accordingly, we extend Dabholkar’s model and propose that when assessing two competing systems, users will form relative cognitive, relative affective, and relative conative elements vis-à-vis the two systems, after which relative conative intentions will determine preferred choice.

The relative affect toward the two competing systems is expected to have a positive impact on the relative continuance intention. Research on customer loyalty has noted that a favorable attitude toward one specific brand may not lead to repeated purchases because customers may hold comparable or better attitudes toward other brands in the same categories (Dick & Basu, 1994; Keiningham et al., 2015). Thus, positive attitudes with little differentiation suggest that customers are equally satisfied with the alternatives and will lead to multibrand loyalty. In contrast, attitudes that are positive but differentiated toward competing brands will translate into high relative attitudes and result in repeat patronage. This is further supported in research by Chiu et al. (2014) who examined cross-channel free riding behavior in a multichannel retailing context. The results showed that consumers’ intention to switch retailers was driven by their relative rather than absolute attitudes toward alternative retail channels. Applying this logic to our context, positive affect with one system may not necessarily lead to active use of that system because users may derive more satisfaction from the use of the competing system. Moreover, recent attitudinal research has demonstrated that incorporating relative attitudes and relative intentions toward alternative courses of action (e.g., drink vs. avoid alcohol) increased the explained variance in intentions (Ajzen & Sheikh (2013; Raats, 1995). This line of research suggested that relative attitudes toward two competing systems could better predict continuance intentions than the absolute attitude toward a single system in isolation. Based on the arguments, we believe that users will form satisfaction and continuance intention toward two competing systems in a relative manner, and they will have a higher intention to continue using SysA when they hold more favorable attitudes toward SysA relative to SysB. Hence, we offer the following proposition:

## Proposition 3a: The relative affective element will have a positive effect on the relative conative intention.

We also expect that the relative cognitive beliefs toward the two competing systems will have positive impacts on the relative affect. The reference-dependent theory of consumer choice posits that consumers evaluate product attributes as gains or losses from a reference point that is endogenous to the choice set, rather than in terms of their absolute value (Tversky & Kahneman, 1991). One commonly used reference point is currently held products (Hardie et al., 1993). This is due to the fact that characteristics of these products are more available in memory. In essence, this theory suggests that an individual’s cognitive perceptions about one system should not be interpreted in isolation. His or her perceptions with respect to the competing system can provide an additional value. Assuming that a user provides a low rating of two for the PU of SysA, the relative satisfaction toward SysA will be higher when the PU of SysB is rated as one rather than seven. There is some empirical support in marketing and IS research as well. Meuter et al. (2000) reported that consumers evaluated benefits provided by self-service technology such as PEoU in comparison with interpersonal alternatives and the resultant relative cognitive beliefs were important predictors of relative satisfaction. Similarly, Schwarz and Schwarz (2014) found relative advantage of using a new system versus the incumbent one was an important determinant of attitudes toward the new system. Thus, we propose that users will exhibit a higher level of satisfaction with one particular system when it is perceived to be clearly superior to other available alternatives in terms of perceived performance.

## Proposition 3b: The relative cognitive element will have a positive effect on the relative affective element.

Finally, relative cognitive beliefs are expected to have direct positive effects on the relative continuance intentions that in turn determine preferred choice. According to the theory of innovation diffusion, relative advantage, which refers to the degree to which a new system is perceived as being better than the incumbent, is one of the most important determinants of switching intention and preference toward the new system (Rogers (2003). Essentially, this theory suggests that users assess cognitive perceptions regarding available alternatives along multiple dimensions such as perceived efficacy of information acquisition and convenience (Choudhury & Karahanna (2008). Based on this theory, we expect that users compare cognitive perceptions of competing systems that fit the tasks at hand and their relative intention to use and preference for one system depends on whether they believe using the system provides better benefits. For example, in a study on e-government service quality, Nishant et al. (2019) observed that people conducted relative evaluations of using e-government systems in comparison to the offline alternatives. The results showed that users would continue using the e-government system as long as they perceived it to be superior to visiting or calling government offices, even when it lagged behind initial expectations. Alternatively, users will switch to alternative options if they cannot see any incremental benefits in using the focal system. For example, Hsieh et al. (2012) found that relative benefits of using social networking sites versus blogs (e.g., relative usefulness) had a positive effect on bloggers’ actual switching behavior via the intention to switch to social networking sites. By and large, we believe that users will compare cognitive beliefs toward competing systems when making system use decisions. If users perceive using SysA as being better than using SysB, they will have a higher continuance intention toward SysA and hence prefer to stay with SysA when they have to make a choice. Therefore, we propose that,

Proposition 3c: The relative cognitive element will have a positive effect on the relative conative intention.

Proposition 3d: The relative conative intention will have a positive effect on preferred choice.

## Method

This study aims to examine the three comparison processes underlying the use of two competing systems. To validate the three models, we mapped the propositions into testable hypotheses (Table 1) based on the IS continuance model, in which “cognitive element” can be operationalized as “perceived usefulness” and “confirmation,” and “affective element” and “conative element” can be operationalized as “satisfaction” and “continuance intention” respectively.<sup>3</sup> For example, the hypotheses for Proposition 2b can thus be stated as follows: “PU of a system will have a negative effect on satisfaction with the competing system (H2b1),” and “confirmation of a system will have a negative effect on satisfaction with the competing system (H2b2).”

Data were collected using a survey in which participants were instructed to compare two competing instant messaging technologies: Yahoo Messenger and Live Messenger. In this section, we discuss the measurement, participants, and data collection procedure.

## Measurement of Constructs

Our constructs were measured using validated scales from prior IS use and consumer choice research (see Appendix B for list of the items). The items for PU, confirmation, and satisfaction were adapted from Bhattacherjee (2001) and the items for intention were from Venkatesh et al. (2003). Constructs in the relative comparison process were obtained using difference comparison mechanism<sup>4</sup> (Dabholkar, 1994). That is, relative constructs were calculated by deducting the measurements for Live Messenger from the corresponding measurements for Yahoo Messenger. For example, relative satisfaction was derived by deducting the satisfaction score of Live from the satisfaction score of Yahoo. Preferred choice was measured by one item from Dabholkar: “If I had to make a choice between SysA and SysB, I would choose…” Some items were dropped or modified to fit the context of our research. For instance, the PU item “easier to do work” was removed because it suggests both usefulness and ease of use (i.e., double-barrel questions) and our student participants did not use instant messaging technologies for work purposes. We thus modified the items to capture the effectiveness of instant messaging for communication purposes. All items, except for the preferred choice, which was binary, were rated using a seven-point Likert scale.

## Pilot Study

A pilot study was conducted to assess scale reliability and validity. The participants were 110 undergraduate students enrolled in an IS course on e-commerce website development at a public university in Singapore. The respondents taking this course were suitable for the pilot study. As a portion of their coursework, they were required to learn two different integrated development environments (IDE) and editors (.Net and Java-based IDE), ensuring that they had some exposure to both environments. In addition, they were asked to choose one environment for their final project on website development. The results suggested that all constructs for the two IDEs possessed adequate composite reliability ( > .70) as well as convergent and discriminant validity (i.e., items from the same constructs loaded higher than those from different constructs) (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Gefen et al., 2000).

## Participants and Procedure

Participants were undergraduates in their early 20s at a large public university in Singapore. As noted earlier, the study was set in the context of two instant messaging technologies: Yahoo Messenger and Live Messenger. We note that the two messaging technologies and the IS continuance model are presented here solely for illustrative purposes. Future research can consider replicating and extending our study by investigating other competing systems (e.g., Safari vs. Chrome) with other IS adoption and use theories such as the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT, Venkatesh et al., 2003) to more closely examine the three comparison processes.

To analyze the preferred choice between two competing systems, it was necessary to ensure that respondents had previously used and were still using both messaging technologies. We began by first collecting more than 15,000 email addresses from students enrolled in different modules. After excluding the duplicated email addresses (as a student might be enrolled in more than one module), an invitation email was sent to 7,662 students based on the screened email addresses. Interested participants were asked to provide demographic information and indicate the instant messaging systems they had previously used and that were installed on their computers. We obtained more than 2,300 responses, out of which 480 respondents had used both Live Messenger and Yahoo Messenger before and had installed these two messaging applications on their computers and were thus qualified to participate in our study. The survey was conducted over a one-week period. Each participant was rewarded with a token of appreciation equivalent to USD \$7. A total of 316 participants completed the survey. We excluded one response that filled out all sevens. Finally, 315 responses proved usable. Out of the 315 respondents, about 65% were women. We examined the response bias by comparing the responses from early respondents versus later respondents (Armstrong & Overton, 1977), and the t-tests of the differences between the earliest 10% of the respondents and the last 10% of the respondents in terms of demographics, type of installed and used messaging systems revealed no significant differences, suggesting that response bias is not a concern here.

## Data Analysis

The three comparison processes were analyzed using LISREL. LISREL enables the simultaneous assessment of the measurement model and the structural model in one procedure; further, LISREL enables unidimensionality analysis and emphasizes overall model fit (Bollen, 1989; Gefen et al., 2000). Several fit measures have been recommended by dif ferent researchers (e.g., Gefen et al., 2000; Hair et al., 1998). We used the following eight fit indices, consisting of six model-fit indices and two error term magnitude estimates, to examine the adequacy of the model: ratio of chi-square value to degrees of freedom < 3, goodness of fit index (GFI) > 0.90, comparative fit index $( \mathrm { C F I } ) \ge 0 . 9 3$ , incremental fit index (IFI) > 0.90, normed fit index $( \mathrm { N F I } ) \ge 0 . 9 0$ , Tucker-Lewis Index $\mathrm { ( T L I ) } ~ \ge ~ 0 . 9 5$ , root mean square of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, and standardized root mean square residual $( \mathrm { S R M R } ) \le 0 . 0 5$

## Measurement Model

The measurement model for all constructs was assessed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using LISREL 8.80 and the sample covariance matrix. For the separate and crossover effect comparison processes, the initial model of nine constructs consisting of 29 items was found to have a poor model fit. Following the guidelines to use LISREL (Gefen et al., 2000; Gerbing & Anderson, 1988), an item that shared a high residual variance with other items was eliminated. Only one item, concerning PU about whether “the software enables the subjects to communicate with others more quickly” was deleted. After this deletion, the indices were all within the acceptable threshold for CFA. Specifically, the results showed that the measurement model fit the data well for the separate comparison and crossover effect comparison processes, which used the same items (÷²(276) = 369, GFI = 0.92, CFI = 1.00, IFI = 1.00, NFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.028, and RMSEA = 0.033). For the relative comparison process, all the indices also suggested a good fit (÷²(68) = 137, GFI = 0.94, CFI = 0.99, IFI = 0.99, NFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.022, and RMSEA= 0.057).

The descriptive statistics and correlations are shown in Tables 2 and 3. The statistics showed the means of all scales were above 4 and the standard deviations were close to or above 1 except for the preferred choice. As expected, all constructs with respect to the same system were positively correlated, whereas those of different systems were either negatively correlated or uncorrelated in the separate and crossover effect comparison model. Also, all constructs of Yahoo (Live) were negatively (positively) correlated with preferred choice, indicating that users preferred to use Live on average. In the relative comparison model, all relative constructs were positively correlated with each other and were negatively correlated with preferred choice, further suggesting overall preference for Live.

The scale reliability and validity of the measures were also assessed. All scales were shown to be highly reliable because the constructs for all comparison processes showed high composite reliabilities (ñ<sub>c</sub>) ranging from 0.87 to 0.98, higher than the acceptable 0.70 threshold for the field research (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). For convergent validity, the average variance extracted (AVE) for all constructs were also high at 0.70 and above, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Two tests were performed to evaluate discriminant validity. The first test evaluated the chi-square of the original CFA with its nine latent variables against other CFAs (for the relative comparison model, the original CFA with its five latent variables against other CFAs), in which any of two latent variables were set to unity (Gefen et al., 2003). The results showed that the chi-square of the original CFA was significantly smaller than any possible union of two latent variables. In the second test, the square root of the AVE for each construct was found to be greater than its zero-order correlation with other constructs in the model (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). These results provided strong evidence of discriminant validity.

We examined the common method bias using three methods: Harman’s single-factor test, comparison of the fit of a singlefactor model against that of the nine-factor measurement model, and the analysis of a marker variable with partial correlations (Lindell & Whitney (2001). Specifically, when we arranged all measurement items from all of the constructs into one principal component analysis, the factor solution demonstrated more than one factor. Further, the single-factor model was worse than the nine-factor measurement model in terms of model fit. Finally, we examined the correlations between the theoretically unrelated variable year-enrolled and other variables used in this study, and found that the relationships between the dependent and independent variables were not inflated by common method variance. In sum, these results indicated that common method bias is not a concern in our study.

## Structural Model

The structural models for the separate, crossover effect, and relative comparison processes are shown in Figures 6, 7, and 8, respectively. For each model, model fit, predictive power and the magnitude and significance of paths are considered. The results are summarized in Appendix D. The detailed model fit estimates as well as model comparison results are shown in Appendix E.

Separate Comparison Process: Overall, most of the fit statistics were within accepted thresholds, except for GFI and SRMR, which were slightly below the recommended 0.90 threshold level and above the 0.50 threshold, respectively. All paths, except for the path from perceived usefulness of SysA to continuance intention toward SysA, were statistically significant. Moreover, this model accounts for over 70 percent of the variance in continuance intention and 60 percent in preferred choice.

In addition, a comparison of SysA versus SysB yielded interesting insights. Generally, the separate comparison process provided higher predictive power for SysA than SysB. For instance, the R-square of PU for SysB was only 27% (95% CI $= [ 0 . 1 9 , 0 . 3 5 ] )$ , representing a moderate effect size, whereas that for SysA was 41% (95% CI = [0.32, 0.49]), representing a large effect size (Cohen, 1988) and an increase of 52% (or

14 percentage points). Similarly, the R-square of intention toward SysB was only 70% (95% CI = [0.64, 0.75]), whereas that for SysA was 74% (95% CI = [0.68, 0.79]), an increase of about 6% (or 4 percentage points). It is also interesting to note that the effect of PU of SysA on continuance intention of SysA was mediated by user satisfaction. In contrast, PU of SysB had a direct effect on continuance intention toward its respective system. Overall, these results suggest that examining two competing systems in a comparative frame of reference, rather than examining a single system in isolation, has the potential to yield richer results.

Crossover Effect Comparison Process: As shown in Figure 7, all fit indices were acceptable. Including crossover effects in the comparison model improved the indices for GFI, NFI, SRMR and RMSEA. Further, the results of difference tests also indicated that the crossover effect comparison process provided a better fit for the data than the separate comparison process $( ( 8 ) = 4 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Similar to the separate comparison process, all within-system effects were significant except for the path from PU of SysA to continuance intention toward SysA. Two crossover effects were supported, namely the path from confirmation of SysA to satisfaction with SysB and the path from PU of SysA to continuance intention with SysB.

The crossover effect comparison process provided somewhat better predictive power relative to the separate comparison process. Although most of the resulting R-squares were almost the same, explained variance in the continuance intention toward SysB and the preferred choice increased by 10 percent each (Figures 6 and 7). To determine whether these increases were statistically significant, we calculated the effect size of the change in R-squares using Cohen’s f<sup>2</sup> statistics, and subsequently, the result from the f<sup>2</sup> statistics was used to compute the pseudo F statistic<sup>5</sup> (Gefen et al., 2011). Regarding the continuance intention toward SysB, Cohen’s f<sup>2</sup> was 0.30—a moderate effect size, according to Cohen’s (1988) definition. The pseudo F statistic was 92.83 $( p <$ 0.001). Regarding the preferred choice, the amount of increase in explained variance corresponded to a moderate effect size of 0.18 (pseudo $F = 5 3 . 8 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). These results indicated that the inclusion of the negative crossover effects from SysA to SysB significantly increased the explanations of the two dependent variables. In sum, we conclude that the crossover effect comparison process is better than the separate comparison process.

Relative Comparison Process: The results of the relative comparison process indicated that all indices were within acceptable ranges (Figure 8). Note that SRMR, GFI, and NFI were slightly better than other two models, suggesting that the relative comparison process provided a better fit. Moreover, as the relative comparison process was not nested within other two models, Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and consistent AIC (CAIC) were employed to compare model fit. As shown in Appendix E, the relative comparison process had the smallest value of AIC and CAIC. This further indicates that the relative comparison process best fits the data among the three models. All paths were significant (p < 0.001).

We subsequently compared the results of the relative comparison process to those of the crossover effect comparison process because the crossover effect comparison process is more comprehensive than the separate comparison process. The relative comparison process provided slightly better predictive power for all dependent variables except for the preferred choice (see Figures 7 and 8). For example, the R-square of relative PU was 52% (95% CI = [0.44, 0.59]) but those of crossover effect comparison process ranged from 28% (95% CI = [0.20, 0.37]) to 40% (95% CI = [0.31, 0.48]), an increase of 30 and 86 percent, respectively. Similarly, the Rsquare of relative intention in the relative comparison process was 80% (95% CI = [0.76, 0.84]), but those of the crossover effect comparison process ranged from 75% (95% CI = [0.70, 0.79]) to 77% (95% CI = [0.72, 0.81]), an increase between 4 and 7 percent. However, the crossover effect comparison process provided a better prediction for preferred choice. The R-square of preferred choice in the relative comparison process was 54% (95% CI = [0.48, 0.61]), but that of the crossover effect comparison process was 66% (95% CI = [0.59, 0.72]), a decrease of about 22 percent.

In summary, the relative comparison process, where a comparison is made in each stage of the CAC framework, emerged as a better and more parsimonious model among the three with the highest explanatory power for all dependent variables, except for preferred choice. In contrast, the crossover effect comparison process, the least parsimonious model, had the highest R-square for preferred choice and provided a more nuanced view of the comparison process by highlighting the effects of the competing systems flowing through the cognitive, affective, and conative stages. The separate comparison process, the model that only considers the effects of the competing system at the choice formation stage, had the worst fit to the data as well as the lowest explanatory power among three models.

Continuance Intention as a Mediator: To examine whether an alternative model using PU and satisfaction to predict preferred choice directly would be better than our proposed model, we conducted mediational analyses for the three processes. Specifically, the effects of PU and satisfaction on preferred choice became nonsignificant with the inclusion of continuance intention in the separate comparison process and crossover effect comparison process. For the relative comparison process, the effect of satisfaction on preferred choice decreased significantly when we included continuance intention. The explained variance in preferred choice in the alternative model was lower compared to our proposed model. Taken together, these results suggest the importance of continuance intention as a mediating variable between PU, satisfaction, and preferred choice.

## Discussion

The present work extends the IS continuance model to examine user comparison of two competing systems that they have previously used and are currently using. Specifically, we proposed and tested three comparison processes (i.e., separate, crossover effect, and relative comparison processes) based on a multidisciplinary literature review. Our results provided strong empirical support for the three comparison processes, all of which demonstrated a good fit with the data collected in this study. Further, the three comparison processes explained about 70 percent to 80 percent of the variance in continuance intention, a noticeable improvement over the baseline IS continuance model (i.e., 41 percent) (Bhattacherjee (2001). The explained variance in preferred choice was also substantial, ranging from 54 percent to 66 percent, which were comparable to those reported by studies involving a system choice scenario (e.g., Lin et al., 2006; Szajna, 1994). These findings suggest that the inclusion of the competing systems can provide additional richness and predictive validity for the IS continuance model.

Before highlighting the contributions of the work, we first acknowledge the limitations that may have affected our results. First, preferred choice was measured using selfreported data. Researchers have expressed concerns with the validity of self-reported measures in predicting actual behavior. However, this is less of concern in our study because the intention-behavior association is expected to be stronger when users are evaluating multiple alternatives in a comparative mode than when they are making an isolated evaluation (Sheppard et al., 1988). Second, our confirmation scale only captures confirmation and not disconfirmation because satisfaction only occurs by confirmation. Bhattacherjee also pointed out that “confirmation is positively related to satisfaction with IS use because it implies realization of the expected benefits of IS use” (2001, pp. 355-356). However, future research can include disconfirmation when using the IS continuance model. Besides, we used direct measurements of confirmation that may distort the joint effects of component measures (i.e., pre-exposure expectation and post-exposure experience) on IS use outcomes (Brown et al., 2012; Venkatesh & Goyal (2010). Future research could separately measure the components of disconfirmation and use polynomial modeling and response surface analysis to capture the differential impacts of the magnitude and direction of disconfirmation. Finally, the results may be limited to the point of time when the study was conducted. That is, the results may be different as the messaging landscape evolves. For instance, the competitive effects of Live Messenger may be stronger after it is merged with Skype. It is thus necessary for companies to conduct timely and regular analysis on competing systems.

This work on comparison of competing systems also opens exciting avenues of research. First, future research can extend our comparison processes in different system use settings with other IS adoption and use theories such as UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003). It would be interesting to examine the role other important predictors and contingencies of system use can play when more than one system is being investigated.

For instance, cognitive antecedents of a focal system, such as PEoU, compatibility, and performance expectancy, may manifest different negative effects toward the competing system, and it would be important to identify factors that are most salient in affecting the competing system. Another direction for future research is to investigate the use of competing systems with richer datasets. For example, researchers can consider using econometric approaches, such as discrete choice models, to analyze the factors that are important in determining system use and choice behavior. Finally, the use of instant messaging applications in the workplace might be different from the use of the messaging services among student populations examined in our study. Therefore, researchers may want to extend this research to workplace system use in an organizational context in the future.

## Contributions to Research

One major contribution of this work was to incorporate the effects of competing systems into the explanation of system continuance intention and preferred choice. As one of the most mature streams in the IS discipline, prior research has accumulated considerable knowledge on the critical factors and contingencies that motivate individuals to adopt and use a single system. Yet, little is known about how individuals make comparisons of system use when presented with multiple competing systems. To address this gap, we developed and tested three comparison processes to capture different mechanisms through which a competing system could affect IS use based on the IS continuance model. The separate comparison process proposes that the comparison only occurs at the choice formation stage, and found that user preferred choice was jointly determined by the intention to continue using the focal system as well as the competing system. The crossover effect comparison process further underscored the effects of competing system by including negative crossover effects at affect, conation, and choice formation stages. One interesting result was that PU of Yahoo Messenger not only had a positive effect on the continuance intention toward Yahoo Messenger, but also had a negative effect on the continuance intention toward Live Messenger. Finally, the relative comparison process posits that users form mental comparisons for competing systems in each of the cognitive, affective and conative stages. For instance, relative PU had a positive effect on relative satisfaction that in turn had a positive effect on preferred choice via relative intention.

Second, this work answers the call for context theorizing in IS literature (Hong et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2016). There is a strong interest in IS research to embrace context when theorizing about an individual’s use of system (Benbasat & Zmud (2003; Hong et al., 2014; Orlikowski & Iacono (2001). For example, Venkatesh et al. (2016) claimed that adopting IS use theories to identify new context-effects theories or to refine current context effects is a promising direction for future research (p. 342). Indeed, recent studies noted that system assessment and use decisions varied depending on contexts of use (i.e., evaluating individual systems separately versus evaluating multiple systems simultaneously) (Ho et al., 2020; Valacich et al., 2018). Distinct from this line of work, we focus on the comparison processes underlying the evaluation of two competing systems. By incorporating cognitive, affective, and conative states of individuals toward the competing system as new explanations of IS continued use and preferred choice, our research could significantly refine the theoretical mechanisms underlying system use and increase the practical relevance of IS use theories.

Our work also contributes to IS literature by suggesting that some of the inconsistent findings found in IS adoption and use research could be explained by the evaluation context. Specifically, some studies have reported negative or nonsignificant path coefficients between user cognitive perceptions of system (e.g., PU and PEoU) and system use (see reviews by Deng et al., 2005; King & He (2006). There have been a number of explanations for these inconsistencies, including, for example, varied conceptualizations and operationalizations of the same construct (Benamati et al., 2010), neglect of context-specific factors that are relevant to system, user, and usage context characteristics (e.g., Hong et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Our research enriches this line of inquiry by suggesting that some inconsistencies could be explained by an excessive focus on the use of a single system in isolation. To elaborate, if we were to apply the IS continuance model to study Yahoo Messenger as a single system, which was similar to the traditional approach adopted by most previous studies, the results—although encouraging—would be misleading if individuals were actually considering multiple systems side by side. Specifically, the mean value of continuance intention toward Yahoo Messenger was moderately high at approximately 5.56 (see Appendix C), illustrating that users might intend to use Yahoo. However, when taking the competing system Live Messenger into account, one can see that users exhibited an even higher continuance intention toward Live (M = 6.4) and that intention to use Yahoo had a negative effect on preferred choice, indicating that users prefer to continue use Live Messenger. Consequently, when interpreting the results from single-system studies, one should be cautious of the potential effects from the competing system.

## Contributions to Practice

This research has several implications for practice. First, our results suggest that managers should not only investigate their own systems, but also other competing systems to gain a holistic picture of user preference. They can apply our comparison processes to map the position of their own company as well as their key rivals. For example, our results on the crossover effect comparison process revealed that the competitive effect of Live Messenger on Yahoo Messenger was somewhat limited compared to that of Yahoo. Specifically, users’ confirmation and perceived usefulness of Yahoo had negative effects on their satisfaction with and intention to continue using Live respectively, but not vice versa. These results suggest that it should be easier to encourage the users of Live Messenger to convert to Yahoo, but it would be more difficult to persuade Yahoo users to switch to Live. Again, we need to note that we have used student samples, and the results might differ among other populations. Still, our work can help vendor companies focus their competitive strategies among similar rival systems or conduct their own studies in the context of their systems and organizations.

Second, our work can provide useful implications for system design. For example, our results showed that users put more weight on system usefulness when evaluating Live Messenger than when evaluating Yahoo Messenger. The separate comparison process results revealed that, when users develop separate cognitive perceptions for competing systems, PU was a more dominant factor in predicting the continuance intention toward Live Messenger than Yahoo Messenger. And the effect of PU of Live Messenger on intention to continue using this messaging system became even stronger when the crossover effect from Yahoo Messenger was considered (â = 0.19, t = 3.42 vs. â = 0.26, t = 4.93; see Figures 6 and 7). Thus, software development managers of Live Messenger should undertake more efforts on functionality improvement to satisfy the needs of users. Another recommendation is that designers should periodically evaluate the competing systems to more effectively support their users as nowadays systems release feature updates more frequently.

Third, our work provides insights for IS managers in making informed plans for system planning and implementation, particularly for those organizations that cannot afford multiple competing systems. Before implementing a new system, managers should first examine the possible dependence between the new system and existing similar systems. This approach could help them understand the important predictors of system use when more than one system is considered. Moreover, when organizations have decided to migrate to the new system, managers could apply our comparison processes and persuade employees to use the new system by emphasizing its relative benefits (e.g., relative PU) from users’ perspective.

## Conclusion

This research aims at furthering our understanding of user comparison of competing systems. While prior research has revealed a variety of factors and contingencies involved in individual adoption and use of a single system, little is known about how users evaluate and compare competing systems. To fill this void, we developed and discussed three comparison processes by drawing on the IS continuance model, the CAC framework, and other theories from marketing and psychology literature. The results showed that the relative comparison process was the most powerful and parsimonious model underlying the comparison of systems. Overall, this work advanced the IS continuance model in particular and the IS adoption and use research in general by providing key insights into the influence from competing systems. We believe our work is particularly relevant in a competitive world in which consumers are presented with strong and credible alternatives.
