# How do IT outsourcing vendors develop capabilities? An organizational ambidexterity perspective on a multi-case study

Wenyu (Derek) Du<sup>1</sup> , Shan L. Pan<sup>2</sup> and Junjie Wu<sup>1</sup>

Journal of Information Technology 2020, Vol. 35(1) 49–65 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2020 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621988775DOI: 10.1177/0268396219887756 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Vendor capabilities are an important research stream in the IT outsourcing literature. The extant studies have generated rich findings on how vendors develop capabilities, but these findings have mainly been based on the condition of vendors improving their existing business. How vendors develop capabilities to move up the value chain has received little prior research attention. A key challenge for vendors in moving up the value chain is the development of new capabilities while maintaining the existing ones. This challenge is consistent with achieving exploration and exploitation in organizational ambidexterity. Hence, we adopt organizational ambidexterity as our theoretical lens. By conducting a case study on four IT outsourcing vendors in China, we identify four classifications for achieving ambidexterity in capability development and the context for each classification. These classifications are based on combinations of different structural arrangements and processes for achieving ambidexterity. Our study contributes to the research on IT outsourcing vendor capabilities by improving our understanding of capability development in vendors moving up the value chain. It also contributes to the organizational ambidexterity literature by identifying the factors that influence the choices of different structural arrangements and processes.

## Keywords

IT outsourcing, vendor capabilities, capability development, value chain, exploitation, exploration, organizational ambidexterity, process, structure, case study

## Introduction

IT outsourcing (ITO) remains an important research topic because the market continues to grow (Lacity et al., 2016). According to Gartner, the worldwide ITO market grew 4.6% in 2016 and reached approximately \$283.5 billion that year (Huntley and Blackmore, 2017). Today, clients are outsourcing not only basic services for cost savings but also more sophisticated services that are critical to their core business (Oshri et al., 2015, 2018). Successful vendors develop new capabilities for these higher value services and move up the value chain.

Vendor capabilities have been an important research stream in the ITO literature. The prior studies have focused on three topics: what the capabilities are (Ethiraj et al., 2005; Feeny et al., 2005; Levina and Ross, 2003; Nevo and Kotlarsky, 2014), how the capabilities drive performance (Han et al., 2013; Palvia et al., 2010; Plugge et al., 2013), and how to develop these capabilities (Su, 2013; Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008; Levina and Ross, 2003).

This study focuses on capability development. Although extant studies have generated rich findings, they have mainly focused on the condition of vendors improving their current business and overlooked how vendors develop capabilities to move up the value chain. Findings from extant studies are mostly about practices that strengthen and adjust the current capabilities, which do not lead to new capabilities for high-value services (Lacity et al., 2010;

Levina and Ross, 2003). In fact, Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) suggested that such practices can trap a vendor in the existing business and prevent it from moving up the value chain.

Our study intends to fill this gap by answering the following question: How do ITO vendors develop capabilities for moving up the value chain? Vendors moving up the value chain need to develop new capabilities, but at the same time, they also need to maintain current capabilities. Maintaining current capabilities not only provides the financial resources and knowledge for building new capabilities (Garud et al., 2006) but also sustains the client relationships that are needed for new capability development (Su, 2013; Su et al., 2014).

However, coping with these two activities is challenging because they compete for resources (Levina and Ross, 2003) and rely on contradictory mechanisms that can cause conflicts (Garud et al., 2006). These challenges are consistent with those in achieving exploration and exploitation in organizational ambidexterity (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; Gupta et al., 2006). Hence, we adopt organizational ambidexterity as our theoretical lens and intend to examine how vendors achieve ambidexterity when developing capabilities for higher value services.

Regarding how vendors achieve ambidexterity, we identify two concepts from the ambidexterity literature: the structural arrangement and the process. Existing studies have identified different structural arrangements and processes and examined their strengths and weaknesses but have not examined how to choose between them. These choices are important to ITO vendors because capability development in ITO is context-specific (Ethiraj et al., 2005; Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008), and different vendors are likely to adopt different structural arrangements and processes.

Guided by these two concepts, we conduct a multi-case study of four Chinese ITO vendors that have moved up the value chain and, during the process, effectively coped with the development of new capabilities and the maintenance of existing capabilities. Chinese ITO vendors provide an attractive research context to answer our research question because, compared to the multinational vendors and established Indian vendors, Chinese vendors are still in an early stage and striving to move up the value chain (Su, 2013, 2015). Based on the dimensions of structural arrangement and process, our data analysis unveils four classifications for achieving ambidexterity and the context for each classification.

Our study makes two important theoretical contributions. First, it extends the capability development literature from the condition of improving existing business to the condition of moving up the value chain. Instead of offering a generic approach for all vendors, our study identifies four capability development approaches that vendors can choose from to respond to higher-end demands. Second, our study extends the ambidexterity development literature by identifying the factors that explain the choices between different structural arrangements and processes.

## Literature review

## Existing perspectives on ITO vendor capabilities

The studies on ITO vendor capabilities have adopted three perspectives, answering three questions (see Figure 1). The component perspective addresses what the capabilities are, the implication perspective addresses how the capabilities drive performance, and the development perspective addresses how to develop these capabilities. The component perspective is the foundation of the other two because researchers need to first identify the capabilities before examining their implications and development. These three perspectives are not mutually exclusive. For example, Levina and Ross (2003) examined both the components of vendor capabilities and the mechanisms for developing them; Su (2013) examined both the implications of vendor capabilities on vendor internationalization strategy and the processes of developing those capabilities.

For the component perspective, the seminal work of Levina and Ross (2003) identified three components of vendor capabilities: client management capabilities reflect the resources and routines that align vendor practices with client demands; process capabilities reflect the resources and routines in system design, development, and deployment; and human resource capabilities reflect the resources and routines that produce a capable workforce. Ethiraj et al. (2005) identified two types of capabilities: client-specific capabilities arise from repeated interactions with a client and reflect the tacit knowledge of the client’s business domain, and project management capabilities reflect technical capabilities in the software development process and can be leveraged across clients.

Moreover, Feeny et al. (2005) identified 12 fine-grained capabilities that clients can use to evaluate vendors and organized them into three types, that is, delivery, relationship, and transformation capabilities. Nevo and Kotlarsky (2014) extended Levina and Ross’s (2003) findings to the context of a primary vendor utilizing crowdsourcing in service delivery. They found that all three capabilities identified by Levina and Ross (2003) need to be extended to accommodate the online crowd workforce.

For the implication perspective, studies examined how capabilities drive outsourcing results and vendor performance. Palvia et al. (2010) found that vendor capabilities contribute positively to partnership quality, service quality and deliverable quality, which in turn improve vendor performance. Han et al. (2013) found that vendor capabilities contribute positively to project performance, and the contribution is stronger when they are complemented by client capabilities. Plugge et al. (2013) found that adjustments in vendor capabilities can lead to enhanced outsourcing perfor mance, and the enhancement is stronger when there is a fit between vendor capabilities and their organizational structure. Su (2013) found that vendors’ capabilities can influence their internationalization strategy, and this influence can be either direct or mediated by client relationships.

For the development perspective, studies examined the mechanisms and processes of capability development. Although not the focus of their study, Levina and Ross (2003) identified several mechanisms for developing each of the three capabilities they identified. For example, client management capabilities can be developed through enhanced client communication and expertise sharing, process capabilities can be developed through investment in the capability maturity model (CMM) and identification of best practices, and human resource capabilities can be developed through staff rotation, training programmes and reward structures. Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) extended these mechanisms to the context of small subcontractors working with a primary vendor instead of the end client. They found that for small subcontractors, the implementation of these mechanisms is constrained by the limited access to the end client, and to overcome the constraint, new learning mechanisms are needed.

Studies also found two general processes for capability development, that is, deliberate investment and learning by doing. Ethiraj et al. (2005) found that capabilities can be developed through either deliberate, persistent investment or learning-by-doing processes. Similarly, Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) found that some vendors develop capabilities through extensive, deliberate training, while others develop capabilities through trial-and-error learning.

Ethiraj et al. (2005) explained the choice based on the type of the capability: deliberate investment is suitable for capabilities that can be leveraged across clients, while learning by doing is suitable for capabilities specific to a client. Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) explained the choice based on the scale of the vendor: large vendors are more likely to develop capabilities through deliberate investment, whereas small vendors lack the resources for such investment and are more likely to leverage trial-and-error learning. An important conclusion from the prior studies is that there are no universally best practices for capability development, and vendors should choose their practices based on their contexts.

Despite the rich findings, the extant studies have mainly considered the condition of improving existing business and overlooked the condition of moving up the value chain. The capability development practices uncovered based on the former condition cannot effectively explain practices in the latter condition. An important reason is that in the latter condition, capability development faces the challenge of coping with the conflicting demands of developing new capabilities and maintaining the current ones. For example, when Infosys develops new consulting capabilities while maintaining its existing technical capabilities, significant conflicts arise in the training programmes and reward structures applicable to these two operations (Garud et al., 2006).

Our study intends to extend the prior studies to the condition of vendors moving up the value chain by examining how vendors cope with these two conflicting demands. Although Garud et al. (2006), based on the case study of Infosys’s movement from low-end software development services to high-end consulting services, have identified an emergent-platform approach, the study does not take a capability development perspective and is only based on one organizational context.

The emergent-platform approach recommends that vendors build generative properties into the organizational design and form a platform for new capabilities to emerge during daily operations. Vendors in different contexts may use different approaches. For example, although not the focus of their study, Su et al. (2014) and Su (2013) have suggested approaches that are different from the emergentplatform approach. Su et al. (2014) found that some vendors develop new capabilities by setting up new ventures rather than letting new capabilities to form during daily operations; Su (2013) found that some vendors develop new capabilities by following a planned, strategic resourceseeking process rather than an emergent process.

## Existing perspectives on achieving ambidexterity

Organizational ambidexterity refers to an organization’s ability to simultaneously pursue two contradictory activities such as exploitation and exploration (Duncan, 1976; Gupta et al., 2006). Exploitation refers to the refinement of existing knowledge and resources and involves learning in the current trajectory, while exploration refers to the acquisition of new knowledge and resources and often involves learning in a new trajectory (Benner and Tushman, 2003; March, 1991; Oshri et al., 2005; Rivkin and Siggelkow, 2003). Maintaining current capabilities consists of exploitation activities, while developing new capabilities consists of exploration activities.

Many studies on how organizations achieve ambidexterity have focused on structural arrangements. These studies can be divided into two categories. The first category advocates structural differentiation, where organizations assign exploitation and exploration activities to two different business units to minimize the conflicts and integrate them at a higher management level (Jansen et al., 2009; Taylor and Helfat, 2009). Structural differentiation can also occur across organizations. For example, some organizations focus their internal operations on exploitation activities while acquiring external parties such as startups for exploration activities (Puranam et al., 2006; Rothaermel and Deeds, 2004).

The second category advocates structural integration, where organizations integrate exploitation and exploration activities in one business unit by encouraging individual employees to balance these two tasks in their daily activities (Andriopoulos and Lewis, 2009; Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; Raisch et al., 2009). Although structural integration is an organization-level property, it manifests in the actions of individuals throughout the organization (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004). Structural integration is also known as contextual ambidexterity because conducive organizational contexts such as performance expectations and social support are needed to encourage individuals to cope with conflicting demands in daily activities (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; Im and Rai, 2014).

Both structural arrangements have their strengths and weaknesses. Structural differentiation minimizes the tensions between exploitation and exploration activities but increases the managerial overhead to coordinate them (Benner and Tushman, 2003; Raisch et al., 2009; Smith and Tushman, 2005). Structural integration minimizes the managerial overhead but increases the cognitive pressure on individuals who need to alternate between conflicting tasks in daily activities (Du et al., 2013; Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; Raisch et al., 2009).

The prior studies have further examined the factors that can enhance the effectiveness of these two arrangements. Structural differentiation can be enhanced by mechanisms that facilitate coordination between exploitation and exploration activities. One example is the individuals who facilitate information flow and asset sharing between groups. These individuals are known as organizational linkages (Taylor and Helfat, 2009) or cross-functional interfaces (Jansen et al., 2009). Structural integration can be enhanced by mechanisms that empower individuals to alternate between the two conflicting tasks. Examples include IT-enabled learning mechanisms (Kane and Alavi, 2007), knowledge management tools (Du et al., 2013; Im and Rai, 2008), and IT infrastructure (Benitez et al., 2018).

Despite the rich findings around these two structural arrangements, Raisch et al. (2009) suggested that these findings mainly take a static perspective, assuming that an organization can achieve ambidexterity by adopting a differentiation or an integration configuration. However, achieving ambidexterity is a process that requires constant managerial intervention, investment, and learning.

The extant studies have also examined the process of achieving ambidexterity. Some studies suggested a planned process. For example, Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) found that an organization can purposefully blend exploitation and exploration activities. Similarly, Siggelkow and Levinthal (2003) found that an organization can balance exploitation and exploration activities by first focusing on exploration activities and then shifting attention to exploitation activities. Other studies suggested an emergent process. For example, Garud et al. (2006) found that an organization can build a platform for exploration activities to emerge from exploitation activities. Similarly, Khanagha et al. (2014) found that an organization can balance two competing business models by spontaneously alternating between exploitation and exploration.

A planned process allows organizations to prepare resources and organizational contexts beforehand, but it is not adaptive to changes (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004; Montealegre, 2002; Siggelkow and Levinthal, 2003). In contrast, an emergent process is flexible in that it allows organizations to respond to emergent situations, but it is more costly to coordinate resources due to the lack of plans (Garud et al., 2006; Khanagha et al., 2014).

Although existing studies have identified different structural arrangements and processes and examined their strengths and weaknesses, the questions of how to choose between them remain to be answered. One possible reason is that these arrangements and processes have not been juxtaposed in one study.

## Methodology

We chose the case study methodology for two reasons. First, this research intends to explore how vendors develop capabilities for moving up the value chain. A ‘how’ question is better answered through inductive methods (Walsham, 1995). Second, ambidexterity and the efforts to achieve it are embedded in a complex organizational context (Smith and Tushman, 2005). The case study methodology allows researchers to immerse themselves in the context and to generate embedded insights (Pan and Tan, 2011). Our case study followed the interpretive paradigm. We followed Walsham’s (1995, 2006) instructions on how to use theory, conduct fieldwork, analyse data and construct findings.

## Case setting

We selected four Chinese ITO vendors. All four vendor moved up the value chain, and during the process, managed to develop new capabilities while maintaining existing ones. Vendor A used to be a successful software provider that developed embedded systems for Japanese car manufacturers. After the initial success, some clients started to request consulting services from the vendor, such as the development of an enterprise system. Vendor A developed new capabilities to address these demands and expanded into the consulting business. During the expansion, Vendor A maintained client satisfaction in its software development business.

Vendor B used to be a call centre which an international enterprise software provider set up in China to support its Japanese clients. Employees answered clients’ calls regarding how to use the parent company’s complicated systems such as the enterprise resource planning (ERP) system and the customer relationship management (CRM) system. In 2007, the parent company intended to outsource more advanced tasks to the vendor, such as diagnosing clients technical issues and offering technical workarounds. Vendor B developed new capabilities to address these new demands and expanded into the technical support business. During the growth, Vendor B maintained high-level client satisfaction in its call centre business.

Vendor C used to be a corporate messaging service provider. In 2008, clients started to request services that could enhance their productivity to be added to the messaging system. Vendor C developed new capabilities to respond to these new demands. New services such as teleconferencing, enterprise blogs, and CRM were added to the messaging system. During the expansion, Vendor C maintained its leadership in the corporate messaging business.

Vendor D used to be an IT infrastructure provider specializing in telecommunications and data storage. In 2008, clients started to request green IT services that could reduce their energy footprints. Vendor D developed new capabilities to respond to these new demands and then expanded into the green IT business. During the expansion, Vendor D maintained its leadership in the infrastructure business.

## Data collection

Data collection occurred between June 2009 and April 2014. Interviews were the primary source of data. In total, we interviewed 55 informants from the four organizations: 11 from Vendor A, 14 from Vendor B, 16 from Vendor C, and 14 from Vendor D. The informants ranged from senior managers to middle managers to line employees (for the informants’ roles, please see Appendix A). Interviews across different levels allowed researchers to triangulate the data and prevent elite bias (Klein and Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995). Each interview session lasted from 45 min to 1 h. The interviews were conducted in Chinese. All the authors can speak Chinese fluently. All the interviews were recorded and later transcribed.

Interview questions were guided by the ambidexterity theory. They aimed to explore the structural arrangements and the processes for achieving ambidexterity. For senior managers, we asked questions about whether exploitation and exploration activities were pursued in the same or different business units and the rationale for such arrangements. These questions corresponded to the structural arrangement for achieving ambidexterity. We also asked senior managers questions about the milestones and critical changes during capability development and the rationale for these changes. These questions corresponded to the process for achieving ambidexterity. For middle managers and line employees, we asked questions about how they pursued existing and new tasks simultaneously. We also asked them questions about how organization-level changes affected the ways they pursued existing and new tasks. To measure capabilities and track their development, we adopted Levina and Ross’s (2003) three types of capabilities (for an example of the interview protocols, please see Appendix B).

We also collected archival data. All four case organizations were very supportive of our research. They shared with us rich archival data, such as annual reports, progress reports, sales presentation slides, and business cases. Data from different sources increased the reliability of the findings (Klein and Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995). To leverage the flexibility of the case study method, data collection and analysis occurred in tandem (Pan and Tan, 2011). When a new theme emerged from data analysis, and data relevant to the theme were insufficient, we conducted follow-up interviews. Data collection ended when we reached theoretical saturation, where newly collected data failed to reveal new themes (Eisenhardt, 1989).

## Data analysis

For theoretical findings to emerge from the data, we performed three rounds of coding. First, we read the interview transcripts and secondary data to code statements that illustrated activities related to achieving ambidexterity in capability development. During this round, we followed a temporal bracketing strategy to organize the data (Langley, 1999). We divided capability development into phases and analysed the structural arrangements and the activities in each phase. The research team met regularly to review emerging concepts and to ensure consistency of the coding (Klein and Myers, 1999; Pan and Tan, 2011). The result of the first-round coding was a set of categories. For an example of the data structure, see Figure 2.

The second round coding was similar to Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) notion of axial coding. We clustered the emergent categories from the first round into concepts and compared them with the guiding theory (Miles and Huberman, 1994). For example, when we asked Vendor D’s employees about the structural arrangement for achieving ambidexterity, we derived categories such as ‘adding new services to existing operations’, ‘experimentation in daily operations’, ‘adding new equipment requirements while maintaining existing ones’, and ‘support for task integration’. These four categories were clustered into the concept of structural integration (Andriopoulos and Lewis, 2009; Raisch et al., 2009). When we asked Vendor D’s employees about the process of achieving ambidexterity, we derived categories such as ‘planning as it goes’, ‘bottom-up initiatives’, ‘internal competition’, and ‘incremental changes’. We clustered them under the concept of emergent process (Garud et al., 2006).

In the third round of coding, we linked the concepts from the second round to form a coherent set of findings (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). During this phase, we adopted the strategy of visual mapping (Langley, 1999), which allowed us to map the structural arrangement, the process, and their interaction. Based on the map, we derived four classifications for achieving ambidexterity. For an abstract of the map, please refer to Figure 3.

## Findings

The four cases reveal four classifications for achieving ambidexterity. For each case, we focus on the structural arrangement and the process.

## Vendor A

Vendor A used to be an embedded system provider for Japanese car manufacturers. Because of the success of earlier projects, clients intended to outsource more advanced projects to the vendor. Many of the new projects were consulting based and related to the development of enterprise systems. Meanwhile, competition in the software development business started to intensify. In 2006, the company decided to respond to these new requirements and expand into the consulting business.

![](/api/attachments/EQ9TJCCZ/fulltext/images/591d02a38f5e6cf034e95f77a0e782987d394fd8bca9d015afb7e0b819b063d1.jpg)  
Figure 3. Four classifications for achieving ambidexterity in capability development.

Ambidexterity was achieved through structural differentiation (see Table 1). The company created new consulting teams to respond to clients’ new requirements. The consulting teams consisted of onsite consultants and offshore engineers. Consultants analysed clients’ needs and prepared specifications. Engineers developed the systems according to the specifications. In the earlier days, consultants were recruited from the market because the company did not have enough consultants internally. For engineers, most came from the software development teams.

During the expansion, software teams continued to attend to existing client requirements for embedded systems, which still constituted the company’s main source of revenues. Although some employees were transferred to the consulting teams, the software teams encountered few changes in ways in which they operated. A new category of training courses was created for the consulting teams. Many courses focused on client engagement. Engineers from the software teams were not encouraged to attend these courses, which were deemed a distraction for them. For each client, its software and consulting projects were coordinated by a senior manager, who ensured that although these two projects were delivered by two different teams, the client’s experience was seamless. At the corporate level, top management team oversaw the software and consulting divisions and allocated resources between them.

Vendor A followed a planned process for achieving ambidexterity. The separation of consulting and software teams was designed by the top management team to spare engineers from client communication. Effective client communication required both language ability and the ability to appreciate the Japanese business culture. Most of the engineers did not have these skills. The company started the expansion from clients with which it had close relationships and gradually expanded to the rest. The expansion was scheduled based on the availability of qualified consultants and engineers.

The senior managers made proactive investments in training and methodology. To overcome the shortage of consultants, they launched a 3-year training programme to train 1000 consultants. To transform software development from ad hoc practices into defined steps and formal practices needed by the consulting projects, senior managers invested in CMM. In 4 years, the company achieved the highest level of CMM. CMM streamlined the development process and enhanced coordination between the offshore engineers and the onshore consultants.

## Vendor B

Previously, these technical issues were recorded and sent to solution centres.

Ambidexterity was achieved through structural integration (see Table 2). Instead of assigning communication and technical tasks to two different business units, top management requested employees to develop the ability to handle both tasks. Employees learned new technical skills while maintaining existing communication tasks. Many spent their weekends learning these new skills. The company extended the communication training courses by adding technical courses and required employees to take both types of courses. To reduce the workload of handling both tasks, the company built an interactive system. Clients submitted issues through the system, with which they described the issues by following a standard template. The system forwarded the issues to employees with relevant skills based on their descriptions and facilitated further communication between clients and employees. An employee could invite colleagues to join a task and address the issue together.

Vendor B followed a planned process in achieving ambidexterity. The workforce was designed by the top management team to meet the new requirements. Building the workforce required recruitment at the high end. Instead of recruiting people with either communication or technical skills, the company only considered people with both skill sets. Although the senior managers later realized that people with both skill sets were difficult to find, they did not compromise or settle for people who were less qualified. The company also made proactive investments to produce the workforce. For example, it sponsored a double major programme at the best university in the region. Students from the programme graduated with a major in engineering and a major in Japanese. It also devised a 3-year training programme. After finishing the programme, employees would obtain the highest certificate in Japanese communication and become experts in a technical domain.

By 2012, Vendor B had successfully expanded into the technical support business and become the largest solution centre of the parent company in Asia. During the process, it maintained high-level client satisfaction in handling both communication and technical tasks.

## Vendor C

Vendor C used to be a leading corporate messaging service provider. Beginning in 2008, many clients expressed the need for services that could increase productivity. The company responded to these new demands and expanded into new services.

Ambidexterity was achieved through structural differentiation (see Table 3). New teams were built to explore new business opportunities and to develop new products. Existing teams maintained the messaging service and continued its improvement, such as the development of a mobile platform. The two teams operated under different modes. The new teams focused on experimentation and risk-taking, while the existing teams focused on incremental improvements. Because of the differences, the two teams had minimal interactions. The new team was given the freedom and support to explore new opportunities until its product reached a mature stage. Then, the product was integrated with the messaging system. Senior managers coordinated the integration. Senior managers also allocated resources between the existing and new teams. In general, the new teams were given more resources than the existing teams because they were expected to launch a product to test the market quickly.

Vendor C followed an emergent process in achieving ambidexterity. Senior managers did not give specific directions for the expansion because clients’ requirements and market conditions were difficult to forecast. Instead, they encouraged product managers and developers to initiate new projects based on their observations. Product managers and developers had closer observations of the market than senior managers. New teams did not start with a comprehensive plan either. They quickly built a prototype, tested it with clients, collected real-time feedback, and further refined the prototype. The company did not commit major resources at the beginning. If a product was not well received by clients or did not address a real client need, it would be cancelled without incurring further cost. This not only gave the company the flexibility to respond to unexpected situations but also built a sense of urgency for new teams to deliver results.

By 2012, Vendor C had successfully launched several new services, such as teleconferencing, enterprise blogs, and CRM, and had become a major web-based enterprise solution provider. During the expansion, its messaging service still dominated the market.

## Vendor D

Vendor D used to be an IT infrastructure provider specializing in telecommunications and data storage. In 2008, clients raised the new requirements for IT services that could reduce their energy footprint because many were under increasing pressure from the government to be ecologically responsible. Vendor D responded to these new requirements and expanded into the green IT business.

Ambidexterity was achieved through structural integration (see Table 4). Top management posited that green IT services should not be separated from existing operations because otherwise, they became cosmetic projects. Therefore, the company encouraged employees to add new services to existing operations and experiment with new ideas in daily operations. For example, the building solution division added temperature monitors to routers and used the routers to adjust heaters and air conditioners in the buildings automatically. The provision of green IT services also required cooperation from equipment suppliers. The company introduced new green requirements for its equipment suppliers while maintaining existing requirements on price and quality. Because it was challenging for business units to launch green initiatives while performing daily operations, a green office was established to provide technical support and to disseminate best practices.

Vendor D followed an emergent process in achieving ambidexterity. Although top management devised a roadmap at the onset, it consisted of only a general direction and did not include specific instructions. Top management encouraged business units to propose new initiatives based on their situations. To encourage green initiatives, the company leveraged internal competition. For example, the green office ranked business units based on the performance of green IT initiatives. This peer pressure encouraged business units to consider green IT implica tions in their daily operations.

Vendor A adopted a partition approach, whereby it divided exploitation and exploration into two business units and created a separation of existing and new capabilities. The partition approach was supported by planned actions such as systematic recruitment and training. Vendor B adopted a hybridization approach, whereby it encouraged employees to learn new skills while maintaining existing tasks and formed a hybrid of existing and new capabilities. The hybridization approach was also supported by planned actions, such as disciplined recruitment and training, which aim at creating a high-end workforce.

Vendor C adopted a self-extension approach, whereby it encouraged the spinoff of new teams for new capability development while focusing existing teams on exploiting current capabilities. The self-extension approach was supported by emergent actions such as real-time learning and bottom-up initiatives. Vendor D adopted a self-generation approach, whereby it encouraged existing business units to develop new capabilities while performing daily operations. The self-generation approach was also supported by emergent actions such as learning by doing and incremental changes.

## Discussion

Here, we discuss how the findings are corroborated by the existing literature and how they extend the extant literature. The partition approach can be explained by the notion of deliberate strategies, which emphasizes the benefits of planning before acting and acting according to a plan (Mintzberg, 1978; Mintzberg and Waters, 1985). For Vendor A, the dual structures are similar to the strategy that the vendor adopts for achieving ambidexterity (Su et al., 2014), and the planned process can be seen as the actualization of that strategy.

The hybridization approach can be explained by the notion of contextual ambidexterity (Gibson and Birkinshaw, 2004). For Vendor B, organizational contexts such as highperformance expectations and premium training are put in place to encourage employees to balance exploitation and exploration activities during their daily activities. However, for these contexts to take effect, a high-end workforce capable of both communication and technical tasks is needed. Vendor B follows a planned process to build such a workforce because it requires continuous, persistent investment (Su, 2013; Montealegre, 2002).

The self-extension approach can be explained by the notion of ambidextrous acquisition, which suggests that an organization can achieve ambidexterity by acquiring innovative startups while focusing its internal operations on exploitation (Puranam et al., 2006; Rothaermel and Deeds, 2004). The new teams of Vendor C are similar to those external startups because they are given the autonomy to explore new capabilities and are re-integrated with the existing operations when their products become mature. The spinoff and re-integration are not planned but rather spontaneous responses to emergent situations.

The self-generation approach can be explained by Garud et al.’s (2006) notion of emergent platforms, which suggests that an organization can develop new capabilities during its daily operations by building generative properties into organizational design elements such as people and processes. For Vendor D, the generative properties include the awareness of green IT implications and rewards for green IT initiatives. These properties interact during daily operations and enable business units to gradually develop new green IT capabilities.

Juxtaposing these two structural arrangements and two processes in one framework allows us to derive the factors that influence the choices between them. First, our study reveals that structural differentiation is adopted when new and existing operations can occur with limited interaction. In Vendor A, the software and consulting services can be conducted without much interaction; they are thus separated into two business units, each focusing on one type of services. Structural differentiation is also adopted when the two operations face significant conflicts. In Vendor C, the improvement of existing messaging services and the development of new services rely on contradictory practices; they are thus separated into two business units to minimize the conflicts.

In contrast, structural integration is adopted when the new and existing operations are highly interdependent. In Vendor B, the communication and technical tasks cannot be easily separated because resolving a technical issue requires both tasks simultaneously. Similarly, in Vendor D, the traditional and green IT services cannot be separated because providing green IT services heavily depends on the expertise from the traditional services. In these two cases, if the new and existing operations are separated into two business units, the coordination costs will be too high, and the synergies between them will be limited.

Second, our study also reveals that a planned process is adopted when the new market for which the new capabilities are developed is predictable. Both the new market of consulting services in Vendor A and the new market of technical resolution services in Vendor B face limited uncertainty and can be predicted. As a result, the new capabilities and their relationship with the current capabilities can be predicted as well. A planned process is thus adopted, which ensures continuity and reduces variants in capability development.

However, a planned process is not suitable when the new market is difficult to predict. Both the new market of productivity-enhancing services in Vendor C and the new market of green IT services in Vendor D face much uncertainty and are difficult to predict. As a result, the new capabilities needed and their relationship with the current capabilities are difficult to predict. An emergent process is thus adopted, which ensures flexibility and responsiveness in capability development.

## Contributions

Our study makes important theoretical contributions to the literature on ITO vendor capabilities, particularly studies that focus on vendor capability development. Although the extant studies have generated rich findings on the development mechanisms and processes, these findings are derived based on the condition of improving existing business and cannot effectively explain how vendors develop capabilities to move up the value chain. Our study fills this gap by identifying four approaches from the ambidexterity perspective. Our study also extends Garud et al.’s (2006)

emergent-platform approach and integrates approaches that have been mentioned in prior studies, such as new venture creation (Su et al., 2014) and strategic resource seeking (Su, 2013). Our study also identifies the context suitable for each approach. Instead of offering a generic approach for all vendors, it provides different vendors with different approaches. For example, for vendors who are expanding into an uncertain market and whose new operation is intertwined with the existing one, the self-generation approach is appropriate.

Our study also provides an alternative explanation for choosing between deliberate investment and learning by doing in capability development. The existing explanations have focused on the internal perspective, such as the capability type (Ethiraj et al., 2005) and the vendor scale (Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008). Our study extends these explanations by adding an external perspective: deliberate investment is suitable when the new market for which new capabilities are developed is predictable and learning by doing is suitable when the new market is difficult to predict.

Our contributions are timely. Given that clients are expecting vendors to handle more sophisticated tasks (Oshri et al., 2015, 2018), vendors are under increasing pressure to build capabilities to move up the value chain. Moreover, as the outsourcing market is becoming more turbulent and shocks in client demand are becoming common, vendors that are merely strengthening their current capabilities without developing new ones are no longer sustainable (Su et al., 2014).

Our study also contributes to the ambidexterity literature. The existing studies have identified two structural arrangements and two processes for achieving ambidexterity but have yet to examine how to choose between them. Based on four ITO vendors’ approaches to ambidexterity, our study juxtaposes these arrangements and processes and identifies the factors that influence the choices. First, the interdependence between the new and existing operations influences the choice of structural arrangements. Structural differentiation is more effective when the two operations have limited interactions, while structural integration is more effective when they are highly interdependent. Second, as mentioned above, the predictability of the new market for which the capabilities are developed influences the choice of the processes. A planned process is more effective when the new market is predictable, while an emergent process is more effective when the new market is difficult to predict.

Our study has important practical contributions as well. First, it provides four approaches that ITO vendors can choose when they develop capabilities to move up the value chain. Meanwhile, our findings can be extended beyond ITO and used to guide IT practitioners for coping with conflicting activities. For example, IT practitioners responsible for digital transformation may find our classifications useful, because they face the challenge of building new routines while maintaining existing ones (Chen et al., 2014;

Cui and Pan, 2015); IT practitioners responsible for maintaining a digital ecosystem may also find our classifications relevant, because they face the challenge of acquiring external knowledge while refining internal knowledge (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012).

## Limitations and future research

Despite the contributions, the study also has several limitations, which provide opportunities for future research. First, statistical generalization is impossible with only four case studies. The objective of this study is to explore new theoretical grounds rather than to test the statistical generalization of the findings (Walsham, 1995). However, we posit that these findings have the potential to be generalized beyond the four case organizations because they are corroborated by the extant studies in ITO, organizational ambidexterity, and general management. In other words, they are generalized at the analytical level (Lee and Baskerville, 2003). The statistical generalizability can be a future research direction whereby researchers collect a large data sample to verify the findings statistically.

Second, although our case selection responds to the lack of studies of Chinese ITO vendors (Su, 2013, 2015), it does not examine ITO vendors in other major outsourcing countries, such as India. Given the different national and cultural backgrounds, vendors’ approaches for coping with exploitation and exploration in capability development could be very different. A future research direction is to examine whether our findings still hold in other countries and to compare the results.

Third, our study focuses on a regular ITO model, in which the vendor handles all the projects without subcontracting them. It does not examine the mediated outsourc ing model, where a vendor subcontracts low-end tasks to smaller vendors or workforce on the crowd (Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008; Nevo and Kotlarsky, 2014). Capability development practices in the mediated model are different from those in the regular model (Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008; Nevo and Kotlarsky, 2014). In the future, it could be fruitful to examine how vendors in the context of the mediated model move up the value chain and how they achieve ambidexterity during the process.

## Appendix B

Interview protocol (using Vendor A as an example)

Background questions about the informants. Describe your role in your team and a typical day of work.

Describe your department/team and its role in the organization.

Describe your experience working with clients.

Background questions about capability development. Describe when, how, and why the client requirements changed. Describe how the company responded to these changes and moved up the value chain.

Describe the key challenges in responding to higher-end requirements and the mechanisms to overcome these challenges.

Describe the tensions between the software business and the consulting business. Describe how the skills, processes, and client-specific management practices differed across these two businesses.

Questions about the structural arrangement for achieving ambidexterity. Describe the rationale for separating the software and the consulting division

Describe how the software team and the consulting team were coordinated.

Describe how the software team and the consulting team were staffed and trained

Describe how consultants were equipped with the ability to overcome language and cultural barriers in client communication.

Questions about the process for achieving ambidexterity Describe how tensions between the consulting and the software teams were managed over time.

Describe how the company prepared for the consulting business and linked it with the existing software business.

Describe key milestones during the expansion into the consulting business and how these milestones affected the capability development.

Describe the long-term plans for staffing the software and the consulting teams and why such plans were important.
