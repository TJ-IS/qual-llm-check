# Is the V-form the next generation organisation? An analysis of challenges, pitfalls and remedies of ICT-enabled virtual organisations based on social capital theory

## Abstract

ICT-enabled virtual organisations (VOs) are regarded as the appropriate organisational response to turbulent markets. Although much has been written about the structura characteristics and propositions of VOs, their inherent limitations and structural shortcomings remain largely unaddressed. Based on the structural properties of VOs, most frequently discussed in the literature, we introduce the V-form organisation as an ideal type of VOs. A conceptual analysis, however, reveals a mismatch between espoused benefits of the ideal type and preconditions in terms of collaborative practices needed to succeed in a turbulent environment. Dealing with complex tasks in a collaborative environment requires shared understanding, shared values and a level of social cohesion, which do not simply emerge in a virtual setting. Social capital theory addresses the social and cultural preconditions of collaboration. Our analysis shows preconditions for successful value creation in VOs and suggests management measures to foster and facilitate environments for the development of social capital.

Keywords: virtual organisation; social capital; V-form organisation; virtual teams; collaboration; knowledge sharing

## Introduction

G <sup>lobalisation,</sup> <sup>driven</sup> <sup>by</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>emergence</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>new</sup> <sup>infor-</sup>mation and communication technology (ICT), causes mation and communication technology (ICT), causes established market structures to erode while the costs of market entrance are decreasing (Bleecker, 1994) and new competition is arising (Franke, 2001). An increasing demand for customised products and the fragmentation of markets call for new and efficient ways to organise value creation. Significantly lower costs of processing and transmitting information within and between firms spur radical changes in their operations. Furthermore, companies face an increasing importance of knowledge for value creation and an increasing dependency on innovation and new services. Today, even the largest companies are no longer able to access and control all necessary information and knowledge (Ciborra, 1992; Ireland et al., 2002). Scholars have coined terms such as ‘the permanently new economy’ (Ritzer, 1989), ‘postindustrial capitalism (Heydebrand, 1989), ‘the new rules of competition’ (Hage, 1988), ‘agile competition’ (Goldman et al., 1995) and ‘the age of the temporary company’ (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002) to describe market changes, which influence how companies organise their value creation. For example, companies increasingly cooperate in network arrangements (Ebers, 1999) and share risks in joint initiatives, such as product development (Goldman et al., 1995). Companies try out new strategies and change their structures by experimenting with new organisational forms (Bleecker, 1994; Venkatraman and Henderson, 1998).

For many scholars, the virtual organisation (VO) is both the result of and the organisational answer to these trends. In a VO, companies aim to achieve collaborative advantages by pooling their resources in inter-firm networks (Tuma, 1998). Making use of ICT to overcome spatial dispersion (Khalil and Wang, 2002), these arrangements are typically limited in duration (Saabeel et al., 2002) and involve complex and often innovative tasks such as research and design or customised manufacturing (Franke, 2001). While the literature is articulate about the promises and intended benefits of the VO, such as achieving flexibility, collaborative excellence (Goldman et al., 1995) or virtual size by pooling resources (Riemer et al., 2001), it is almost ignoring the challenges. Existing literature mainly concentrates on the structural properties of the VO and in doing so takes a technocratic view: the waiving of formal structures increases flexibility (Pihkala et al., 1999; Talukder, 2003) and distributed collaboration is facilitated by means of ICT (Tianfield and Unland, 2002). In contrast, the social and cultural challenges of enabling and managing collaboration in these virtual inter-firm arrangements tend to be overlooked. Moreover, there is a clear gap between the VO promises and the empirical evidence (Watson-Manheim et al., 2002; Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004). Only few studies investigate issues of collaboration at the team level (Hughes et al., 2001; Clases et al., 2003) or take a critical stance on the VO and its propositions (Introna, 2001).

## Method and overview

In this conceptual paper, we identify inherent contradictions and challenges in the design of the VO by juxtaposing its structural characteristics with the requirements of value creation in virtual environments. Social capital theory explains some of the gaps between structural flexibility and social embedding of virtual collaboration. In particular, it clarifies that VOs are not an environment in which these social structures usually prosper.

We begin by delineating an ideal type of VO, the V-form organisation. We then analyse the V-form organisation by juxtaposing its organisational characteristics and the requirements of collaborative value generation. The focus of our analysis of value generation is on the team level with an emphasis on complex and novel tasks. Contingency reasoning is widely used – explicitly or implicitly – to justify the V-form by arguing that a virtual organisational set-up is the appropriate response to a turbulent business environment. However, we will scrutinise whether the Vform organisation is actually properly designed for solving complex tasks by virtual teams (i.e., we apply the contingency reasoning on a task and group level). We corroborate our analysis with a number of VO examples. The findings lead us to suggest some remedies of how to incorporate the required social structures to the VO concept.

## Virtual organisations

The term ‘VO’ is somewhat vague and obscure; there are many slightly different interpretations of what a VO is and what it looks like. Hence, we will suggest an ideal type of VO, the ‘V-form’, as an ICT-based network organisation. The purpose of an ideal type is to clarify the core characteristics of a phenomenon (Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004). The V-form synthesises prominent views in the literature (Bekkers, 2003).

## Types of VOs in the literature

In the literature, two approaches to define the VO can be identified (Franke, 2001; Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004). The first one (the functional form) describes the VO as an ICT-enabled corporation and the second approach (the institutional form) highlights the network character of the VO in which independent organisational entities cooperate and jointly form a virtual network organisation (Byrne et al., 1993).

The first approach sees the VO as an ICT-enabled corporation, a single firm that builds its operations ‘by placing IT at the centre.’ (Venkatraman and Henderson, 1998: 34). It is a corporation with headquarters in different countries whose employees communicate only through electronic means (Shin, 2004). Following this notion, a VO is a geographically dispersed organisation, in which travel is minimised due to the electronic exchange of information (Moller, 1997) and whose workforce includes a significant proportion of remote workers (Bleecker, 1994; Cohen, 1997). This view of VO has its roots in concepts like computer-support collaborative work and teleworking, (Wigand et al., 1997; Talukder, 2003) and is based on the emergence of virtual teams (Lipnack and Stamps, 2000). ICT is seen as an enabler for bridging space, time and cultural distances in order to enable global coordination of activities (Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2001; Khalil and Wang, 2002); the view of ICT as an enabler is typically very positive and optimistic: ‘Through IT enabling, there is no difference either between working in a geographically dispersed or co-located manner’ (Tianfield and Unland, 2002: 368). Furthermore, ICT facilitates decentralisation (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002). And yet, the notion of ICT is typically used vaguely and highly abstract.

The second approach sees the VO as a network organisation (Bekkers, 2003; Travica, 2005), ‘as a cooperative partnership (y) of independent economic actors that join forces on a temporary basis in order to achieve a common goal.’ (Franke, 2001: 49). It can be seen as a ‘temporary, project-dependent portfolio of core competencies’ (Tuma, 1998), where each partner brings in specialised expertise (Hughes et al., 2001; Talukder, 2003). A VO thus is an organisational network where firms collaborate to achieve ‘virtual size’ while retaining their own flexibility (Riemer et al., 2001). It is opportunistic in that it tries to flexibly capitalise on arising market opportunities (Goldman et al., 1995) and it has a short- to medium-range planning horizon (Tuma, 1998).

The first approach describes a VO as a geographically dispersed set-up that relies on ICT for communication and cooperation, while the second one describes a VO as a network organisation that is made up of independent organisational entities. Whereas some authors concentrate on either the ICT-based view or the network view, it is the combination of both – the VO as a geographically dispersed network of independent organisations with ICT at its core – that has inspired most authors (Tuma, 1998; Franke, 2001; Hughes et al., 2001; Khalil and Wang, 2002; Saabeel et al., 2002; Tianfield and Unland, 2002; Bekkers, 2003; Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004; Travica, 2005). In this sense, the VO can be seen as a special type of inter-firm network differing from other networks by the central role of ICT as instrument for communication and collaboration, as well as the emphasis on project-based collaboration and organisational flexibility (Child and Faulkner, 1998).

## The V-form as an organisational response to changing business environments

Every era has its dominant organisational form; the industrial or U-form organisation (unitary form) has dominated the early 20th century, whereas the M-form organisation (multi-divisional form) emerged in the 1960s as its more flexible successor (Pihl, 2002). While the U-form is based on the separation of business functions such as production and marketing (e.g. the Ford Motor Company before World War II), the M-form features a matrix organisation and is often divided into product or geographic divisions (e.g. multinationals like General Motors) (Zhou, 2005). Both forms are based on hierarchical coordination, albeit with some differences. In the U-form authority is concentrated in the hands of the CEO, while the M-form distributes power across divisions with general managers as key figures in the hierarchy (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1993). In that respect, the M-form represents a flexibilisation and distribution of control compared to the U-form; responsibilities are distributed to semi-autonomous units that are responsible for product lines or geographic regions (Chi et al., 2003). However, Bartlett and Goshal argue that the M-form does not adequately describe modern organisations that feature a ‘proliferation and subsequent aggregation of small independent entrepreneurial units from the bottom up’ (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1993). In these organisations, resources are decentralised and management attention shifts from facilitating internal administrative activities to managing value creation processes (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1993: 42). Consequently, the authors argue that new models to describe organisational forms are needed.

Pihl describes the ‘W-form’ as a multinational enterprise that is highly geographically dispersed and relying on ICT for coordination and communication (Pihl, 2002). While the W-form strongly resembles the above discussed ICTbased corporation, we suggest the term ‘V-form’ organisation to denote VO as an ICT-based network of organisations. In the shift away from U-form and M-form a trend towards de-integration, decentralisation and flexibilisation, as well as a decline of administrative structures is noticeable. The V-form extends this trend into the interorganisational realm, in that it describes a networked form of organising that is made up of independent firms. Which cooperate in a flexible way enabled by ICT and with a minimum of formal structures (see characteristics below).

Some scholars argue that the VO is becoming the ‘new paradigm of organization design’ (Khalil and Wang, 2002) or ‘the corporate model of the future’ (Talukder, 2003) that could ‘define work in the 21st century, just as the industrial organisation defined it in the 20th’ (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002: 31). Hence, we describe the V-form as an ideal type of a next generation form of organisation.

## The V-form as an ideal type of organisation

Based on the review of the literature, the V-form can be described as a combination of technological, organisational and temporal virtualisation. Technological virtualisation refers to the intensive usage of ICT. Virtual in this sense means ‘immaterial, supported by ICT’ (Bultje and van Wijk, 1998) and is connected to the notion that ICT enables spatially dispersed teams (Franke, 2001).

Organisational virtualisation refers to firms forming organisational networks. Virtual in this sense means ‘unreal, looking real’ (Bultje and van Wijk, 1998): the Vform is an arrangement assembled of independent firms acting as a single coherent organisation vis-a\`-vis its customers and the marketplace (Tianfield and Unland, 2002). The VO is a ‘virtual’, an ‘as if’ organisation, in that it is a group of independent firms linked together as one – often temporary – organisation (Khalil and Wang, 2002), flexibly coordinating their activities (Hughes et al., 2001). Flexibility in the V-form is achieved by waiving formal structures and management mechanisms. Distinct from hierarchies, the V-form sets aside ‘traditional hierarchical modes of central direction or supervision’ (Hughes et al., 2001). VO is seen to be ‘free from bureaucratic traditions’ (Travica, 2005: 53). In the V-form hierarchical control is replaced with other governance mechanisms such as selfregulation and trust (Pihkala et al., 1999). In fact, trust and a shared understanding are seen as characteristics of the VO that can replace formal governance modes (Pihkala et al., 1999; Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004). However, in most articles the question is left unanswered how trust and a shared understanding develop under conditions of virtuality.

The underlying logic for networking is that of a ‘best of everything organisation’ (Tuma, 1998), or metaphorically that of an All Star Team (Goldman et al., 1995), which integrates the best players and competencies to represent superior strength, respectively to gain collaborative advantages in the marketplace. The idea is to combine skills and resources in order to achieve common goals (Hughes et al., 2001). The V-form integrates superior expertise and competencies from its member organisations in order to create innovative, non-standard products (Travica, 2005) or to achieve mass customisation (Saabeel et al., 2002). The inter-organisational specialisation and division of labour implies that companies have to collaborate to integrate the capabilities of their people in joint projects. As we will argue, this can be a challenging task since diversity, different organisational backgrounds and cultural differences among the network members have to be addressed in establishing project teams.

The third characteristic of the V-form is the temporal virtualisation that refers to the flexible (re)combination of individual competencies in order to form projects to swiftly exploit fast-changing market opportunities (Goldman et al., 1995; Pihkala et al., 1999). This aspect of VO is called the switching principle (Mowshowitz, 1999): competencies are swiftly recombined to form projects of limited duration (Saabeel et al., 2002). The result is a high degree of flexibility in fulfilling customer orders (Franke, 2001). The downside however is a high degree of volatility that is challenging for setting up, managing and working in project teams.

To sum up, the V-form is the result of three types of virtualisation (see Table 1). Watson-Manheim et al. (2002) refer to these as time, space and organisational discontinuities. Similarly, Shin (2004) describes a VO as an organisational form characterised by virtuality in the spatial, temporal, cultural and organisational dimension (we subsume the cultural under the organisational dimension). Furthermore, the V-form exhibits flexibility through cutting back on bureaucracy and instead relying on lean formal management structures and trust-based governance. Literature claims that projects in the V-form are innovative, for example concerned with new product development, and relying on the core competencies and expertise of the network members.

## Group and task structure

On a structural level, the V-form seems to be a promising response to a changing business environment and appears to have comparative advantages over other forms of organising. However, whether the espoused benefits come to fruition depends on the actual processes of value creation in virtual project settings (see Figure 1 for illustration). Hence, in order to understand the organisational and managerial challenges of the V-form in facilitating value creation (and innovation) in project settings, we need to scrutinise the group and task structure. The linkage of capabilities of member organisations and the integration of individuals in teams is particularly challenging for the V-form. Surprisingly, the challenges and requirements on the task level and the required structures and capabilities on a managerial and group level have not been sufficiently addressed so far. In order to illustrate our argument, we will discuss particular task–group (actor) constellations. By pointing out group-level requirements of complex tasks, we will eventually challenge the structural propositions of the V-form organisation. Inherent contradictions within the concept become only obvious when looking at the task level of collaboration (Figure 2).

## Complex group work needs social integration

Complex tasks require close collaboration between individuals since they cannot be readily separated and specified. Complex tasks are ambiguous in the sense that they can be fulfilled by various members of a group and in different ways. For highly innovative tasks, the outcome is often not clear ex ante nor are there proven heuristics for how to tackle the problem. Hence, it is important for the group members to engage in a process of joint sense making and reflection on division of tasks and task assignment (Levine and Moreland, 1991). Team members have to learn to collaborate and to establish a shared conceptual frame in order to engage in knowledge-intensive tasks. As Levine and Moreland state, ‘(work) groups function best when their members view the world from a common perspective (1991: 273).

According to Nonaka and Takeuchi, knowledge creation is a social process undertaken by individuals that generally begins with a process of sharing tacit knowledge in order to derive a group-level understanding as the basis for successful collaboration (1995). Experts within the V-form come from different firms with different backgrounds, and therefore are likely to have different views of the world.

Before they can engage in complex tasks, they have to find a shared cognitive basis for their collaborative work. They have to ‘learn to communicate more effectively by developing a better understanding of how each uses language, the categories that are important to them, the heuristics they employ, and the forms of verbal and nonverbal shorthand and codes they use’ (Nohria and Eccles, 1992: 299). As Clark and Brennan put it, two people or a group cannot begin to coordinate the task at hand ‘without assuming a vast amount of shared information or common ground – that is mutual knowledge, mutual beliefs, and mutual assumptions’ (1991). Because the more mutual knowledge exists between individuals, the less interactions are required to understand the situation and to deal with the task (Olson and Olson, 2000).

## Preconditions for distributed work

While the VO literature acknowledges the need to mobilise competencies and resources throughout the network and to flexibly coordinate the network members in light of different projects, it rarely elaborates on specific collaborative competencies and preconditions for efficient coordination in light of complex, ill-structured tasks. While this might seem just like an omission and lack of detail, Introna (2001) has claimed that the organisational structure of VOs is essentially incompatible with the processes and routines of knowledge sharing and collaboration required for the sketched processes of value generation. The literature claims that companies are looking for ways to extend their resource base in light of growing environmental challenges (Ciborra, 1992). In order to benefit from specialisation advantages integration of distributed work and indeed sharing of expert knowledge are needed. ICT is seen as a facilitator of distributed work combined with lean management structures. However, Introna and Tiow (1997) object that this view does not consider the prerequisites of efficient, distributed collaboration. Their verdict is that VOs are only feasible in situations where competencies are explicit and no integration is required.

We will first elaborate and indeed support their objection by using social capital theory to further illustrate and explain the voids of the VO literature. In a second step, however, we will contest Introna and Tiow’s (1997) verdict. We will suggest that the manageable complexity within VOs can be extended by developing organisational and collaborative competencies. While recognising the challenges illstructured tasks pose in a distributed collaboration context, we will argue that under the right circumstances, social capital, which enables sharing of tacit knowledge, can still be built in VOs. We will elaborate on management implications regarding the creation of social capital at the end of the paper.

## Social capital and complex tasks

## Social capital theory

Social capital theory is an emerging body of concepts that acknowledges the inherent value of social structures. Social capital can generally be defined and interpreted on an individual and on a group level (Blyler and Coff, 2003). On the individual level, it is defined as ‘the ability of actors to secure benefits by virtue of membership in social networks or other social structures’ (Portes, 1998: 8). For further definitions refer to (Adler and Kwon, 2002). Since we are analysing group-level issues, we will concentrate on the group-level interpretation that refers to social capital as the value of social structures in facilitating social action. Social networks and relationships in this respect function as valuable organisational resources that enable individuals to act as groups and to undertake complex actions like knowledge creation (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

Social capital thus can be seen as a complement to human capital (Coleman, 1988). Whereas human capital refers to the knowledge and capabilities of the collaborating individuals, social capital takes into account the social fabric among these individuals that is necessary for effective collaboration to take place. It thus refers to the capabilities of a team to act collaboratively, which involves issues like trust, shared cognitive models and the ability to process complex information. In general, social capital refers to the value of social structures (e.g. relationships and their properties); it emerges in successful social interactions and functions as a resource, for example in again enabling social interactions. According to Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) we can distinguish three dimensions of social capital: the structural, the relational and the cognitive dimension:

\- The structural dimension of social capital comprises the connections between individuals of a social group (Adler and Kwon, 2002). Through social ties people get access to resources (like information) that are held by others. Social relationships in and between groups thus constitute information channels allowing people to exchange information, which is one prerequisite for knowledge creation to take place (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Besides this, network structure in terms of density or closure refers to the degree of interconnection between the members of a social group and is defined as ‘the average strength of the relationship between team members’ (Reagans and Zuckerman, 2001: 503). Network density allows for the observance of social norms (Coleman, 1988) and facilitates trust and thus strength ens the so-called ‘absorptive capacity’ of a group, the ability to accumulate and create new knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990).

\- The relational dimension refers to the willingness of people to act together by subordinating their individual desires to group objectives (Leana and van Buren III, 1999). It comprises trust, norms and obligations. Individuals need to trust to be willing to collaborate with others. Trust is defined as the willingness to take a risk or to accept the vulnerability towards others in an interaction (Meyerson et al., 1996). Norms refer to a form of immanent control enforced by a social group on its individual members. Norms like openness, teamwork and an emphasis on collaborative rather than competitive behaviour are likely to enforce cooperative individual behaviour (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Finally, obligations are created by acting collaboratively towards others. Obligations can function as social credits and ensure collaborative behaviour by others in the future.

\- The cognitive dimension refers to the ability of people to act together and comprises aspects of shared understanding (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998) and collective goal orientation (Leana and van Buren III, 1999). As argued above, people have to align their mental models and to establish a shared language and codes to be able to collaborate effectively. This dimension of social capital connects with a stream of research in social psychology termed ‘socially shared cognition’ (Levine and Moreland, 1991). It acknowledges that human mental functioning is socially situated. For effective social action in (work) groups, people have to find a ‘common ground’ as a shared reference for effective communication (Clark and Brennan, 1991) and establish ‘mutual knowledge’ (Krauss and Fussell, 1990): ‘mutual knowledge is considered to be a precondition for effective communication and the performance of cooperative work’ (Cramton, 2001: 349).

Drawing from this detailed view of social capital, the benefits of social capital are twofold. Social capital has informational and collaborative benefits. Individuals are able to access information through their social, often informal relationships. And groups are able to process information effectively when they possess sufficient social capital especially in the relational and cognitive dimensions. Collaborative benefits of social capital refer to the willingness and ability of group members to act together (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). The collaborative ability results from group closure, trust, norms and a shared group understanding as introduced above. Hence, social capital provides the conditions that are necessary for the informational and collaborative processes in the creation of knowledge (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). People have to have relationships for exchanging and combining information and they have to be willing and able to collaborate with others in the process of knowledge creation.

Social capital is a prerequisite for successful V-form collaboration Drawing on the characteristics of complex tasks and the discussion of social capital, we argue that only groups with sufficient social capital will be able to manage complex tasks. In the V-form organisation, experts from different firms have to form teams and collaborate on complex tasks. These people have to establish relationships to be able to exchange knowledge with other individuals in the V-form network, and they have to be willing and able to communicate and collaborate with others. Experts typically come from different organisational backgrounds and see the world with different eyes. To be successful they have to establish trust and common ground. In other words, they have to invest time to create social capital to cater for effective collaboration and group work.

A successful V-form has to produce social capital as a necessary complement to the excellence in human capital that stems from the integration of core competencies and members’ expertise. The analogy of the All Star Team suggests that having the best players does not automatically lead to superior team play. In order to act as a team and capitalise on the individual superior capabilities, players have to be willing to play together by subordinating their own interests and by learning to adapt to other players in the team. By training and practising, they learn the style and specialities of their teammates and derive a shared understanding for the game. This involves tacit team understanding as well as explicit team tactics.

## Structural contradictions in the V-Form design

While achieving effective group work is challenging within companies, it becomes even more challenging in the interfirm set-up of the V-form organisation, where people come from different organisational backgrounds, which raises questions of diversity and conflict. The extensive usage of ICT for communication and the temporal aspects of V-form collaboration pose additional hurdles for the development of social capital. This suggests – like Introna (Introna and Tiow, 1997; Introna, 2001) has argued using a different conceptual framework – that the V-form may not be the appropriate organisational environment to develop social capital. The emergence of social capital is dependent on effective group formation processes in the V-form set-up. However, the three dimensions of virtualisation as core structural properties of the V-form design impede or delay the development of social capital. To support our argument, we draw on existing research in the areas of virtual teams, dispersed collaboration and computermediated communications.

## Organisational virtualisation – team diversity challenges the emergence of social capita

The V-form is based on the idea of heterogeneity and best of breed collaboration that brings together specialised experts from different organisations. Hence, the V-form can well capitalise on superior expertise and human capital. Yet at the same time, diversity and cultural barriers are obstacles for the development of social capital, which is a prerequisite for the functioning of V-form organisations.

According to Jarvenpaa and Leidner (1999), similarity in teams contributes to the willingness to collaborate, whereas diversity renders the establishment of strong bonds a time consuming task. Thus, with increasing group diversity the development of social relationships and a shared grouplevel understanding becomes more difficult and time consuming. Consequently, members of teams with diverse knowledge and different professional and organisational backgrounds face the challenge of integrating their knowledge and are likely to have higher levels of social uncertainty than homogeneous teams: ‘The major challenge with these teams is enhancing the likelihood that integration and (knowledge) creation actually occurs’ (Anand et al., 2003: 27). Different (organisational) cultures pose major barriers (Hall, 1995). People from different organisations do things differently; they say things differently and behave differently. Collaboration across these organisational boundaries can lead to serious problems of ‘us vs. them’ thinking (Hughes et al., 2001). Hence, the necessary alignment process becomes even more challenging.

Trust (or distrust), although often discussed in the context of VOs, might not be the main issue in the V-form design. Research has shown that cooperation in teams, even in temporary and virtual ones, can be based on a form of swift trust that is not relationship based. Swift trust can be created in initial communications (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1999), people can be trusted by virtue of being a member of the V-form network (Pihkala et al., 1999) or because they are experts in certain areas or trained in a specific profession (role-based trust) (Meyerson et al., 1996). However, even if initial trust and the willingness to collaborate exist, effective collaboration requires a shared knowledge basis between the individuals that can only be achieved through relationship building. If groups and their managers are not able to establish shared cognitive basis they might enter a ‘downward spiral of misunderstandings, mistrust of intention, conflict and a broken relationship (Hall, 1995: 5).

Consequently, we argue that the players in the All Star Team example might be willing to collaborate in order to win a match. The players might not know each other when they first meet, but they can trust in each others’ abilities. However, since the players come from different teams with different styles, tactics and communication behaviour, we cannot assume that they will be able to play as a team without training. Without a social alignment process in which players become familiar with each other, distrust and competitive behaviour might prevail. The main point is that the inter-organisational nature of the V-form sets the bar high for the formation of teams.

## Technological virtualisation – geographical dispersion challenges the emergence of social capital

Using ICT to support work in geographically distributed teams is particularly challenging for the development of social structures. While ‘ICT allows organisations to bridge time and distance barriers with once undreamed of ease (Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2001: 240), it creates boundaries at the level of the work unit (Breu and Hemingway, 2004) and new social barriers (Nohria and Eccles, 1992). Research in this area is often dominated by issues of trust creation (Lipnack and Stamps, 2000; Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2001). However, the creation of cognitive social capital and the underlying communication problems pose the main obstacles.

Scholars have shown that computer-mediated communication can lead to severe problems of interpreting transmitted information: ICT lacks the ability to deliver tacit, non-verbal but ‘visual cues that are easily observable in face-to-face setting’ (Andres, 2002: 46), comprising facial expressions, gestures, tone of voices, etc. (Putnam, 2001). As Cohen and Prusak (2001) argue, ICT only transports a fraction of communication richness that is required for social capital building. Members of distributed work groups experience misunderstandings in communication and have problems forming and maintaining social ties and group structures (Armstrong and Cole, 2002). Brennan (1998) describes electronic communication as ‘impoverished’, where people experience communication problems due to failures of grounding.

Cramton found that ‘a central problem of dispersed collaboration is maintaining mutual knowledge [y so that] failure to establish and maintain mutual knowledge can have serious consequences for the viability of the collaboration’ (2001: 363). Spatially dispersed teams experience further problems due to a lack of awareness of others’ availability, activities, progress in work processes, as well as the different perspectives held by the team members (Travica, 2005). In dispersed collaboration, people are often left out of important decision processes that can cause conflicts (Grinter et al., 1999). Moreover, these teams are also disadvantaged in the management of conflicts; Andres observed that ‘face-to-face group members were better able to immediately discuss conflicts and problems directly with each other to make mutually beneficial agreements that contributed to successful collaboration’ (2002: 46). In addition, co-location and face-to-face communication support informal conversations and activities like eating and drinking together that are crucial for establishing rich social relationships as the basis of social capital (Nardi and Whittaker, 2002). Moreover, ICT-based communication can seriously impede the sharing of informal knowledge necessary to build trust and relationships due to a lack of trust in the confidentiality level of such technologies (Breu and Hemingway, 2004).

These research findings suggest that dispersed teams using ICT for communication are in a weak position for building social capital, especially in the cognitive dimension. This is even more problematic when people are engaged in complex tasks. Nohria and Eccles argue that people who fulfil novel and imaginative work under conditions of ambiguity and uncertainty have to negotiate a shared understanding of the context, which cannot be done using electronic networks: ‘It crucially depends on face-to-face interaction’ (1992: 292). ‘Tightly coupled work is very difficult to do remotely. Technology at least today, does not support rapid back and forth in conversation or awareness and repair of ambiguity’ (Olson and Olson, 2000: 163). Andres argues that when it comes to complex tasks, face-to-face interactions are still superior to ICT-based communication, despite the recent advances in ICT (2002). Huysman and Wulf (2006) have shown that ICT so far mainly supports the creation of social capital in the structural dimension, for example by establishing contacts and supporting communications in social networks, while ICT is far less effective in supporting relational and cognitive social capital (2006). We conclude that ICT-based communication delays or impedes the emergence of rich social capital on the group level. Hence, it also poses a threat to the success of collaboration in complex tasks.

## Temporal virtualisation – volatility and switching reduce incentives to invest in social capital

Temporal virtualisation refers to the temporary nature of the V-form and the flexible re-configuration of resources in terms of the switching principle. The flexible (re)combination of individual competencies to form projects in response to market opportunities enables the VO to adapt to market changes (Goldman et al., 1995). This results in flexible and fluid arrangements (Franke, 2001) with limited duration of actual projects (Saabeel et al., 2002). The limited duration of projects and the switching principle, however, create a volatile work environment that provides little incentives for people to invest in social capital or to engage in time-consuming social alignments. In fact, people brought together in such a volatile set-up will face the paradox that they are expected to form teams quickly in order to collaborate, but that in-depth collaboration normally only develops in medium or long-term relationships (Larsen and McInerney, 2002). The VO literature, however, does not properly address the challenge of creating incentives for participants to contribute to short-term projects, nor does it elaborate on the challenges of managing the dynamics of collaborative competence development and social capital investments. We state that volatility hampers the emergence of social capital in the Vform design, and thus threatens effective collaboration that is required to carry out complex tasks.

## Illustration and empirical evidence: typical VO examples

So far, our analysis was mainly conceptual. In order to corroborate our argument with some empirical evidence, we will now scrutinise existing examples of VOs that are frequently mentioned in the VO literature. Here again, we concentrate on the network-type VOs and do not discuss the VO as an internal phenomenon. For the latter, large multinational companies that disintegrate and split in separate globally distributed units are often mentioned as typical examples (e.g. General Motors, see Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002).

A typical VO example is the Virtual Factory (http:// www.virtuelle-fabrik.org). The Virtual Factory comprises 37 SMEs in engineering, development and production located in the Lake Constance region in the northwest of Switzerland. The Virtual Factory aims at designing, developing and building industrial products to the specifications of customers in changing project configurations. While the Virtual Factory shows typical characteristics of the V-form design like organisational virtualisation, core competence concentration, short-term projects, switching, etc., it also exhibits some characteristics that alleviate the challenges discussed above. Firstly, it consists of a regional network of partners that are located in relative proximity to each other. Team communications thus does not have to rely on ICT, and frequent face-to-face interactions allows for the emergence of social relationships (Plu¨ss and Huber, 2005). Secondly, the Virtual Factory is well-known for its elaborate management structures and organisational roles for the coordination of operational as well as managerial aspects (Go¨ransson and Schuh, 1997; Katzy and Schuh, 1998; Plu¨ss and Huber, 2005). The group of networking firms is also quite stable. Thus, it is obvious that the Virtual Factory cuts back on the full set of V-form characteristics in terms of technological virtualisation and flexibility aspects.

Besides the Virtual Factory there are other examples of regional VOs, for example. in engineering, automotive or fashion and textile (see Santoro and Bifulco, 2005). The most popular textile industry examples are found in Italy: the Mezzogiorno in Southern Italy (Aniello, 2001) and the Prato region in Tuscany, Northern Italy (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002; Piscitello and Sgobbi, 2004). Again, these networks are concentrated in a small regional area and rely on a form of hierarchical coordination. In the Prato case, the so-called ‘impannatori’ act as central network brokers assembling and coordinating a network of textile companies for the duration of a contract (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002). We conclude that in regional networks, members do not face the severity of spatial dispersion. Regional networks are normally characterised by existing long-lasting relationships between companies and individuals that can provide the social infrastructure for collaboration (Pihkala et al., 1999). The examples further exhibit forms of central control and hierarchical coordination, which on the one hand compromises the flexibility argument, but on the other hand reduces the need for informal coordination.

Another type of example often discussed under the VO label comprises companies like Dell, Cisco Systems, Benetton, Nike, Puma and the OEMs in the automotive industry. The common characteristic of these companies is core competence concentration and the management of a network of supply side partners. The companies have significantly reduced their part in the overall value creation by outsourcing most of the activities to a (virtual) network of partner organisations. Dell, for example, is well-known for the management of its just-in-time sourcing network of assembly partners (Lawton and Michaels, 2001). Cisco Systems nowadays concentrates on the development and sales of products and owns only two of its 34 production sites; partners provide nearly every aspect of production and assembly (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002). Similarly, Benetton, Nike and Puma have outsourced their production to networks of firms around the globe (mainly in Asia) to fulfil everything, but design and market development (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002). While these examples show V-form characteristics such as geographical dispersion and technological virtualisation, core competence concentration, and to a certain degree the flexibilities of switching, they also show significant differences. Firstly, the networks are hierarchically organised by the dominant brand owners, often in a rather tight fashion. They resemble hierarchical supply networks. Secondly, virtualisation often concentrates on capacity aspects with well-specified production contracts handed out to the partners. Hence, interpersonal collaboration requirements are significantly lower since tasks are less complex than in joint engineering or development projects.

Open source software (OSS) development initiatives like Linux, Apache and Mozilla are frequently used as examples of successful virtual collaboration. OSS networks are usually made up of individuals and not firms, and thus do not fit the V-form definition. Yet, they exhibit sufficient structural parallels in order to justify that they can be used as a test case for our argument. Hence, we include OSS networks in our analysis and examine whether they indeed contradict our line of reasoning. An OSS network is often referred to as an ‘informal group, working without managers and connected mainly through the Internet’ (Rahman and Bhattachryya, 2002: 30). Trust is seen as the main characteristic for the internal stabilisation and coordination in OSS networks (Gallivan, 2001). However, studies investigating the working of OSS teams have found that these networks exhibit both hierarchical structures and well-structured governance mechanisms (Watson-Manheim et al., 2002), and ‘rely on explicit forms of control to a much greater degree than on trust’ (Gallivan, 2001: 297). OSS initiatives use a multi-tier structure of influence and control and an elaborate role structure. At the centre of an OSS project, a charismatic leader (e.g. Linus Torval) or a small group of people is in charge of the project. As Gallivan states: ‘The project leaders have authority for setting ground rules (y) and for deciding which code submissions to accept, modify or reject’ (Gallivan, 2001: 291). The second tier of the network consists of the software developers; the much larger third tier is involved in testing and feedback. In terms of V-form characteristics, OSS networks are truly geographically dispersed using ICT for communication and they show core competence aspects and the flexibility of switching. But OSS teams face less challenges of diversity through an existing shared background of the individuals rooted in what is termed a ‘hacker culture’ (Markus et al., 2000). Furthermore, software development is divisible, that is, it can be decomposed into modules (Malhotra et al., 2001). While the creative software design processes are undertaken in a very small inner circle of project leaders, the overall software development can be broken down into subtasks. Hence, well-specified tasks can be handed out to the larger development network. Task complexity thus is moderate to low with a significantly lower need for complex interpersonal interactions that would need social capital. Finally, hierarchical structures and control mechanisms are used to coordinate work in the network.

The last example to be discussed here is Boeing-Rocketdyne, an intriguing and rare case of successful virtual collaboration, which exhibits most of the above discussed V-form characteristics. Malhotra et al. (2001) describe a virtual team of eight engineers from four companies successfully developing a novel rocket engine over a period of 10 months. The task was innovative and complex, the team crossed organisational boundaries, and the work was carried out using sophisticated knowledge management ICT. The authors present the case as a special case that runs counter to existing research in the domain; it shows that complex collaboration in highly virtualised setups is possible. While the paper describes the mode of collaboration, management structures and the appropriation of technology, it does not provide an explanation for the subsequent success of the team. It is more than likely that social capital emerged quickly as the team had only eight members, which – despite different areas of specialisation – shared a common background as engineers and thus shared a professional culture. Moreover, basic rules for the collaboration had been set by management that provided a mix of structure and freedom to develop appropriate forms of coordination, collaboration and practices of using technology.

Not surprisingly, our case analysis shows that VO examples rarely exhibit all characteristics of the V-form ideal type (see Table 3). When exhibiting some characteristics others are absent. In fact, two types of examples can be identified: First, in cases with complex tasks virtualisation is only present in some dimensions, for example often geographical proximity provides the environment for social capital emergence (Virtual factory, regional networks). Furthermore, management structures are designed to reduce the need for and enable the emergence of social capital, for example through the set-up of coordination roles (Wehmeyer and Riemer, 2007). Second, in cases that exhibit geographical dispersion, ICT-based coordination and organisational virtualisation task complexity is generally low and tasks are well separable (Dell case, OSS networks). In these cases, we also find hierarchical management structures. As a rare exception, the Boeing-

Rocketdyne case demonstrates that under the right circumstances and embedded in the right management and technological structures complex collaboration in a highly virtualised setting is still possible. However, the cases also show that in order to better understand the managerial and technological measures that may overcome barriers created by virtualisation, further research is needed.

## Discussion

While the VO literature is outspoken about the goals – flexibility, agility and a superior resource base – it remains vague about the preconditions of achieving a high performing project team within a virtual set-up facing a complex task. Ironically, virtuality and volatility, the core characteristics of the virtual organisation are also known to be the most hampering factors for the emergence of social capital (Prusak and Cohen, 2001). Our analysis leads us to the identification of inherent contradictions and management dilemmas in the V-form ideal-type design.

## Inherent contradictions in the V-form structural definition challenge collaboration on the group level

There is an inherent contradiction between task requirements (complex tasks require rich group-level social capital) and the organisational structures of the V-form (virtualisation creates diverse, dispersed and volatile environments). As we have shown, social capital is a necessary prerequisite for the mastery of complex tasks and the creation of new knowledge (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Social capital thus is a necessary complement to human capital: Individual competencies are embedded in social and institutional structures within companies and cannot be as easily transferred to a new context (e.g. a Vform network) as other forms of capital. New social structures (social capital) have to emerge in the inter-firm setting of the V-form to provide the appropriate environment for complex collaboration to take place.

However, our analysis also shows that in comparison to traditional companies, the V-form typically is a sparse environment for the emergence of social capital. Whereas social capital proliferates quite naturally in the traditional company, its emergence in the V-form is hampered by diversity, social and spatial distances as well as volatility resulting from the three dimensions of virtualisation.

On top of this, the V-form organisation as an inter-firm network actually needs more and not less social capital than a traditional company. Due to its lack of formal structures much of the coordination in the inter-firm networks has to rely on inter-personal negotiation and alignment processes that need trust and social capital in order to be effective. Furthermore, effective ICT-based communication also needs more social capital than traditional face-to-face communication. In order for people to effectively communicate via ICT, sufficient social structures (i.e., social capital) have to emerge first (Nohria and Eccles, 1992). In the same way, Kiesler and Cummings (2002) propose that ICT is more likely to be effective when teams already possess high levels of group cohesion. ## A balanced and more elaborate view of VOs is needed

Our conceptual analysis suggests that virtualisation in the three dimensions usually impedes the emergence of social group structures and thus social capital necessary to support complex collaboration in VO projects. Hence, when it comes to innovative and complex tasks we question the feasibility of the V-form as an ideal-type organisation design. Our argument is further supported by the VO examples found in the literature, which almost always exhibit only some of the V-form characteristics while making compromises in other areas (see Table 2). The case analysis shows that in the presence of complex tasks the right conditions for the emergence of social capital need to be created, while at the same time a high degree of virtualisation is possible when tasks are less complex and well separable.

Based on our analysis, we question the idea that the Vform will be the next generation organisation per se. We see the design and management of the value creation processes as key factor, which determines the fit between market environment and the V-form organisation (acc. to Figure 1).

We do not argue that virtualisation is only appropriate for well-structured task environments; rather we have explained the precarious structure of the V-form when it comes to addressing complex tasks. We conclude that when introducing the V-form, task and group structure have to be taken into account. The V-form can be applied in settings of well-structured tasks and established social capital: ‘The various success cases were all examples of loosely coupled work, or work where team members had a lot of common ground’ (Olson and Olson, 2000: 163). In case of ill-structured tasks, measures have to be taken to ensure the social and cultural embedding of the group. While management cannot simply create social capital, it clearly has a role to play in shaping environments that facilitate the development of social capital.

## Management implications

In order for managers to create virtual organisations that do not exhibit the contradictions identified in this analysis the following three strategies might be feasible. Firstly, compromising the flexibility argument by implementing hierarchical management structures can reduce the need for informal coordination and stabilisation on the social level; this approach requires a certain standardisation of tasks. Secondly, the creation of long-term networks as an element of the VO structure allows for the emergence of social capital; this is often observed in regional clusters. Thirdly, management attention in the VO should be devoted to supporting the emergence of social capital.

Hierarchical management structures and standardisation of tasks Some scholars argue that work in the virtual organisation should be governed by a central management entity (Tuma, 1998; Franke, 2001). However, for doing so tasks have to be standardised or limited to well-structured tasks (O’Sullivan, 2003). Standardisation of tasks aims at reducing the range of possible interpretations of the work and its outputs to allow individuals who come from different companies to form accurate and convergent expectations of their work (O’Sullivan, 2003). Standardisation reduces (or even replaces) the need for group-level alignments to be made and consequently the need for collaborative capabilities. When tasks are prespecified to avoid ambiguity, the usage of ICT for the coordination of work also seems to be appropriate. Introna suggests limiting the VO to projects with low- to medium-level task complexity in order to reduce the requirements for social integration and alignments on the project level (Introna, 2001).

Franke introduced the concept of the net-broker as a central coordination entity that maintains the pool of companies, forms the temporary project team and manages the entire project life cycle (2001). As stated above, the introduction of a central coordinator contradicts the idea of a VO as a flexible, non-hierarchical network organisation (Tuma, 1998): ‘Clearly, the broker’s tasks and responsibilities need to be limited if the VO is to maintain its competitive advantages of flexibility and independence’ (Kasper-Fuehrer and Ashkanasy, 2004: 45). On the other hand, it makes feasible geographically dispersed virtual operations that concentrate on capacity aspects and on less complex tasks.

## Interpreting the VO member pool as a breeding ground for socia capital

Typical success factors of inter-organisational networks like a shared understanding of joint tasks, trust among the participants, and commitment and identification need time and a perspective that there is a good reason to invest in social capital with a long-term pay-off. Hence another, however, less recognised, structural property of virtual organisations might provide a way out of the dilemma of short-term projects and the requirements for long-range investments: the distinction between pool and project. Following the pool idea, the VO is based on ‘an open-ended collection of prequalified partners that agree to form a pool of potential members of virtual organizations’ (Goldman et al., 1995). The pool as a long-term structure is made up of the participating companies and can act as a breeding ground for social structure to emerge (Plu¨ss and Huber, 2005). From the pool short-term projects are created, driven by a market opportunity or other immediate goals. Generally, projects are an excellent organisational form for innovative work, but they challenge the development of lasting social networks due to their goal orientation and finite life span, especially when cutting across institutional boundaries (Bresnen et al., 2003). Thus, adding the pool to the VO structure envisions adding the required structural vehicle for social networks and social capital to emerge.

In the existing literature on virtual organisations the pool is interpreted in an abstract way and from a top-level perspective; the usefulness of the pool as the long-term element is stated but its benefits and relevance are not sufficiently explored. Literature focuses – from a resource perspective – on the selection of pool members and the boundaries of the pool. However, it says little about the relationships among the pool members and the interactions going on between them (Goldman et al., 1995; Franke, 2001; Saabeel et al., 2002). As individual competencies are embedded in social and institutional structures within companies, they cannot be as easily transferred to a new context as other forms of capital. New social structures have to emerge in the inter-firm network to provide the appropriate collaborative setting. The pool of participants can provide the required long-term basis for the development of social capital. Managers in the VO have to acknowledge the relevance of social capital and to nurture its emergence and maintenance in the pool as a basis for an effective and successful set up of short-term projects. Ongoing discussions among pool members help building trust, norms and a shared understanding, which facilitates collaboration. Pool-level social capital will then have positive effects on the formation of projects in terms of finding and selecting project participants. Moreover, the facilitation of teamwork will be much easier with existing social relationships among the individuals.

## Management for social capital

The VO management faces the challenge to positively influence the emergence of social capital in a scarce environment. Social capital, however, cannot be managed easily. Informal team structures cannot be blueprinted and designed, they have to emerge and they depend on internal leadership, rather than formal management from the outside. Drawing on recent management literature, the following aspects might be a starting point for how to deal with social capital within the VO: Firstly, opportunities for social capital investments have to be created by bringing people physically together in initial workshops, casual offsite events or even for longer periods of joint co-location, especially in the beginning of VO projects. Clases et al. (2003) point out that sharing frequent experiences with other network members in the realisation of projects plays a very important role in establishing a culture of trust and shared understanding: ‘The development of a common history leading to a mutual understanding of each others’ objectives – a salient denominator of trust – is based very significantly on concrete interaction’ (Clases et al., 2003: 23). Or as Prusak and Cohen state: ‘Social capital grows when team members meet face-to-face and work side-byside’ (Prusak and Cohen, 2001: 88). Secondly, in selecting project team members, managers should capitalise on existing social capital within the pool of collaborating partners in the VO and select those people who already played a vital role in past VO projects and who have existing relationships with employees in the cooperating companies. Thirdly, managers themselves have to display trustworthy and cooperative leadership behaviour that positively encourages and rewards relationship building and cooperative behaviour. Besides, selected people might act as boundary spanners or facilitators with the distinct role of establishing relationships, bringing people together and nurturing the social interaction process in the VO network. Pauleen and Yoong (2001) have shown the importance of facilitators in the process of merging the individual cultures of virtual team members into a team culture. Finally, the application of ICT has to cater for and support social interactions and be embedded in a broader communication strategy that takes into account the importance of temporal co-location and face-to-face interaction. This is especially important since people may ‘distrust electronic media for communicating content of a personal, sensitive and confidential nature’ (Breu and Hemingway, 2004: 201). In addition, ‘groups may also need to more explicitly agree on communication strategies in a ‘‘virtual’’ environment than in a more traditional environment’ (Watson-Manheim et al., 2002: 201).

## Conclusions

Throughout this paper, we have taken a critical look at the promises and structural characteristics of VOs contingent on the nature of innovation and complex tasks. We have introduced the V-form organisation as an ideal-type concept and identified numerous challenges and inherent contradictions, which underline that the V-form is neither a one-size-fits-all model nor a simple solution to a difficult problem. While we do not question that virtualisation is a feasible strategy in modern market environments, we argue for a more realistic and critical view of the concept and its limits. We suggest that VOs should either be restricted to well-structured tasks and governed by hierarchical management mechanisms or the organisational and managerial challenges of virtual collaboration need to be addressed in at least two ways.

By including social capital in the VO discussion, we believe that the concept can gain credibility: VO challenges are outspoken and specific measures are described that address these challenges, complementing diversity, virtuality and volatility with structural amendments and management measures to facilitate social capital. These issues have to be addressed by managers responsible for VO ventures, as well as researchers who have an interest in understanding and enhancing the VO concept and supporting it with ICT (e.g. communityware). Additional empirical research is needed to specifically address the relationship between task requirements, organisational and social structures and information systems. We believe that social capital theory is useful to guide this research. Further research should focus on social structures and practices within existing VOs in order to understand mechanisms for social capital facilitation.

More research is needed on the nature and role of ICT in VOs. In the VO literature, the notion of ICT is typically used vaguely and highly abstract. While throughout this paper we did not elaborate on specific technological aspects for distributed setups, we have however argued elsewhere that new genres of ICT are emerging, which – properly embedded into organisational structures and practices – can indeed facilitate distributed work (Riemer et al., 2007). A practice lens is useful to study such collaborative ICTs at the group level in VOs. To that end, more in-depth case studies and ethnographic research are needed. Finally, we also question the usefulness of the term ‘VO’ as a unique differentiator, because the literature presents a rather diverse set of concepts and examples under this term (see Table 3). Consequently, authors should clarify their interpretation of the VO and use a set of criteria to characterise the examples they are dealing with.

## About the authors

Kai Riemer holds a Diploma in Information Systems and a Ph.D. from Muenster University, Germany. In his current position, he works as an assistant professor in the Department of Information Systems at Muenster University. Dr. Riemer has held positions with University College Dublin and the University of Melbourne. Dr. Riemer has participated in various European and national research projects. His publications and research interests cover the areas of e-Collaboration, Communication and Collaboration Systems, Organisational Networks and e-Commerce. He currently chairs the e-Collaboration research group at the European Research Centre for Information Systems (ERCIS) in Muenster, where he focuses on the organisational and social impact of new ICT, in particular on the role of the social aspects in computer-supported collaboration within and between firms.

Stefan Klein is Professor for Interorganizational Systems and a Director of the European Research Center for Informations Systems (ERCIS) at the Department of Information Systems, Muenster University. He is also a research associate at University College Dublin. Stefan Klein has held teaching or research positions at the University of Linz, Austria, University of Koblenz-Landau, Germany, University of St. Gallen, Switzerland, Harvard

University, German National Research Center for Computer Science (GMD), and University of Cologne. His current research focuses on Electronic Business policies and strategies, ICT impact on industry structures, interorganizational arrangements and collaboration. He has been programme committee member or track chair of international Information Systems conferences and is a member of the editorial board of several international IS journals.
