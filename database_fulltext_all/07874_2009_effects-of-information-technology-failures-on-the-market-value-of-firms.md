# Effects of information technology failures on the market value of firms

## a b s t r a c t

IT failures abound but little is known about the financial impact that these failures have on a firm’s market value. Using the resource-based view of the firm and event study methodology, this study analyzes how firms are penalized by the market when they experience unforeseen operating or implementation-related IT failures. Our sample consists of 213 newspaper reports of IT failures by publicly traded firms, which occurred during a 10-year period. The findings show that IT failures result in a 2% average cumulative abnormal drop in stock prices over a 2-day event window. The results also reveal that the market responds more negatively to implementation failures affecting new systems than to operating failures involving current systems. Further, the study demonstrates that more severe IT failures result in a greater decline in firm value and that firms with a history of IT failures suffer a greater negative impact. The implications of these findings for research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

In the summer of 1993, Greyhound Lines rolled out a new reservation system, Trips. The system turned out to be slow and crash-prone, impeding service quality and leading to a 12% drop in ridership. As this development was reported to the market, Greyhound’s stock tumbled nearly 25%. Over the course of a few months, as problems with Trips continued to surface, the stock price dropped another 60 percent (Tomsho, 1994).

In 1999, a software glitch brought online auction pioneer eBay’s network to its knees for three days in a row, costing the company an estimated \$2 million a day (Li, 1999). Frustrated customers flooded Internet bulletin boards with threats to leave the service. Investors sent eBay stock tumbling almost 10% with more than twice the normal trading volume in the third day of outages.

In February 2001, sports apparel giant Nike informed stock analysts that due to problems in the implementation of a new supply-chain system, the company was unable to meet their third-quarter earnings estimate. The next day, Nike stock lost 20 percent of its value (CNET News, 2001; Worthen, 2002).

Research on runaway projects and information technology (IT) failure has often been motivated by the financial costs of failure and the loss in effectiveness and competitiveness resulting from failures (Keil, 1995; Lee and Myers, 2004; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987). Research studies and industry reports have repeatedly pointed to the high frequency of IT failures as well as the financial burden they constitute (Goldfinch, 2007; Johnson, 1995; Standish Group, 2004). Failure rates are often estimated at 30% and the frequency of troubled projects at 46–53% (Goldfinch, 2007; Johnson, 1995; Standish Group, 2001).

One type of financial consequence that has not been considered in IT failure research is the potential loss in firm value that might result from IT failures as they become known to investors. Investors, as owners, are atop the corporate governance structure and their reaction to IT failures is not only a measure of the value loss from IT failure but also a measure of the importance assigned to IT failures by a key stakeholder group. Therefore, investor reactions to IT failures can also be seen as an indication of whether owners are likely to exercise influence over corporate boards and top executives with regard to IT governance. Should the market be tolerant to IT failures, this could suggest a lack of pressure from owners to bring down the frequency and severity of IT failures. On the other hand, should the market indeed care, it would suggest an additional motivation for executives and IT professionals alike to improve corporate capabilities in deploying and operating IT.

While the literature on IT failure has devoted much attention to the roles of various stakeholders (Larsen and Myers, 1999; Mitev, 2000; Sauer, 1993; Wilson and Howcroft, 2005), the role of investors has received scant attention. Coverage of market value loss from IT failures in the practitioner press (Koch, 2004; Wailgum, 2008) and anecdotal evidence (such as the Greyhound, eBay and Nike examples above) provide further impetus for this study.

Using event study methodology, we provide in this paper the first systematic, empirical evidence of the impact that IT failures can have on the market value of a firm. In so doing, we contribute to the literature on IT failure as well as to the body of literature on strategic management and the impact of IT.

Event studies examining stock market reactions to announcements involving strategic technology decisions have reported positive value relevance of announcements such as decisions to make investments in IT resources (Dos Santos et al., 1993), creation of new Chief Information Officer positions (Chatteriee et al., 2001), and acquistions of Internet firms (Uhlenbruck et al., 2006). However, we found no event study dealing with the value impacts on the level of specific information systems, whether systems in operation or systems being implemented. Furthermore, prior IT-related event studies all concern announcements about decisions or actions that may come to have business impacts at some later point in time (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Hunter et al., 2003; Koh and Venkatraman, 1991). Announcements of positive initiatives require of the market to assess the expected value of benefits resulting from the initiative as well as the probability that these positive results will actually materialize. Perhaps because of this, some prior studies have shown mixed results, for example identifying significant market valuation effects only for subsets of the sample (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Hunter et al., 2003). In contrast, IT failures are events that have materialized and the challenge for the market is to assess the expected loss in value from the failure. Thus, in contrast to previous IT event studies, this research examines the value impact of events that relate directly to actual shortcomings in the firm’s IT capabilities and have direct consequences for business performance.

Previous studies use the resource-based view (RBV) (Barney, 1991; Teece et al., 1997) to demonstrate the market value impact of IT-related announcements by associating IT capabilities and firm value. Following this lead, we are able both to add to existing knowledge about IT failures and how they are assessed and to link IT failure studies to RBV-based studies of the business value of IT (Barua and Mukhopadhyay, 2000; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Wade and Hulland, 2004).

The underlying questions driving this study are: (1) How much do investors care about IT failures? and (2) Do circumstances under which failures occur impact the market’s assessment? In other words, when corporate resources and capabilities fail unexpectedly, how does this impact firm value and does the extent of value loss vary under different conditions? Our study helps provide answers to these questions.

## 2. Background and Hypotheses

A variety of aspects of IT failure have been covered in the literature. Typologies of failure have been developed, particularly for systems that fail during development (Bussen and Myers, 1997; Lucas, 1975; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987). Factors that promote failure have been identified (Beynon-Davies, 1995; Bussen and Myers, 1997; Fitzgerald and Russo, 2005; Irani et al., 2001) and processes and mechanisms that contribute to failure have been described (Myers, 1994; Sauer, 1993).

An underlying view of much of the IT failure literature is that failure is caused by shortcomings in knowledge, competencies and abilities concerning processes of IT governance, IS development, change management, IS implementation, IS operation and IS use (Fitzgerald and Russo, 2005; Irani et al., 2001; Keil, 1995; Markus and Keil, 1994; Sauer, 1993). IT failures have also been characterized as organizational failures (Goulielmos, 2003, 2005) and have been seen as having long-term consequences for organizational learning abilities (Lyytinen and Robey, 1999).<sup>3</sup> While the consequences of IT failures are often characterized as highly negative, few if any attempts have been made to quantify the consequences of failure for firms or their shareholders.

The view of IT failures as being caused by a deficit in organizational capabilities can be directly linked to the resourcebased view of the firm. For example, studies within IS that employ RBV commonly include the following IS/IT capabilities related to avoidance of IT failures: capability to manage IT-related change, practices and capabilities for information processing, ability to experiment with new technologies and conduct technical innovation, and ability to assimilate IT resources into organizational processes (Wade and Hulland, 2004).

The resource-based view portrays firms as heterogeneous bundles of idiosyncratic resources and capabilities (Barney, 1991). Capabilities are ‘‘information-based tangible or intangible processes that are firm-specific and developed over time through complex interactions among the firm’s resources” (Amit and Schoemaker, 1993, p. 35). Using the rubric of RBV, a firm’s IT capabilities have been described as valuable business resources that underpin its competitive position by improving operational efficiency (Litan and Rivlin, 2001) and enabling key strategic initiatives such as customer relationship management, supply chain integration, and business process integration (Ray et al., 2004). Numerous studies have used RBV as a theoretical lens for examining how firms are able to leverage IT resources and competencies (Amit and Zott. 2001: Gregor et al., 2006; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997; Ray et al., 2004; Rivard et al., 2006; Tippins and Sohi, 2003). For example, Powel and Dent-Micallef (1997) showed how firms have outperformed their competition by using IT to leverage complementary human and business resources, Tippins and Sohi (2003) examined the role of organizational learning in mediating the effects of IT competency on firm performance, and Tarafdar and Gordon (2007) showed how specific IT competencies can affect the implementation of process innovations. At this point, it is well accepted that IT applications provide value only when the are successfully implemented, adopted and used in day-to-day operations and decision-making (Markus and Keil, 1994).

Although IT systems are likely to differ in their strategic value to the organization, they nevertheless cause significant disruptions to a firm’s business operations when they fail. The Greyhound Trips system failure described at the beginning of this paper was one in which the failure of an IT system impeded the company’s ability to issue tickets and led to significant delays for travelers, ultimately leading to a decline in ridership. Over and beyond the direct costs associated with fixing the crashed system and losses due to lower productivity, IT failures can result in indirect costs as well. As firms increasingly digitize their supplier and customer facing processes, the consequences of IT failure are greater and more visible than ever before, as illustrated by the Nike example at the beginning of this article. Failures adversely impact not only the firm’s operations but also its external reputation and can result in investors questioning the capability of the firm to achieve growth and revenue targets. In the RBV sense, since the competitive advantage of a firm lies in its ‘‘. . .managerial and organizational processes shaped by specific asset positions” (Teece et al., 1997), IT failures can raise significant doubts about the quality of the underlying IT assets and the organizational processes that are enabled by these IT systems.

The resource-based view emphasizes that IT resources contribute to business performance through combination with other resources (Clemons and Row, 1991; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997), such as when information systems complement highly effective business processes (Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007). For example, an off-the-shelf supply-chain system, while itself a commodity resource, can combine with existing supplier trust and relationships to form a mutually reinforcing and advantage-creating resource bundle. Consequently, when IT systems fail, they weaken the strength and value of the complementary resource bundle. For example, supplier trust can erode and the entire supplier relationship network can be affected when supply-chain systems cause operational disruptions. These in turn can impact the growth and revenue prospects of the firm, and as a result we expect investors on average to perceive the strong potential for negative returns due to IT failures. Thus, we state the following hypothesis:

H1: IT failures will result in negative abnormal returns for the firm.

## 2.1. Type of IT failure: operating versus implementation

In this study, we differentiate between two types of IT failures: operating failures that impact existing systems and implementation failures that involve new systems development projects.<sup>4</sup> From an RBV point of view (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000; Teece et al., 1997), operating IT failures can be seen as evidence of shortcomings in the operational capabilities of the firm, the capabilities to execute processes that produce firm outputs. Similarly, implementation failures can be seen as resulting from shortcomings in the firm’s dynamic capabilities, its capacity to adapt to changing conditions and to improve its functioning and performance (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000; Teece et al., 1997; Winter, 2003). We posit that both types of IT failures can result in business losses and can therefore be expected to affect earnings.

Operating failures, or failures of existing systems, represent temporary glitches that can lead to business disruptions. When such failures are unexpected (e.g., a hardware crash), they can cause significant havoc, as in the example of eBay above, in which the company’s servers crashed, causing \$2 million a day in losses to the company (Li, 1999). While such failures can also signal a weaker capability in IT operations (Wade and Hulland, 2004), they are however, less likely to have long-term implications since normal business operations often resume when the systems are restored.

In contrast to maintaining operational systems, the implementation of information systems requires that firms possess dynamic capabilities related to experimenting with new technologies (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1998) and bundling them creatively with other resources into effective business processes (Rivard et al., 2006; Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007). Consequently, when news about an IT implementation failure become public information, this sends a signal to the market that, in RBV terms, the firm does not have the dynamic capabilities required to successfully make use of a technology resource in order to improve business processes.

Investments in new IT systems represent growth options for the firm (Benaroch, 2002) and therefore implementation failures such as the ones experienced by Greyhound and Nike often have both short-term and long-term implications. In the shortterm, a firm may incur additional costs to turn-around the troubled project (Keil and Robey. 1999) or to write it off, Over the long-term, an implementation failure may delay critical infrastructure, thereby eroding the value of a firm’s growth options. In the case of strategic information systems, such failures could prevent a firm from entering new markets or offering new products and services (Litan and Rivlin, 2001). An implementation failure thus indicates that a firm’s future competitiveness (which is based on its core capabilities)—and by consequence the firm’s future earnings—can be put into question.

Since operating failures involving existing systems are not expected to hamper a firm’s growth options, whereas implementation failures involving new systems may reduce the value of a firm’s growth options and its expected future earnings, we expect that the type of failure will be significant in how the market reacts. Specifically, we state the following hypothesis:

H2: The negative abnormal returns due to implementation (new system) failures will be larger when compared to operating (existing system) failures

## 2.2. Severity of IT Failure

Regardless of the type, IT failures can differ significantly in the extent of damage they cause to the firm’s operations, growth potential and earnings capacity. For example, failures of strategically important systems (Kanter, 2001; Litan and Rivlin, 2001) and failures of systems on which the business is operationally dependent (Mähring and Keil, 2008; Montealegre and Keil, 2000) are generally likely to yield more severe consequences than failures involving systems that are not critical for production or service delivery. Further, when existing systems or new system implementations fail, they can cause disruptions for both the firm and its customers, as seen in the Greyhound and eBay examples given earlier. In the eBay incident, the severity of the outage impeded it from doing business and shook investors’ faith in the company. Severity of failure is conceived here as the extent to which the failure causes a minor or major disruption in customer transactions (a proxy for the strategic importance of the system to the firm) and whether the scope of the failure is narrow or broad (i.e., number of systems or people affected).

In the resource-based view, a more severe IT failure, such as a complete network outage for a telecommunications carrier provides a stronger indication that underlying capabilities are deficient. Conversely, a less severe failure, such as a failed deployment of a beta release in a small test market, constitutes a weaker indicator of IT capability deficiencies. Furthermore. more severe failures indicate deficiencies in both IT capabilities and managerial capabilities (Mahoney and Pandian. 1992: Penrose, 1959). Managerial capabilities are linked to the firm’s capacity for dealing with contingencies, prioritizing, making judgment calls and adapting to change (Adner and Helfat, 2003; Castanias and Helfat, 1991)—in other words, getting the most important things right and fixing problems effectively when they occur. Consequently, firms with well-developed managerial and IT capabilities (Bharadwaj, 2000; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997; Wade and Hulland, 2004) will be much less likely to mismanage a full-scale implementation of a mission-critical system, or neglect maintenance of such systems to the point of large-scale failure events occurring. Based on this argument, we suggest the following hypothesis:

H3: The negative abnormal returns will be larger for IT failures of greater severity.

## 2.3. History of IT Failures

In her analysis of organizational failures, Vaughan (1999) characterizes them as events that deviate from both formal design goals and normative standards either in the fact of their occurrence or in their consequences. While some failures might result purely from chance (e.g. an unexpected virus attack on an IT system), the management literature suggests that many failures occur because of the characteristics of the organizational system (Durkheim, 1966; Vaughan, 1999). Firm characteristics such as structure, processes, and problem-solving routines interact to produce failures (Elsbach and Sutton, 1992). Thus, repeated failures in a firm may be symptomatic of organizational pathologies, such as a tendency to engage in escalation of commitment to failing courses of action (Staw, 1997).

Extending this logic to IT systems, it may be argued that the conditions for repeated IT failures are often embedded in the firm’s IT operations and its learning abilities. Since capabilities are evidenced in repeated patterns of action (Zollo and Winter, 2002), repeated failures that pertain to the same set of capabilities provide stronger and more certain evidence of shortcomings in operational and/or dynamic capabilities.

Lyytinen and Robey (1999) investigated a culture of organizational IT failure and examined why certain IS organizations are prone to repeated failures in their systems development practices. Among the major reasons cited for repeated failure are poor software development and ineffective project management practices that have persisted over extended time periods and made IS departments impervious to change. Other factors such as limits on organizational intelligence, disincentives for learning, and organizational designs that inhibit information and knowledge sharing also contribute to repeated failures in IS projects (Ciborra and Lanzara, 1994; Lyytinen, 1987). In the resource-based view, these reasons taken together translate into inadequate dynamic capabilities (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000; Zollo and Winter, 2002), the capabilities associated with the organization’s ability to learn and change, including the ability to question and change established procedures and modus operandi in a manner akin to double-loop learning (Argyris and Schön, 1978). Repeated IT failures thus serve as signals to the investment community of inherent deficiencies in capabilities that cause repetitive breakdown of key IT systems. Furthermore, they are indicative of the firm’s inability to develop crucial core capabilities through learning from past failures (Andreu and Ciborra, 1996). Finally, in the case of repeated operating failures, they also signal the need for new investments targeted at improving the robustness and reliability of IT systems. ## 3.2. Control variables

Firm and industry characteristics are important controls in this type of analysis. Larger firms with sufficient organizational slack may be less affected by a failure as they can quickly redeploy back-up resources. Firms in high-growth mode are more likely to be critically reliant on their IT systems for expansion and therefore more likely to be impacted by failures. Finally, we expect the impact of failures to be more pronounced for IT intensive industries such as telecom and financial services than for less IT intensive industries such as mining. We accounted for these differences by introducing appropriate controls for firm size, firm growth rate, and industry IT intensiveness. To further control for industry, a second control was introduced using the more traditional manufacturing/services categorization. The study variables are described in Fig. 3.

## 3.3. Event study methodology

We used the event study methodology to estimate the abnormal stock price effects associated with IT failures. The methodology is based on the market model of daily stock price returns, which is used to estimate the abnormal return (AR) for stock i on day t, as $\begin{array} { r } { \mathsf { A R } _ { \mathrm { i t } } = \mathsf { R } _ { \mathrm { i t } } - ( \alpha _ { \mathrm { i } } + \beta _ { \mathrm { i } } R _ { \mathrm { m t } } ) . ^ { 6 } } \end{array}$

As explained in this section, we followed McWilliams and Siegel's (1997) recommendations to insure that the results of our event study analysis were robust. First, our sample size of 213 failure events over a 10-year period was large enough to guard against the possibility that a few outliers might be influencing the results.<sup>7</sup> Our data set was also pre-checked for influential outliers before we ran the regressions; no significant outliers were detected. Second, in keeping with the recommendation of using the smallest justifiable event window, we opted for a narrow 2-day event window comprising the event day and the day prior to it, to account for the possibility that information about the failure might have reached the market the day prior to the actual report in the newspapers. In order to estimate abnormal returns, we used an estimation window of 120 trading days prior to the event and ending 2 days before the event (-120 to -2). Two alternate estimation windows of -200 to -50 and -250 to -50 were also used to verify the robustness of the results. The reason for using generalized least square regression analysis instead of standard multivariate regression analysis was that 36 of the firms in our sample experienced multiple failures over the analysis time period, which raises the issue of nonindependence of observations. The use of GLS regression corrects for this potential problem because it allows for the modeling of within-sample dependencies, provides correct standard errors and ensures that firms providing more data (i.e., with multiple failure events) do not unduly influence the results. Moreover, we used clustered robust standard errors based on the Huber–White sandwich estimator and thus corrected for non-independence of observations impacting the standard errors used.

## 5. Discussion and implications

We now discuss the results of our study and its implications, using the two guiding research questions as an organizing framework.

## 5.1. RQ1: How much to investors care about IT failures?

The strategic management literature has seen a growing interest in understanding the complex relationship between IT and business value. To date, these studies have drawn on RBV to show how IT-based resources can be leveraged to improve firm performance. Previous event studies in this area have examined positive stock market reactions to announcements of strategic IT investment decisions (Lee et al., 2001; Uhlenbruck et al., 2006). Our study contributes to this growing body of literature, as well as to the study of IT failure, by examining the value loss associated with actual IT failures, and thus provides a different angle for testing the tenets of RBV. The results of this research show that when IT resources fail unexpectedly, they most definitely have a negative impact on firm value. Indeed, our results show that there is, on average, nearly a 2% decline in market value when firms experience IT failures. This average is greater than the corresponding positive market value effect observed in studies of announcements concerning intra-firm IT investments (Dardan et al., 2006; Dos Santos et al., 1993; Oh et al., 2006), with CAR values ranging from 0.09% to 0.36%, and up to 1.03% for a sub-sample.

By demonstrating that operational and implementation failures of IT systems negatively impact firm value, our study puts the spotlight on an important stakeholder category, investors, which is often neglected in IT failure studies. In short, the evidence clearly indicates that investors do care about IT failures.

## 5.2. RQ2: Do failure circumstances impact the market’s assessment?

Our results also show that the market’s assessment takes into account the circumstances under which failure occurs. For example, investors were found to respond more negatively to implementation failures than to operating failures. Further, the study provides evidence that more severe IT failures extract a greater penalty from investors, and that firms that have a history of IT failures (i.e., those that had multiple failures during the time period) tend to suffer greater negative impact.

These results suggest that, from an RBV perspective, investors behave quite rationally in their assessment of IT failures. Implementation failures cause operational problems and indicate deficits in IT deployment capability. Since these failures indicate shortcomings in dynamic capabilities that put the firm’s future earnings capacity in question, it is logical that investors react strongly to news about implementation failures.<sup>10</sup> At the same time, operating failures represent more than just a temporary glitch; they are manifestations of underlying operational capability deficiencies that cannot simply be neglected in the assessment of firm value. Similarly, repeated failures provide stronger evidence of capability deficiencies and consequently the investment community extracts a greater penalty from firms that have failed to learn from past mistakes. Finally, more severe failures can hamper the firm’s operations and future growth options to a greater extent than less severe failures. In essence, our findings indicate that the market cares enough and knows enough to assess IT failures differently depending on their nature and circumstances.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

The fact that the market pays attention to IT failures can also provide useful input to research on IT governance and executive involvement in IT. Specifically, our results suggest that corporate boards and executives could be motivated to engage in IT governance activities by the market value impact of IT-related events that become known to the market. It should be of interest to study if attention from owners can, over time, make executives pay closer attention to the state of the firm’s existing systems as well as current development projects.

Presumably, executives, particularly those holding stock options or company shares in their retirement plans, would not like to see a 2% in market value over a 2-day period. Yet, this is precisely what they will see on average when their firm experiences an IT failure. Indeed, the results of our study suggest that when a firm’s market value is negatively impacted by an IT failure, the average market value loss amounts to \$490 million over a 2-day event window.<sup>11</sup> Such a significant market reaction to IT failures underscores the importance of ensuring the reliability of existing IT systems as well as avoiding costly implementation failures. The demonstrated negative market reaction to IT failures should help managers make the case for organizational changes needed to improve the reliability of existing IT systems and to ensure the success of new IT system implementations. It should also provide motivation for boards and executives to ensure that corporate IS/IT strategies and related IT governance practices—including project governance practices, regular assessment of core systems, business continuity risk analyses, and disaster recovery procedures—are appropriate, proactive and effective in minimizing the occurrence of IT failures. It is also important that these aspects, which all relate to ensuring business continuity, business agility and business improvement, are well covered in the firm’s IS/IT strategy.

While executives should constantly reassess their firm’s IT capabilities and take appropriate steps to reduce the probability of failures, they also need to proactively mitigate the impact of failures when they do occur. Knowing that stock markets will react negatively to reports of IT failures, it would be advisable for management to be proactive and engage in damage control to allay investor concerns during IT failures. This means that not only internal but also external impressions need to be carefully and proactively managed when closing down a failed project (Iacovou and Dexter, 1996; Montealegre and Keil, 2000).

Results from our study could be used by executives, chief information officers and other organizational actors as a justification for greater investments that can increase the robustness and reliability of a firm’s core IT systems. Executives should also be aware that investors are more understanding when it comes to unexpected failures of existing systems than they are with respect to failures involving new implementations. Our data thus suggest that from an investor relations standpoint executives should be particularly sensitive to the need to successfully manage new system implementations. Executives should also keep in mind the old adage: ‘‘Fool me once, shame on you! Fool me twice shame on me!” Our results clearly indicate that the when firms face multiple failures, the investment community is not willing to play the fool and expects them to learn from their mistakes and take the necessary precautions to prevent future failures.

## 5.4. Limitations and directions for future research

As is common across all event studies, this research makes a number of standard assumptions. First, is the assumption of market efficiency, which implies that the release of new information about a firm is immediately reflected in its share price. Second, the study assumes that initial newspaper reports of IT failures represented ‘‘surprises” to the market. It is possible that in certain cases—for example, large implementation failures—some information about the impending failure leaked to the market prior to the announcement. Another possible limitation is that the market may react to negative news more severely than is warranted, leading to exaggerated estimates of the impact (Arrow, 1982; DeBondt and Makhija, 1988).

As noted earlier, we have followed McWilliams and Siegel’s (1997) recommendations to avoid some of the pitfalls that can occur with event studies. By collecting our sample over a 10-year period and having a robust sample size of 213 events, we have addressed the most common limitation of event studies, which has to do with small samples and the possibility that a few outliers might be influencing the results. By keeping the smallest justifiable event window, we avoided possible confounding events that could have influenced share prices. We also demonstrated the robustness of our results by using multi ple estimation windows. Finally, a number of alternate analyses with varying control variables and exclusion of potentially confounding events were performed to further test for result robustness, with encouraging results.

As discussed earlier, the 2% decline in market value that we observed following an IT failure is greater than the positive market value effect observed in other studies of announcements concerning intra-firm IT investments. However, more research is needed to test whether this result holds up for positive and negative events concerning the same specified type of IT resource, drawn from the same sources spanning the same time period.<sup>12</sup> Should this be the case, it would suggest that market reactions to positive and negative events are different.

We have also shown that the market responds more negatively to implementation failures than to operating failures. It should also be noted that the market’s attention to operating failures as such is interesting, since the IT failure literature is largely focused on implementation failures (e.g., Fitzgerald and Russo, 2005; Lucas, 1975; Myers, 1994; Sauer, 1993). Our findings suggest that further investigation of operating IT failures is warranted. In order to help balance attention to operating as well as implementation failures, a business continuity lens could prove useful. The institutional theory perspective of business continuity planning, (Zsidisin et al., 2005) represents one such approach that has recently been employed in the supply chain literature with promising results. Further research could provide a finer grained analysis of particular types of implementation failures and operating failures and their relative impact on firm value.

While this study demonstrates that the decline in firm value is greater for more severe IT failures, additional research is needed to develop and test alternative measures of failure severity and to discern what influences failure severity the most, from the perspective of the investor. There are obviously many potential factors that can affect the severity of an event and there is currently no information to suggest what the relative weights of these factors are.

Finally, our results indicate that firms with a history of IT failures exhibit a greater decline in firm value when an IT failure occurs. Future research should be directed toward understanding the response pattern to multiple failures in a more nuanced way, so that we may determine the exact nature of the relationship between the number of failures a firm has experienced in the past and be able to predict the penalty that will be associated with the next failure.

Several other research questions could be posed based on the findings reported here. Does the market extract equivalent penalties for failures involving IT systems for internet-based firms versus more traditional, non-internet-based firms? Do IT failures that involve breach of sensitive data such as customer credit history and medical information cause larger losses? Does the market extract equivalent penalties for outsourced versus insourced IT system failures? A more fine-grained examination of IT failures can provide answers to these interesting questions. Turning to the issue of reporting of IT failures, have the requirements of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (which include regulations on disclosure of operational risks, including IT risks) increased the openness of firms with regards to their IT failures? Conversely, does the common practice of tying executive compensation to stock prices reduce the propensity of firms to disclose IT failures? We recognize that this study represents a beginning and not an end to the investigation of how IT failures affect firms. It is our hope that this study will encourage future research in this interesting and important area.

## Appendix A. Are IS success and IS failure mirror images?

A partial correspondence exists between studies that identify factors in IS development that produce either success (De-Lone and McLean, 2003) or failure (Fowler and Horan, 2007). For example, the presence or absence of top management support, effectiveness in project management and user involvement have proven important in both sets of studies (e.g., Bussen and Myers, 1997; DeLone and McLean, 2003; Doherty and King, 2001). A few studies have made attempts to directly compare success and failure factors (Fitzgerald and Russo, 2005; Fowler and Horan, 2007; Liebowitz, 1999) and some studies have explored the dynamics that may reverse success into failure or vice versa (Irani et al., 2001; Larsen and Myers, 1999).

However. IT failure and IT success are not mirror images. While there are overlaps in factors that promote versus impede success in development and use the dynamics of success and failure are different (Mähring and Keil. 2008: Montealegre and Keil, 2000; Myers, 1994), as are the consequences of success versus failure (Brooks, 1975; Lyytinen and Robey, 1999). Consider, for example, the case of ERP implementation: Successful ERP implementation can provide a new and better platform for running the business and for developing business processes (Broadbent et al., 1999; Srivardhana and Pawlowski, 2007).

As such, a successfully implemented and used ERP system can from an RBV perspective be seen as a new organizational (IT) resource (Peppard and Ward, 2004). This resource can over time become intertwined into organizational processes in a way that leads to improvement of organizational capabilities (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000) in areas such as logistics, inventory management and production (Peppard and Ward, 2004; Srivardhana and Pawlowski, 2007; Wade and Hulland, 2004). Thus consequences (effects, benefits) often appear gradually over a longer time period and are often difficult to attribute directly to any one IT resource because effects from IT resources are indirect (Clemons and Row, 1991). In contrast, a failure in development, implementation and use of an ERP may lead to quite different consequences, because it may carry with it an immediate deficiency in organizational resources and a breakdown of operative capabilities (Hendricks and Singhal, 2003). In the cases of Hershey (Stedman, 1999) and FoxMeyer Drugs (Bulkeley, 1996), failed ERP implementations led to breakdowns in logistics and order fulfillment from which the firms struggled to recuperate, in the case of FoxMeyer even failing to do so. Consequently, we argue that from an RBV perspective, the organizational consequences of IT failure versus IT success can be significantly, qualitatively different.
