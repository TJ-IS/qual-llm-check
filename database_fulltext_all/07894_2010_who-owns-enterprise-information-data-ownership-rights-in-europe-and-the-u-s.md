# Who owns enterprise information? Data ownership rights in Europe and the U.S.

Elad Harison

Shamoon College of Engineering, Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Bialik St. Be’er Sheva, Israel

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 15 October 2007 Received in revised form 29 August 2008 Accepted 27 November 2009 Available online 4 January 2010

Keywords: Databases Business information Ownership rights Database Directive Intellectual property

## A B S T R A C T

U.S. legislation provides protection to database contents by intellectual property rights (IPRs) only if ‘‘a minimal degree of creativity’’ was involved in their creation. Although data collection may involve significant investments, factual content is not protected.

The goal of the European Database Directive was to guarantee legal provisions for database content and digitized collections. However, the Directive has prompted a public debate about the accessibility to data sets for public use. We reviewed the legal status of database components and assessed the impact of both legislation regimes on a firm’s strategies and policies

## 1. Introduction

The database industry is growing at a very rapid pace. Apart from a dramatic rise of the numbers of digitized records and databases worldwide, there has been an increase in the use of online and CD-ROM-based IR technologies. For example, between 1975 and 2003 the number of databases in the U.S. increased by a factor of 55, while the amount of stored records multiplied by 170 (Fig. 1). During this period, the number of database producers grew from 105 to 3289. Similar growth trends occurred in Europe since the 1990s. In financial terms, the turnover of the European database industry increased from 3.1 billion Euros in 1990 to 11 billion Euros in 2005.

The global information markets are dominated by U.S. producers and publishers of databases. In 1997 the U.S. industry was estimated at 82.4 billion dollars—29% of the total electronic processing market in the OECD countries. The E.U.’s share of the market was estimated at 19% (53.4 billion dollars) [7] and 26% in the case of scientific, technical and medical databases [6], based on EC COM (93)-156, which defines the guidelines for the EC’s IMPACT program and EADP’s estimates [3]. Recent figures, however, may be significantly higher, due to the rapid growth of Internet databases, online B2B directories, and Internet data collections used in advertising.

Before the 1980s, information goods (intangible and knowledge-based commodities provided in digital format—consisting of databases, software programs, and computer files, etc. and\characterized as non-rival and non-excludable goods) were often perceived as public domain, being freely disclosed for public use. However, since then commercialized information goods have started to dominate the major segments of the consumption and media markets. As these databases were commercialized and became sources of profit, issues of appropriation emerged. And in the course of time, other problems have arisen from the substantial differences between the U.S. rulings and the E.U. legislation with respect to the ownership rights to databases and digitally stored data (see the evolution of database protection regimes in Table 1).

## 2. Legal protection of databases and the E.U. regime

## 2.1. Background

Doctrines of intellectual property rights (IPR) distinguish between contents that are digitally stored within a database (e.g. files, photographs, literary art, and facts) and the internal organization, structure and processes embedded in a database (e.g. its access to records, indices, and related database management applications) in terms of their legal status and the regime that protects them as IP.

Although the method of database organization is patentable in the U.S. and in some cases in Europe too, the protection of database contents as IP is under debate. Facts and other objective items of information cannot be protected by copyrights, as they lack creative properties. Artistic and literary works (such as books, music, and photographs), however, are given copyright protection in all their forms.

Information production is seen as a labour-intensive activity, involving various levels of human skills required in conducting surveys, collecting and aggregating crude data, loading information into a database structure, and making skillful analyses. Generating new databases or creating derivatives from existing databases often require investment in IT, surveys, data procurement, and human capital.

Unlike the large investments necessary to formulate and produce a database, the costs of copying data as well as extracting and using them are only minor. In addition, as the information is publicly disclosed to authorized users, the reproduction of accessible records and the creation of a new commercialized variant of this information from an existing database can be achieved at small cost compared to the initial cost of organizing the original database. In order to avoid the unauthorized and illegitimate use of databases, technical means to restrict access to their contents (such as passwords and encryption) are employed for online databases. The Digital Millennium Copyright Act, enacted in the U.S. in 1998, supported anti-circumvention regulations that prohibit copyright infringements on technically protected information by hacking techniques. In addition to the possibilities of protecting databases by technical means, legislation plays a major role in the protection of content.

Accessibility to data and information is essential in firms’ dayto-day operations and for enhancing their innovative capabilities via knowledge acquisition [8,9]. Firms, however, particularly SMEs, should be aware that, after the approval of the EC Database Directive, their access to and use of European datasets has gradually been restricted. First, in most technical fields, the IPRs have been strengthened and their duration has been significantly elongated. In the case of databases, the Directive has transferred contents that were traditionally considered as public domain into a proprietary form. Consequently, database publishers are free to charge for them. Costly databases have become inaccessible to many small firms and start-ups. Second, databases formerly operated by governmental organizations (e.g. in the fields of life science and geocartographics) have been out-sourced and commercialized. Licensing and access fees for data that were previously publicly available have increased. The accepted statutory guidelines roughly divide the elements of corporate databases into four types, based on their legal status as IP and consequent need for protection:

(1) The method of organization includes search algorithms, indexing and cataloguing methods for data, facts, information items and works. These elements may be protected by patents in the U.S., but cannot, as such, be protected in Europe (i.e. be protected as software inventions) unless they form part of a physical invention, such as electronic equipment or a chemical process.

(2) Contents of the database that demonstrate originality and creativity are protected by copyright, as defined by the Berne Convention and its derivatives in the U.S. and in Europe. Unlike patents, which inventors must register with the national Patent Office, copyright protection is ‘‘automatically’’ provided to artistic and literary works by the author recording the material in a tangible medium, even when it is not formally registered anywhere.

(3) In most member states of the Berne Convention, contents are not copy-protected if no intellectual effort has been involved in their creation. For example, copyright protection does not apply to personal records and news facts. However, since the approval of the Database Directive by the European Parliament in 1996, the database records in the E.U. Member States have been protected by a sui-generis (unique, specially tailored, or one of its own) laws.

## 2.2. The E.C. Database Directive

Based on the EEC’s IMPACT research program recommendations, the background work for the EC Directive for the Legal Protection of Databases was formally initiated in the early 1990s. Its original aims included the implementation of legislation based on a statutory policy for non-copyrightable contents of databases, as well as the formulation of guidelines to harmonize the legislation of database IPRs in the Member States. The Directive followed a legal model adopted from the Danish Copyright Act, which granted a term of 10 years for the protection of contents in printed and electronically stored databases. Unlike the Danish Act, the Directive initially included only information elements that were electronically stored.

The first draft was submitted in 1992, after which it was extensively modified in response to comments from and interests of European information industrialists, scholars in Science and Technology, and advocates for free information flow. As Britain’s market share in the electronic data processing market was 28.5% of the total share of the E.U. Member States, the British database producers’ voice in the initial discussions, as well as in further hearings, was dominant in the initial discussions of the Directive and in further hearings. To compare: Germany’s share of the market was 15.7%; Ireland’s share 14.7%; the Netherlands’ only 6.4%. The final version of the Directive was approved in March 1996 as DIRECTIVE 96/6/EC, which includes a sui-generis section, providing protection to database contents that are not copyrightable by the terms of the Berne Convention. To emphasize the issue of the sui-generis law, the E.U. Member States that had fully accepted and signed the Berne Convention agreed to the establishment of a harmonized regime that did not substantially differ from the other copyright legislation regimes. The accepted Directive consisted of compulsory guidelines for the E.U. legislation and contained an additional section having a broader focus on uniformity than the Berne Convention aspired to achieve; it expanded the scope of protection of databases in the E.U., in contrast to regimes in other member states of the Convention.

Since contents that did not involve innovation or creativity would not qualify for copyright protection, European producers and database publishers became concerned about the vulnerability of these (generally factual) data sets to unauthorized access and use by users and rivals. As stated in the Directive:

‘‘Whereas the making of databases requires the investment of considerable human, technical and financial resources while such databases can be copied or accessed at a fraction of the cost needed to design them independently. . . Whereas the unauthorized extraction and/or re-utilization of the contents of a database constitute acts which can have serious economic and technical consequences. . . Whereas such an investment in modern information storage and processing systems will not take place within the Community unless a stable and uniform legal protection regime is introduced for the protection of the rights of makers of databases’’.

Yet, the EC Directive only provided legal protection in countries that applied a similar sui-generis regime, thereby excluding U.S. databases from the scope of protection in European markets. This had a significant impact on trade between the U.S. and the E.U. The Directive was accepted in violation of WIPO’s aim of harmonizing national and regional IPR regimes and avoiding international legislation variations. Furthermore, as E.U. information industries were underdeveloped compared to those in the U.S., a strong IPR regime would not necessarily serve the purpose of the development of information industries in the Inner Market. Other institutional schemes, such as subsidies and tax incentives, may well foster industrial evolution, whereas a protective E.U. regime might hinder it, as the possibilities for mergers and cooperation with American affiliates decreased due to these differences in legislation.

In the E.U., the Directive grants databases 15 years of protection. Since evidence has indicated that the average life of commercial databases is 3 years, this 15-year period was accepted as a compromise between policy makers and representatives of the European information industry. In economic terms, most of the ROI is received within 3 years [10]. Further, if ‘‘substantial’’ changes and investments are made, a new 15 years term of protection starts. Therefore, enterprises producing European content may adopt a mixed strategy as follows: during the first 3 years, the database contents are distributed as commercial products. Then, when their value has marginalized, they are made free to the public to signal the value of new data to a larger population of users and thus foster their use by new and potential customers. Similar hybrid strategies can be applied to software products [5] by using freely distributed Open Source modules to foster the purchase of complementary and proprietary features, services, and technical support.

## 3. Database protection in the U.S. after Feist

The U.S. Copyright Act protects the contents of databases as IP, defining them as compilations. Protection is provided when the data are ‘‘selected, co-ordinated and arranged in an original way’’.

In 1991 the U.S. Supreme Court defined guidelines for the protection of database contents in the Feist vs. Rural Telephone Service ruling. The defendant’s actions, consisted of copying database records that contained telephone numbers and addresses (similar to the phone company’s White Pages) were found to be legally acceptable and therefore not an infringement of the copyright of the plaintiff. More importantly, the verdict stated that ‘‘facts do not become original through association’’ and ‘‘constitutional mandate [is] to reward the fruits of the creative process and not the labour involved in the process itself’’. Justice O’Connor explained the Court’s verdict as follows: ‘‘Many compilations consist of nothing but raw data—i.e., wholly factual information not accompanied by any original written expression. On what basis may one claim a copyright in such a work?. . . The sine qua non of copyright is originality. To qualify for copyright protection, a work must be original to the author. Original, as the term is used in copyright, means only that the work was independently created by the author (as opposed to copied from other works), and that it possesses at least some minimal degree of creativity.’’ Finally, he argued that ‘‘common sense tells us that 100 uncopyrightable facts do not magically change their status when gathered together in one place’’ (Section II-A).

The Feist ruling is important in two ways: first, the verdict clearly defined the necessity of ‘‘a minimal degree of creativity’’ needed in the production of a database to justify its protection by copyright. Thus factual databases are not covered by copyright provisions, as the digitization of facts is not a creative activity and does not differ from other forms of collection. Second, prior to the verdict, factual contents of databases had been given the somewhat ambiguous status of being semi-protected, was and thus open to lawsuits and actions. The ruling clearly reduced the level of protection of database contents.

This decision has created legal differences between the U.S. and Europe. Since this ruling curtails the protection provided to database producers in the U.S., the European producers o have a competitive advantage over American rivals. Similarly, users of American data have an advantage because data from U.S. sources were available at no costs. Additionally, however, the ruling applied to database use in the U.S., regardless of its source, including data produced in Europe and distributed in the American market. Hence, European information producers and database publishers were confronted by commercial threats by competitive advantage of the information industry in the U.S.

Because of the weakening influence of IP rights, the U.S. Department of Commerce predicted a decline in the pace of developments in the U.S. information industry. Moreover, the suggestion to establish an E.U. Database Directive, approved by the European Parliament in 1996, would be likely to give European firms an advantage, since it promoted a stronger protection of their databases. U.S. policy makers perceived a possible exposure of data collections to unauthorized (though legal) duplication and use as a potential threat to the development of their information industry. Major actors in the area presented scenarios of market failure and innovation delay in a wide variety of scientific and technological fields.

## 4. The impact of the U.S. legislation and the Database Directive on scientific and technological enterprises

Scientific research relies heavily on computing in testing relations among system variables and generating new hypotheses. This field is the second largest user of databases (see Fig. 2). New results are built on derivatives of data sets collected over time (see for example the growth of the Genbank database, an annotated collection of publicly available DNA sequences, in Fig. 3).

Databases therefore enable researchers to expand their activities by the analysis of collected records. Some data, e.g. meteorological data, are freely accessible via the Internet. Such easy access permits the use of large quantities of data in examining a complex theoretical framework.

If access to scientific data is restricted by the EC Directive, it may slow down the production of scientific knowledge; for example, geologists using the Landsat Earth imagery database are prohibited by the Directive’s guidelines from disseminating derivative works that were established from the contents of database they used in their research [1]. Moreover, despite the efforts of European database producers to fortify their position in the market, the growth in their market share after enactment of the Directive was only short-lived: since 2001, the market share of U.S. databases has grown while the share of European producers has continuously decreased (see Fig. 4).

In addition, the principle of first buyer needs to be reviewed. According to this principle, copyright protection allows the reproduction of works originally purchased by public libraries and institutes (‘‘first buyers’’) by the public under the restrictions of Fair Use, which applies to quotations in scientific reviews, books borrowed from libraries, and article photocopying for educational purposes by faculty and students. Currently, as publishers digitize the contents of scientific and technical articles, libraries and research institutes prefer database access to hardcopies. Hence, publishers may improve their profitability and legal protection by converting works that are not covered by the Copyright Act into databases that are legally protected by the Directive and therefore not subject to Fair Use exemption. Photocopying the hardcopy of an article has thus become impossible since it is legal only under the Fair Use exemptions. Moreover, as articles are now regarded as database contents, to which accessibility is more restricted, research (and educational) activities may be impeded.

As libraries have shifted to online access through publisher databases, scientific journals have become available online, as a result of which distribution agencies are losing their traditional role and, because these databases now have strong IPR protection, alternative sources are becoming unavailable. We will see an increasing dependence of research institutes and libraries on direct access from the publisher, giving journals a monopoly on the supply side. The Directive contains several restrictive Fair Use provisions, which made access even more restrictive for researchers. First, the sui-generis approach differs from traditional copyright regimes in that it includes non-original artifacts in its scope of protection, thereby excluding them from the public domain; the new IPR regime grants rights for a long duration even if the copyrighted works are merely collected and digitized. The transition of libraries and research institutes from purchasing printed volumes of scientific journals to subscribing to online collections exemplifies some of the problems associated with the legal guidelines of the Database Directive. When libraries subscribe to online collections, they are granted access to the contents for the subscription period only. Researchers cannot access the contents when the subscription ends, while printed media are available from the time of purchase. Second, the Directive narrows the distinction between original content and material already in the public domain. It grants ownership to collections of freely distributed contents if the database owners can show that investments and efforts have been involved in generating the database. Moreover, if substantial changes are made to existing databases, the term of protection is extended by 15 years, continuing the ownership. Third, as the integration of content into external databases is restricted, both the accessibility and compilation of information become limited. Furthermore, the exclusive rights allow the publisher unrestricted licensing of the databases without allowing for compulsory licensing to research and educational institutions. Hence, although they are generated from existing sources, the new databases are given unregulated niche-monopolies over the knowledge. Previously, the academic community involved in basic public research was able to access information for their research activities t. Although the Directive included the sui-generis exception it did not completely support the free use of factual databases. This may be detrimental to basic research.

As retrieval technologies enable the storage and selection of video, audio, etc., researchers are equally threatened by the recent legislation. A broad range of Fair Use exemptions in database use, or else compulsory licensing for researchers at reduced costs, can prevent the vulnerability of scientific research to over-priced access fees and over-fencing by protective legal regimes. However, although these suggestions were presented in the early stages of the hearings during the Directive’s legislation process, none were accepted by the policy makers.

## 5. The value of public and private data: evidence from recent cases

5.1. Opportunities and limitations of protecting public data: the cases of sports fixture lists

Since the enactment of the Database Directive by the EC, firms and organizations throughout Europe have begun to recognize the value of their data, seeking new opportunities for exploiting and protecting them as a new form of IP.

Not surprisingly, most of the cases in which firms claimed infringement on their database rights was in the use of competition records in the sports gambling industry, especially football matches and horse racing records. The vast volumes of transactions and the possibility of improving the prospects of winning bets through analyzing information on past performance of competitors and the changes in their health and stamina over time has fostered the acquisition of sports data by bookmakers, who offer it as free information on horses, matches, and sports players as a premium service for their clients, thereby attracting new clients. The case of the British Horseracing Board (BHB) vs. the William Hill Organization (U.K. case no. C-203/02) demonstrated instances of legal disputes, interpretations, and data protection means resulting from the Database Directive.

The BHB manages the horse racing industry in the UK. One of its activities is compiling and maintaining a database that contains information on registered horses, their owners, trainers, and jockeys. In addition, the BHB documents the performance of horses in races and compiles fixture lists of horses to participate in races. The database contains 20 million records and the cost of maintaining it is approximately 4 million GBP per annum (BHB recovers a quarter of this amount by fees charged to third parties for using its records).

In 2000 William Hill, a U.K.-based bookmaking agency, expanded its betting services to the Internet by offering BHBsupplied information to clients via its website. BHB submitted a lawsuit against the firm, claiming that, according to the Database Directive, it owned the rights to the database. BHB; thus William Hill’s use of the data was unauthorized and illegal. William Hill indicated that the sui-generis protection only covered the structure of the database and not the facts that it contained; therefore, BHB’s rights were not violated.

In the first stage of the legal proceedings, Britain’s High Court of Justice accepted BHB’s arguments, concluding that the investment in gathering the data was an infringement of database rights, as the Directive included the investments required for creating, obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents of databases. The Court also concluded that William Hill had extracted substantial parts of BHB’s data in violation of Article 7 of the Directive.

In an appeal to the European Court of Justice (ECJ), William Hill raised issues regarding the interpretations of the key terms of the Directive. In November 2004, the ECJ resolved these issues, but not in BHB’s favor, ruling that the Directive secured the investments in obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents of databases, whereas the investments in creating them cannot be protected. Further, the database owners had to prove that substantial investments (qualitative or quantitative) were made to qualify for the protection of contents by the sui-generis right. The ECJ did not consider BHB’s efforts in producing horse lists as the creation of data, since BHB had not made any investments in obtaining them. Due to the ECJ’s interpretation of the Directive, the investments made in creating these data could not be considered when assessing whether the database contents were protected. In response to the ECJ’s ruling the British Court of Appeals finally ruled that BHB’s database did not qualify for the sui-generis right of the Directive and therefore William Hill’s actions were not considered as an infringement of BHB’s database rights.

The ECJ’s ruling established legal guidelines for the protection of database contents which were applied in similar lawsuits held in several EU nations during the same period. Following the ECJ’s ruling, several European national Courts rejected a series of lawsuits that were submitted by Fixtures Marketing, a company that grants licenses for the use of British football fixture lists outside the U.K. It initiated similar lawsuits for the unauthorized use of its databases against Oy Veikkaus AB (in Finland), AB Svenska Spel (in Sweden) and Prognostikon Agonon Pododfairou AE (in Greece). Courts concluded that despite Fixture Marketing’s investments in creating the fixture lists and databases, amounting to 7 million GBP per annum, these expenditures did not apply to obtaining, verifying, or presenting the data. Thus the Database Directive did not protect these contents and therefore Fixtures Marketing could not prevent their use by other firms.

5.2. Protecting business information via database ownership rights: the Crowson ruling

Besides the application of the Database Directive for the protection of sports and allied data, its legal use was expanded to the protection of enterprise databases of former employees or potential rivals. The interpretation of the Directive by the Court took a new turn in the case of Crowson Fabrics vs. Ryder, Stimson and Concept Textiles (U.K. case no. EWHC 2942; December 2007).

Mr. Ryder was a former product and distribution director and Mr. Stimson a former export manager at Crowson Fabrics, a leading U.K. firm in the decoration apparels sector. Toward the end of their employment, both were requested to provide the company with lists of suppliers and clients, including names, addresses, terms of payment, and lead-time, to preserve business information and their know-how within the firm, and to make it available to the other employees. Importantly, Ryder and Stimson’s employment contracts did not include confidentiality terms.

After they had left Crowson Fabrics, Ryder and Stimson set up a rival company—Concept Textiles. They used Crowson’s database of clients and suppliers to determine the prices and expected leadtimes of similar products. Further, they started to contact Crowson’s agents and clients, intending to offer better terms.

After reviewing the evidence in this case, the Court concluded that Crowson had failed to prove that the defendants’ actions had caused any substantial losses and therefore no damages could be determined on this basis. However, the ruling affirmed Crowson’s ownership rights to its databases in accordance with the Directive’s orders, despite the fact that Ryder and Stimson had composed the database on the basis of their own knowledge and experience, and had made it available to other employees. Consequently, the Court concluded that the defendants had infringed Crowson’s database rights and suggested ‘‘to consider whether the appropriate relief will be to grant damages. . . by reference to what would be a reasonable price the Defendants ought to pay for using the Claimant’s documents as a shortcut to setting up their business’’.

## 6. Possible developments

Several proposals for the sui-generis regime have been submitted to the US Senate and Congress, specifically designed to protect the contents of databases and preserve the incentives for database production in the private sector. HR354 was presented as an initial policy paper for discussion. It elaborated on the consequences of current laws and proposed means to both safeguard the interests of the information industry and maintain the free flow of information. The Coalition of Commercial and Not-For-Profit Interests also submitted a proposal, which mainly focused on short-term possibilities to preserve a free information flow and guarantee accessibility to databases. The Senate Discussion was based on HR354, but it introduces different solutions and suggested an alternative regime. Table 2 compares the current database protection regime and the proposals for change.

Fully adopting one particular proposal may not be best,. A future regime accepted by U.S. legislators will probably be aimed at harmonizing the strengths on the basis of the initial drafts. According to HR354, data ownership rights will be granted to database owners for both existing market segments and those that could be developed in future on the basis of the current databases. Thus, it could foster the emergence of a monopolistic market structure in which a sole database provider dominates the actual market niches as well as future (potential) markets. Under the market rules of HR354, new entrants may be pre-empted from both current and future markets, as the development of new technologies on the basis of existing databases will become extremely difficult.

The regime as proposed by the Coalition may discourage investment in data collection and organization. The proposal attempted to preserve accessibility and free use of databases by non-profit organizations with need for information, but decrease the possibility of database producers making long term profits. Although the legal provisions can be seen as a means to obtain a free flow of information, this may be achieved at the expense of quality of the data sets and the variety of information sources. Moreover, the lack of incentive for database production may result in infrequent updating. Further, the strictness could cause an increase in the price of the database information, its data derivatives and complementary services, since data owners are in fact penalized by free duplication. Consequently, the prices of information-related services could be expected to rise, as downstream suppliers (providing derivatives of processed informations) would raise their price levels to maintain their profit margin.

The current IPR regime in the U.S., based on a single Court ruling (Feist), provided database producers with minimal copyright protection, as it excluded factual contents. Although the basic framework of the U.S. legislation is as not yet being altered, technological developments will call for modifications to the protection of database contents i. Moreover, the U.S.’ attempts to maintain dominance of the information industry globally will motivate U.S. legislators to consider further changes.

## 7. Conclusions

U.S. legislation was formulated prior to the dramatic growth of the volume of digitally stored data due to the use of the Internet. Factual content is not protected unless it requires ‘‘a minimal degree of creativity’’ to give it copyright protection. In order to avoid market failure resulting from under-investment by database publishers whose collections are not protected, the EC Database Directive was aimed at providing legal measures that protect databases, even though their content are only factual.

This Directive has prompted a public debate about the differences between the legislative frameworks of the E.U., the U.S., and WIPO, and the extent to which the regime can protect databases without hindering access to scientific and technical data.

The scope of applications and uses of customer and enterprise data is continuously expanding. Commercial and private data consumers use a broad spectrum of information sources as inputs for decision making, profit generation, and knowledge acquisition. Hence, CTOs and CIOs of private firms should be aware of the possibilities for generating profit and derive value from enterprise datasets. Alternatively, firms should consider the potential legal risks and restrictions (beyond those stated in their licensing agreement). These conditions can differ substantially in the E.U. and the U.S.

European firms are exploring the legal means granted by the Database Directive to guarantee their ownership rights to their data against potential rivals and unauthorized users, thereby securing their revenues.

Interestingly, the ECJ’s interpretation of the BHB case excluded database producers who ‘‘create’’ rather than ‘‘obtain’’ their data, while offering new opportunities to other firms to extract and utilize their data. Such database producers were among the primary proponents of the Directive’s enactment in response to the Feist ruling. Cases such as these also demonstrate how firms’ awareness of the Database Directive expanded beyond the media sector to other industries as well.
