# Assessing the Credibility of Decisional Guidance Delivered by Information Systems

ANDREW HARDIN, CLAYTON A. LOONEY, AND GREGORY D. MOODY

ABSTRACT: This work investigates the independent and joint influences of suggestive guidance and credibility indicators in a repeated choice environment laden with risk and uncertainty. Consistent with expectations, results from two studies reveal that aggressive and conservative suggestive guidance influence participant decision making. Credibility indicators partially moderate these relationships such that low credibility indicators lead to increased risk taking when suggestive guidance is conservative and decreased risk taking when suggestive guidance is aggressive. Post hoc analyses designed to examine differences in results across the two studies reveal unexpected differences in risk trends across participant type. Together, these findings contribute to the decision support system (DSS) literature by illustrating how credibility indicators can push users toward or away from either beneficial or detrimental suggestive guidance, and further, by challenging the commonly held assumption that inexperienced and experienced decision makers accept similar levels of risk in DSS settings where risk and uncertainty prevail.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: computer-supported decision making, credibility indicators, decision biases, decision guidance, decision support systems.

Consistent with the contemporary studies of human–computer division of labor [1], a rich tradition of research indicates that features embedded within an information system can supplement and enhance human decision making [10, 11, 16, 23, 48]. Relevant to the current context, Looney and Hardin [26] extended this important body of work to the domain of repeated, risky-choice environments by demonstrating that individuals overcome cognitive biases when following suggestive guidance delivered by decision support systems (DSSs). While noteworthy, this finding represents only the first step in understanding how the allocation of tasks between humans and computers can influence decision making in repeated, risky-choice environments. Naturally, many decisions warrant conservative rather than aggressive approaches. Because Looney and Hardin [26] evaluated only users willingness to follow aggressive suggestive guidance, understanding whether users are similarly willing to follow conservative suggestive guidance remains an open empirical issue. Moreover, Looney and Hardin [26] ignored the interactive effects of design features commonly employed in contemporary, computer-mediated decision-making environments. For instance, credibility indicators influence decision making in a number of areas [39], indicating that perceptions of quality may also affect whether users accept decisional guidance in risky-choice environments.

The goal of the present research is to evaluate the independent and joint influences of suggestive guidance and credibility indicators on a user’s willingness to accept risk over time. Toward this end, this manuscript begins by describing decisional guidance and its relevance to the human–computer division of labor.

## Theoretical Background

Decisional Guidance and the Human–Computer Division of Labor

The increasing capacity of computers has led to a resurgence of interest in the division of tasks between humans and machines [53, 57, 58]. The automation of rules-based processes and routine analytical tasks has liberated humans from mundane activities that would normally keep them from completing higher value-added endeavors [22, 40]. In the realm of decision making, effective DSSs help compensate for the inherent weaknesses of human judgment while still enabling humans to adjust model advice based on prevailing circumstances [20]. When effective, these information systems also display actionable, nonmodeled information that influences how users allocate portions of the decisional task between the DSS and their own judgments [18].

While normative approaches offer optimal solutions to many types of risky, uncertain decisions, relying on these methods requires users to expend considerable cognitive effort. As is well-documented, when faced with effort–accuracy trade-offs, individuals favor minimizing cognitive effort in lieu of achieving optimal outcomes [56]. Thus, DSS features must simultaneously guide users toward more optimal solutions while also reducing their cognitive effort.

One promising approach involves configuring a DSS to deliver suggestive guidance. Rather than reinforcing the use of more effortful normative solutions, suggestive guidance conserves users’ cognitive capacities by recommending a particular course of action. Specific to risky-choice environments, aggressive suggestive guidance also helps users overcome the overly cautious tendencies predicted by predominant descriptive theories [13]. Despite the benefits of following the appropriate suggestive guidance, however, user preferences can sometimes constrain the influential effects of the DSS recommendations. Although the decision maker’s preferences tend to move in the direction of the advice, his or her judgment also carries considerable weight [55, 56]. Thus, from a human–computer workload perspective, it appears that humans may be reluctant to relinquish major portions of decisional tasks to computers.

In these situations, DSSs can provide additional nonmodeled credibility information to help push users toward accepting or rejecting the model advice. People use positive or negative information provided by others during decision making [51], as evidenced by consumers, who tend to rely heavily on sources they find credible when making purchase decisions [25, 53]. Similarly, DSSs can assist decision makers by providing information that assesses the quality of the suggestive guidance. Escalating the influence of the nonmodeled information, evaluating model advice credibility requires users to increase their cognitive effort. When faced with potential cognitive overload, people tend to rely on source characteristics as a convenient heuristic to help guide their decisions [8]. Thus, in uncertain, riskychoice environments, the provision of credibility information may help nudge users toward or away from DSS model advice.

## Hypotheses Development

Implementing overly restrictive guidance is unrealistic in today’s information age [13]. Providing users with guidance designed to aid their choices, however, is a generally accepted practice [54]. Credibility indicators are commonly used to signal recommendation quality [39]. Consequently, as depicted in Figure 1, the interactive effects of decisional guidance (conservative and aggressive suggestive guidance), and credibility indicators (low and high star ratings)— are empirically assessed during two studies to ascertain their interactive influence on risk taking in a repeated, risky-choice environment. The simultaneous examination of these two features helps build knowledge related to the research question: Do decisional guidance and credibility indicators influence decision making in repeated, risky-choice environments?

## Decisional Guidance

Information systems can be designed to assist users when decision outcomes are probabilistic or uncertain [20]. The design of these systems commonly requires individuals to exercise judgment during the decision-making process, however, users can also rely on decisional guidance as they contemplate the many choices available to them [43, 44]. Decisional guidance “enlightens or sways its users as they structure and execute their decision-making processes—that is, as they choose among and use the system’s functional capabilities” [45, p. 107]. Decisional guidance in the form of suggestive guidance is particularly useful for nudging individuals toward an appropriate decision. Suggestive guidance proposes a specific course of action, more strongly influencing the decision-making process than its close counterpart, informative guidance, which provides guidance without making any specific recommendations [34, 45, 46].

The current study uses two specific types of suggestive guidance. The first type is aggressive suggestive guidance that is designed to move individuals away from overly conservative choices [26]. Individuals frequently rely on recommendations to reduce uncertainty associated with risky decisions [56], and tend to revise initial judgments in the direction of advice [14]. This proclivity to revise initial judgments helps explain how aggressive suggestive guidance can push decision makers toward riskier decisions.

This study introduces conservative suggestive guidance as a second form of suggestive guidance capable of nudging individuals toward more conservative choices. Unlike aggressive suggestive guidance, which decreases perceived riskiness, conservative suggestive guidance should increase perceptions of riskiness through the recommendation of safer choices. In other words, recommending conservative choices focuses the individual on the riskiness of some decisions. In the current context, users experience scenarios that favor riskier options. Despite the benefits of incorporating these riskier choices into their portfolios, however, we expect that participants will yield to the conservative suggestive guidance, and contribute less to the riskier choice than individuals presented with aggressive suggestive guidance.

Hypothesis 1: Contributions to the riskier option will be greater when aggressive suggestive guidance is present than when conservative suggestive guidance is present.

## Suggestive Guidance and Credibility Indicators

Indications of credibility provide support for recommendations to follow a particular course of action. Credibility indicators come in many forms, such as numerical content ratings [39], online product reviews [9], and advice quality [14]. Prior studies have frequently demonstrated the effectiveness of credibility indicators for influencing decision-making processes. For instance, consumers rely on ratings websites as the primary mechanism for optimizing purchase decisions [3, 32], and advice quality drives the extent to which users incorporate recommendations into their decision-making processes [14]. As a result, in the current study credibility indicators should moderate the effects of suggestive guidance on risk taking. Exactly how credibility type (high versus low) moderates decision making is less clear, however. While high credibility can be effective for shaping attitudes [38], other research has shown low credibility to be more efficient for this purpose [5, 6]. Still other research has reported no difference in behavioral outcomes across low- and high-source credibility [52]. Muddying the waters further, there is limited research available to guide predictions related to the impact of credibility indicator type on the effectiveness of suggestive guidance in repeated, risky-choice environments.

Evidence from the advice literature, however, suggests that people rely on personal judgment when combining information gleaned from multiple sources. This relatively noisy process introduces inconsistencies into the decision-making process because a great deal of cognitive effort is required to integrate information from multiple sources [14], particularly when the individual lacks expertise [29, 36]. Decision makers instead tend to reduce cognitive effort by combining the conflicting information [56], often averaging their own judgments with the adviser’s, or, being swayed more strongly by advice that appears more credible [39]. Such a strategy may increase perceptions of decision effectiveness, especially when estimates fall on the opposite sides of the true answer [42].

In some decision-making contexts, such as the one used in this study, no true answer is available. In these settings, people will likely weigh the suggestive guidance in consideration of the credibility indicators. For instance, individuals who would normally reduce risk levels when provided with conservative suggestive guidance might take more risk in response to users’ negative perceptions of the accuracy of the DSS suggestive guidance. This possibility arises through a common reactance against perceived low-credibility information [19, 47]. When lacking expertise, user’s make use of credibility as a heuristic to evaluate information quality [49]. If the presumption is that the source is credible, the vulnerable individual often follows the advice, believing that the source possesses the expertise to evaluate the available information and make an accurate assessment. However, if the source is perceived as lacking credibility, it is more likely that the individual will engage in counterargumentation, responding in a manner opposite to the proffered advice [6, 28]. In sum, when deployed in conjunction with aggressive guidance, low-credibility indicators should reduce contributions to the riskier choice, while high-credibility indicators should increase contributions to the riskier choice. In contrast, the combination of conservative guidance and low-credibility indicators should prompt individuals to increase contributions to the riskier option to a level that exceeds the suggestive guidance recommendation. When paired with high-credibility indicators, conservative suggestive guidance should lead to contributions to the riskier option consistent with the recommendation.

Hypothesis 2: Decisional guidance and credibility indicators will interact such that: (a) When presented with conservative suggestive guidance, individuals will contribute more to the riskier option when low-credibility indicators are present than when high-credibility indicators are present. (b) When presented with aggressive suggestive guidance, individuals will contribute more to the riskier option when high-credibility indicators are present than when low-credibility indicators are present.

## Study 1

A 2 (suggestive guidance: conservative vs. aggressive; between-subjects) × 2 (credibility indicator: low vs. high; between-subjects) × 30 (risk; within-subjects) mixed experimental design was used to test the hypotheses. Participants in a controlled experiment were 289 students enrolled in a major university . As described in Table 1, Study 1 used student participants in a controlled laboratory experiment, while Study 2 examined the behavior of retirement-plan participants in a field experiment. A retirement-planning scenario served as the decision-making context for three reasons; (1) the ability to estimate accurately the probabilities and outcomes from the historical record, (2) the trillions of dollars at stake in individually managed retirement plans, and (3) the common practice of managing retirement plans online. Combined, these factors helped establish the ecological validity of the study.

The decision-making task required participants to consider two choices—a safeand a risky-investment alternative. The dependent variable, portfolio risk, is the amount contributed to the riskier choice each period. The risky-choice environment was augmented by three additional design factors; both evaluation and decision frequency were set to frequent (one-period), and the respective asset choices were displayed as short-term (one-year returns). All three manipulations in isolation increase riskiness perceptions [13, 26]. In combination, a risky-choice environment was established.

The manipulation of suggestive guidance occurred at two levels—conservative suggestive guidance and aggressive suggestive guidance. Depending on the experimental condition, either conservative or aggressive suggestive guidance was delivered on the same page that the investment allocations were entered. The conservative suggestive guidance recommended allocating a small percentage to the risker choice (\~15 percent), and a large allocation to the safer choice (\~85 percent). Aggressive suggestive guidance recommended the opposite: \~85 percent to the riskier choice and \~15 percent to the safer choice. The percentages varied slightly for each individual over the course of the simulation to convey that the information system was providing advice based on his or her previous decisions. Conservative guidance varied between 10 percent and 20 percent, while aggressive guidance varied between 80 percent and 90 percent. To ensure that participants did not feel obligated to comply with the recommendation, a disclaimer at the bottom of the screen informed decision makers that they were free to reach their own conclusions regarding its appropriateness.

The manipulation of credibility indicators occurred at two levels using a 0- to 5- star scale. To convince participants that other users were continuously updating the credibility indicators over the course of the simulation, the DSS varied low-credibility indicators between 0.5 and 1, and high-credibility indicators between 4.0 and 4.5. Regardless of credibility indicator level, a counter displayed next to the scale also indicated the number of users who had previously rated the suggestive guidance. The number of users displayed at the beginning of the simulation was 24, and was incremented slightly over time.

Following an earlier study in this area [13], data collection took place in a controlled environment in a computer laboratory. Upon entering the computer laboratory, participants received an instruction sheet that specified the URL to the experimental website. The sheet specified an account identifier and password for the participants to access their individual accounts. Once logged in, the system randomly assigned participants to one of the between-subjects conditions. To avoid competition, procedures ensured that participants were unaware of their peers’ choices and performance. A series of questionnaires captured demographic information before the experimental task began. Upon completion, a post-test questionnaire gathered data for an unrelated study focused on website design. ## Control Variables

Covariates help reduce error variance, allowing outcomes to be isolated more precisely [7]. Males demonstrate higher preference for riskier alternatives than females [2]. Gender was therefore included as a dichotomous variable (0 = female, 1 = male). Age, coded as a continuous variable, is inversely related to risk taking [17]. Experienced investors exhibit more extreme risk preferences than less experienced investors [12]; therefore, investing experience was captured as a continuous variable in terms of years. Willingness to take risks is influenced by risk tolerance [26]. Prior period performance was calculated as the percentage gained (or lost) during the preceding year, and was included because individuals exhibit a greater willingness to take risks following gains than losses [50]. Time and time squared were included as fixed-effects covariates to capture whether risk taking varied within subjects in a linear or curvilinear fashion by time of measurement.

## Discussion

Supporting H1, individuals took significantly more risk when suggestive guidance was aggressive than when it was conservative. Partially supporting H2, when suggestive guidance was conservative, low-credibility indicators led to significantly higher risk levels than when credibility indicators where high. When suggestive guidance was aggressive, however, risk levels were similar regardless of credibility indicator type. This result suggests that the nonmodeled information did not push users toward accepting the model advice. Given these unexpected results, an examination of potential boundary conditions associated with Study 1 was necessary.

## Study 2

One readily evident boundary condition associated with Study 1 is the reliance on students, who have less investment decision-making experience than retirement-plan participants do. While Hardin and Looney [13] report that students and retirementplan participants accept similar levels of risk, they also acknowledge the need to evaluate whether these findings generalize to other contexts. Differentiating their study from the current effort, Hardin and Looney [13] investigated methodological inconsistencies associated with studies that use a hypothetical lottery,<sup>4</sup> rather than the investment choices used in the current study. Because they lack the relevant expertise and motivation necessary to process relevant information [36], inexperienced students may have been more or less susceptible to the suggestive guidance and credibility indicators presented by the DSS than experienced investors would have been. To explore this additional boundary condition, H1 and H2 were reexamined using data collected from 229 retirement-plan participants.

## Experimental Procedures

Study 2 replicated the experimental procedures from Study 1 with two major exceptions: (1) recruitment of retirement-plan participants by a professional market research firm that prequalified participants based on self-management of their retirement plans, and (2) a field experiment to facilitate the inclusion of retirement plan participants. In total, 229 individuals participated. After passing the screening questions, participants received an electronic mail correspondence that explained the purpose of the study, specified a URL to access the experimental website, and supplied a unique code and password that allowed them to access their accounts. Once the website was loaded, participants completed a questionnaire capturing demographic information before beginning the simulation. Several screening questions embedded in the demographic section confirmed that participants met the participation criteria (e.g., currently self-managing a retirement portfolio). After completing the simulation, the website thanked participants for their time and provided instructions for receiving compensation, which was dependent on portfolio performance. ## Discussion

Study 2 results mostly replicated those of Study 1. Retirement-plan participants presented with aggressive suggestive guidance incorporated more risk into their portfolios than those presented with conservative suggestive guidance. Similar to Study 1 the suggestive guidance by credibility indicator interaction was significant supporting H2. Dissimilar to Study 1, however, when suggestive guidance was aggressive, a significant difference between low- and high-credibility indicators also emerged, supporting H2b.

For students, credibility indicators mattered only when suggestive guidance was conservative. For retirement-plan participants, credibility indicators mattered regardless of whether suggestive guidance was conservative or aggressive. At first glance, these results appear to indicate that the retirement-plan participants were more susceptible to the credibility of the suggestive guidance than were students. Fortunately, the repeated measures design affords the opportunity to examine the results over time.

## Post Hoc Analyses

Using repeated measures designs provides an opportunity to gaze inside the “black box” of repeated, temporally related decisions. This additional scrutiny provides valuable insight into decision processes normally masked by between-subject approaches. In the current study, differences in simple effects across participant type emerged. Further, covariate results for the retirement-plan participants indicated a significant, curvilinear risk trend, while the students demonstrated no such trend. Additional analyses more closely examined the decision-making trends.

## Implications, Limitations, and Conclusion

Our research generates a number of interesting insights. Combined, student and retirement-plan participant results partially support the interactive effects of suggestive guidance and credibility indicators. While significant interactions materialized in both Study 1 and Study 2, simple effects differences emerged for the suggestive guidance by credibility indicators interaction, warranting further investigation of portfolio trends across the participant groups. Curve estimation results revealed important differences in risk patterns over time. Provided below are the theoretical and practical implications of these results.

## Theoretical Implications

The main effect of suggestive guidance was consistent with expectations. This finding is important, as different risk-taking strategies are appropriate depending on the decision-making scenario. While aggressive suggestive guidance may be suitable in some situations, conservative suggestive guidance may be more appropriate in others. In the current study, these respective forms of suggestive guidance strongly influenced temporally linked decision making. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate these respective forms of decisional guidance as needed. For example, it would be interesting to see whether conservative recommendations toward the end of the simulation curb the risk appetites of students.

The suggestive guidance by credibility indicator interaction provides insight into how information systems can help researchers understand the psychological principles associated with prominent descriptive theories such as myopic loss aversion. Lowcredibility indicators—by engendering counterargumentation—discredited the conservative suggestive guidance leading to less conservative choices. Individuals frequently average personal estimates with the estimates of advisers to improve decision-making accuracy [42]. In a similar fashion, individuals in the current study appeared to weigh both the suggestive guidance and the credibility indicators initially, constructing an “average” across the conflicting conservative suggestive guidance and low-credibility indicators. Providing an interesting area of new research in this context, the lowcredibility indicators pushed users away from conservative suggestive guidance that reinforced the detrimental effects of loss aversion.

High-credibility indicators, on the other hand, reinforced conservative suggestive guidance for both groups. Despite a decision-making scenario that favored contributions to the riskier asset, perceived credibility augmented the conservative suggestive guidance leading to overly conservative choices. Future decision-making studies should account for the ability of credibility indicators to reinforce or weaken the influence of conservative suggestive guidance. When viewing the results through the lens of human–computer division of labor, researchers should be wary that credibility information could push users toward suboptimal suggestive guidance.

Predictions were that low-credibility indicators would cause individuals to question the viability of the aggressive suggestive guidance, and decrease risk taking as a result. For students, however, no significant difference across credibility indicator type materialized. Risk trends suggest that students learned they could experience better outcomes by disregarding the credibility indicators and embracing the suggestive guidance. Credibility indicators are more persuasive when information is limited [15, 38], and repeated interactions with the system allowed the students to evaluate the accuracy of the credibility indicators. In this case, repeated interaction with the DSS delivered an external source of credibility information that overrode the information provided by the low-credibility indicators. In this case, the external source of information pushed users toward the beneficial suggestive guidance resulting in portfolios that were more profitable.

H2 stated that high-credibility indicators would push users toward accepting the aggressive suggestive guidance. The combination of high credibility indicators and aggressive suggestive guidance lowered loss aversion for students, resulting in a nonsignificant trend line that revealed a consistently high level of risk. Static approaches would not have revealed these insights. To our knowledge, no study to date has explored how the risk trends of students might differ from other respondent types.

For aggressive suggestive guidance, low-credibility indicators appeared to influence the initial decision making of retirement-plan participants as evidenced by the significantly lower risk levels in this condition. While the slight difference in initial risk levels across low- and high-credibility indicators resulted in an overall mean difference, the most meaningful insight comes from the discovery that retirementplan participants tended to ignore the suggestive guidance as the time horizon shortened. This capital preservation strategy occurred in both conditions, demonstrating that information outside the DSS may sometimes be more powerful than information provided within the system. Retirement plan participants followed the aggressive suggestive guidance confirmed by the high credibility indicators increasing their initial risk trends. This initial increase, combined with the tendency to ignore advice later in the simulation, led to a significant quadratic trend for the retirement-plan participants.

In sum, theory should reflect these new insights, and future studies should investigate associated factors using repeated measures designs like the one applied in this research. Most studies on repeated, risky-choice environments examine each allocation individually. Researchers have devoted insufficient effort toward understanding how decision making evolves over time. As a result, studying how risk-taking trends develop can potentially extend predominant descriptive theories in this area. Statistical tools such as linear mixed modeling can facilitate the investigation of repeated measures designs that incorporate time-varying covariates.<sup>5</sup> Curve estimation, scatterplots, and so on, can then be used to examine risk trends that reveal important decision-making differences over time.

These findings also illustrate the importance of considering credibility indicators within the context in which they are used. Even though specific treatments determined how the DSS delivered the credibility indicators, participants believed other users provided them based on their interactions with the system. While retirement-plan participants followed the suggestive guidance in accordance with the credibility indicators during the initial half of the timeline, as the investment horizon narrowed, these indicators were heavily discounted, if not ignored. Retirement-plan participants adopted a more conservative approach to reduce the risk of potential losses to their retirement accounts, regardless of the credibility of the guidance. Thus, evaluating the credibility of suggestive guidance relative to the context appears to be an interesting avenue for future research.

## Practical Implications

The consideration of different investment scenarios is just one example of how information systems can help overcome decision-making biases. The investment scenarios in the current study are complex due to the longitudinal nature of the decisions and users who were unequipped to deal with complex decision-making scenarios. Decision support systems and recommendation systems aid users who lack experience or expertise in their respective areas, thus these findings have important implications for the development of these systems. The interesting interaction between suggestive guidance and credibility indicators both internal and external to system provides additional insight into the future development of these systems.

An extensive literature has evolved around the use of recommendation agents [21] and recommendation systems based on reputation or feedback mechanisms [4, 35]. The current study uncovers some potential complications associated with the use of these systems.<sup>6</sup> When individuals lack domain-specific knowledge or prior experience, prior work has shown that the recommendations are more likely to be followed [21]. This is consistent with findings reported in the advice literature. However, our work indicates that the perceived quality of systems recommendations can influence the interpretation of the proffered advice. In other words, users might reject highquality advice if its perceived quality is low, irrespective of the actual quality of the advice. More pronounced effects might occur during complicated decision making such as instances that involve repeated decisions over time, as opposed to a one-time purchase of a product. Thus websites that offer recommendation agents should consider carefully how the reputations of these artifacts are perceived by users, as they may produce behavior contrary to the desired recommendations.

Additionally, technologies developed to aid people engaged in investing activities have proliferated. One such technology automatically trades for users and provides advice on how to make investments in future periods. Our work has direct implications for the development of such technologies as our results show that the different forms of suggestive guidance influence users in different ways. Further, suggestive guidance is subject to credibility information that may push users toward or away from advice provided by recommendation agents. Designers should consider these findings when developing technologies to ensure the delivery of automated advice at the appropriate time, and that the reputation of the system recommendations signals that the advice is of sufficient quality.

Finally, our work has interesting implications for systems that allow users to rate the quality of their recommendation agents or decision-aids. Unlike the experimental manipulations used in this study, most decision support systems use actual user rankings. Previous work in this area has shown that users are more likely to provide feedback when outcomes are extreme [33, 41]. Given that aggressive guidance in this context is more likely to produce extreme outcomes, users may be more likely to provide feedback in this condition. Conversely, because conservative guidance produces safer outcomes, users may be less inclined to contribute feedback. Further, given that the retirement-plan participants and students responded to the guidance and credibility indicators differently, it is also important to consider user demographics. For example, future research should include psychological factors to assess whether systematic individual differences influence reactions to suggestive guidance and credibility indicators.

## Limitations

Any research has limitations and this study is no exception. The investment scenario used in this study described only one set of probabilities and payouts. While historical market returns determined the percentages of the respective investments, other investment scenarios were possible. Future studies should investigate other combinations of probabilities and payoffs to evaluate the robustness of the results. Additionally, researchers should design future studies in which actual investors provide user ratings rather than the experimental manipulations used in this study. User psychological factors beyond those previously discussed in the literature [27] should also be considered in this context. DSS features such as restrictive guidance were not included in the study. Although this omission promoted parsimony, future research should investigate the impact of other design features in repeated, riskychoice environments. Finally, these results are specific to investment decisions. Future research should consider other types of risky-choice scenarios.

## Conclusion

This study examined the interactive effects of system-delivered suggestive guidance and credibility indicators on temporally linked, repeated decision making. In total, 518 individuals made 30 interrelated decisions. Linear mixed modeling and curve estimation procedures helped glean insights from the repeated measures data. Results revealed the differential effects of the suggestive guidance by credibility indicators interaction across participant type. Most notably, curve estimation results revealed interesting differences in risk trends across participant type. The examination of temporally linked, repeated decisions provides the opportunity to extend prominent prescriptive theories used to explain decision making in repeated, riskychoice environments. These findings should lead to an extensive stream of future research investigating the extension of the predominant prescriptive theories commonly used to explain decision making in risky-choice environments,
