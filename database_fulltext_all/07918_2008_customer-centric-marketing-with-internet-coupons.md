# Customer-centric marketing with Internet coupons

Hsing Kenneth Cheng <sup>a,b,⁎</sup>, Kutsal Dogan <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Warrington College of Business Administration, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611, USA

<sup>b</sup> Visiting Professor, Department of Industrial and Information Management, National Cheng Kung University, Taiwan, ROC <sup>c</sup> Information Systems and Operations Management, School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080, USA

Received 20 June 2006; received in revised form 2 August 2007; accepted 3 September 2007 Available online 12 September 2007

## Abstract

We develop an analytical framework to examine customer-centric marketing with Internet coupons. We show that mass distribution of Internet coupons without a customer-centric information system to identify customers' profile will have adverse consequences that defeat the price discrimination effect of couponing. We analyze alternative strategies including distributing Internet coupons to targeted customers with perfect and imperfect profile information of customers. The targeted coupons with perfect information case amounts to dynamic pricing, but without customers' backlash. In the targeted customers with imperfec information scenario, we consider Internet coupons with fixed face value as well as changing face value cases. We derive the condition under which the firm should opt for the changing face value Internet coupons. As the firm's customer-centric information system improves in terms of enhanced targeting accuracy at a lower cost, the changing face value Internet coupons will become more prevalent.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of the Internet has led to heightened global competition for businesses large and small. In response, it has become fashionable or even essential for corporations to become “customer-centric” organizations [14]. This translates to making their company web sites “sticky”, their customers' “touch points” seamlessly integrated, and, above all, their businesses “customercentric” in the e-business era [2]. The resulting fundamental shift in business and customer behavior entailed by the ubiquitous Internet has never been more pronounced in marketing — a core component of a company's value chain that interfaces with customers [27]. Hence, the goal of becoming a “customer-centric” organization cannot be achieved without the presence of the “customer-centric” marketing.

Customer-centric marketing refers to the practice in which “marketers assess each customer individually and make a determination of whether to serve that customer directly or via a third-party,” and customer-centric marketing “focuses on the needs, wants, and resources of customers as the starting point of the planning process.” [p.57, 28]. Customer-centric marketing is different from one-to-one marketing in that one-to-one marketing adopts a product-centric approach by making product the starting point of the planning process and providing a customized product for each individual customer. A good example of applying information technology to achieve one-to-one marketing is to use consumers' online browsing and shopping-cart data to arrive at better product bundles [18]. Sheth et al. [28] also point out a clear distinction between customercentric marketing and relationship marketing since customer-centric marketing needs to emerge first in order to have the practice of relationship marketing, but not necessarily the other way around.

This paper addresses various interesting issues arising in customer-centric marketing with Internet coupons, an increasingly popular marketing mechanism made possible only by the Internet technologies. Internet coupons, in essence, are those coupons distributed through the Internet. There are a variety of different ways to deliver coupons to consumers over the Internet. Interested readers are referred to Ref. [9] for an extensive review of Internet couponing firms, their business models, as well as advantages and disadvantages of Internet coupons.<sup>1</sup> Firms benefit from using Internet coupons as they provide customer-centric marketing capability, easy tracking and measure, flexible couponing process with short lead-time for coupon drop, low coupon distribution costs, and higher observed redemption rates (reaching up to 20%).<sup>2,3</sup>

The objective of this research is to provide insights into such critical questions involved in implementing customer-centric marketing system with Internet coupons as “How does mass customization and personalization affect business performance?” and “What are the economic effects of the customer-centric system?”. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to provide analytical insights into customer-centric marketing with Internet coupons.

In this paper, we first show that mass distribution of Internet coupons will defeat the price discrimination purpose of using coupons. Our model indicates that the firm's optimal choice is to not provide coupons at all in this case as any level of coupon distribution hurts the firm's profitability. To benefit from distributing coupons randomly over the Internet, the firm should make sure consumers incur some disutility when they use these Internet coupons by requiring registration with the site, downloading software, printing the coupon and so on, which are practices adopted by such Internet coupons firms as Coolsavings.com, Coupons Inc, and ValuPage.

Next, we present the model of a firm that provides a fixed face value Internet coupon to targeted customers identified by customer profiling technologies. Using fixed face value Internet coupons, firms benefit from the low cost of distributing coupons over the Internet. However, this approach does not fully exploit the customer-centric marketing ability afforded by the Internet. With the advent of tracking and personalization technologies, firms can individually target each consumer and tailor its coupons according to individual consumer's preferences and willingness to pay. In this paper, we explore the models of changing face value Internet coupons involving two cases where the firm has perfect information or imperfect information about the consumers.

We find that it is more profitable for the firm to introduce the changing face value Internet coupons when the cost of improving targeting accuracy of the firm's customer-centric information systems decreases. The consumer profiling cost is also an important factor in choosing between a changing face and a fixed face value Internet coupon. The impact of the customer profiling cost, however, is not as significant as that of the targeting accuracy cost.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we first review the literature most relevant to our work. Then, we provide a critical review of Salop's original spatial model to be extended later to examine various issues of customer-centric marketing with Internet coupons. In Section 3, we look at the mass distribution of Internet coupons. Section 4 presents targeted Internet couponing models. The discussion of profits and prices as well as the comparison of different Internet coupon models is given in Section 5. Section 6 provides concluding remarks and describes some useful future extensions of our models.

## 2. Review of literature and Salop's spatial model

## 2.1. Literature review

Extensive research has been devoted to examining the effectiveness of coupons in achieving objectives set forth by coupon providers. Blattberg and Neslin [6] provide an excellent review of the literature on coupons, identifying “price discriminate” and “reach the appropriate target group” as two major objectives of coupons. Levedahl [20] lends support to the price discrimination objective of coupons. White [31] posits that manufacturers use coupons as a price discrimination tool to segment consumers into two classes — those who comparison shop and those who do not. Recent research by Gopal et al. [13] discovers that Internet technologies help segment consumers into those who actively seek out coupons from fatwallet.com and those who don't. Those consumers who actively seek out coupons and promotional materials often engage in reselling at eBay. Narasimhan [21] theorizes that the consumers with lower opportunity cost for their time are most likely to use coupons. He finds empirical evidence that coupon users are more price-sensitive. Babakus et al. [4] offer further empirical evidence supporting the price sensitivity argument. Our analyses focus on the price discrimination dimension of coupons in the dichotomy of [6].

Narasimhan [21] develops the model for individual consumer's decision to use coupons and finds that the trade-off between the value of coupons and the disutility of using coupons determines which decision is made. Chiang [11] endogenizes consumers' decision to use coupons in the overall demand decision and bases this decision on consumers' utility maximization to measure the impact of coupons on the category sales. Our study analyzes a firm's couponing decision which takes into account individual consumers' decision process of Ref. [21].

There is a body of research that looks at the firm's decision process to use coupons as a strategic tool in a competitive environment. Refs. [5,7,11,26] and most recently [22] analyze competitive effects of coupons. Houston and Howe [16] provide a theoretical analysis of coupons as price discrimination tool under monopolistic competition, while Ref. [22] finds support for price discrimination models in oligopoly settings.

The model developed in Ref. [26] is closest to the models developed in this paper. They develop a two-stage game theoretic model of pricing and coupon distribution to compare conventional mass-media coupon drop with targeted coupons based on consumers' past purchases. They use a linear spatial model to compare two types of coupon drop methods. They find that coupon targeting is not necessarily a replacement to mass media distribution of coupons as the two methods work differently in segmenting the market. Our model differs from theirs as we allow for different levels of mass media coupon drop and calculate the optimal level of mass media coupon drop intensity. Another important difference is that their model does not incorporate individual consumers' coupon use decision. A constant fraction of consumer population is assumed to be coupon users and the rest is assumed to be nonusers. Therefore, the face value of the coupon and the disutility of using coupon are not considered in Ref. [26] to determine coupon users and nonusers. Our models allow individual consumers to decide whether or not to use a coupon when they receive the coupon with a certain face value.

Another stream of literature on coupons examines the objective of using coupons as a targeting tool. Refs. [3,24,29,33] give examples of coupon targeting through direct mail programs such as Donnelly Marketing's Carol Wright program. Shaffer and Zhang [26] state that coupons should be targeted to competitive firm's consumers to increase brand sales. With the advent of Internet tracking technologies, firms can now target each individual consumer and tailor its coupons according to individual consumers' preferences and willingness to pay. Instead of one same coupon, different coupons for different consumers can be issued for immediate online use. Kumar et al. [17] construct an Internet coupon distribution prototype to assure a smooth and secure couponing process. Rangachari et al. [23] describe how a consumer's online behavior can be analyzed in order to determine her desire for a product. Adomavicius and Tuzhilin [1] present a data mining model that can be used to develop personal profiles of consumers from their transactional histories. The firm can then decide on the coupon and the terms of the coupon according to the information gathered.

In the next section, we provide a critical review of Salop's spatial model [25]. Using Salop's spatial model as the underlying model, we present several extensions and generalizations to address issues related to Internet coupons in subsequent sections.

## 3. Mass distribution of Internet coupons with no targeting

When a firm does not have a customer-centric information system to acquire individual consumers' preferences, it cannot differentiate its coupon provision among consumers according to consumers' locations in the market, or some proxy thereof. The firm's only choice is to distribute coupons randomly, either through traditional coupons distribution methods or over the Internet. As the cost of distributing coupons over the Internet is lower than the traditional distribution channels, the firm will naturally choose to distribute them over the Internet. Using traditional paper coupons requires certain effort on the consumers' part to clip, organize, and use them. This required effort is what allows firms to use traditional coupons as price discrimination tools. Cheng et al. [10] model this effort and find that this effort is very critical to the success of a couponing campaign. However, for some mechanisms of Internet couponing that we describe earlier, this consumer's effort on using the coupons distributed over the Internet is negligible. In this section we show the undesirable consequence to the firm when the disutility of using Internet coupons becomes essentially zero.

## 4. Models of targeted Internet coupons

Internet coupons are revolutionary mechanisms for the firm to achieve customer-centric marketing capabilities as the Internet technology enables the firm to target individual consumers. This section models a monopolist's decision to use Internet coupons to target consumers according to their preferences for the product with an aim to improve its profits. In Section 4.1, we first analyze a benchmark case where a firm can target consumers perfectly without incurring any costs. This model is rather unrealistic and is used only as the base model for comparison with more realistic models that include costs of consumer profiling and imperfect targeting introduced in Section 4.2. With imperfect targeting and costs of consumer profiling, the firm has two alternatives to induce customers to buy the product by providing them with either a coupon with fixed face value or a different coupon with changing face value for each individual consumer.

For both models in Section 4.2, we follow a two-period pricing and couponing process. In the first period, the firm introduces the product and makes the pricing decision. The firm's customer-centric information system records those consumers who buy the product without a coupon in the first period. The firm targets those consumers with a high fit cost who would not be able to buy the product at the regular price by introducing coupons in the second period. The firm's objective in the second period is to find the most effective form and optimal face value of Internet coupons. We ignore the discount factor in our two-period model for simplicity.

## 4.1. Targeted coupons with free perfect information

When the firm has detailed information regarding consumers' shopping behavior and preferences, it can estimate their willingness to pay. With the flexibility afforded by customer-centric information systems, firms can change the face value of their coupon offerings according to individual consumers' preferences and shopping behavior. Instead of one same coupon, customized coupon for each consumer can be issued for immediate online use. We assume that the coupon is given for one product and it is instantaneous as soon as the face value is determined. Coupons distributed this way will not impose any disutility of usage.

With the help of coupons, a monopolist can charge higher prices to some consumers and lower prices to others and segment consumers effectively. However, with traditional paper coupons the firm is limited to very small number of different coupons. In practice, a conventional firm generally uses one coupon with the same face value for all consumers. Customer-centric information systems enable firms to target their consumers in ways that were considered infeasible before.

We again consider a monopolistic market for a single product with the exact same characteristics described in Section 3 — zero marginal production cost, product characteristics approach to product differentiation, unit circle model with the total number of consumers uniformly distributed and normalized to one, a common reservation price $r ,$ and heterogeneous consumer preferences represented by the fit cost t.

In the model of targeted coupons with perfect information, we assume that each consumer's true location on the unit circle can be identified perfectly with an information gathering mechanism. The firm may offer Internet coupons to interested customers only if they provide relevant information to the firm. For instance, the users of ValuPage.com Internet coupons need to register their preferences along with zip code information in order to receive the desired coupons. We assume that the cost of the perfect information is fixed and normalized to zero. This unrealistic assumption will be relaxed in subsequent models.

The results of this basic model amounts to perfect (firstdegree) price discrimination. Each individual consumer is charged her true reservation price for the product. However, this is not achieved through dynamic pricing but through the use of the changing face value coupons. The end results in these two alternatives are virtually the same; however, they generate different perceptions among consumers. When Amazon.com was found to use its enormous consumer information database to price discriminate, charging higher prices to loyal consumers that are frequent purchasers and supposedly less price sensitive, the consumers' reaction was nothing short of a backlash [30]. Amazon.com denied the allegations of implementing dynamic pricing and claimed that it was merely running price tests. Even though dynamic pricing has been effectively used in such industries as airline and travel industries, its use by an online firm to sell DVDs stirred up a lot of negative emotion among consumers.

The situation is rather different with coupons. The use of coupons as price discrimination tool does not seem to have the same adverse effect that dynamic pricing has on consumers. Jupiter Media Metrix analyst Jared Blank support this view point [32].

“…Merchants that want to price a new product dynamically (especially one that consumers will discuss in chat rooms) should send coupons of differing values to customers, rather than adjusting the price of the item on the site. Consumers are not offended when others receive coupons of differing values; this happens often with consumer packaged goods…”

One possible reason for consumers' more favorable reactions to achieving the same outcome of dynamic pricing by dynamic coupons may be the fact that the price is announced and known by all consumers. All consumers observe the same disclosed price but not necessarily the face value of coupons when they visit the firm's online establishment. With the use of dynamic coupons, consumers might very well feel that they are getting a good deal on the product. Much research is needed to uncover the exact psychological reasoning of consumers. However, it is beyond the scope of this study. We are not interested in why consumers react differently, but rather what the firm should do, given these different reactions, to effectively price discriminate, serve more consumers, and improve its profits with Internet coupons along the way. In the next section, we relax some of the assumptions made in the benchmark model and analyze Internet coupon targeting with imperfect and costly information.

## 6. Conclusions, limitations and future extensions

We develop an analytical framework to analyze different Internet couponing strategies for a monopolistic firm providing a single product. Our models show that the distinct characteristics of Internet coupons call for a careful selection of a firm's couponing strategy that suits the firm's technological capabilities. The firm that does not have the ability of a customer-centric information system to identify and profile individual consumers will not be able to benefit from Internet couponing through mass distribution of their coupons over the Internet. We find that the firm will increase its price to the segment of the consumers who have lower fit costs and will buy the firm's product without coupons when it is more profitable to distribute targeted Internet coupons even though the information gathered about the firm's customers is not perfect with the implementation of a customer-centric information system. A decreasing cost of customer profiling technologies will result in a higher price for the firm's product. As the firm's customercentric information system improves in terms of enhanced targeting accuracy at a lower cost, the changing face value Internet coupons will become more prevalent.

This research is not without limitations. This paper assumes that consumers are not aware of the two stage process used for identifying purchasers at the regular price and the potential coupon users. One possible approach to relax this critical assumption is to introduce a discount factor for consumers where the consumers with high discount factor would buy in the first stage at the regular price while those with low discount factor would wait for the coupons in the second stage. Since the valuation and discount factor are arguably correlated, it should be a worthy future research to examine whether the main results of the paper still apply in this case.

This research studies the implications of Internet coupons issued by the manufacturer. It should be of interest to analyze the retailer-based coupons such as those for 10% of all apparel. The retailer-based coupons scenario will not satisfy a fundamental assumption of the Salop model [25] and extensions thereof — the product under consideration being a single product, not multiple products. Intuitively, the general predictions of the propositions in this paper should still hold. However, the parameters conditions in Propositions 2 and 5 will be difficult to ascertain.

A natural extension to our work is to examine the firm's decision to use coupons as a strategic tool in a competitive environment. One possible extension to this study is to consider the case of multiple products where the effects of substitution and complementarity among a firm's products are analyzed. Another possible future work is to consider repeat purchases. Our models assume that consumers are in the market for a unit quantity of the product. If we allow for repeat purchases, the benefits of consumer profiling will be even greater. First, the firm will be able to collect more information. Second, it will use the same information to target consumers multiple times, which will result in cost savings.
