# Research Note

# A Dynamic View of the Impact of Network Structure on Technology Adoption: The Case of OSS Development

Gang Peng

Williamson College of Business Administration, Youngstown State University, Youngstown, Ohio 44555, gpeng@ysu.edu

Debabrata Dey

Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington 98195, ddey@uw.edu

W<sup>e</sup> <sup>examine</sup> <sup>how</sup> <sup>network</sup> <sup>centrality</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>closure,</sup> <sup>two</sup> <sup>key</sup> <sup>aspects</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>network</sup> <sup>structure,</sup> <sup>affect</sup> <sup>technology</sup> adoption. In doing so, we consider the content of potential information flows within the network and argue that the impact of network structure on technology adoption can be better understood by separately examining its impact from two groups of alters—current and potential adopters. We contend that increased network centrality and closure among current adopters contribute positively to adoption, whereas the same among potential adopters has exactly the opposite impact. Accordingly, we propose a dynamic view where the fraction of current adopters in the network positively moderates the impact of network centrality and closure. We empirically test the theory by analyzing the adoption of software version control technology by open source software projects. Our results strongly support the theory.

Key words: technology adoption; network structure; network centrality; network closure; dynamic view; software version control technology; open source software

## 1. Introduction

Social actors are embedded in network relations and are connected with one another through various mechanisms to exchange and diffuse information, which has important consequences for the decisions they make (Granovetter 1985). Technology adoption— a major research topic in the information systems (IS) literature—is an information-intensive endeavor in which network structure often plays a particularly significant role (Sykes et al. 2009, Venkatesh et al. 2011). Networks can amplify utilities, act as conduits for information and knowledge flows, and create trust and norms that are instrumental in technology adoption. Technology and innovation can transform organizational structures and business processes, facilitate collaboration and transactions across organizational boundaries, and eventually enhance firm productivity (Agarwal et al. 1997). Therefore, quick adoption and assimilation of technology and innovation are crucial for a firm to achieve business success. Although a variety of factors and theories have been put forward to explain the diffusion and adoption of information technologies, new theories still need to be developed for a richer and deeper understanding of the complexity of the phenomenon (Rogers 2003). In this paper, we propose a dynamic view of technology adoption by explicitly considering two key aspects of network structure and the content of potential information flows within a network.

Investigating the dynamic impact of network structure on technology adoption is of practical and theoretical significance. Estimates suggest that, in recent times, almost half of all new capital investments in organizations have been in information technology. Although social relations and influences have been found to affect technology adoption (Fichman 2004), which specific network structure is optimal for technology adoption is not well understood, and managers and policy makers are often unsure about how to configure their network relations toward facilitating technology adoption. More than a decade ago, (Lee 1999, p. v) summarized this well when he stated that “information technology interacts with a social setting,” and IS research can add value by studying “the rich phenomena that emerge whenever the technological and the social come into contact with, react to, and transform each other.”

The central theme of a network approach to technology adoption is that network structure affords opportunities to access and channel information and resources. It has been noted that the “active ingredient” of a network is the information that flows within it (Robert et al. 2008, Rodan and Galunic 2004). Correspondingly, researchers have proposed that, when analyzing the impact of a social network, the content of information flows within a network is really what matters (Balkundi and Harrison 2006). Pursuing this line of argument, we propose that, when investigating the impact of network structure on technology adoption, focus should be directed to these information flows.

We study two aspects of network structure: network centrality and network closure—both are known to have a significant impact on the decision making of an actor (Burt 1992, Coleman 1988) and are, therefore, important for technology adoption. Network centrality represents the position of a focal actor (ego) in its ego network and is usually defined by the number of direct ties to other contacts (alters) in the ego network (Balkundi and Harrison 2006). In contrast, network closure measures the extent to which alters are connected among themselves or the degree of connectivity among alters (Burt 2005, Shipilov and Li 2008).

We examine the impact of network centrality and closure on technology adoption by open source software (OSS) projects at SourceForge, the largest virtual workspace hosting such projects. An OSS is developed and distributed under an OSS license by a project team. A unique feature of this environment is that OSS projects often draw the expertise of talents distributed worldwide (Lerner and Tirole 2005). Many members end up working for multiple OSS projects, often at the same time. These common members are the “boundary spanners” (Capaldo 2007)— they provide connections or ties between otherwise disjoint projects, channeling information, knowledge, and expertise across project boundaries (Grewal et al. 2006, Mendez-Duron and Garcia 2009). The technology under study is the software version control technology used for OSS development. Specifically, we examine the migration of OSS projects from concurrent versions system (CVS) to subversion (SVN), which addresses several major limitations in CVS. We restrict our analysis to the ego network, that is, we consider only a one-step neighborhood of an ego. In general, when the research question concerns individual entities across different settings, the egocentric approach is prescribed (Borgatti et al. 2009), which makes this approach particularly suitable for our study of technology adoption by individual OSS projects.

## 2. Literature Review

Technology adoption is one of the most prominent research topics in IS (Venkatesh et al. 2007, 2012). Over the years, many theoretical models and perspectives have been proposed. The most salient determinants include users’ satisfaction with a technology, perceived ease of use, and usefulness of a technology (Davis et al. 1989), attitudinal belief and social influence (Brown and Venkatesh 2005, Venkatesh and Brown 2001), user motivation and facilitating conditions (Venkatesh et al. 2003), and resource configuration and management trend (Fichman 2004). A detailed review of this literature can be found in the excellent works by Venkatesh et al. (2003) for individual-level studies and by Fichman (2004) for organization-level ones.

Although network structure has been found to be relevant for decision making (Rogers 2003), its impact has not always been clear. Earlier studies on the impact of network centrality typically propose a positive relationship between network centrality and outcomes (e.g., Balkundi and Harrison 2006)—an ego with a higher level of network centrality occupies a central position and can conceivably access more information and resource possessed by alters. However, negative impact of network centrality has also been identified since network centrality is not always a good proxy for diverse and heterogeneous information and knowledge (Rodan and Galunic 2004). Furthermore, information and influence from alters may not always be beneficial, and exposure to increased number of alters may not necessarily benefit the ego (Adler and Kwon 2002, Lechner and Floyd 2010, Portes 1998, Venkatesh et al. 2011).

Literature on the impact of network closure is also divergent. Two different views have been proposed on how network closure can have an impact on outcomes. The closure view emphasizes the positive effect of dense social ties on the production of social norms and sanctions (against deviants), facilitating trust and cooperative exchanges within a network (Coleman 1988). In contrast, the bridging view claims that network closure is detrimental, and benefits of social networks actually result from a lack of connections between alters in the network (Burt 1992), since it can create brokerage opportunities, provide access to novel and heterogeneous information, and afford informational and control advantages (Gargiulo et al. 2009, Shipilov and Li 2008). Therefore, the overall impacts of network centrality and closure on technology adoption are often hard to predict.

We argue that, in order to understand the overall impact of network structure on adoption decisions, we need to consider the content of potential information flows from alters to an ego. Accordingly, we differentiate between two groups of alters—current adopters and potential adopters (Hsieh et al. 2011). This distinction is necessary because, clearly, the information emanating from these two groups are likely to be quite different in content. Thus, the two groups end up playing different roles. Since current adopters have already adopted the technology, they generally form a favorable attitude toward the technology, as long as it provides additional benefit. Therefore, in affecting the decision of the ego, they face the challenge to impart the technology onto the ego. However, potential adopters are not fully aware of all the benefits of the technology, and to facilitate the ego’s adoption, they first need to overcome their own cognitive lockin and gain a more complete awareness of the technology. Therefore, although the impact of the overall network structure lacks a clear prediction, the network structure within each of these two groups has a seemingly more predictable pattern: increased levels of centrality and closure in the network of current adopters speed up the ego’s adoption, whereas they slow it down in the network of potential adopters. A material implication is that, when investigating the impact of network structure among all the alters, perhaps a dynamic approach is desirable—the more the current (potential) adopters in the whole network, the more salient is the positive (negative) impact of network centrality and closure. Therefore, the fraction of current adopters in the ego’s network should moderate the impact of the overall network structure on technology adoption.

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses

We apply the theoretical lens of social network research to study how network structure affects the adoption of software version control technology. For every project contemplating adoption, we partition its ego network into two subnetworks of alters that are either current adopters or potential adopters (Hsieh et al. 2011). We first examine the impact of each subnetwork separately and subsequently combine them into a dynamic view of the overall impact of the entire ego network. Our empirical analysis is at the project or team (macro) level, even though social network research—the theoretical lens used here—was originally developed at the individual (micro) level. Prior research has argued that social network methods are particularly well suited for dealing with multiple levels of analysis, since “micro and macro can be very similar theoretically and methodologically” (Borgatti and Foster 2003, p. 1001), and “theories and constructs initially developed for the study of interpersonal relationships are frequently applied to the analyses of interorganizational linkages” (Capaldo 2007, p. 588).

3.1. Network Structure Among Current Adopters We assume that adopters, in general, form a positive attitude toward the new technology, and information flows from current adopters to an ego, in an overall sense, favor the technology. This is consistent with Krackhardt’s (1997) assumption that an adopter retains his positive attitude as long as he finds at least one other who agrees with him and is surely reasonable within the context of OSS projects migrating from CVS to SVN—SVN addresses several major limitations in CVS, and an overwhelmingly large majority of teams, after adoption, found SVN to be a much better tool (Collins-Sussman et al. 2011). Even in situations where the user acceptance is not so overwhelming, the existence of “pro-innovation bias” often leads to an overall positive influence (Fichman 2004, Rogers 2003).

The impact of the number of current adopters on future adoption by a project closely resembles the well-known phenomenon of network effect, which argues that social actors are more likely to adopt a technology as they are increasingly exposed to others’ adoption (Tucker 2008). Prior research has examined the impact of network effect on the adoption of a variety of technologies (e.g., Goolsbee and Klenow 2002, Kauffman et al. 2000). In our context, we argue that, in the subnetwork of current adopters, increased levels of network centrality is more likely to induce the ego project to adopt SVN.

First, for many technology products, a new adoption may bring benefits to all the existing adopters in the network and may make future adoption more attractive (Katz and Shapiro 1986). A larger network of SVN adopters may provide additional utility to one another in the forms of better support from SourceForge, availability of helpful tips and resources (such as in end-user forums), and better maintenance and higher quality of service, for example, in terms of more frequent updates and upgrades (Xu et al. 2010). Overall, the utility of adopting a technology is often derived not only from the technology per se, but also from the total number of current adopters in the ego’s network (Kauffman et al. 2000, Tucker 2008). Second, an increased level of network centrality should facilitate learning and information flow. A project considering adoption observes and learns from the increased number of adopters before eventually deciding to adopt the same technology (Hsieh et al. 2008). Through learning, a project can take advantage of connected adopters’ experience and expertise in order to economize its cost of decision making and to improve the odds of making the right decision (Reagans and McEvily 2003, Xu et al. 2010). Increased number of current adopters can create more contact points and learning opportunities, raise the frequency of interaction among them, accumulate stocks of knowledge, and enhance the quality of decision making. Through this process, uncertainty is reduced and adoption, induced (e.g., Mendez-Duron and Garcia 2009). Third, actors in a social network tend to resemble or mimic others for decision making because of normative pressure or institutional regulations (DiMaggio and Powell 1988, Hsieh et al. 2008)—things are done in a certain way simply because it is the only acceptable way of doing them (Zucker 1987). Adoption of a technology evolves over time: as the total number of adopters increases, the technology becomes prevalent and gains legitimacy, which leads to further adoption of the technology. Therefore, it follows that increased network centrality with adopters should contribute positively to a project’s adoption:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Network centrality with current adopters fosters technology adoption.

When alters are all current adopters, the density among them also increases the odds for the ego to adopt a technology. First, closed networks facilitate learning and knowledge exchange, possibly leading to a faster adoption of the technology. Often, an adoption involves gaining related knowledge about the benefits of the technology, usually through knowledge transfer from current adopters to potential adopters (Agarwal et al. 1997, Boh et al. 2007). In a closed network, information about the technology can be more readily verified, triangulated, and better digested, and high-quality information can be easily exchanged and disseminated (Coleman 1988). A network with a higher closure affords more opportunities to projects for information exchange and allows for broader and timelier propagation of information (Burt 2005), as well as quicker convergence toward common schemas (Gargiulo et al. 2009). Effective knowledge transfer also requires trust, which is more likely to be developed through intense social interactions within a closed network (Hansen 1999). Moreover, closed networks enhance the willingness of current adopters to transfer knowledge (Reagans and McEvily 2003).

Second, closed networks can also foster the rapid emergence of social norms and institutions (Reagans and McEvily 2003) and increase the likelihood for actors to undertake concerted actions (Burt 2005). Within a closed network of interactions, norms emerge through externalization of individual and subjective behaviors, which is then followed by the conformity to these norms by others (Brown and Venkatesh 2005, Zucker 1987). The presence of common third parties in a closed network facilitates the enforcement of social norms (Coleman 1988). Alternatively, closed networks create opportunities for actors to go beyond dyadic interactions to form triads and cliques, in which everybody is “Simmelian tied” to everyone else (Krackhardt 1998, p. 24).

With an increased level of connections and monitoring, information about the actor’s deviant behavior— nonadoption in this case—would be more visible to all common third parties, acting as a deterrent toward such behavior (Gargiulo et al. 2009). This analysis shows that in the subnetwork of SVN adopters, an increased level of network closure is more likely to induce a project to adopt the technology:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> Network closure among current adopters fosters technology adoption.

3.2. Network Structure Among Potential Adopters We now shift our focus to alters who have not yet adopted SVN, but could do so in the future. By its very nature, technology adoption forms an uncertain process that deals with the search for new ideas and related resources (Rogers 2003). This notion points to a different role played by the ego network, namely, its ability to allow for the exploration of new ideas and information, rather than its function to disseminate existing knowledge. Therefore, here, the impact of network centrality and closure reverses.

First, not all alters affect a project in a positive way. The impact of network centrality is based on the assumption that alters can channel useful information and knowledge. Therefore, the quality of knowledge and information held by alters sets the upper bound for the resources that are available to the ego. When alters’ resources play such a role that can possibly inhibit or restrict the ability of a project for exploration, the ego may be forced to settle with downward leveling norms or behaviors (Adler and Kwon 2002, Lechner and Floyd 2010, Portes 1998). For example, it has been found that connections to less innovative partners tend to reduce ego’s innovativeness (Zaheer and Bell 2005). In the OSS environment where projects compete for scarce resources (for example, talent, fund, and support) and uncooperative behavior by other groups is common, a greater centrality not only increases the likelihood to connect with potential hindrance groups, but also raises the focal project’s overall visibility, making it more difficult for the project to shelter its activities from those who would oppose it (Lechner and Floyd 2010). In such a case, a higher level of network centrality actually turns out to be detrimental because of the information flows emanating from the adverse alters. The implication is clear. When alters are all potential adopters, a higher level of network centrality may have a negative impact on a project’s SVN adoption:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> Network centrality with potential adopters hinders technology adoption.

The negative impact of network closure on SVN adoption can be predicted from both the bridging and closure views. According to the bridging view, an actor who connects otherwise disconnected alters is in a position of brokerage, and, to secure network benefits, the actor should maximize the proportion of disconnected alters in its ego network, so as to bridge the structural holes in the network (Burt 1992, Zaheer and Soda 2008). Behind the bridging view is the rationale that open networks can enhance the access to a larger amount of diverse and nonredundant information (Gargiulo et al. 2009, Shipilov and Li 2008), which is precisely what is needed if alters are potential adopters and are in need for an access to SVN. In this case, open networks can enhance the ego’s chance of connecting to mutually disconnected alters and, through brokerage, the ego can gain access to new technologies (Burt 1992). A project occupying such a brokerage position is at the confluence of different information flows, and the diverse set of bridging ties permits the project to generate a deeper understanding of SVN and reinforces its capability to absorb and apply the new technology, eventually facilitating the project’s adoption.

In contrast, a closed network among potential adopters sacrifices these inherent advantages and impedes technology adoption. When alters are more densely connected within a network, the ego is less likely to gain new information from its alters, as everyone knows what everyone knows, and the information that can be obtained from any alter is likely to be similar to what has already been obtained from others (Cross and Cummings 2004). Furthermore, when actors congregate in a closed network or a clique, expectedly, they are quite homogeneous even before they come together and are, therefore, more likely to share common views on key issues, including willingness to explore external opportunities, attitude toward new technologies, and resources they possess (Rogers 2003). When this happens among potential adopters in a closed network, the negative impact of closure is further amplified.

The negative impact of network closure in the potential adopters’ network can also be predicted from the closure view (Coleman 1988). Increased closure tends to reinforce existing norms and values, limits the access to and the existence of diverse information and, therefore, dampens the creativity of the network members (Burt 2005). This echoes the previous belief that nonadoption can arise from social influence (Krackhardt 1997, McGrath and Krackhardt 2003)—if all alters are nonadopters, the ego conforms to the behaviors of the alters and is likely to continue to be a nonadopter. Moreover, in a closed network where actors are closely bonded, over-embeddedness may occur as well (Molina-Morales and Martínez-Fernández 2009). This could insulate the network and encourage an inward-looking culture, which, in turn, may further limit the potential access to outside information. Strong solidarity among the actors may over-embed them in relationships to an extent that “the ties that bind may also turn into ties that blind” (Adler and Kwon 2002, p. 30). In addition, with the increased level of connection among alters of potential adopters, a project becomes more likely to mimic others for its own decision making because of normative pressures (DiMaggio and Powell 1988), which should reinforce its nonadoption decision (Gargiulo and Benassi 2000). Taken together, we predict the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> Network closure among potential adopters hinders technology adoption.

## 3.3. A Dynamic View of Network Structure

Given the divergent views regarding the impact of network structure as argued above, it seems that, when both groups of alters are taken into consideration, the impact of overall network structure on technology adoption is difficult to predict: a higher level of network centrality may channel in information and knowledge related to a technology and facilitate its adoption by the ego, but it may also unduly inflict negative influence from potential adopters, thereby hindering future adoption. Similarly, a closed network among all alters might bring about adoption because of the enhanced trust and cooperation induced by current adopters, but it could also limit the ability for potential adopters to explore the technology and escape from their own “net” (Gargiulo and Benassi 2000). Therefore, when contemplating the impact of overall network structure, it is hard to predict how network structure is related to technology adoption. This relationship is, of course, more conspicuous at the two extremes: when alters are all potential adopters, the impact of the overall network structure is negative, and it is positive if alters are all current adopters. Naturally, between these two extremes, there should exist an entire gamut of possibilities that allow the gradual transition from the negative impact toward the positive impact.

When there are only a few current SVN adopters, there are limited chances for a project to be exposed to the technology (Hsieh et al. 2008). Here, increased levels of network centrality and closure may serve as a filter, making it difficult for outside information and perspectives to reach the ego. In addition, relational obligations and norms of reciprocity can generate relational and cognitive lock-in or overembeddedness that isolates the ego from the outside world (Molina-Morales and Martínez-Fernández 2009). As the number of current adopters increases, however, the same mechanism that was earlier hindering adoption now gradually turns into an asset—increased level of centrality and closure can provide better awareness of SVN and breed stronger bonds of mutual understanding and trust, thereby facilitating SVN adoption (Burt 2005). This points to the need for a dynamic view—the relationship between overall network structure and SVN adoption is not uniform; rather, its impact varies depending on the fraction of current adopters in the ego network. In this process, the fraction of current adopters plays the role of a sliding window, adjusting the impact of network structure. Therefore, we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> The impact of overall network centrality on technology adoption is positively moderated by the fraction of current adopters in the network.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> The impact of overall network closure on technology adoption is positively moderated by the fraction of current adopters in the network.

## 4. Data Set and Variable Definitions

Concurrent versions system and subversion are two main categories of the software version control technology, which serves as the foundation of OSS development. Developed back in 1986, CVS supports networked access, simultaneous development, and intelligent merging of the code contributed by different members. It quickly gained dominance in the OSS community and once was the de facto standard for version control. Over time, however, developers noticed a few limitations: CVS only tracks the history of individual files, not the whole directory tree of the software; it also does not support atomic update of codes. SVN was designed in 2000 to be the successor to CVS as a version control technology, and it does address most of the limitations in CVS (Collins-Sussman et al. 2011).

SourceForge started supporting SVN in December 2005; before then, all projects were using CVS. We obtained an OSS development data set, collected between January 2006 and December 2007. For this two-year period, the data set contains bimonthly snapshots of project characteristics, membership, and SVN adoption status for all projects. In this study, we are interested in how soon an OSS project adopts SVN since its exposure to the tool. Hence, we consider only those projects that started before December 2005 and were, therefore, all using CVS prior to the time period of our study. The final sample includes 5,127 projects with a total project-month observation of 54,608. We track the adoption of SVN by all these projects for every two-month time period. When a project eventually adopts SVN, we stop tracking it for potential adoption; otherwise, the project moves on to the next period for further observation (Jenkins 1995). We also know all other relevant project characteristics that vary over time as well.

To analyze the impact of network structure on the technology adoption decision by OSS projects, we need to construct a social network among these projects. In an offline setting, a social network is typically confined within a physical boundary, and members who are close to each other geographically become neighbors (e.g., Goolsbee and Klenow 2002). However, in virtual workspaces where OSS is developed, this geographical boundary disappears and members can reach each other through the Internet. In the OSS community, many members often work for multiple projects at the same time. These common members provide connections or ties between these projects (Grewal et al. 2006). Accordingly, we define the social network among projects using an adjacency matrix between projects, in which an entry is 1 if two projects share one or more common members, and 0 otherwise.

The dependent variable in this study is a binary variable indicating whether or not an OSS project adopted SVN during the observation period—since adoption of SVN is tracked over time for all projects, we know the exact two-month time period in which SVN was adopted by a project, if at all. For every project that had still not adopted SVN, we identify all its alters based on the adjacency matrix described earlier. We first measure centrality and closure for the entire ego network of each project. Since we want to capture the extent to which a project has direct relationships and access to other projects, we use degree centrality: we count the total number of alters in the ego network of an ego project as a measure of network centrality. The variable network closure is measured as the number of connections among all the alters (including both current and potential adopters) of an ego project, as a fraction of the total number of possible connections among them.

For every ego project, we further categorize its alters into two groups: current adopters and potential adopters (Hsieh et al. 2011). We calculate network centrality for each of these two groups to obtain the variables current adopter centrality and potential adopter centrality. Similarly, we calculate network closure within these groups to obtain current adopter closure and potential adopter closure. Finally, the fraction of current adopters, , is the number of current adopters in the ego network of a project divided by the total number of alters of the project and is similar to the construct personal network exposure in prior literature (Hsieh et al. 2008, Valente 1995).

To mitigate alternative explanations, we add several control variables. The first one is project expertise. Expertise not only improves the ability to understand information, but also enhances the ability to learn from experiences and observations (Boh et al. 2007, Rodan and Galunic 2004). In the adoption process, the ability to recognize, absorb, and comprehend information emanating from alters is critical. Therefore, a project with a higher expertise is more receptive of, and responsive to, information related to new technologies (Boh et al. 2007). SourceForge asks all project members to reveal their skill competence as well as their skill experience (in years) for every skill identified. The skill competence is measured on a five-point scale, where a higher value indicates a higher level of competency. For every project included in the sample, we average the skill competence and experience of all its members and normalize them to obtain the skill competence and experience level within the overall project. These two indices for a project are then averaged to derive the variable project expertise.

Second, we include project communication as another control variable. Technology adoption and diffusion is a process where members within a social system create, communicate, and share new ideas with one another to reach a mutual understanding (Rogers 2003). Communication within a project allows project members to articulate their opinions, avoid ambiguity, make better interpretation, and transfer more information about the technology, thereby facilitating technology adoption (Agarwal et al. 1997, Boh et al. 2007, Hansen 1999). We measure the extent of communication within a project by the logarithm of the number of messages posted at the project’s discussion forums. The more messages posted and exchanged, the ego project is better able to discover, access, and make use of new knowledge and information, leading to a quicker adoption of the new technology.

Third, we also control for project size and project age. It has been found that larger teams or organizations tend to have more slack resources and be more innovative (Papa 1990). As a proxy for project size, we use the logarithm of the total number of project members within a project. We include project age as a control variable since it has also been found to have an influence on technology adoption tendency (Katz and Shapiro 1986, 1994). Project age is measured by the elapsed time (in months) since a project was registered at SourceForge until the observation period.

Finally, we control for project characteristics variables including: (i) six possible stages of an active project (five indicator variables), (ii) other project characteristics, such as intended audience (five indicator variables), operating systems (five indicator variables), and project topics (19 indicator variables). These variables have been shown to be relevant for decision making in OSS development (Lerner and Tirole 2005). Detailed variable definitions are provided in Table 1. ## 5. Estimation Model and Results

The data set used in this study is best described as a time-to-event data set, with the adoption of SVN representing the event under study. To study this event, we use duration analysis, which could be either continuous or discrete (Greene 2003, Valente 1995). Here, we use a discrete model since the observations are two months apart. When a discrete duration model is used on a panel data set, it is equivalent to a logit model for panel analysis; for more details, see (Jenkins 1995, Tucker 2008). To explicitly account for the causality issue in examining network influence, we take advantage of our panel data set and employ the independent variables in one time period to predict the adoption of SVN in the following (Valente 1995). By doing so, we explicitly specify that the adoption choice at time t is determined by the network structure at time t − 1, and not vice versa.

Finally, a word about interpreting our results is in order. In our empirical testing, we assume that every project that has not adopted the technology yet is a potential adopter. However, some of these potential adopters may be nonadopters, in the sense that they may already have decided to never adopt the technology. Unfortunately, we do not have that information and are left with the only possibility of treating every ego yet to adopt as a potential adopter. However, despite this limitation of the data set, our conclusions do not change, since our results are actually conservative estimates of what one should really expect. ## 6. Discussion

This research examines the impact of network structure on the adoption of SVN by OSS projects and makes both theoretical and practical contributions.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

Despite the prominence of technology adoption as a research topic (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2007, 2012) and the increasing application of social network theory to IS research (e.g., Robert et al. 2008, Sykes et al. 2009, Venkatesh et al. 2011), the impact of network structure on technology adoption is not always clear. In this study, rather than taking a static view of the impact of network structure—as has mostly been done in prior literature—we propose a dynamic view. The central argument of this view is that the impact of overall network structure is not uniform; rather, it is moderated by the fraction of current adopters in the network. In its essence, this fraction determines the potential content of information flows within the network and manifests itself as the impact of network structure. When network constituents are predominately potential adopters, a higher level of centrality and tighter connectivity among them are detrimental to the ego toward its adoption decision. However, as projects within the network gradually adopt the technology, the increased levels of centrality and connectivity start playing the role of a catalyst for championing the technology and bringing about a change in the impact of network structure.

Our results echo the findings that network closure has two faces: it benefits those egos who are information acquirers and taxes those who are providers (Gargiulo et al. 2009). Rather than categorizing the information flows based on the tasks faced by the ego, in this study, we focus on the role played by the alters and argue that the impact of network closure also depends on the types of alters: closure among current adopters benefits the ego project, whereas that among potential adopters restrains it. The ego project plays the role of an information acquirer, drawing in information and knowledge related to the technology, which can only be provided by the current adopters more effectively. In a recent study, Venkatesh et al. (2011) find that increased connections to doctors actually decrease the chance for adopting medical information technology (IT). In other words, network centrality among the network of doctors actually has a negative impact on the use of IT. The rationale is that doctors tend to be content with traditional practice of medicine, and they generally form a negative attitude toward IT that tends to confine their freedom for practicing. Our results corroborate their findings that network centrality does not always contribute positively to technology adoption. When the information flows across the connection is negative, increased connection may slow down technology adoption.

Our dynamic view also resonates with the findings from (Krackhardt 1998), where two alters are “Simmelian tied” to the ego if they are also tied to each other. When Simmelian ties are present, the ego and the alters form a clique, and the ego is likely to be less independent and much more constrained. From this perspective, an ego that possesses many Simmelian ties is equivalent to being embedded in a closed network that facilitates the emergence of common norms and behaviors. However, this argument applies only when members in the clique share common norms. When the ego is connected to two or more cliques that hold distinctive or even conflicting norms, Simmelian ties can further constrain the ego, who finds it even more difficult to satisfy the norms from all the connected cliques. Our results corroborate this finding. When the ego is tied to two groups of highly connected alters exhibiting contrary norms and behaviors, the ego is expected to conform to the opposing adoption behaviors of the two groups, and the overall impact of network closure, consequently, is less clear. However, this impact is much easier to predict when the fraction of adopters is either small or large, since in both cases, the Simmelian ties among the two groups degenerate gradually into those of a single group, with an overall impact that is predictably either negative or positive.

## 6.2. Practical Implications

Our results have several practical implications. First, it contributes to the understanding and application of network effect. Traditionally, network effect maintains that future adoption of a technology increases with the total number of current adopters of the same technology, that is, it focuses on network centrality (Tucker 2008). Network effect can help lock in customers and technology standards, establish first-mover advantages or barriers to entry, obtain monopoly positions in marketplaces, or promote the use of technologies (Katz and Shapiro 1994). Correspondingly, to facilitate future adoption, the main instrument that managers and policy makers could use was the expansion of the installed base of current adopters to reach a critical mass (Shapiro and Varian 1999). In this study, we find that network closure plays an equally important role in affecting future adoptions, which can afford managers and policy makers another tool to trigger network effect—they can realize the positive feedback loop without having to necessarily expand the installed base. This is particularly helpful when a firm or a government agency is limited by its resources and cannot invest in expanding its installed base. By facilitating the interactions among current adopters, more adoptions can be induced, and, built on this increased installed base, network effect can exert even stronger influences by leveraging both the components of network effect, centrality and closure, thereby further expanding their installed bases. Consequently, the positive feedback loop suggested by traditional network effect can be further accelerated and strengthened by the incorporation of network closure.

Second, our study provides useful guidelines for facilitating technology adoption. Organizations contemplating technology adoption face a dual task: (i) they need to gain access to information and ideas related to the technology, and (ii) they must ensure, too, that such novel ideas are evaluated and, if deemed useful, are adequately absorbed and implemented. To those ends, an ego can deliberately configure its network structure by forming new network connections or by dissolving some of the existing ones (Zaheer and Soda 2008). In light of our findings, an organization aspiring to adopt a new technology needs to actively connect to current adopters and, at the same time, encourage the formation of a dense network among those alters. One way to achieve this is to establish connections to only current adopters with ties to other current adopters. This way, structural holes bridged by current adopters can be filled, and more alters can be effectively called upon for acquiring high-quality information related to the new technology in a more cooperative way (Gargiulo et al. 2009). In contrast, when alters are predominately potential adopters who are closely connected, an ego may intentionally dissolve some of the existing connections and devote its resource to develop new connections so as to extricate itself from their cognitive and technological lock-in.

Third, our study also suggests a more effective way for managers, marketers, and social planners in their efforts to promote the adoption of technologies, products, practices, or new ideas. Despite the various measures that have been taken, examples abound in industries that clearly demonstrate that, often, the adoption process is rather slow (Rogers 2003). Earlier studies have suggested that network effect may be used as a tool to facilitate the adoption process (e.g., Goolsbee and Klenow 2002). As current adopters reach a critical mass, the adoption process may start the positive feedback loop and become self-sustaining (Shapiro and Varian 1999). However, this line of argument excludes another group of contacts with whom a potential adopter might also interact: those who have not adopted the technology yet. As indicated in our study, current adopters and potential adopters exert quite different influences on egos’ future adoption decision. Therefore, managers, marketers, and social planners ought to take both groups into consideration when taking actions to facilitate adoption of new products, practices, or new ideas.

## 7. Conclusion

It has been recognized that network structure can potentially channel useful information, knowledge, and resources and can induce positive outcome. However, one needs to realize that the active ingredient of networks is the flow of information. The impact of network structure depends on what types of alters the ego can tap into. Resources held by connected alters set the upper bound as to how useful and beneficial the network structure will be. Since the two groups of alters, current and potential adopters, hold differing information and knowledge, it is expected that the impact of network structure within these two groups will be quite different. In this study, we propose and empirically show that the impact of network structure is dynamic rather than static.

Our study, although informative, also has a few limitations. First, we restrict ourselves to the analysis of the ego network, that is, only a one-step neighborhood of an ego. Given our research question, it seems to be the logical choice. However, it is possible that technology adoption may also be affected by indirect ties. One way to generalize our results would be to consider the n-step neighborhood of every ego, and observe the impact for different values of n. Such an analysis would only strengthen our results. Second, we build our social network based on common members across OSS projects. However, we do not observe the actual realized content of the information flows. Furthermore, there may exist other channels through which projects connect and communicate. Analyzing the actual flows and integrating all potential communication channels would provide a more complete picture of the social network and is an interesting direction for future research. Third, we classify all projects into two groups, current and potential adopters, and assume that current adopters generally form a favorable attitude toward the technology and potential adopters are willing to adopt the technology after they are fully aware of its benefits. Future research should try to elicit the true attitude among the two groups. Despite these limitations, we would have achieved our goal if our study has succeeded in drawing the attention of researchers on the need to further explore the relationship between network structure and technology adoption.
