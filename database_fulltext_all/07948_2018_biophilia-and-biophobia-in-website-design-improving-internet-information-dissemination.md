# Biophilia and biophobia in website design: Improving internet information dissemination

Amir Riaz<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Shirley Gregor<sup>a</sup>, Aleck Lin<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> The Australian National University, Australia <sup>b</sup> National Dong Hwa University, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Evolutionary psychology Biophilia Biophobia Website-design Emotional responses Information recall Information dissemination

## A B S T R A C T

The Internet is increasingly a source of information for populations around the world, and thus theorizing that website design, which leads to enhanced recall of web-based information, has important theoretical and practical implications. On the basis of evolutionary psychology, biophilia and biophobia theories show how humans developed emotional responses to aspects of their natural environments so as to enhance survival by triggering approach or avoidance behaviors. Enhanced memory for biophobic elements such as predators and biophilic elements such as food would be further <sup>fi</sup>tness enhancing. This study aimed to show how website design based on biophilia and biophobia theories can elicit emotions and improve recall of web-based information. An ex: periment was performed with simulated national park websites that contained images designed to elicit emotional responses. The emotional responses (e.g., valence and arousal) were associated with increased recall of information presented on the websites. The study contributes by showing that biophilia theory and biophobic theory can guide website design research and improve information recall.

## 1. Introduction

Governments are increasingly disseminating critical information to the public through websites [1], a phenomenon referred to as online information dissemination (OID) [2]. OID focuses on improving the public’s knowledge of speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics. However, the increasing number of informational websites and amount of web-based information makes it harder for people to remember web-based information and, hence, hinders governments from improving the public’s knowledge. To address this problem, one can induce a<sup>f</sup>ective responses that research has found to be positively associated with recall of web-based information in online users [3].

Research on a<sup>f</sup>ective responses in information systems and hu man–computer interaction has increased in recent years [4]. Design scholars such as Norman have moved from usability-centered design (1983, 1993) to emotional design (2004) [5–7]. However, in her review, Zhang [4] noted that few if any scholars have systematically and theoretically examined a<sup>f</sup>ective phenomena in the context of in formation and communications technology (ICT), which indicates we need further theory-based work in this area.

Theorizing about a<sup>f</sup>ective concepts outside ICT shows promising developments. For instance, Kahneman [8] has recently developed the dual processing theory that depicts two modes of processing: intuition (system 1) and reasoning (system 2) (see [9]. System 1 processes are intuitive, fast, unconscious, and linked to emotion, while system 2 processes are conscious, slower, and more controlled. For example, a perceived threat (e.g., a snake) may cause one to quickly and instinctively experience fear, which, in turn, may lead to a <sup>fl</sup>ight (avoidance) or <sup>fi</sup>ght (approach) response. Dual processing theory is an evolutionary approach to understand a<sup>f</sup>ective concepts and views emotions as serving to enhance humans’ survival. It sees system 1 responses as residing in the older parts of the brain; the amygdala (a part of the limbic system or reptile brain ) [10]. Kock [11] reviews information systems theorizing from an evolutionary perspective. Such theories provide a promising avenue for investigating emotional responses in the online context, and we adopt them in this study.

Biophilia theory suggests that humans have an “innate emotional a<sup>fi</sup>liation with living organisms” and that exposure to natural elements that helped early humans to survive leads to positive emotions [12], p. 31). Biophobia theory suggests that exposure to natural elements that endangered early humans’ survival leads to negative emotions [13].

Some studies have investigated biophobia e<sup>f</sup>ects on individuals. For instance, Kock et al. [14] investigated biophobia e<sup>f</sup>ects and showed that participants better recalled information from modules that were presented just before or after a screen presenting a snake in an attack position along with a hissing sound. However, the extent literature has paid little or no attention to biophilia e<sup>f</sup>ects in the online context [15]. As such, our study extends Kock et al. [14] by investigating both bio phobic and biophilia e<sup>f</sup>ects.

In particular, we investigate the following research question

Do website-design features that incorporate biophilia and biophobia e<sup>f</sup>ects enhance online users’ information recall by evoking their emotional responses?

We performed an experiment with national park websites that contained simulated images of biophilic and biophobic elements in nature. The websites also contained information about safety precautions when visiting a national park. The outcomes we assessed were: (1) the emotional responses and (2) the degree of information recall. The biophilic and biophobic website-design features successfully induced emotional responses in participants and were associated with an increased recall of information.

This study contributes to both theory and practice. It extends the theory of website-design by theorizing the e<sup>f</sup>ects of both biophobia and biophilia-based design features on outcomes of web usage and testing these new theories in a laboratory experiment. It extends Kock et al. [14] by grounding design features <sup>fi</sup>rmly in theory that shows that biophilic and biophobic website-design features can in<sup>fl</sup>uence emotions and information recall. Further, compared to Kock et al. [14], it better represents a real-life website, which commonly features images and textual information but not sounds such as hissing. We believe that these theoretically developed and empirically tested biophilic and biophobic website-design features not only contribute to ICT design theory in general but also extend theory of website-design in particular by o<sup>f</sup>ering researchers new avenues on which they can build when investigating website-design features with a focus on evoking emotions.

On the practical front, website developers and managers can use our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for general guidance on how to develop websites and use both biophilia and biophobia e<sup>f</sup>ects to enhance information recall.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Evolutionary theory, emotions, and information communication

Biophilic and biophobic e<sup>f</sup>ects have their roots in evolutionary psychology. The biophilia and biophobia hypotheses are based on an argument that the rewards and dangers associated with natural settings in human evolution were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enough to give advantages to in dividuals who learned and remembered positive/approach (biophilic) and negative/avoidance (biophobic) behaviors for certain stimuli [13]. An important aspect of these hypotheses is that they do not just posit a relationship between natural stimuli and human responses but also argue that these responses have a partly genetic basis and, thus, likely occur across cultures—a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant issue when designing systems for widespread use.

Researchers believe that psychological traits that arise from evolu tionary processes developed during the environment of evolutionary adaptation (EEA), a period from approximately 1.8 million years ago to the emergence of modern humans around 100,000 years ago [16]. Humans developed a genetic disposition to acquire and then consistently retain responses to natural elements and contexts that increased their chances of survival because they were related to primary necessities such as water, food, and security. Savanna-like environ ments with open grasslands that had abundant food supplies and lower risk from hidden predators had advantages for early humans, and considerable evidence supports the assertion that a major part of human evolution occur in savannas [13]. Persistent psychological traits arising from this evolutional background include positive emotional responses to natural elements such as open spaces, scattered small trees, uniform grassy surfaces, water, green vegetation, and <sup>fl</sup>owers. Less preferred contexts include arid or desert environments where food and water would be sparse [13]. Conversely, persistent negative responses arose for threatening elements in nature such as harmful animal life, closed spaces, and heights. Considerable empirical support exists for both the biophilia and biophobia hypotheses, including the genetic component $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ , see [13]).

The biophobia and biophilia hypotheses involve emotional responses and are consistent with a functional view of emotion, in which emotions serve an adaptive purpose (e.g., as in evolutionary and Darwinian theories) [17]. Emotions as subjective feelings motivate humans to perform actions. Perceptions of a threat lead to fear and then <sup>fl</sup>ight (avoidance) or <sup>fi</sup>ght (approach). Perceptions of stimuli that indicate a reward lead to positive emotions and approach behaviors [18]. These emotional responses can occur very quickly and at low levels of the human nervous system.

Further, research has linked the emotional responses in the two hypotheses to cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ects. Kock et al. [14] linked situations that lead to surprise to enhanced memorization, particularly where the surprise arose due to a perceived threat. Kock and Chatelain-Jardon [3] reason that surprise leads to enhanced memorization because a surprise situation such as a snake attack would have equipped hominid ancestors to better respond to an attack situation in the future. This theory proposes that evolution predisposed humans to easily learn and retain associations and responses that have aided survival when they encounter positive and negative stimuli (but not neutral). More generally, research across many <sup>fi</sup>elds has found that individuals pay more attention when a situation involves emotion-inducing stimuli. When an emotion-inducing stimulus has engaged an individual’s emotions and attention, the individual may continue to pay attention at the location even after the stimuli has passed, which may encourage the individual to process subsequent non-emotional material that appears at the same location. Much empirical evidence supports this view (see [19,20]).

## 2.2. Nature of emotional responses

To study the biophilia and biophobia hypotheses further, it is helpful to consider the nature of the emotions implicated in more depth. In the consensual, componential model of emotion, an emotional response arises when one appraises the personal signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of a sti mulus, which, in turn, leads to an emotional response involving physiology, subjective experience, and behavior [21]. Research largely categorized emotions based on two perspectives: the discrete and dimensional perspectives. The discrete perspective identi<sup>fi</sup>es several distinguishable emotions, such as anger, sadness, and contempt. The dimensional perspective, on the other hand, categorizes emotions along several fundamental dimensions: for example, valence, arousal, and dominance. In a review, Mauss and Robinson [21] found strong support for the dimensional model, which they say captures the “lion’s share of variance” in self-report studies of emotion (p. 213). Note that Kock et al. [14] studied the response of fear-related “surprise” in relation to the biophobic e<sup>f</sup>ect. Although one can classify surprise as an emotion, its status is ambivalent, and others argue that it is a bridge between emotion and cognition and that cognitive assessments of likelihood and intuition shape it [22]. Dimensional perspective of emotion gives a clearer link to the biophilia and biophobia hypotheses, which both explicitly mention broader emotional responses in general rather than a single speci<sup>fi</sup>c emotion.

In this study, we adopt the dimensional “circumplex model” of emotional responses, which depicts two primary dimensions of emotional response: valence and arousal [23]. Indeed, Zhang [4] has proposed this model as a basis for theorizing a<sup>f</sup>ective concepts in the ICT context. The valence dimension represents variation in levels of pleasure (e.g., unhappy to happy, displeased to pleased). The arousal dimension represents variation in levels of arousal (e.g., quiet to excited, sleepy to wide awake). Research has reported that arousal leads to the release of energy in body tissues, which, in turn, leads to subjective feelings of energy mobilization so that the individual can deal with threats and opportunities [24].

## 2.3. Implications of the biophilia and biophobia theories for website-design

Following the studies of Lee and Kozar [25] and Kock and Chate lain-Jardon [3], we focus on website-design from an evolutionary perspective. Website-design can enable websites to provide online users with experiences they <sup>fi</sup>nd in o<sup>fl</sup>ine settings [26]. It also allows users to perceive cyberspace as having similar properties to physical space [25]. Additionally, images of natural elements shown on computers can be a reliable surrogate for those elements [27,28] and play a crucial role in improving users’ online experiences [11].

From the evolutionary perspective, one can classify websites' design according to their similarities either with the EEA (see [29,25] or with the EEA’s natural elements (see [3]. We refer to the features that evoke the perceptions of the EEA itself, such as coherence, complexity, legibility, and mystery, as molar-level features. Additionally, we refer to the features that evoke the perceptions of the EEA’s natural elements, such as color and images of natural elements (e.g., snakes, edible animals, grains, etc.), as molecular-level features.

Some studies have investigated molar-level website-design. For example, Singh et al. [30] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of molar-level website design (i.e., coherence, diversity, and mystery) on users’ attitudes toward website and behavioral intention. Lee and Kozar [25] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of molar-level website design on cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective appraisal, and, in turn, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on users’ purchase intention. However, some studies have reported that molecular-level website-de sign features induce higher levels of emotional responses and have a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on information recall [14] than on molar-level website-design features [31].

In this study, we focus on molecular-level features because little existing research has investigated molecular-level website design from an evolutionary psychology perspective (i.e., images of natural elements). In one exception, Kock et al. [14] examined the impact of one molecular-level website-design feature (i.e., the image of a snake and a hissing sound) on information recall and found a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between fear-related surprise and information recall.

Empirical research examining images of natural elements as visual website-design features is sparse. From theory (e.g., see [11–13]), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the images of natural elements that once maximized the survival of early humans in the EEA as biophilic website-design features and the images of natural elements that minimized the survival of early humans in the EEA as biophobic website-design features. The biophilic websitedesign features may, therefore, include images of edible plants such as grains, small mammals, vegetation, and water, while biophobic website-design features may include images of snakes, spiders, rotten food, dead animals, and polluted water.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Our research model arose from our synthesizing the theory of evolutionary psychology, emotional responses, and information recall (see Fig. 1). We describe the model in more detail in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 with reference to theory previously discussed.

## 3.1. Biophilic and biophobic website design and emotions

The evolutionary environment in which early humans evolved included natural elements that either increased or reduced their survival chances. According to biophilia and biophobia theories, one can categorize those natural elements into biophilic (maximized survival) and biophobic (minimized survival) elements [13].

Through website design, we can provide online users with an experience of o<sup>fl</sup>ine environment by using digital images of o<sup>fl</sup>ine ele ments (e.g., digital images of natural elements in the context of this study) in a website, which can act as surrogates of those elements [26].

The proponents of evolutionary psychology, particularly that of biophilia and biophobia theories (e.g., [12,13], suggest that early humans’ exposure to natural elements that increased or decreased their survival chances led them to develop particular emotional responses to these stimuli. Their repetitive encounters with biophilic (biophobic) elements led them to develop positive (negative) emotions about them. Because modern humans still share the brain mechanisms of early humans [11], we can expect that exposure to natural elements that evoke the elements of the EEA will evoke their corresponding emotional responses.

Congruent with the dimensional theory of emotions Russel and Pratt [23] adopted in this study, we map emotional responses (both positive and negative) on two dimensions: valence and arousal. Valence determines the direction of the emotional response (i.e., whether the emotional response is positive or negative), whereas arousal determines the intensity of such feelings [29]. Valence and arousal are independent of each other: an individual could be very unhappy (happy) but not aroused (see [20]. Several studies provide evidence for the dissociable e<sup>f</sup>ects of valence and arousal [32,33]. High levels of arousal can occur with both positive and negative stimuli, which, together with a neutral level, can be represented by a U-shaped relationship. In the context of evolutionary adaptation, however, the biophobic e<sup>f</sup>ect is often associated with life-threatening stimuli such as predatory animals and seen as eliciting stronger responses than the biophilic e<sup>f</sup>ect (see [13]. That is, risk from threats arouses humans more than rewards such as food sources.

Fig. 2 shows the three categories of stimuli that we studied and their hypothesized e<sup>f</sup>ect on both valence and arousal. This <sup>fi</sup>gure expands the left-hand side of the research model in Fig. 1 (Hypotheses 1 and 2).

The three categories for stimuli are: (i) biophobia (e.g., snake), (ii) biophilia (e.g., small mammal), and (iii) neutral (e.g., utensil). Note that, following the circum<sup>fl</sup>ex model, this conceptualization has a continuum from low (strong negative valence) to high (strong positive valence) with a state of negligible valence occupying an intermediate position. When we consider the relationship between information recall and valence in Section 3.2, we are also interested in valence “strength”; that is, the degree of valence whether positive or negative. Valence strength depicts an emotional response’s distance from the central point of neutral valence in both positive and negative directions. We expect, for example, a strong feeling of unhappiness to have the same e<sup>f</sup>ect as a strong feeling of happiness. As such, we hypothesize:

H1a. Biophilic website-design features will lead to higher levels of valence compared to biophobic and neutral website-design features.

H1b. Biophobic website-design features will lead to lower levels of valence compared to biophilic and neutral website-design features.

H1c. Both biophilic and biophobic website-design features will lead to higher levels of valence strength compared with neutral website-design features.

H2a. Biophobic website-design features will lead to higher levels of arousal compared to biophilic and neutral website-design features.

H2b. Biophilic website-design features will lead to higher levels of arousal compared to neutral website-design features.

We include H1c because it follows from H1a and H1b: if we <sup>fi</sup>nd support for H1a and H1b, then we must necessarily also <sup>fi</sup>nd support for H1c.<sup>1</sup>

## 4. Research method

We conducted an experiment to examine the impact of biophilic and biophobic website-design features on emotional responses and in formation recall.

## 4.1. The information technology artifact—a national park website

We designed a website that provided information to the general public on national parks by using information from a real-life website. We chose a national park website for two reasons. First, governments use these types of websites to disseminate information to the public. Second, information recall is an important factor for governments that provide information to the public because they want to ensure not only that visitors and the park itself stay safe but also that visitors enjoy themselves. We created three similar websites that di<sup>f</sup>ered only in the image that they displayed. The layout, design, information, and the size and location of the images were the same across all three versions (see Appendix A).

## 4.2. Experimental design and manipulations

The experiment had three separate treatment conditions that re presented categories of interest in website design based on biophilia and biophobia theories: (1) biophilia, (2) one neutral condition, and (3) biophobia. We developed and pretested a criterion for selecting website images that represented biophilic, biophobic, and neutral conditions (see Appendix B). We took the images from the International A<sup>f</sup>ective Picture System (IAPS) [45], which has norms established for emotional responses for valence and arousal. We positioned the images to the left on the websites because individuals process left-positioned images early during their initial exposure to websites [46].

We conducted a pilot study by using the experimental conditions with 21 participants to test the experimental manipulations and re<sup>fi</sup>ne the procedure. The participants also provided feedback on the images size and location, on the information’s font size, readability, and ap propriateness, and on the amount of time they had to view the website. We also conducted a pretest with 12 participants to test if the website treatments di<sup>f</sup>ered only on biophilic and biophobic features and not on their relevance, color, attractiveness, legibility, and coherence [25]. The t-statistics results showed no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence for any of the above variables for two conditions (e.g., biophilia and bio phobia) except attractiveness (see Appendix C). Participants perceived the biophobic website condition as not attractive at all. On the basis of the pretest, we can say that website stimuli di<sup>f</sup>ered in their biophilic and biophobic features.

## 4.3. Participants

We recruited participants through advertisements in a public university. After removing cases with missing values, 132 usable responses remained for analysis. Table 1 describes the participants. All the participants used the Internet daily. They reported their familiarity with the presented information, and all showed a high level of familiarity. They also reported the di<sup>fi</sup>culty level of the information, and all reported that the information was easy to comprehend.

## 4.4. Procedure

We instructed the participants to play the role of an online user who was planning to camp in a national park and, therefore, was evaluating information on a national park website about what to take with them. We randomly assigned each participant to one of the three experimental conditions.

The experiment had several steps: (1) a study phase in which the participant read the camping-related information on a website for 70 s (the pretest showed this amount of time was su<sup>fi</sup>cient for the task), (2) a measure of emotional response [self-assessment manikin (SAM)]; (3) a distraction phase [47] for 2 min to clear working memory; (4) a test phase in which the participants had to write down as many items as they could recall from the ones they had read from the website (a free recall test) and then a multiple choice-type recognition test (Appendix D); and (5) a questionnaire that asked for demographic information and some qualitative feedback.

## 4.5. Instrumentation and analysis

After participants evaluated the website and read the information provided on it, they rated their emotional responses on a visual scale termed the SAM [48]. SAM uses graphic <sup>fi</sup>gures of a manikin to represent valence and arousal (Appendix E). We also measured “valence” on a scale from 1 (negative valence) to 9 (positive valence). We measured information recall in two ways: (1) free recall, whereby participants had to recall as many items as they could and (2) recognition recall, whereby participants had to answer 10 multiple-choice questions. For free recall, we scored each successfully recalled item with a single point with a maximum possible overall score of 18. Two in dependent coders who followed a protocol for scoring the tests calculated the score for free recall. The inter-rater reliability was high (ICC (2,2) = 0.88). The maximum possible score for recognition recall was 10. Both the free recall and the recognition recall for each participant provided a single-item measure for each of the two recall tests. We followed [49] in measuring information communication e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness such as information recall as a single-item re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct in structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis (see [49]. We analyzed the collected data <sup>fi</sup>rst with MANOVA (e.g., SPSS 20) and then with SmartPLS 3.

We employed an SEM approach to analyze the data. The major advantage of a SEM-based approach is that it tests structural and measurement models simultaneously [50]. We chose partial least squares SEM (PLS-SEM) (which is variance based) over covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) techniques such as AMOS and LISREL for several reasons. First, our model involves single-item constructs, and research has argued that PLS-SEM is a better approach when dealing with single-item constructs because using CB-SEM with single-item constructs “generally leads to model under-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation” [52], p. 423). Second, CB-SEM requires a larger sample size than PLS SEM—“at least 200 observations to avoid non-convergence and improper solutions” [53], p. 333)—whereas our sample size was 132. Finally, PLS-SEM better suits both con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory and exploratory studie than CB-SEM, which is more suitable for con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory studies [54].

We believe that, because we measured emotional responses and information recall by using di<sup>f</sup>erent scales, common method bias was not an issue in this study [55].

## 4.6. Manipulation checks

We performed manipulation checks by directly asking the partici pants two questions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst question concerned the valence of the experimental condition (“How did the website make you feel?”), to which they could answer with neutral, pleasant, or unpleasant. The second question concerned arousal (“How did the website make you feel?”), to which they could answer with neutral, wide awake/excited/ aroused, or sleepy/calm/unaroused. In situations where the manipulation test indicated substantial di<sup>f</sup>erences between the SAM responses and the manipulation questions, we excluded that data set. The qualitative responses in the <sup>fi</sup>nal step in the experiment also indicated that manipulations were e<sup>f</sup>ective.

## 5.1. Post hoc analysis and qualitative data

We analyzed the qualitative feedback received from the participants relating to their experience when visiting the website. We used an open coding technique to code their responses. We then applied a thematic analysis technique to identify and build higher-level concepts. Appendix H lists participants’ comments. Three themes emerged from this analysis: (1) emotional responses, (2) intention to revisit the website, and (3) perceived relevance of the website images to the text. Several participants used discrete emotions when commenting on their experience, such as excitement, enjoyment, peacefulness, and love in biophilia conditions and fear, scariness, anxiousness, worry, and unpleasantness in biophobia conditions. Though we did not ask participants to provide their feedback on whether they would revisit the website in future, two participants reported their intention to revisit the website in high arousal conditions (i.e., biophilia and biophobia). Participant 1 in the biophobia (i.e., snake) condition said: “The picture of the snake scared me. I won’t visit this website again.” Participant 97 in the biophilia (i.e., small mammal) condition said: “I would like to use this type of website again. I actually want to go to a park now to see the lovely animal”. The <sup>fi</sup>nal theme that emerged concerned the perceived relevance of the images with the text. It seemed that some participants who commented on the relevance were expecting to see a picture that was related to the presented information.

## 6. Discussion

In line with prior research (e.g., [3,11,25,29], we investigate how one can use theories from evolutionary psychology to investigate website-design features, emotional responses, and their subsequent effects on online users. We conducted a study in which we expected that website-design features would induce emotional responses in online users (e.g., valence and arousal) and, in turn, enhance online users’ recall of web-based information. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose that one can draw novel website-design features from theories of biophilia and biophobia, which we de<sup>fi</sup>ne as biophilic and biophobic website-design features. We found that biophilic website features induced positive emotions, and biophobic features induced negative emotions. We also found support for our hypotheses for the relationships between emotional responses and information recall with one exception: we found no association between arousal and free recall of information.

A possible explanation for the statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between arousal and free recall of information can be that arousal modulates memory through the amygdala, while valence modulates memory through the orbitofrontal cortex [32]. In addition, other research (e.g., [58,59] points out that the recall performance that depends on the amygdala requires time delays. It is possible that the amygdala might not have played much role in consolidating memory traces in our participants because we conducted an immediate recall test. Hence, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the relationship between arousal and free recall that we expected. However, our results for this relationship was in the direction we expected (e.g., positive path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, Fig. 4), and they would perhaps become more evident with a larger sample and more power in the statistical test and with a delayed recall test. We need further work in this regard.

As a post hoc test, we found that arousal and valence strength were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly correlated. This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports a view that valence and arousal evolved simultaneously to facilitate behavioral responses. For example, positive valence (negative valence) was necessary to facilitate approach (avoidance) behavior, whereas arousal was necessary to mobilize the organism to act accordingly. Prior studies that employed an evolutionary perspective for website-design also found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlations between valence and arousal (e.g., [29]).

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions and implications

This study signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhances our understanding of the factors in user interface design that induce emotional responses and improve the information retention of online users from an evolutionary psychology perspective. With this study, we contribute to theory in several ways. First, we proposed and subsequently tested two novel theory-based website-design features: biophilic and biophobic website features. The results show that biophilic website-design features can evoke positive emotions, and that biophobic website features can evoke negative emotions in online users. Given that IS researchers are increasingly adding “a<sup>f</sup>ective dimensions” in examining the design of user interfaces (e.g., websites) [60,61], our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are both timely and important. This study, therefore, contributes to how one can apply theories of biophilia and biophobia from evolutionary psychology to user perceptions of website images in IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Second, we extend prior work (e.g., [14] that has investigated only biophobia’s e<sup>f</sup>ect on information recall in website design in two ways. One, we tested biophilia’s e<sup>f</sup>ect on users and argue that, apart from biophobia theory, one could also use biophilia theory in theorizing website design. Two, we also, as compared to Kock et al. [14], used biophobic website-design features in a more realistic scenario (i.e., adding only images to the textual information as compared to showing image on a separate screen and adding a hissing sound). We believe that doing so has provided valuable insights into the potential of biophilic website-design features and that IS researchers who are interested in investigating evoking positive emotions can build on our theory.

Third, we also extend the IS literature that focuses on improving individuals’ information recall. For example, several IS studies have investigated di<sup>f</sup>erent design elements, such as multimedia [43], text illustrators [62], and lists and matrices [63], and their e<sup>f</sup>ect on improving individuals’ information recall. We contribute to the above line of research by proposing that one can enhance users’ information recall through evoking emotional responses in them.

Finally, we also contribute to the dimensional theory of emotions Russel and Pratt [23] which IS design research has increasingly begun to employ (e.g., see [29]), by explaining the underlying mechanisms of emotions and how such an explanation allows one to investigate web site interfaces and their impact on information recall (especially in an online context). Therefore, IS researchers and information providers may <sup>fi</sup>nd our theoretically developed and empirically tested model in this study to have great value.

## 6.2. Practical contributions and implications

Our study also contributes to practice in several ways. With the increasing number of websites and amount of web-based information, people are <sup>fi</sup>nding it harder to remember web-based information, which a<sup>f</sup>ects an information provider’s objective to improve a recipient’s knowledge. Our study provides a solution to this problem by proposing how information providers can help their targeted audience better retain information.

The study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be applicable in several scenarios. For example, governments run behavioral-intervention campaigns such as discouraging people to quit smoking. They do so by showing unpleasant images of illness and harm that smoking does to humans’ physical health. Many such images fall under the category of biophobic elements. We believe websites that feature such biophobic images can help governments run e<sup>f</sup>ective campaigns in an online context such that they would induce negative emotions in online users, and, thus, lead to avoidance behavior. As another example, governments spend large sums of money to fund awareness campaigns about climate change. We believe that governments could run online campaigns for climatechange awareness that feature images of dead animals, polluted environments, and toxic water (e.g., biophobic website-design features) to induce negative emotions and activate survival-related brain mechanisms that would convince people to take actions to combat climate change and remember messages about it. Indeed, many other organizations can use biophobic website-design features for similar goals. Health websites could use biophilic website-design features. For example, websites that feature biophilic elements that facilitated early humans' survival can have a soothing e<sup>f</sup>ect on people and induce approach behavior, reduce stress, and keep online users reading healthrelated information and help them remember that information.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations that readers should consider when interpreting the results. We used only a single website category (national parks) and need further work to extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in other contexts. We also used a static website that did not allow participants to click on links and had very minimal interaction. Although prior IS research has used static websites as experimental stimuli (e.g., [56,29], we suggest that future research should test the proposed research model by using interactive websites as experimental stimuli. Further, fol lowing several other studies from the psychology and neuroscience <sup>fi</sup>elds, we only used biophilic and biophobic website-design features that we expected to induce heightened levels of emotional responses. However, several other features could induce lower levels of emotional responses that one could use in the websites. Future studies can explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of such features on emotional responses and their subsequent e<sup>f</sup>ect on other variables of interest including information recall. Finally, we chose one set of images for the di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions from the IAPS, which has norms established for emotional responses for valence and arousal. With another set, results could di<sup>f</sup>er to some extent (e.g., one could <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive image that is more arousing than a negative one). In future work, one could also extend the work of Kock and Chatelain-Jardon [3] who proposed four principles based on biophobia theory for website design. New principles based on biophilia theory could be developed.

## 7. Conclusion

This study demonstrates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of website images of natural elements on information recall and the potential of evolutionary psychology for theorizing the design of web-based user interfaces. The results support the research hypotheses that website images of natural elements induce emotional responses and, in turn, enhance the recall of web-based information. We found that the valence and arousal dimensions of the emotional response were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in improving information recall. The study is important, given the increasing trend by organizations to disseminate information through websites and the in creasing focus on the role of emotions in human–computer interaction.
