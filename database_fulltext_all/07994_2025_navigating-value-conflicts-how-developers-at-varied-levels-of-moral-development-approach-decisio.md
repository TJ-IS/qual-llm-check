# Navigating Value Conflicts: How Developers at Varied Levels of Moral Development Approach Decision-Making in Information Systems Development

Khalid Durani,<sup>1</sup> Andreas Eckhardt,<sup>2</sup> Tim Kollmer<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Innsbruck, Austria, Khalid.Durani@uibk.ac.at <sup>2</sup> University of Innsbruck, Austria, Andreas.Eckhardt@uibk.ac.at <sup>3</sup> University of Innsbruck, Austria, Tim.Kollmer@uibk.ac.at

## Abstract

Information systems development frequently involves value conflicts (e.g., reconciling compliance requirements with user autonomy), thereby requiring developers to engage in moral decision-making regarding what is desirable, right, and just. Drawing on the theory of moral development, this study examines how developers—whose moral reasoning emphasizes self-interest (pre-conventional), maintaining social norms (conventional), or higher-order principles (post-conventional)—navigate these conflicts. We identify varying moral decision-making patterns among developers at the preconventional, conventional, and post-conventional levels, structured around four interrelated themes: (1) problem framing, (2) solution conceptualization, (3) responsibility orientation, and (4) duty fulfillment. Our analysis further indicates the pivotal role of developers’ responsibility orientation in guiding moral decision-making. Specifically, this orientation encompasses how developers attribute causal responsibility (e.g., assigning blame) in framing problems, allocate accountability (e.g., taking ownership) in conceptualizing solutions, and balance obligations (e.g., honoring commitments) in fulfilling their duties. Thus, our study shows how distinct responsibility orientations among IS developers at the pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional levels critically shape their navigation of value conflicts, ultimately influencing project outcomes.

## 1 Introduction

Decision-making in information systems (IS) development (ISD) is notoriously “wicked” (McCarthy et al., 2018). At the heart of this complexity are multifaceted challenges, ranging from uncovering fundamental issues in the ISD problem space (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007) to devising corresponding solutions—particularly technological artifacts—capable of satisfying the diverse interests and desires of heterogeneous stakeholder groups (Klein &

Hirschheim, 2001). Scarce resources (Conboy, 2010; O’Connor et al., 2023), technical constraints (Ulrich, 2001a), and competing imperatives (Anderson et al., 1993) further compound these challenges, which, in turn, may escalate into conflicts (Barki & Hartwick, 2001; Hekkala et al., 2022).

Beyond these practical complexities, such conflicts are often inherently value laden (Friedman et al., 2013), thereby requiring IS developers to engage in a moral decision-making process regarding what is desirable, right, and just. For example, balancing compliance requirements with user autonomy (Dadgar & Joshi, 2018) implicates stakeholder desires and interests that cannot be addressed solely through factual analysis, given that “is” does not imply “ought” (Friedman et al., 2013). Navigating these value conflicts thus demands moral considerations related to ensuring stakeholder welfare, mitigating harm, and resolving other normative concerns (Klein & Hirschheim, 2001; Vartiainen, 2010b; Yetim, 2011). Although numerous formal frameworks—such as codes of ethics (Anderson et al., 1993) and recent digital responsibility initiatives (Carl & Hinz, 2024; Mueller, 2022)—have aimed to ensure that novel artifacts serve the public good and avert adverse effects, extant research reveals wide variation in IS developers’ moral decision-making.

A core ethical principle in these approaches is that IS developers should embrace responsibility for all stakeholders affected by an artifact’s design and deployment (Collins et al., 1994; Yuthas & Dillard, 1999). In practice, however, developers experience, interpret, and enact responsibility in diverse ways (Schmidt et al., 2025) as part of their moral decisionmaking (Vaast, 2025). On one end of the spectrum, they may abdicate responsibility by uncritically deferring to directives from influential actors (e.g., managers) (Chatterjee et al., 2009). On the other end, they may actively contest external mandates that impinge on their sense of responsibility—for instance, convictions about who should benefit from the artifacts they create (Boland & Day, 1989).

Despite the far-reaching ramifications of these differing stances on project trajectories (Chatterjee et al., 2009; Vaast, 2025), our understanding of how IS developers navigate moral decision-making, particularly when confronted with value conflicts, remains limited. A promising lens for gaining deeper insights into this phenomenon lies in Kohlberg’s (1969) theory of moral development. In this view, moral development progresses across three levels—pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional—each representing a distinct mode of moral reasoning (Kohlberg & Hersh, 1977). At the preconventional level, individuals predominantly rely on instrumental, self-serving rationales, at the conventional level, they prioritize adherence to social norms, and at the post-conventional level, they draw on higher-order principles such as justice (Rest et al., 1999). Prior research in psychology (Rest & Narváez, 1994) and IS (Banerjee et al., 1998; Davison et al., 2009; Myyry et al., 2009) suggests that these modes of moral reasoning often align with characteristic decision-making patterns across professional contexts.

Accordingly, IS developers’ level of moral development may shed light on how they reconcile value conflicts in ISD. For instance, developers at the pre-conventional level may invoke instrumental, self-protective rationales when resolving contradictory stakeholder desires and interests, whereas their post-conventional counterparts might champion principle-driven, collective justifications. Because these decisions become inscribed in the artifacts developers create (Klein & Hirschheim, 2001), they not only shape project outcomes (Chatterjee et al., 2009) but may also mitigate or exacerbate overarching normative issues such as marginalization (Deng et al., 2016; Kane et al., 2021; Leidner & Tona, 2021; Schlagwein et al., 2019). Given these high-stakes implications, we pose the following research question:

RQ: How do IS developers at the pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional levels navigate moral decision-making amid value conflicts in ISD?

To address our research question, we used a concurrent mixed methods approach (Venkatesh et al., 2023) to examine six ISD teams grappling with a central value conflict regarding how to mitigate shadow IT. In this context, IS developers had to negotiate between compliance with organizational data security policies and employees’ needs for autonomy and efficiency.

We found varying moral decision-making patterns across four interrelated themes: (1) problem framing, (2) solution conceptualization, (3) responsibility orientation, and (4) duty fulfillment. Pre-conventional IS developers typically employ a reductionistic approach to problem framing, conceptualize expedient solutions, display a diffuse responsibility orientation, and fulfill duties pragmatically. At the conventional level, they tend to frame problems in localized terms, conceptualize solutions adaptively, exhibit a harmonious responsibility orientation, and fulfill duties in a conformist manner. Finally, post-conventional developers foreground structural considerations in their problem framing, prioritize reciprocity in their conceptualized solutions, demonstrate an integrative responsibility orientation, and fulfill duties resolutely.

Our analysis further underscores the pivotal role of developers’ responsibility orientation in guiding moral decision-making. Specifically, this orientation encompasses how developers attribute causal responsibility (e.g., assigning blame) in framing problems, allocate accountability (e.g., taking ownership) in conceptualizing solutions, and balance obligations (e.g., honoring commitments) in fulfilling their duties. Thus, our study shows how distinct responsibility orientations among IS developers at the pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional levels critically shape their navigation of value conflicts, ultimately influencing project outcomes.

## 2 Background

In this study, we adopt the distinction between “morality”—specific convictions or norms—and “ethics”—the philosophical inquiry into moral systems (Chatterjee & Sarker, 2013; Stahl, 2012). Building on this distinction and prior ISD research (Anderson et al., 1993; Klein & Hirschheim, 2001; Vartiainen, 2010a; Yetim, 2011), we refer to moral decision-making as the process through which IS developers determine and act upon what is desirable, right, and just.

## 2.1 The Moral Domain of Decision-Making in Information Systems Development

In the pre-development phase, decisions about whether to initiate an ISD project depend on moral assumptions concerning the project’s legitimacy, the means to be deployed, and the ends pursued (Iivari et al., 1998). For instance, an ISD intervention in the military sector necessitates moral considerations that differ from those in healthcare, given the contrasting goals and potential impacts of the resulting artifact (Vartiainen, 2010a). Once IS developers choose to proceed, they effectively endorse—and may reinforce—the ethos of the sponsoring organization (Stahl, 2007).

As ISD projects unfold, developers determine which “problems” warrant attention and whose viewpoints should take priority (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). Transitioning from problem identification to delivering a viable artifact involves navigating multiple—often conflicting—values (Ulrich, 2001a, 2001b). Unlike facts, values cannot be settled by factual analysis alone, as “is” does not imply “ought”; rather, they hinge on stakeholder desires and interests (Friedman et al., 2013). Consequently, negotiating value conflicts, such as balancing compliance requirements with user autonomy (Dadgar & Joshi, 2018), demands moral decisionmaking about which claims to prioritize and which to deemphasize (Klein & Hirschheim, 2001; Yetim, 2011).

Because such choices may benefit certain stakeholders at the expense of others, they often lead to interpersonal conflicts (Vartiainen, 2010a; Ulrich, 2001a). Managing these disputes is vital for fostering shared understanding and commitment, thereby increasing the likelihood of project success (Gregory et al., 2013; Hekkala et al., 2022; Jenkin et al., 2019). Conversely, unresolved conflicts can steer projects toward failure (Barki & Hartwick, 2001). From a moral standpoint, attending to interpersonal disputes is imperative when the underlying values implicate deeper normative concerns, such as preventing harm (Vartiainen, 2010a; Yetim, 2011; Ulrich, 2001a).

In the post-development phase, developers’ moral decision-making becomes embedded in the resulting artifact, potentially reshaping organizational social structures, including employee routines (Stahl, 2007; Yuthas & Dillard, 1999). Consequently, certain artifact configurations may elevate or erode stakeholder dignity (Leidner & Tona, 2021), foster unity or intensify alienation (Stahl, 2009), promote empowerment or deepen marginalization (Deng et al., 2016), or facilitate emancipation or exacerbate oppression (Kane et al., 2021).

## 2.2 Challenges in Guiding Moral Decision-Making in Information Systems Development

Numerous formal approaches have been proposed to integrate ethical standards into the design and development of technological artifacts, including methodologies (Friedman et al., 2013; Mumford, 1995), frameworks (Ulrich, 2001a, 2001b; Wood-Harper et al., 1996), principles (Chatterjee et al., 2009; Hirschheim & Klein, 1994; Yuthas & Dillard, 1999), codes of ethics (Anderson et al., 1993; Rogerson et al., 2000), digital responsibility initiatives (Carl & Hinz, 2024; Lobschat et al., 2021; Trier et al., 2023), and other related approaches (Mingers & Walsham, 2010; Monson, 2021; Nussbaumer et al., 2021). However, applying such guidelines in practice remains challenging (Stahl, 2007; Walsham, 1996; Wilson, 1997).

Consider, for example, the emphasis placed on “responsible” decision-making in the creation of artifacts (Trier et al., 2023; Yuthas & Dillard, 1999). In principle, assigning explicit responsibilities can clarify accountability; however, it may also generate tension between wider moral obligations and rolebased duties. Whereas duties are typically tied to formal roles (e.g., operational imperatives), obligations often transcend these boundaries to encompass additional commitments (cf. Anderson et al., 1993; Collins et al., 1994; Lobschat et al., 2021; Walsham, 1996). Hirschheim and Klein (1989) capture this tension by depicting “archetypes” that range from IS developers who cede responsibility for defining project objectives to dominant stakeholders, to those who champion transformative, emancipatory goals through ISD efforts.

Existing scholarship corroborates these divergent conceptions of “responsibility” in ISD (Boland & Day, 1989; Chatterjee et al., 2009; Schmidt et al., 2025; Vaast, 2025). At one extreme, some IS developers relinquish moral obligations by uncritically following the directives of powerful authorities, thereby sidestepping accountability for stakeholder welfare. At the other extreme, certain developers assert their obligation to prevent harm—even when it requires contesting upper-level mandates—and thus embrace direct accountability for ISD decisions.

Given the substantial implications of these different stances for the ISD process and project outcomes (Chatterjee et al., 2009; Vaast, 2025), we draw on the theory of moral development and the four-component model to deepen our understanding of how IS developers navigate moral decision-making, particularly in the face of value conflicts.

## 3 Theory of Moral Development and the Four-Component Model

Building on Piaget’s foundational work on moral cognition, Kohlberg (1969) proposed that individuals rely on reason to address moral issues (Kohlberg & Hersh, 1977). Drawing on longitudinal studies of participants’ responses to hypothetical dilemmas, he introduced a stage-based framework consisting of three levels of moral development (see Table 1). According to this theory, individuals progress through these levels by continually refining their moral reasoning.

At the pre-conventional level (Stages 1 and 2), morality is primarily viewed as an external requirement enforced by authority figures, with an emphasis on immediate consequences. Stage 1 focuses on obedience and avoiding punishment, whereas Stage 2 reflects a self-focused, instrumental stance. At the conventional level (Stages 3 and 4), moral considerations typically align with prevailing social norms. Stage 3 highlights interpersonal concordance, while Stage 4 underscores law and order. At the postconventional level (Stages 5 and 6), individuals commit to higher-order principles such as justice. Stage 5 centers on the social contract orientation (e.g., balancing social progress and collective welfare), whereas Stage 6 invokes universal ethical principles that transcend local conventions.

Rest’s (1979) defining issues test (DIT) operationalizes Kohlberg’s theory by assessing individuals’ dominant mode of moral reasoning, ranging from self-interest to norm adherence or higher-order principles. Although the DIT enables researchers to classify respondents at the preconventional, conventional, or post-conventional levels (Ho, 2025; Jean-Tron et al., 2022; Murrell, 2014), it does not fully explain how these modes of moral reasoning translate into professional contexts, particularly from a behavioral standpoint (Rest & Narváez, 1994). For example, an IS developer may be classified at the postconventional level; yet precisely how that principle-based stance unfolds in moral decision-making in the face of value conflicts in ISD remains unclear.

Accordingly, Rest (1983) introduced the four-component model (FCM), conceptualizing moral decision-making as consisting of four interrelated dimensions (see Table 2): moral sensitivity (recognizing the moral aspects of a situation), moral judgment (identifying the morally justifiable course of action), moral motivation (prioritizing moral values over competing interests), and moral character (acting in accordance with moral values despite obstacles). These components may emerge in a fluid, nonlinear manner (e.g., moral motivation may precede moral judgment or vice versa).

Empirical research in multiple disciplines (DeTienne et al., 2021; Rest & Narváez, 1994), including IS, suggests that modes of moral reasoning (see Table 1) often correspond to characteristic decision-making patterns (Banerjee et al., 1998; Myyry et al., 2009), even across diverse cultural contexts (Davison et al., 2009). Moreover, the FCM has been employed in studies of IS decision-making (Moores & Chang, 2006), and Vartiainen (2010a) specifically examined moral sensitivity (see Table 2) within conflictridden ISD scenarios.

Building on these insights, our study investigates how IS developers, classified via the DIT at the pre-conventional, conventional, or post-conventional level, navigate moral decision-making amid value conflicts, using the FCM as a conceptual scaffold. Indeed, prior research indicates that the DIT taps into the moral judgment component of the FCM (Bebeau, 2002) and is empirically associated with moral sensitivity and moral motivation (Morton et al., 2006; Myyry et al., 2010). Theoretically, integrating DITbased classifications with the FCM can provide a more nuanced understanding of developers’ varied approaches to moral decision-making (e.g., Vaast, 2025). Practically, this line of inquiry is crucial, as IS developers’ moral choices have enduring ramifications for project outcomes and stakeholder circumstances (Chatterjee et al., 2009).

## 4 Methodology

To address our research question, we used a concurrent mixed methods design (Venkatesh et al., 2023). The DIT is widely regarded as a leading quantitative measure of moral development (King & Mayhew, 2002; van den Enden et al., 2019), enabling us to classify IS developers at the pre-conventional, conventional, or post-conventional levels of moral reasoning (e.g., Ho, 2025; Jean-Tron et al., 2022; Murrell, 2014). Concurrently, the FCM serves as a conceptual scaffold for examining how developers at these three levels actually engage in moral decision-making amid value conflicts (cf. Vartiainen, 2010b). Because no existing quantitative instrument fully captures all FCM dimensions (see Table 2) specifically within the ISD context, we added a qualitative component to complement the DIT-based classifications.

## 4.1 Empirical Context

We conducted our study at ManCorp (pseudonym), an Italy-based manufacturing and distribution firm whose annual revenues exceeded €1.45 billion in 2023. Software procurement at ManCorp relied on a centrally maintained digital catalog of preapproved products, manually overseen by the IT department. Although this system ensured compliance with corporate data security policies, it constrained employees’ ability to respond swiftly to evolving operational demands. Over time, these stringent procurement protocols contributed to the rise of shadow IT, whereby employees installed unauthorized software to meet urgent needs (Haag et al., 2022).

In response, ManCorp engaged external ISD teams to create new digital artifacts to replace the legacy catalog. This context required moral decision-making by IS developers to strike a balance between compliance with data security policies and employee autonomy and efficiency. Granting greater freedom in software installation could boost productivity but heighten data protection risks, whereas imposing stricter controls could improve security but reduce employee agency.

## 4.2 Sample and Data Collection

Our sample comprised 40 IS developers (aged 22-36, approximately one third identifying as women) and one senior manager from ManCorp, all based in central Europe. These individuals were organized into six teams of six or seven members each, consisting of project leaders (for coordination), technical specialists (for front-end/back-end tasks), and business-focused members (for requirements engineering and documentation). A senior manager supervised the project’s scope to ensure strategic alignment with corporate objectives and provided regular executivelevel feedback. Progress was monitored through iterative reviews and checkpoints, culminating in final presentations of the resulting digital artifacts to the organization’s senior executive board.

On the quantitative side, we administered the DIT to all 40 IS developers, using a scenario adapted from Myyry et al. (2009) that centers on employee compliance with organizational information security policies (see Appendix B). We chose this scenario because it closely mirrors the value conflict underlying shadow IT at ManCorp, wherein compliance with data security policies and employees’ needs for autonomy and efficiency were at stake. In addition, we evaluated each developer’s relational and technical knowledge (see Appendix A) to assess their potential impact on ISD projects and outcomes. On the qualitative side, we gathered more than 200 pages of project documentation, performed field observations, and conducted semistructured group interviews with all six teams. The group interviews explored participants’ moral decisionmaking, including how they construed shadow IT, managed key challenges, and reflected on their designed digital artifacts (see Appendix D). The interview with the senior manager offered an external stakeholder perspective on each team’s ISD interventions.

## 4.3 Mixed Methods Analysis

Consistent with mixed methods guidelines (Venkatesh et al., 2023), our study (see Figure 1) integrated quantitative and qualitative strands to generate metainferences that address our RQ. First, we used the adapted DIT (see Appendix B) to classify developers into Kohlberg’s (1969) three levels of moral development (see Table 1). This classification then served to systematically compare the qualitative data across the three levels (Greene, 2007). Specifically, we applied reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022) to qualitatively explore theoretical constructs— namely, the FCM dimensions (see Table 2) and their interrelationships. This integrative approach enabled us to investigate how pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional developers (as gauged by the adapted DIT) navigate the moral decision-making process (within the conceptual scaffolding of the FCM dimensions) amid value conflicts (see Appendix C).

After reviewing the interview transcripts, we performed open coding on relevant data pertaining to moral decision-making, such as how IS developers interpreted the value conflict concerning shadow IT. We then mapped these coded segments to the FCM dimensions. For instance, the moral motivation dimension centers on value hierarchies in decisionmaking (Rest et al., 1999). Hence, data excerpts illustrating scenarios in which client interests outweighed IS developers’ desires (or vice versa) were categorized under moral motivation.

Next, we derived themes by comparing the coded data with insights from the FCM and related literature (see Appendix C). Throughout this iterative process, we adhered to three criteria adapted from Braun and Clarke (2022): Each theme had to (1) capture the underlying meaning of the coded data, (2) correspond to the FCM dimensions (see Table 2), and (3) yield a coherent analytical account of IS developers’ moral decisionmaking when addressing value conflicts. For instance, we initially considered “willpower” (Curzer, 2014) as a candidate theme within the moral motivation dimension. However, this concept proved incompatible with other emerging themes across different FCM dimensions, prompting us to refine our analysis.

In addition, we sought to identify sub-themes that reflect patterns of moral decision-making across preconventional, conventional, and post-conventional IS developers. Inspired by Braun and Clarke’s (2022) methodological strategies, we organized and compared data extracts by moral development level, explored potential latent connections (as shown by dotted lines in Figure 1), and enriched our interpretations in light of relevant scholarship (see Appendix C). Notably, prior research on the DIT suggests that pre-conventional individuals prioritize self-interest, whereas those at the post-conventional level embrace higher-order principles in their moral reasoning (Rest & Narváez, 1994). Accordingly, we examined whether—and how—these modes of moral reasoning appeared in our derived themes grounded in the FCM dimensions.

Finally, we analyzed contradictory or unexpected cases in the data. For example, in certain instances, preconventional IS developers handled value conflicts at the interpersonal level arising from how to mitigate shadow IT, whereas their post-conventional counterparts resolved those disputes within their teams, ultimately influencing project outcomes (see Appendices C and D). By iteratively examining these varied moral decisionmaking approaches, we refined our thematic definitions, clarified their interrelationships, and revised the associated sub-themes.

## 5 Findings

We found diverse moral decision-making patterns, organized around four interrelated themes (see Table 3): (1) problem framing, (2) solution conceptualization, (3) responsibility orientation, and (4) duty fulfillment. Our analysis further highlights the pivotal role of IS developers’ responsibility orientation in guiding moral decision-making amid value conflicts.

In framing problems, this orientation surfaces in how IS developers attribute causal responsibility (e.g., assigning blame). These initial framing choices subsequently inform solution conceptualizations (e.g., envisioning design configurations). As developers consider potential solutions, their responsibility orientation also emerges in how accountability is allocated (e.g., taking ownership) for the ISD process and its long-term ramifications. Ultimately, these solutions are operationalized through duty fulfillment. In performing their duties, developers’ responsibility orientation is evident in how they balance obligations (e.g., weighing personal commitments against the directives of peers or external stakeholders). We identified three distinct responsibility orientations, each corresponding to a different moral development level:

1. Pre-conventional IS developers exhibit a diffuse responsibility orientation.

2. Conventional IS developers display a harmonious responsibility orientation.

3. Post-conventional IS developers demonstrate an integrative responsibility orientation.

Section 5.1 illustrates how IS developers at each moral development level approached the value conflict surrounding shadow IT. In Section 5.2, we describe how they addressed value conflicts at the interpersonal level that arose from divergent moral decision-making approaches to mitigate shadow IT. Finally, Section 5.3 examines how these conflicts influenced ISD project outcomes. For clarity and consistency, we label each project leader as P1, each technical specialist as T1-T3, and each business-focused member as B1-B4. We append -PreC, -C, or -PostC to indicate preconventional, conventional, or post-conventional levels, respectively.

## 5.1 Moral Decision-Making Patterns in Navigating the Value Conflict Related to Shadow IT

Next, we illustrate how pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional IS developers approached the shadow IT value conflict, wherein compliance with organizational data security policies and employees needs for autonomy and efficiency were at stake.

## 5.1.1 Pre-Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Pre-conventional IS developers commonly used a reductionistic approach to problem framing, dissecting shadow IT into simpler, more fundamental components. From a technical perspective, they often attributed the proliferation of shadow IT to organizational inefficiencies in the legacy catalog, such as protracted software procurement processes or poorly timed upgrades. From a social perspective, some preconventional developers attributed shadow IT’s prevalence to ambiguous institutional guidelines or inadequate employee awareness.

In their problem framing, these IS developers’ diffuse responsibility orientation became evident through their selective assignment of blame. They frequently attributed causal responsibility to powerful stakeholders, particularly upper management, citing inadequate employee training or lax policy enforcement. As G1T3‐PreC noted, IT departments were also culpable because they are “so rigid” that they compel employees to “find workarounds.” Conversely, some pre‐conventional IS developers faulted end users for disregarding organizational rules. Similarly, G3B3‐ PreC described shadow IT as an inevitable aspect of human nature, suggesting that strict rule adherence is “quite boring,” thereby fostering noncompliance.

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by their problem framing that reduced shadow IT to discrete factors while attributing causal responsibility to a narrow set of stakeholders, pre-conventional IS developers frequently conceptualized expedient solutions targeting these presumed root causes. When IT departments were deemed responsible for shadow IT, some suggested granting employees greater administrative access. Conversely, if employees were considered the main source of the problem, some preconventional developers advocated stricter IT governance by curtailing user autonomy. For instance, G2B4-PreC supported enforcing sanctioned software usage. Extending this logic, G6T1-PreC and G4B3- PreC envisioned tools to detect and deter unauthorized installations:

I think the most efficient solution would be quite drastic, but it would probably be some kind of software that runs in the background and immediately detects when an employee uses a tool or software that isn’t in the catalog. Then some sort of pop-up would appear, saying, “Hey, be careful. This tool isn’t allowed to be used. Please contact IT,” or something like that. … The employees would probably feel very uncomfortable because they’d immediately feel like they were being watched. I’m not even sure if that would be feasible from a data protection standpoint. But in terms of combating shadow IT, it would certainly be very efficient. (G4B3‐PreC)

By attributing the conflict in shadow IT largely to employee compliance, G4B3‐PreC supported a digital monitoring system to enforce obedience. Although several pre‐conventional IS developers recognized potential adverse consequences (e.g., privacy breaches, employee discomfort), they nevertheless regarded the ends as justifying the means to expedite the resolution of the core value conflict. Guided by their diffuse responsibility orientation, they assumed ownership of the ISD lifecycle yet effectively disclaimed accountability for the project’s long‐term consequences (e.g., erosion of employee agency) resulting from the deployment of their envisioned artifacts.

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Because pre-conventional IS developers typically adopted a pragmatic stance toward duty fulfillment, their initial expedient solutions were often revised to accommodate emerging constraints before final operationalization. For example, G6T1‐PreC noted that they would “wisely focus only on what’s realistic” and consider expanding the scope of their envisioned digital artifact only if time allowed. In Team 6, both formal and informal leaders streamlined collective decision‐making by consulting senior management to “let him prioritize” (G6B3‐PreC) their contemplated solutions and “derive tasks” (G6P1‐PreC). Likewise, G5T2-PreC demonstrated a willingness to delegate specific responsibilities to peers with greater expertise: “Oh yeah, I’m the business expert … I’m trying like to help G5T1- C … like scheduling, managing, but mostly G5T1-C is responsible for that, and I’m just trying to help him”

In fulfilling their duties pragmatically, pre-conventional IS developers prioritized project completion as their core obligation, potentially undermining behavioral integrity in two key ways. First, their envisioned solutions often underwent pragmatic revisions prior to operationalization, indicating a limited commitment to personal ideals. Second, they often fulfilled roleassociated duties in a deferential manner. In particular, pre‐conventional project leaders often delegated decision-making authority to peers or influential stakeholders to sustain—and ideally accelerate—project progress. For instance, G6T1‐PreC relied heavily on senior management’s directives to steer Team 6’s project trajectory, while G4P1‐PreC adopted a supportive leadership style intended to leverage team members’ unique expertise. Likewise, G2P1‐PreC professed a desire “to always be involved and know what’s going on,” yet rarely exercised direct oversight, instead primarily serving as a facilitator.

## 5.1.2 Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Conventional IS developers typically framed problems in localized terms, remaining alert and receptive to established and emergent intra‐project and extra‐project contingencies. Within their team environment, they commonly absorbed and echoed their peers’ perspectives on the origins of conflict. Externally, they also considered the viewpoints of external stakeholders and explicit organizational norms, such as formal policies, when interpreting shadow IT.

Unlike their pre‐conventional counterparts, conventional IS developers seldom asserted personal convictions when identifying causal responsibility. Guided by a harmonious responsibility orientation, they instead leaned toward examining how both pre‐ and post‐conventional teammates framed the problem space to see whom or what they held responsible. Concurrently, conventional IS developers incorporated insights from external stakeholders and organizational norms to identify transgressions and ascertain who or what was responsible for shadow IT’s extensive presence.

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by their localized approach to problem framing, conventional IS developers conceptualized solutions that reflected the expectations of their teammates as well as external stakeholders. Rather than merely following instructions, they often synthesized and refined them adaptively. For example, G4B1-C recalibrated a solution proposed by organizational actors, grounding its requirements in the organization’s ethos while striving for end user inclusivity:

I think one of the most important requirements of the senior manager was that the digital artifact be easy to use. So that not only those who, let’s say, have a bit more understanding of IT or such applications can use the tool but also those who are less familiar with it and only need to use it occasionally. I think it's very important that it is clearly organized and that it’s not difficult to learn or adopt the tool, but that you can understand it as soon as you use it.

However, this adaptable position occasionally led them to place external dictates above their own moral judgments. In Team 5, for example, G5T1-C expressed concern about a surveillance-based digital artifact but still defended it by appealing to the organization’s formal rules or policies:

No, you can detect it [shadow IT activities], but you also have to consider the ethical side. I mean, if it is stated [in] the … [organizational] rules or policies, then I would do so. … I would still do so because I am told to do so …, but ethically, I believe it’s wrong. (G5T1-C)

This adaptable stance illustrated conventional IS developers’ harmonious responsibility orientation, wherein accountability encompassed both internal team considerations and extra-project mandates. In contrast to pre-conventional IS developers, who tended to confine accountability to the ISD lifecycle, conventional developers accepted ownership of the development process as well as the long-term impacts of their artifacts. However, this sense of accountability largely arose from reactive compliance with prevailing norms rather than proactive personal moral agency.

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Although conventional IS developers adapted their solutions to both intra‐ and extra‐project instructions, they generally fulfilled their duties by conforming to team directives. Whereas their pre‐conventional counterparts maintained a pragmatic stance toward both internal and external dynamics, conventional IS developers displayed notable loyalty to colleagues in close physical proximity. This conformist inclination occasionally led them to take on responsibilities beyond their formal roles. For instance, G1B2‐C sought clarity regarding her duties and proceeded to undertake additional UX design tasks, as described by G1P1‐PostC:

I was already somehow in the middle and tried to organize it [the team meeting]. … Here, we also assigned G1B2-C for somehow the aesthetics of the project. The UX design expert, somehow, and that she is most responsible for the mock-ups, for example. (G1P1-PostC)

Unlike their pre-conventional counterparts, who tended to prioritize project closure, conventional IS developers, guided by their harmonious responsibility orientation, committed primarily to meeting proximal team obligations. Even in formal leadership roles, this conformity to team demands prevailed. In Team 5, for example, G5T1-C relied heavily on teammates’ guidance despite serving as technical lead. Similarly, G2T2-C described their role in Team 2’s technical division as “support,” even though they held an equivalent authority level to that of their peers.

## 5.1.3 Post-Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Contrary to preconventional developers, who often reduced shadow IT to discrete elements, post-conventional IS developers frequently adopted a structural perspective that highlighted the overarching social and technical forces shaping the problem space. For example, after consulting external stakeholders, G2T1‐PostC characterized shadow IT as an informally tolerated practice embedded in ManCorp’s culture, while G3T2‐PostC directed attention to the dynamic interplay of social (e.g., awareness) and technical factors (e.g., software procurement processes).

Consistent with their integrative responsibility orientation, post‐conventional developers generally avoided attributing blame to any single group in their problem framing. Instead, they underscored collective causal responsibility among all involved stakeholders, each potentially involved in the rise and expansion of shadow IT. As G5P1‐PostC explained:

Well, I think it depends. If the company provides tools to complete certain tasks and an employee still uses shadow IT, that’s the wrong of the employee. But if the company fails to provide adequate resources, then it’s for sure the company’s fault. (G5P1-PostC)

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by a structural problem-framing view that recognized broader social and technical factors and collective causal responsibility across multiple stakeholders, postconventional IS developers commonly rejected solutions that might benefit one group at the expense of others. Indeed, G3T1‐PostC strongly opposed digital artifacts that facilitated surveillance to penalize employee transgressions, cautioning that “such coercive instruments would result in a strained and pressured relationship, which should rather be overcome by establishing mutual understanding.”

Although G5B4‐PostC acknowledged that digital monitoring could be justified if it genuinely served the collective good, this perspective diverged from the expediency-driven approach of pre-conventional developers and the adaptive stance of conventional developers. Instead, post‐conventional IS developers consistently prioritized reciprocity in their solutions. For example, G5B3‐PostC stressed that artifacts should improve both organizational processes and employees’ daily workflows. Echoing this reciprocitydriven philosophy, G1P1‐PostC remarked:

I think … shadow IT is a social dilemma or a process problem. … Technology is a great tool to overcome this problem. So, we somehow use current technologies to create better processes and better social interactions to overcome this issue. … You have to define how people work with each other, and if everyone tries to figure out their way or how they would like to do that, it wouldn’t work. Of course, shadow IT, in my opinion, would be a consequence.

By interpreting shadow IT as symptomatic of broader organizational structures, G1P1‐PostC championed solutions that offered reciprocal benefits to all stakeholders, reflecting an integrative responsibility orientation. Moving beyond the immediate ISD lifecycle, G1P1‐PostC assumed responsibility for ManCorp’s long‐term welfare and encouraged other stakeholders to embrace a similarly expansive sense of accountability (“if everyone tries to figure out their way or how they would like to do that, it wouldn’t work”). Likewise, other post-conventional developers proposed inclusivity‐focused initiatives (e.g., workshops) to complement the rollout of digital artifacts.

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Unlike pre-conventional IS developers, who tended to favor pragmatic role execution, post-conventional developers demonstrated strong behavioral integrity in their day-to-day work. Such integrity was evidenced in resolutely fulfilling their duties to operationalize reciprocity‐based solutions aimed at tackling perceived structural problems underlying shadow IT. For instance, G2B3‐PostC endeavored to accommodate the needs and interests of multiple stakeholder groups, insisting that “attention to detail … differentiates a very successful piece of software from a normal software.” Elaborating on their reciprocity-based design ethos, G2B3‐PostC explained:

I do a lot of sketches. I do a lot of prototyping in an ideal scenario. I show the prototypes. I rate each prototype, what is good about it, and what is bad about it, and then I kind of merge the best ideas together and sort out the not-so-good ideas. Brainstorm about each feature: What is missing? What may the user like? What may they not like? What may the client not like? So, details are referring to everything. The client, the user, it should be a holistic view, I think.

Within Team 3’s technical division, post‐conventional members G3T1‐PostC and G3T2‐PostC likewise collaborated to navigate ManCorp’s infrastructure constraints and deliver a digital artifact that provided mutual benefits. As G3T2‐PostC noted, their primary objective was “to ensure users can understand and navigate the designed digital artifact within just a few minutes, or ideally immediately, so they can use it right away.” When post‐conventional IS developers assumed leadership roles, they demonstrated notable resolve in fulfilling their duties. Whereas pre‐conventional project leaders typically deferred to others, G1P1‐PostC and G5P1‐PostC steered collective decision‐making through formal task delegation, structured meetings, and clearly defined milestones, thereby unifying their teams around reciprocity-driven objectives.

Such resolute behavioral integrity illustrated that postconventional IS developers felt primarily obliged to operationalize solutions rooted in the principle of reciprocity. Nevertheless, this approach to fulfilling duties, guided by an integrative responsibility orientation, revealed two key drawbacks. First, it was often less practical than the pragmatic stance of preconventional developers. Indeed, G2B3-PostC conceded that their design principles would succeed only under “ideal” conditions, and G3T2-PostC acknowledged that their proposed solution might not be fully feasible unless circumstances were optimal. Second, their resolute commitment to reciprocity occasionally produced more authoritarian team dynamics. Although Teams 1 and 5 employed agile methodologies, their post-conventional project leaders implemented hierarchical structures more extensively than their pre-conventional counterparts in Teams 2, 3, 4, and 6.

## 5.2 Moral Decision-Making Patterns in Navigating Interpersonal Conflicts

As the ISD projects advanced, we found that divergent moral decision-making approaches regarding the mitigation of shadow IT often triggered interpersonal conflicts. For instance, while pre-conventional IS developers often conceptualized expedient solutions operationalized through pragmatic duty fulfillment, their post-conventional counterparts resolutely pursued reciprocity-focused solutions. In the following section, we illustrate how the patterns introduced in Section 5.1 played out as developers navigated these value conflicts at the interpersonal level.

## 5.2.1 Pre-Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Pre-conventional IS developers typically reduced interpersonal conflicts to a seemingly irreconcilable state. For example, G1T3-PreC initially sought to implement advanced AI-driven features—such as facial and voice recognition—into their digital artifact. When these ambitions collided with the senior manager’s vision, G1T3-PreC labeled him as an “operational kind of a guy,” implying he was incapable of “thinking in terms of innovation.” In a parallel instance, G3T3-PreC attributed Team 3’s friction to the manager’s presumed lack of expertise. Reflecting their diffuse responsibility orientation, pre-conventional IS developers tended either to internalize or deflect blame for interpersonal conflicts. In one instance, G1T3-PreC, sensing a misalignment with the senior manager, retrospectively labeled their initial proposals as “stupid”:

After the first meeting, and before that, I had other ideas. … My idea of simplicity was different … By the first meeting, I’d made a mock-up of a chatbot but realized that’s not what they were looking for … thinking back, it was a stupid idea. (G1T3-PreC)

More commonly, however, pre-conventional developers shifted blame outward. In Teams 2, 3, and 6, for instance, they ascribed impediments to the senior manager’s demands and constraints.

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by their tendency to reduce conflicts to a seemingly intractable state—while either internalizing or deflecting blame—pre‐conventional IS developers often opted for expedient solutions to preserve project momentum. In Teams 2 and 4, for example, they voiced concerns over limited resources but chose to work within organizational constraints rather than challenge senior management. Likewise, in Team 6, differing interpretations of high‐level requirements created friction among pre‐conventional members, yet no one sought additional clarification:

So first, we intensively discussed how everyone understood the client’s requirements. And there, the opinions diverged. So, if I may say so, G6P1-PreC, you were quite agitated at the beginning whereas I saw it as a bit more relaxed. (G6B3-PreC)

So maybe to put it in more concrete terms. So, I just looked completely at the customer side and at the customer requirements. …. And regardless of whether it was realistic or unrealistic, … I had already painted a huge picture. And then I was brought back down to earth by the team, so to speak, which was also important. But I was fighting with that for a while. (G6P1-PreC)

Their reluctance to engage openly with disagreements typified the diffuse responsibility orientation of preconventional IS developers. Eager to expedite progress, they preferred the path of least resistance and, accordingly, frequently relinquished ownership of pivotal decisions—such as design specifications—to powerful actors like senior management. This preference for expediency confined accountability to the immediate ISD lifecycle, while essentially placing the long-term organizational consequences of their decisions outside their sphere of responsibility.

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Although the expedient decision-making of pre-conventional IS developers periodically eased immediate tensions, such straightforward solutions often introduced further complications, especially when team priorities diverged. In response, these developers tended to discharge their duties pragmatically by deferring to influential stakeholders. For example, in Team 1, G1T3-PreC initially sought to accommodate the senior manager’s proposals; however, the project leader, G1P1-PostC, used formal authority to refocus internal collaboration, prompting G1T3-PreC to embrace the team’s collective vision. A similar situation occurred in Team 2: Preconventional developers clashed with the senior manager’s expectations but ultimately proceeded pragmatically with a solution largely designed by a postconventional technical division member.

These pragmatic compromises periodically allowed preconventional IS developers to sidestep impending project stagnation. In Team 4, for instance, when the senior manager introduced a late-stage request for a chatbot, the pre-conventional technical lead incorporated features enabling future chatbot expansion rather than abandoning the original concept. In some cases, however, these concessions devolved into subservience to dominant external stakeholders. For example, G3T3-PreC, who led the technical division in Team 3, followed the senior manager’s directives over the objections of post-conventional colleagues, citing time constraints:

We’ve already had this discussion. … Let’s put it this way: of course, you always come to a common ground, you must. But a lot of time was spent having this discussion. … I’m not sure whether the senior manager really understands how much maintenance effort will be required to make the tool truly functional … because I don’t think [the senior manager] is aware of what’s coming, and second, because he never listed that as a requirement. … But currently, it’s not our problem, and it’s not within the project’s scope. (G3T3-PreC)

In pragmatically fulfilling their duties, pre-conventional developers again exhibited relatively low behavioral integrity. Reflecting their diffuse responsibility orientation, they treated timely deliverables as their main obligation, operationalizing whichever instructions promised rapid progress, while devoting limited attention to the nature of each decision (e.g., specifications of design solutions) and its long-term repercussions. Although their pragmatic stance alleviated immediate bottlenecks, its benefits at times proved short‐lived, necessitating additional changes. In Team 6, for instance, pre-conventional developers initially entertained the senior manager’s chatbot request; however, this solution deviated from the team’s priorities, prompting another pragmatic shift toward a viable design configuration based on “what was feasible” (G6T1-PreC).

## 5.2.2 Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Conventional IS developers displayed a marked aversion to interpersonal conflict, yet they frequently grappled with disputes extending across intra- and extra-project boundaries. In such situations, they tended to frame problems in localized terms, focusing primarily on near-term team concerns rather than broader organizational or contextual factors. Unlike post-conventional developers, who were more willing to challenge teammates openly, conventional IS developers generally avoided criticizing colleagues within the immediate group. For instance, G2T2-C acknowledged limitations in G2T1-PostC’s development trajectory but ascribed these shortcomings to stringent deadlines and other external constraints. In some cases, conventional IS developers reinforced teammates’ views that shifted causal responsibility to external actors:

Yes, what G4B3-PreC just mentioned also surprised me quite a bit—that they had actually identified a problem … but communicated it poorly or not at all to the employees. … What I feel is a problem is that the senior manager is mostly, or almost only, focused on the technical functionality of the solution. … And I believe that no matter how good the solution is, if it isn’t properly communicated to employees, it won’t be very effective. (G4B1-C)

Consistent with their harmonious responsibility orientation, conventional developers typically aspired to maintain cordial team dynamics and thus avoided instigating conflicts. When disagreements did arise, they gravitated toward deflecting causal responsibility outward, including endorsing critiques that pinned faults on external actors or conditions.

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by their localized approach to problem framing, conventional IS developers usually opted not to mediate disputes—even amid opportunities to offer their judgments. Across Teams 1 and 3, for example, G1B2-C and G3B3-C chose not to intervene in tensions between pre- and post-conventional teammates. Guided by their harmonious responsibility orientation, they instead adapted to the solutions crystallized through team consensus.

Consequently, conventional IS developers exhibited a reactive rather than proactive stance toward accountability. This approach shielded them from initiating or amplifying conflicts, but it also introduced two drawbacks. First, resembling their pre-conventional counterparts, they rarely asserted their personal convictions in critical decisions—such as the finer details regarding design solutions—to preserve team cohesion. Second, they commonly relied on others, even when their formal roles called for more decisive ownership. A pertinent example arose in Team 5, where its members struggled to converge on a shared project vision. Although G5T1-C was formally responsible for technical leadership, they hesitated to take control and accept accountability. Only after an informal discussion led by G5P1-PostC did G5T1-C assume ownership of Team 5’s digital artifact development process:

I mean, looking at shadow IT and providing a solution through IS artifact is quite— I couldn’t merge the two concepts together. So, yeah, I was just like, what are we going to implement this time? (G5T1-C)

The solution that we are developing right now actually came out just the first walk pretty much that I went on with G5T1-C. And okay, let’s do this. … And then the next meeting, … we proposed that solution to the group, and then everyone seemed to agree. (G5P1-PostC)

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Conventional IS developers did more than adapt solutions to meet team needs; they also executed colleagues’ proposed solutions over other options. This tendency toward conformity appeared in two key ways. First, they often performed role-specific tasks under direct peer supervision. In Team 1, for instance, an extra-project conflict was largely resolved through G1P1-PostC’s intervention, leading G1B2-C to adhere to the project leader’s guidance rather than engage external stakeholders. Second, conventional developers followed peers who effectively served as proxies for external authorities. In Team 3, for example, G3B3-C pursued a solution championed by G3T3- PreC—even though it ultimately originated from senior management’s directives.

In each scenario, conventional IS developers primarily understood their core obligation as reducing dissonance and friction with close collaborators. Consistent with their harmonious responsibility orientation, they consequently conformed to team directives in fulfilling their duties, while essentially relegating personal convictions and external instructions to the periphery.

## 5.2.3 Post-Conventional IS Developers’ Moral Decision-Making Patterns

Problem framing and responsibility orientation (causal responsibility attribution): Motivated by their reciprocity-based ideals, post-conventional IS developers often triggered interpersonal conflict by critiquing what they perceived as structural limitations in others’ viewpoints. For example, G1P1-PostC cautioned that the senior manager’s suggestions, including the proposed solution, might be overly superficial and urged Team 1 members to remain cautious before committing to any concrete design configuration. Similarly, in Team 3, G3T1-PostC contested the technical lead’s decision to comply with external directives regarding how the digital artifact they were creating would interface with the organization’s existing infrastructure:

Some parts of the discussion were initiated by me because, when it comes to choosing the environment for development, I always think that it needs to fit the company. And the senior manager completely ignored that. … Still, I found it quite important. In other words, it should somehow work within the company’s environment, with the systems they use, and by the people working there, who will probably use the technologies already in use elsewhere in the company, so it should also be maintained and used by them. (G3T1-PostC)

Rather than assigning blame to any one party, postconventional developers often used a confrontational stance to foster a sense of collective causal responsibility. Upon noticing what they viewed as an overemphasis on short-term expedience or other structural shortcomings, they encouraged all involved stakeholders to acknowledge the downstream impacts of their decisionmaking both within and beyond the ISD lifecycle.

Solution conceptualization and responsibility orientation (accountability allocation): Informed by their structural problem-framing perspective, post-conventional IS developers actively facilitated open, critical dialogue among diverse stakeholders, steering discussions toward a reciprocal consensus regarding the optimal path forward. G3T1‐PostC’s remarks mentioned above exemplified this stance, and similar dynamics emerged in other teams. In Team 5, for instance, the post‐conventional project leader initiated informal “walking discussions” with the technical lead to craft a solution that addressed employee needs, met organizational requirements, and gained team-wide approval. A parallel situation arose in Team 1 when a preconventional member seemed inclined to accept the senior manager’s proposals without careful scrutiny. Noting this potential oversimplification, G1T1-PostC advocated for critical discussions to formulate solutions based on reciprocal consensus:

And I tried, in this case, to say to everything and somehow make it clear that when we’re thinking of mock-ups or any ideas, we shouldn’t just focus on a clear technology, for example, a chatbot, because we should be open-minded to that point that we are bringing all the ideas we got of seven persons together and then we are evaluating about the clear solution.

Guided by their integrative responsibility orientation, post-conventional developers proactively assumed accountability for urgent structural issues—both within and beyond the project lifecycle—while encouraging others to share ownership of the artifact’s creation and its subsequent ramifications. While this approach promoted transparency and opened space for reciprocal consensus, their critical questioning occasionally disrupted team cohesion.

Duty fulfillment and responsibility orientation (balancing obligations): Undeterred by obstacles, postconventional IS developers resolutely fulfilled their duties in pursuit of reciprocity-based solutions. For instance, in Team 2, pre-conventional IS developers and the senior manager clashed over resource constraints, whereas G2T1-PostC steered key design decisions to ensure the resulting artifact would meet administrators’ and end users’ needs. Likewise, G3T1-PostC and G3T2-PostC continued to prioritize optimal usability to achieve reciprocal benefits, even while acknowledging limitations in their designed artifacts:

I think our current software product cannot fully combat and reduce [shadow IT]. That’ why I think our goal should really be to focus on our software product and to achieve a very high level of usability. But in the sense that a many users use this software. And if we achieve that, that would definitely be a great success. (G3T2-PostC)

When post-conventional developers assumed formal leadership roles, they exhibited strong resolve in carrying out their duties. In Team 5, for instance, G5P1-PostC dispelled early uncertainties by stating that obstacles “shouldn’t be too difficult” and by directing pivotal design decisions, setting regular meetings, and leveraging formal authority to “speed up tasks.” In some cases, their resolute behavioral integrity elicited resistance to higherlevel directives. Dissatisfied with the senior manager’s viewpoint, G1P1-PostC consulted another employee— without explicit approval—to gain additional insight into shadow IT’s structural issues:

I was lucky to know another employee at [ManCorp]. And therefore, I tried to somehow interview him and ask him some questions about how he perceives shadow IT. ... And my first impression after this talk to him, I saw it differently afterward. (G1P1- PostC)

In resolutely fulfilling their duties, post-conventional developers treated their principal obligation as achieving reciprocity-based solutions to address structural issues— even if doing so required challenging others’ viewpoints, regardless of hierarchical rank. However, this resolute behavioral integrity sometimes interfered with role-based duties (e.g., adhering to established procedures). Such tension emerged in G1P1-PostC’s unapproved outreach to another employee and in G5P1-PostC’s leadership style, which cultivated a more vertical structure to manage Team 5’s early struggles to coalesce around a unified vision for mitigating shadow IT.

## 5.3 Influence of Interpersonal Conflict Management on Project Outcomes

Because conventional IS developers, guided by a harmonious responsibility orientation, tended to conform to team expectations, conflict management was primarily handled by pre-conventional or postconventional developers across the six teams. Consequently, the way these developers handled conflict substantially influenced ISD project outcomes (see Appendix D).

## 5.3.1 Influence of Conflict Management by Pre-Conventional IS Developers

Team 6 encountered interpersonal conflict when the senior manager’s proposal clashed with G6P1-PreC’s vision. Rather than seeking clarification, G6P1-PreC opted for an expedient approach by relinquishing control, which spiraled into further conflict with G6B3-PreC over the manager’s requirements. In response, these preconventional IS developers initially adopted the senior manager’s chatbot concept without critical examination, but this approach proved impractical and was eventually “dropped” (G6T1-PreC). Following another abrupt pivot, Team 6 delivered a minimum viable digital artifact—a basic web-based search platform lacking essential features (e.g., a built-in user-feedback channel). Owing to its reduced scope, G6P1-PreC anticipated the potential for a “180-degree turn” in the artifact’s design. Nevertheless, ManCorp’s senior management ultimately deemed Team 6’s project unsuccessful.

Team 6’s trajectory illustrates how a diffuse responsibility orientation can generate cascading negative effects on collective decision-making. By attributing project hurdles solely to the senior manager—and avoiding accountability by deferring excessively to external mandates—key pre-conventional members, notably the project leader, construed their primary obligation as merely completing the project. This dependency on external directives undermined the team’s unified vision, leading to multiple pragmatic pivots that culminated in a functionally limited artifact.

Team 4 experienced a similar conflict when the senior manager requested a chatbot feature. The team’s preconventional technical lead reacted pragmatically by modifying the existing app store solution to allow future chatbot integration, rather than discarding the concept they had designed entirely. Notably, this conflict surfaced only after Team 4 had established a cohesive concept for its app store, enabling members to reconcile the senior manager’s request with the team’s original plan. The resulting digital artifact offered scalability (including potential chatbot integration), flexibility, and proactive notifications for administrators, while providing end users with advanced search and filtering, software requests, app organization, and social sharing features.

Team 4’s handling of interpersonal conflict stemmed from interventions by pre‑conventional IS developers, particularly the project leader’s facilitative leadership. Guided by a diffuse responsibility orientation, G4P1- PreC prioritized expedient decision-making by granting the team considerable autonomy. Within this decentralized structure, Team 4 formed a collective vision before the conflict emerged. By reaching common ground early, Team 4’s members, particularly the pre-conventional technical lead, had sufficient leeway to evaluate the senior manager’s requests in light of their agreed-upon concept instead of discarding the existing app store. This case shows how preconventional IS developers can successfully navigate conflicts through expedient solution conceptualizations, provided external directives are pragmatically evaluated against internal considerations to preserve mutual understanding and commitment.

Team 3 grappled with a conflict between G3T1-PostC and G3T3-PreC. G3T1-PostC advocated a userfriendly, maintainable solution, rooted in a reciprocitybased ideal that ran counter to organizational demands and constraints. In contrast, G3T3-PreC prioritized project completion and thus followed the senior manager’s suggestions, despite harboring doubts about their feasibility. Although Team 3’s resulting chatbot featured basic user-engagement functions, it lacked key features (e.g., feedback mechanisms). When questioned about the artifact’s utility, the team expressed skepticism about its ability to combat shadow IT. Indeed, G3T3-PreC refused accountability for these shortcomings, instead attributing blame to “a stubborn manager who has not been trained accordingly or whatever.” Nonetheless, senior management deemed the project unsuccessful, notwithstanding Team 3’s efforts to accommodate their directives.

Team 3’s experience highlights tensions between differing moral decision-making approaches. Guided by an integrative responsibility orientation, G3T1-PostC’s resolute commitment to reciprocity conflicted with G3T3-PreC’s pragmatic mindset, rooted in a diffuse responsibility orientation. Acting on behalf of senior management, G3T3‐PreC followed the manager’s directives, thereby fracturing commitment within the technical division. Like Team 6, this case underscores how excessive reliance on external directives—while dismissing internal considerations—can erode shared understanding and ultimately jeopardize project success.

## 5.3.2 Influence of Conflict Management by Post-Conventional IS Developers

In Team 2, a conflict arose between pre-conventional members and external stakeholders over restricted access to end user data. Concurrently, G2T1-PostC resolutely guided key design decisions within these constraints. As the project progressed, G2P1-PreC adopted a facilitative leadership style, expediting the operationalization of G2T1-PostC’s artifact by allocating resources pragmatically. With additional support from other team members, including G2T2-C, who conformed to team directives, Team 2 maintained a shared understanding and collective commitment to advancing G2T1-PostC’s concept. The outcome was a digital search engine that provided reciprocal benefits: end users obtained advanced search and filtering capabilities for approved software, along with the option to request unavailable tools; administrators gained analytics features and catalog updates that improved relevance and user insights. This solution inspired confidence within Team 2 and garnered positive feedback from senior management.

Team 2’s experience demonstrates the synergistic interplay among varying moral decision-making approaches, driven by distinct responsibility orientations, thereby leading to a successful project outcome. Consistent with an integrative responsibility orientation,

G2T1-PostC devised a reciprocity-based solution intended to reconcile the pluralistic interests of end users and administrators. In addition, pre-conventional members’ diffuse responsibility orientation was reflected in the expedient avoidance of direct confrontation with external stakeholders and in their pragmatic pursuit of G2T1-PostC’s design. Finally, G2T2-C, who exhibited a harmonious responsibility orientation, fulfilled duties in a conformist manner by supporting Team 2’s plan to operationalize G2T1-PostC’s proposed solution.

Conversely, Team 1 experienced a three-way clash of divergent moral decision-making approaches. At the core of this conflict was a discussion with the senior manager, who proposed a chatbot and urged simplicity in artifact design. G1T3-PreC was ready to hand ownership of pivotal design decisions to the senior manager, and G1B2-C similarly endorsed these upper-level suggestions. In contrast, G1P1-PostC declined to adopt directives merely because influential external stakeholders promoted them. Exercising formal authority, G1P1-PostC launched reciprocity-based discussions while seeking external input from another employee. Ultimately, these efforts led Team 1 to abandon the chatbot concept in favor of a digital search engine emphasizing user experience through application discovery, management, advanced filtering, and software requests. The project was deemed successful by both internal and external stakeholders.

Like Team 2, Team 1 shows how varying moral decisionmaking approaches, underpinned by distinct responsibility orientations, can operate productively together. In this case, however, synergy emerged only after the post-conventional project leader, guided by an integrative responsibility orientation, asserted formal authority to steer the team. Despite Team 1’s success, this example highlights how post-conventional IS developers’ resolute commitment to reciprocity-based goals may temporarily disrupt team rapport and occasionally elicit unsanctioned actions (e.g., G1P1-PostC contacting stakeholders without formal approval).

Mirroring Team 2’s experience, the post-conventional project leader of Team 5, G5P1-PostC, established a more vertical structure—despite ostensibly adhering to agile principles—by resolutely fulfilling leadership duties. This included setting a structured weekly schedule, coordinating supplemental meetings, and hosting informal gatherings (e.g., “walking discussions”) to forge a unified vision for addressing shadow IT. Central to Team 5’s challenges was the technical lead G5T1-C’s early reluctance to assume ownership of pivotal design decisions. Through these informal dialogues, the team ultimately converged on a digital artifact featuring filtering options, a catalog of approved products, a request channel for additional tools, and a ticket system for user feedback. In addition, administrators gained analytics and modification capabilities. Compared with Teams 2 and 4, however, Team 5’s artifact did not incorporate advanced communication or social collaboration features. As a result, senior management did not view the project as successful, and Team 5’s members held mixed opinions on their designed artifact.

Team 5’s experience indicates the potential downsides of intersections among diverse moral decision-making approaches. In this scenario, the technical lead’s harmonious responsibility orientation was evident in an overreliance on team directives, despite holding a formal leadership role. Indeed, the post-conventional project leader may have intensified this dynamic through resolute interventions aimed at fostering reciprocal solutions, reflecting their integrative responsibility orientation. This dynamic could explain why the conventional technical lead took accountability only in a reactive manner, rather than proactively guiding crucial design decisions. As a result, the final artifact strongly reflected the postconventional project leader’s guiding hand—potentially to an undue degree—notwithstanding their attempts to foster shared ownership of the project.

## 6 Discussion

Based on our findings, we propose a theoretical framework (see Figure 2) that illustrates how IS developers’ moral development manifests in diverse patterns (see Table 4) across four interrelated decisionmaking themes: (1) problem framing, (2) solution conceptualization, (3) responsibility orientation, and (4) duty fulfillment. These themes and patterns capture IS developers’ dominant modes of moral reasoning, as indicated by our adapted DIT, and show how these modes translate into actual moral decision‐making, mapped onto the FCM’s dimensions. Prior research supports this linkage: DIT‐based measures of moral reasoning frequently correlate with characteristic decision‐making patterns (Banerjee et al., 1998; Ho, 2025; Myyry et al., 2009; Rest & Narváez, 1994), tap into the FCM’s moral judgment dimension (Bebeau, 2002), and are associated with moral sensitivity and moral motivation (Morton et al., 2006; Myyry et al., 2010).

At its core, our framework indicates that value conflicts— exemplified by shadow IT and related interpersonal disputes—shape IS developers’ moral decision‐making, especially through their responsibility orientation. The salience of this orientation aligns with the FCM, where moral motivation involves prioritizing values (Rest et al., 1999)—for example, assuming responsibility for decision-making when organizational mandates must be reconciled with competing stakeholder values (Bebeau, 2002). Prior research similarly underscores developers’ sense of responsibility in ISD, whether in handling morally ambiguous situations (Boland & Day, 1989; Chatterjee et al., 2009; Vaast, 2025), reconciling broader obligations with role-based duties (cf. Anderson et al., 1993; Lobschat et al., 2021; Walsham, 1996), or determining accountability for both the design process (e.g., AI systems) and its outcomes (Schmidt et al., 2025).

Expanding on these insights, our framework delineates a multifaceted conceptualization of developers responsibility orientation, comprising how they (1) attribute causal responsibility in framing problems, (2) allocate accountability in conceptualizing solutions, and (3) balance obligations in fulfilling duties. For example, pre-conventional IS developers, guided by a diffuse orientation, often used a reductionistic approach to framing problems, wherein they attributed causal responsibility to specific stakeholder groups (e.g., management or end users). Hence, this approach informed expedient solutions (e.g., enhancing employee flexibility or tighter control) aimed at these presumed causes. In conceptualizing such solutions, they accepted accountability for the immediate ISD tasks yet regarded long-term implications beyond their scope of responsibility. In contrast, post-conventional IS developers, guided by an integrative responsibility orientation, adopted a structural perspective emphasizing overarching social or technical factors and highlighting collective responsibility among multiple stakeholders. Informed by such framing choices, they prioritized reciprocity in their envisioned solutions while simultaneously advocating a shared sense of accountability (e.g., encouraging others to actively participate in determining the path forward) for both the ISD process and its outcomes.

As ISD projects advance, these divergent moral decision-making patterns can spark value conflicts at the interpersonal level. This dynamic aligns with prior research indicating that while moral deliberations critically shape how value conflicts are reconciled, they can also trigger further discord among stakeholders (Friedman et al., 2013; Vartiainen, 2010a; Yetim, 2011). Our findings suggest that especially post-conventional

IS developers often feel obliged to operationalize solutions grounded in reciprocity through resolute duty fulfillment, thus challenging perspectives they perceive as structurally insufficient to meet this ideal. For example, G3T1-PostC confronted Team 3’s preconventional technical lead about how specific design choices would affect the maintainability and usability of their digital artifact post-deployment.

Our analysis indicates that the patterns summarized in Table 4 persist when developers face interpersonal value conflicts. Because conventional IS developers, guided by a harmonious responsibility orientation, typically fulfilled their duties by conforming to team demands, conflict resolution in our cases was primarily orchestrated by either pre- or post-conventional developers. Consistent with earlier research (Barki & Hartwick, 2001), the observed cases reveal that how these conflicts were managed influenced project outcomes, particularly by fostering or eroding shared understanding and commitment (McCarthy et al., 2018).

In Teams 3, 4, and 6, pre-conventional members, who favored expedient solutions operationalized through pragmatic duty fulfillment, periodically eased tensions by readily accommodating directives. However, as Teams 3 and 6 illustrate, their inclination toward rapid decision-making occasionally devolved into acquiescence to external mandates rather than cultivating a shared vision, ultimately culminating in project failure. For instance, Team 6 experienced several pragmatic yet abrupt pivots triggered by preconventional developers’ overreliance on senior management, yielding a suboptimal digital artifact.

Conversely, in Teams 1, 2, and 5, post-conventional IS developers addressed conflicts through a more confrontational, dialogue-driven approach. Teams 1 and 2 illustrate how this strategy can lead to successful outcomes through reciprocal consensus, yet we identified three notable drawbacks. First, postconventional developers frequently acted as catalysts for interpersonal conflicts by challenging perceived structural issues in others’ viewpoints, potentially disrupting team cohesion. Second, their resolute behavioral integrity sometimes clashed with official role-based duties (e.g., when the post-conventional project leader in Team 1 sought feedback from another employee outside authorized channels). Third, postconventional IS developers’ resolute fulfillment of duties occasionally contributed to a more vertical hierarchy. For example, in an early impasse within Team 5, G5P1-PostC directed key design decisions, while other members, including the conventional technical lead, operationalized those decisions rather than proactively taking ownership of molding crucial aspects of their designed artifact.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

Several themes in our framework resonate with extant ISD scholarship, including problematization (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007), (digital) responsibility (Boland & Day, 1989; Chatterjee et al., 2009; Trier et al., 2023), and the tension between wider obligations and role-specific duties (Lobschat et al., 2021; Walsham, 1996). Theoretically, our framework offers a lens for understanding how IS developers navigate moral decision-making (cf. Boland & Day, 1989; Chatterjee et al., 2009; Vaast, 2025), especially when facing value conflicts.

For instance, Vaast (2025) identifies three types of “moral ambivalence”—limited, moderate, and existential—to illustrate how developers respond to ethical ambiguities that can range from strict compliance to whistleblowing or even exit. Our findings both support and extend this perspective by revealing diverse decision-making patterns (see Table 4) corresponding to pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional levels of moral development. These patterns also align with Kohlberg’s (1969) framework (see Table 1) and DIT-based research (Rest & Narváez, 1994; Rest et al., 1999); for example, our findings suggest that post-conventional developers often favor solutions anchored in the higher-order principle of reciprocity.

As our framework indicates, a central aspect that drives these patterns is developers’ responsibility orientation. Hence, it reinforces earlier ISD insights (Boland & Day, 1989; Chatterjee et al., 2009) and extends discussions of digital responsibility (Carl & Hinz, 2024; Lobschat et al., 2021; Trier et al., 2023). Specifically, our framework delineates a three-faceted conceptualization of responsibility: (1) attributing causal responsibility in framing problems, (2) allocating accountability in conceptualizing solutions, and (3) balancing obligations in fulfilling duties. Each facet has notable implications for studying the moral and ethical aspects of ISD.

Obligations have long been central to debates regarding developers’ responsibilities (Anderson et al., 1993; Collins et al., 1994; Lobschat et al., 2021; Yuthas & Dillard, 1999). Our findings suggest that developers prioritize obligations in diverse ways, ranging from a pragmatic emphasis on project completion (pre-conventional) to maintaining team cohesion (conventional) to seeking reciprocity (postconventional). These varied commitments mirror the archetypes outlined by Hirschheim and Klein (1989), spanning from a “systems expert” who implements management directives to an “emancipator” or “social therapist” who aims for genuine consensus in ISD.

Likewise, we build upon Schmidt et al. (2025), who demonstrate that IS developers’ accountability perspectives differ based on whether they emphasize the procedural aspects of AI creation or the broader outcomes these systems generate. Our findings suggest that (1) preconventional developers tend to confine accountability to the ISD lifecycle, (2) conventional developers embrace accountability reactively in response to established and evolving project conditions, and (3) post-conventional developers foster a collective view of accountability, urging all stakeholders to share ownership of both the ISD process and its outcomes.

Although causal responsibility has received limited attention in prior ISD research, our findings highlight its importance in how problems are framed, which, in turn, informs how solutions are conceptualized (see Figure 2). Pre-conventional developers, for example, often assign causal responsibility to a specific stakeholder group (e.g., inflexible IT departments), which informs expedient solutions devised to target these root causes (e.g., enabling greater flexibility for employees). By contrast, post-conventional developers stress collective causal responsibility, which informs reciprocity-based solutions intended to benefit multiple stakeholder groups.

In contemporary debates on digital responsibility (Carl & Hinz, 2024; Mueller, 2022), the distinct orientations we identify highlight the need for greater theoretical clarity with respect to the three facets detailed in our framework. Indeed, causal responsibility in problem framing remains relatively unexplored, appearing indirectly under the rubric of “liability” (Carl & Hinz, 2024). By contrast, accountability has received more extensive attention in the discourse on digital responsibility (Trier et al., 2023). Furthermore, the tension between wider obligations and role-based duties, a concern identified in Walsham’s (1996) critique of professional codes of ethics, has recently regained prominence (Lobschat et al., 2021). Our findings thus reinforce calls for research addressing such collisions within the domain of digital responsibility.

Although the varying patterns across pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional developers echo longstanding skepticism regarding rigid prescriptive rules (Stahl, 2007; Vaast, 2025; Wilson, 1997), moral philosophy can nonetheless advance digital responsibility initiatives. On the one hand, a relativist ethical stance may underpin digital responsibility. For instance, postmodern ethics, with its emphasis on accountability (Chatterjee et al., 2009) and obligations (Yuthas & Dillard, 1999), underscores how continuous stakeholder engagement can recognize diverse viewpoints while mitigating marginalization in technology design. From this perspective, moral decision-making around causal responsibility, accountability, and obligations becomes a collective endeavor, facilitated by stakeholder proximity. On the other hand, procedural ethical frameworks—such as Habermasian discourse ethics—offer systematic strategies for addressing moral concerns, including value conflicts, in the IS context (Mingers & Walsham, 2010; Schlagwein et al., 2019; Yetim, 2011). Applied to digital responsibility, discourse ethics could illuminate how open, rational dialogue among diverse stakeholders assists IS developers in navigating questions of causal responsibility, accountability, and obligations.

Advancing the discourse on digital responsibility also promises to yield enhanced project outcomes. More precisely, we find that the intersection of diverse moral decision-making, grounded in diffuse, harmonious, and integrative responsibility orientations, can either forge or undermine shared understanding (e.g., Gregory et al., 2013; Hekkala et al., 2022; Jenkin et al., 2019; McCarthy et al., 2018), thereby leading to interpersonal conflicts. Prior ISD scholarship (Barki & Hartwick, 2001) has examined conflict-management styles, including asserting, accommodating, and compromising. Similarly, our findings link these styles to IS developers’ moral development levels. Postconventional developers, guided by an integrative responsibility orientation, frequently initiate and manage conflicts through reciprocity-based dialogue. By contrast, conventional developers, steered by a harmonious orientation, rarely instigate conflict; instead, they adapt to team demands and fulfill assigned tasks in a conformist manner. Finally, preconventional developers, guided by a diffuse orientation, often avoid conflict to maintain momentum, gravitating toward expedient solutions operationalized pragmatically.

Chatterjee et al. (2009) and Yuthas and Dillard (1999) caution that diffusing responsibility can undermine genuine accountability and allow dubious decisions to remain unchallenged—a point corroborated by our observation of pre-conventional developers in Team 6 who repeatedly shifted responsibility rather than accepting it. At the same time, our data also show how multiple responsibility orientations can interact synergistically. For example, Teams 1 and 2 illustrate how three orientations can converge productively, whereas Team 5 reveals challenges—such as a conventional technical lead’s reluctance to take ownership—that can intensify when juxtaposed with a post-conventional developer’s resolute drive for reciprocity. Moreover, Team 3 demonstrates that clashes between diffuse and integrative orientations can compromise cohesion and lead to suboptimal outcomes. In this way, the intersection of various responsibility orientations can prove more consequential than any single orientation in isolation.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Building on our findings and framework (see Figure 2), we propose a set of practical measures—presented in descending order of importance—to mitigate the disruptive consequences that can arise from divergent moral decision-making approaches among IS developers (see Table 4).

## 6.2.1 Engage in Ongoing Reflection

Ongoing reflection in ISD involves continuously scrutinizing existing assumptions and observing shifting project conditions, thereby fostering ongoing learning that enhances adaptability, collaboration, and organizational effectiveness (Mathiassen, 1998). Through such reflection, IS developers can critically assess the decision-making tendencies (see Table 4) of both themselves and other stakeholders. As a result, they may uncover and question counterproductive inclinations—such as imbalanced obligations. For example, they might ask whether an emphasis on pragmatic project closure (as demonstrated by preconventional IS developers) risks generating unintended adverse consequences. Conversely, they might recognize that a resolute commitment to particular ideals (as demonstrated by post-conventional IS developers’ focus on reciprocity) could undermine practicality and team cohesion if left unchecked.

A useful tool for fostering ongoing reflection is the eternal triangle (Ulrich, 2001b). This framework emphasizes three interconnected elements in problemsolving: (1) the chosen reference system, (2) empirical observations or facts, and (3) evaluative values. Reflective engagement with these elements can help IS developers navigate moral decision-making. For instance, by examining how they attribute causal responsibility in framing problems, developers can discern which stakeholder values (i.e., their interests and desires) become privileged and which are overlooked, thereby informing potential solutions and questions of accountability (e.g., recognizing the impact of specific design choices on stakeholder welfare). More concretely, post-conventional developers might realize that prioritizing particular principles (e.g., reciprocity)

may not be fully realizable under specific conditions; conversely, pre-conventional developers may recognize that an overemphasis on pragmatism may undermine certain values or principles that must be upheld, such as ensuring stakeholder welfare.

## 6.2.2 Constructively Address Value Conflicts at the Interpersonal Level

Even with thorough reflection, divergent moral decision-making approaches may persist, potentially leading to interpersonal conflicts with considerable implications for ISD interventions. Below, we present three strategies to manage such disputes.

First, moral courage—the ability to take action when ethical concerns surface (Comer & Schwartz, 2017)—is essential for addressing disputes before they escalate. As part of ongoing reflection, routine dialogues and systematic feedback on requirements, designs, and prototypes (Mathiassen, 1998) enable developers to detect emerging risks and potentially harmful conditions early on. During these evaluations, IS developers (especially those at the pre-conventional level) may acknowledge that specific principles (e.g., preventing harm) are non-negotiable, even if they conflict with other stakeholders’ priorities. To nurture moral courage in tandem with ongoing reflection, we recommend tools such as group decision support systems with anonymized messaging or idea voting (cf. Markus & Silver, 2008). These tools can foster open communication by reducing social or emotional barriers (e.g., fear or embarrassment) that might deter conflictaverse developers (such as those at the conventional level) from contributing.

While prior scholarship offers conflict-resolution strategies (Mingers & Walsham, 2010; Ulrich, 2001b; Yetim, 2011), we propose a second recommendation: adopt a “could mindset” (Zhang et al., 2018). Rather than defaulting to a “least undesirable” compromise, this mindset proactively seeks moral insights beneficial to all parties. By moving beyond rigid prescriptive views (see Table 4), IS developers can become more open to others’ perspectives, exploring how seemingly divergent stances might be reconciled. Much like ongoing reflection (Mathiassen, 1998), a “could mindset” demands adaptability, contextual awareness, and receptivity to complex, evolving scenarios. For instance, post-conventional developers may find that their reciprocity-driven solutions can yield significant collective benefits but are more attainable when combined with the pragmatic insights offered by their pre-conventional counterparts.

Lastly, we recommend that teams incorporate retrospectives into their ongoing reflection cycle (Mathiassen, 1998) to revisit lessons learned, evaluate new challenges, and pinpoint areas for enhancement. Under Scrum, for instance, a portion of sprint planning can be dedicated to fostering open dialogue and mutual commitments, while the sprint retrospective can uncover tensions arising from conflicting stakeholder interests and needs. In a Kanban environment, board reviews can help detect emerging disagreements and chronic bottlenecks, enabling rapid conflict resolution. In a waterfall approach, project teams could hold an immediate meeting after requirements are gathered to harmonize key objectives before moving to design and development, reconvening at key milestones to address any unresolved conflicts.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Directions for Research

We acknowledge several limitations in our study concerning the empirical context, sample characteristics, data collection, and practical recommendations. First, although mitigating shadow IT illustrates a salient example of value conflict, the extent of moral decision-making required in ISD projects may differ substantially across organizational settings (Friedman et al., 2013; Vartiainen, 2010a). Consequently, our proposed framework and the identified patterns may be context specific. We encourage future research to investigate moral decision-making within ISD settings spanning diverse sectors and contexts (e.g., healthcare, AI).

Second, our sample did not capture the complete range of possible team compositions. Notably, we did not observe project leaders at the conventional level, nor teams composed entirely of IS developers at a single moral development level. Moreover, demographic factors (e.g., culture, gender, age, experience) might limit the generalizability of our findings. Future work could adopt experimental designs to examine the interplay among developers’ moral development, decision-making, and project outcomes. For instance, scholars could measure developers’ moral development prior to team formation to explore how teams consisting solely of a particular level perform. Such research could clarify how teams navigate value conflicts—whether, for example, a pre-conventional group’s pragmatic stance accelerates project completion, whereas a team composed only of postconventional developers might enhance stakeholder satisfaction through reciprocity-based solutions, albeit at the cost of additional project duration.

Third, our data largely captured episodic snapshots of each ISD project. As a result, the moral decisionmaking patterns we observed and their outcomes may have been influenced by factors beyond our scope, such as resource constraints or unexamined stakeholder dynamics. Future research should gather richer qualitative and quantitative data, including realtime observations, to validate and extend these findings. For instance, such efforts could examine how power dynamics tied to formal roles intersect with moral decision-making (cf. Hekkala et al., 2022; Klein & Hirschheim, 2001). Moreover, Kohlberg’s moral development theory has been criticized for privileging rational, justice-oriented reasoning (DeTienne et al., 2021), a concern that remains salient when using the DIT. Accordingly, while our adapted DIT scenario (Myyry et al., 2009) is well-suited to the shadow IT context, scholars employing alternative DIT scenarios or assessment instruments could identify different moral development classifications.

Finally, our practical recommendations need additional empirical validation. Not all ISD projects demand the same extent of moral deliberation and thus the applicability of our guidance remains unclear. For example, design science research may rest on distinct epistemological and axiological assumptions (Kane et al., 2021; Sein et al., 2011). Although our recommendations could be beneficial to practitioners beyond the ISD context, additional studies are necessary to test their applicability across diverse professional environments.

## 7 Conclusion

Value conflicts are often an inevitable reality of ISD, arising from the pluralistic desires and interests of heterogeneous stakeholder groups. Our study reveals that developers at the pre‐conventional, conventional, or post‐conventional levels navigate these conflicts in markedly different ways. Central to these differences is the responsibility orientation that guides their moral decision‐making. When diffuse, harmonious, or integrative orientations intersect, they can strengthen or undermine shared understanding and commitment, thereby steering project trajectories. We hope our study motivates additional research into the ethical and moral dimensions of ISD and inspires targeted measures— particularly within the evolving sphere of digital responsibility—to help IS developers address the “wicked” nature of decision-making and craft artifacts that contribute to the public good while averting harmful consequences.

## Appendix B: Quantitative Analysis (Defining Issues Test)

The defining issues test (DIT) instrument used in this study (see Table B1), adapted from Myyry et al. (2009), focuses on a hypothetical scenario involving compliance with information security policies. We chose this scenario because it closely reflects the value conflicts underlying shadow IT, wherein compliance with organizational data security policies and employees’ needs for autonomy and efficiency were at stake. Following standard DIT procedures, participants first rated the importance of items on a 5-point scale and then ranked the four most important items. One of these items was intentionally constructed as a meaningless statement (“M-item”) and served as a validity check. Participants who rated and ranked this Mitem as the most important were deemed to have provided an invalid response and were therefore excluded from subsequent analysis, thereby ensuring data integrity. Moral development was assessed using the p-score, a well-established metric that captures the extent of post-conventional moral reasoning (Rest, 1979). To calculate this score, participants selected the four most important items from the scenario, with each chosen item then weighted according to its rank: four points for the most important, three for the second, two for the third, and one for the fourth. The p-score represents the proportion of these weighted points allocated to post-conventional items (i.e., items corresponding to Stages 5 and 6). Adapting prior DIT research (e.g., Ho, 2025; Jean-Tron et al., 2022; Murrell, 2014), we classified individuals as follows: a p-score below 0.25 indicates a pre-conventional orientation, a score between 0.25 and 0.50 indicates a conventional orientation, and a score above 0.50 indicates a dominant post-conventional orientation. After excluding one participant for failing the M-item criterion, p-scores were calculated for the remaining 39 IS developers across the six teams. We applied a Kruskal-Wallis test to examine differences in moral development among the six teams. The test yielded a significant result (χ2(5) = 13.006, p = 0.021), indicating that p-scores differed significantly across teams.

## Appendix C: Mixed Methods Data Analysis Procedure Illustrations

Figure C1 illustrates our mixed methods analysis procedure. The solid lines represent overt connections to the FCM, corresponding to dimensions identified explicitly in initial coding. In contrast, the dotted lines represent latent connections, capturing deeper interpretive insights developed through iterative re-analysis.

In Table C2, we outline our analysis process for linking coded data excerpts to the FCM dimensions and deriving themes. This process adhered to three key criteria adapted from Braun and Clarke (2022). Specifically, each theme had to (1) capture essential meanings in the coded excerpts, (2) align with the FCM’s dimensions (informed by the candidate themes in Table C1), and (3) provide a coherent analytical account of how IS developers navigate moral decision-making in ISD amid value conflicts. Although Braun and Clarke (2022) do not formally require alignment with an external theoretical framework, we introduced this second criterion to ensure coherence with the FCM—a researcher-imposed step consistent with a theoretically informed approach to reflexive thematic analysis.

For example, from the potential candidates in Table C1, we developed the theme “responsibility orientation” under the moral motivation dimension. Conversely, certain candidates prompted us to introduce new themes not explicitly addressed in earlier FCM research. One such theme, “duty fulfillment,” is typically only implicitly mentioned in prior depictions of moral character (e.g., diligence; see Table C1). Notably, this theme also appears in prior research on ethical and moral dimensions of ISD (e.g., Walsham, 1996). Similarly, “problem framing” (moral sensitivity) and “solution conceptualization” (moral judgment) were developed based on our data, in conjunction with insights from the literature on ethical and moral dimensions of ISD (e.g., Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Klein & Hirschheim, 2001; Ulrich, 2001b). Within these themes, the resulting subthemes captured moral decision-making patterns across pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional IS developer (see Tables C2, C4-C6). This iterative process of developing themes and sub-themes aligns with reflexive thematic analysis, which encourages researchers to actively guide both interpretation and analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022).

We derived the central theme of “responsibility orientation” from our observation that “problem framing,” “solution conceptualization,” and “duty fulfillment” varied according to IS developers’ moral development levels, yet certain inconsistencies remained. For instance, although pre-conventional IS developers often endorsed digital monitoring to mitigate shadow IT, we also discovered a post-conventional developer who did the same—an unexpected finding that prompted a closer examination of how our derived themes interrelate (see Tables C2-C6). For example, we examined whether problem-framing choices differed in aspects such as perspective-taking (see Table C1) and whether these differences were tied to specific IS developer roles.

Ultimately, we refined the theme “responsibility orientation” by focusing on how IS developers attributed causal responsibility in framing problems, allocated accountability in conceptualizing solutions, and balanced obligations in fulfilling duties (see Tables C3-C6). Following Braun and Clarke’s (2022) guidelines, we iteratively developed these focal points through engagement with our data and relevant scholarship on the FCM, the DIT, and the ethical and moral dimensions of ISD. Notably, allocating accountability—such as taking “ownership”—aligns with key concerns in prior ISD research (e.g., Chatterjee et al., 2009; Friedman et al., 2013). Meanwhile, balancing obligations—such as how IS developers weigh personal commitments against the directives of peers and external stakeholders—resonates with the moral motivation component of the FCM (see Table C1) and has been discussed in prior work on the ethical and moral dimensions of creating artifacts (cf. Anderson et al., 1993; Collins et al., 1994; Walsham, 1996). Table C3 presents the questions we used to analyze “Responsibility Orientation” reflexively. Finally, we identified the sub-themes “diffuse,” “harmonious,” and “integrative” to capture how IS developers at the pre-conventional, conventional, and postconventional levels, respectively, attributed causal responsibility in framing problems, allocated accountability in conceptualizing solutions, and balanced obligations in fulfilling duties (see Tables C4-C6).
