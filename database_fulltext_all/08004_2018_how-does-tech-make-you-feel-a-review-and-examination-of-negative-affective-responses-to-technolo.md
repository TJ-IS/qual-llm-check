# “How does tech make you feel?” a review and examination of negative afective responses to technology use

David Agogo<sup>a</sup> and Traci J. Hess<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, Florida International University, Miami, FL , USA; <sup>b</sup>Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Amherst MA USA

## ABSTRACT

The study of individual, afect-related consequences from technology adoption and use is gaining traction in the information systems discipline. Eforts to explore afective reactions to technology have considered positive, afective constructs (e.g., enjoyment, playfulness, and flow), with a more recent focus on the dark side of technology use and constructs such as technostress, technophobia, and computer anxiety. While some research has examined these negative afective responses to technology, construct definitions and relationships are not welldefined or theoretically grounded. In this research, an integrative literature review is conducted on computer anxiety, technophobia and technostress, and the known antecedents, dimensions, and outcomes of each concept are organised into nomological networks. These nomological networks are then combined to identify inconsistencies and omissions in the literature. The Afective Response Model, a recently advanced, theoretically grounded taxonomy of afective responses to technology, is applied to diferentiate the three constructs and to introduce technology-induced state anxiety (TISA), a new temporal (state-like) negative response to a specific instance of technology. Two empirical studies are conducted using existing and newly developed scales, and demonstrate that computer anxiety, technophobia, technostress and TISA are conceptually and empirically distinct and provide insight into how these constructs are related. Future research opportunities on afective responses to technology are described based on the integrated nomological network and empirical findings.

## 1. Introduction

The study of afective concepts has recently become mainstream in the Information Systems (IS) discipline, with a growing number of journal articles and special issues examining afective responses to technology (e.g., Djamasbi, 2007; Djamasbi & Strong, 2008; Loiacono & Djamasbi, 2010; Tarafdar, Gupta, & Turel, 2013). This interest in feelings towards IT artefacts is timely and important, given that feelings are significant factors in many research areas, ranging from financial decision-making to human resource management (e.g., Ashkanasy & Daus, 2002; Kahneman, 2003; Lucey & Dowling, 2005; McGrath, 2006). For decades, IS researchers focused primarily on eficiency and efectiveness evaluations of technology as drivers of adoption (Turel, Soror, & Steelman, 2017), but as technology invades many aspects of our work and personal lives, an understanding of emotional responses to technology is critical (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010). The dark side of technology use and negative emotional responses are increasingly reported (Tarafdar et al., 2013). Stress, forgetting/poor retention, depression, illegal behaviour, physical well-being, misuse and addiction are just some of the negative responses and outcomes documented from technology-related interruptions, overload and overuse (D’arcy, Gupta, Tarafdar, & Turel, 2014; Soror, Hammer, Steelman, Davis, & Limayem, 2015).

While research on positive afective responses to technology has been underway for some time (e.g., Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010; Van der Heijden, 2004), negative afective constructs or “dark side” variables have more recently received attention (Tarafdar et al., 2013). Research on the “dark side” of technology use has considered the negative efect of technophobia (e.g., Brosnan, 2002) and anxiety (e.g., Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002) on IT perceptions and behaviour, and is beginning to explore topics such as technology-related stress (e.g., Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011; Ragu-Nathan, Tarafdar, Ragu-Nathan, & Tu, 2008). However, many research gaps exist with conceptual clarity and measurement, the theoretical foundation, and empirical examinations of how these negative responses to technology afect technology-related evaluations and performance (Riedl, 2012; Tams, 2015; Tarafdar et al., 2013).

The growing interest in negative afective responses to technology highlights the need for better integration and theoretically grounded research on the topic. Early psychology research noted the ambiguity surrounding negative responses to technology, stating that “whether we call it ‘computer anxiety,’ ‘technostress,’ or ‘computerphobia,’ all estimates indicate that as many as one out of three adults sufers from aversive reactions to computers and computer-related technology” (Weil, Rosen, & Sears, 1987, p. 180). IS research on these concepts has taken diferent theoretical perspectives, including social cognitive theory (SCT) (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002), transaction-based models of stress (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar, Tu, & Ragu-Nathan, 2010), person–environment models of work strain (Ayyagari et al., 2011), and physiological responses (Fischer & Riedl, 2015; Riedl, Kindermann, Auinger, & Javor, 2012), with critics describing the current state as atheoretical and lacking integration (Tams, 2015). Theoretical grounding and integration of the existing literature is essential to the progress of research (Gregor, 2006; Rivard, 2014). The disjointed conceptualisation of dark side variables is also a barrier to developing interventions to reduce negative afect toward technology, and interventions are an area largely ignored in IS research (Pirkkalainen & Salo, 2016). Further, no known study has simultaneously examined more than one negative afective response, and there is limited research on the process through which negative responses to IS influence outcomes, such as IS use, resistance, and performance (Zhang, 2013).

In this research, a literature review on three negative afective responses to technology, computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress, is conducted. These dark side concepts are the most commonly discussed afective responses to technology in the IS literature (i.e., not cognitive responses or behavioural outcomes). Nomological networks are presented for each construct based on the existing literature, and are then integrated to create a combined nomological network documenting the state of IS research. A recently developed IS framework, the Afective Response Model (ARM) (Zhang, 2013) based on the psychology literature, provides timely theoretical grounding and a taxonomy of five afective dimensions (residing, temporal nature, particular vs. general stimulus, object vs. behaviour stimulus, and process vs. outcome-based evaluations) to better diferentiate these dark side constructs. Further, gaps and inconsistencies in the literature are identified, and a new afective construct, the technology-induced state anxiety (TISA), is proposed to better explain the process through which afect influences technology-related outcomes. The following research questions guide this examination of negative afective responses to IS:

(1) How are computer anxiety, technophobia and technostress conceptually distinct?

(2) What are the similarities and diferences in the studied antecedents and outcomes of these concepts based on existing IS (and related) literature? What gaps exist?

(3) How are these concepts related to one another and to other afective concepts?

Two empirical studies, a survey and an experiment, are conducted to assess construct validity and relationships among the negative afective constructs with diferent types of IS. Existing and new scales are used to better measure and validate these constructs. In the following sections, the literature review is presented and the ARM framework applied to diferentiate these constructs and generate research propositions. Next, the research design and empirical results are reported. Finally, theoretical and practical implications, and future research opportunities are described.

## 2. Literature review

Because the modern workplace is flooded with technology, people have little choice whether or not to use it (Venkatesh, 2000). Whereas several decades ago apprehensive individuals could opt to avoid technology, that option barely exists today. The increased, often mandatory use of technology underscores the importance of studying the negative emotional experiences that may result from interactions with technology. This objective is first addressed by reviewing the literature on the three, most common, negative afective concepts in the IS literature – computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress. Cognitive and behavioural concepts were not considered.

## 2.1. Methodology

The methodology for conducting this literature review was informed by the guidelines of Levy and Ellis (2006). The primary source of articles was high quality, peer-reviewed IS research journals as identified through the AIS Senior Scholars Consortium (2011) and ranking studies of IS journals (e.g., Lowry, Romans, & Curtis, 2004). A total of 14 IS journals, listed in Table 1, were searched for articles that contained the words “stress”, “anxiety”, and “phobia” anywhere within the article. This initial search resulted in 1542 research articles, which seemed appropriate given that the objective was to identify as many unique definitions, antecedents, outcomes, and source theories for these concepts as possible. Subsequently, by reviewing the titles and abstracts of the 1542 papers, a subset of 190 were deemed potentially relevant and selected for closer review. Within this subset, papers in which either computer anxiety, technostress, or technophobia were conceptualised or measured were reviewed in detail. Reverse citations were used to find additional articles from other IS journals that addressed these concepts. Technophobia was not as commonly mentioned in the IS literature as the other two constructs, thus reverse citations were carried out to find articles and books outside of the IS discipline that referenced this concept, mostly from the psychology literature.

## 2.2. The computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress overlap

Nearly twenty years after the United States Census Bureau ordered the first commercial computer produced in the US, a wide range of attitudes towards computers was documented in a national survey (Lee, 1970). Dominant attitudes toward computers were found to reflect one of two perspectives: the computer as a helpful instrument of man’s purposes, or the computer as a relatively autonomous entity (i.e., invoking awe and a sense of inferiority). A class of “negative concepts” emerged to describe the negative feelings associated with technology use. The main ones, in chronological order of first appearance in the literature, are computer anxiety (Masters, 1967), technophobia (Paschen & Gresser, 1974) and technostress (Brod, 1982). Other negative, afective constructs referenced in the IS literature (e.g., technology addiction, computer-mediated communication anxiety) are excluded from this review as those constructs are viewed as a mode of usage behaviour rather than an afective evaluation, or are a specific form of computer anxiety.

The popularisation of these terms can be partially ascribed to books by psychologists Brod (1984), Rosen and Weil (1997), and Brosnan (2002) which laid the foundation for many IS research studies in this area. In breaking new ground, these authors and others often describe the spectrum of technology responses using terms interchangeably, without clearly distinguishing them or explaining how they are related (Chua, Chen, & Wong, 1999; Rosen & Maguire, 1990; Tu, Wang, & Shu, 2005). For example, Brod states that “(computer) anxiety is a symptom of ambivalence, fear or reluctance towards computers” and “technostress manifests as a struggle to accept computers” (1984, p. 16), but does not conceptually connect both phenomena. Rosen and Weil acknowledge that “technophobia … is used to describe a variety of negative reactions to technology” and then state that this negative experience is technostress (1997, p. 13). Further, Brosnan unequivocally posits that technophobia is comprised of both computer anxiety and negative computer attitudes (2002). Despite the lack of conceptual clarity, the psychology field is consistent in recognising the significance of these negative responses to technology, suggesting that over thirty per cent of users have major, aversive reactions to technology (Weil et al., 1987). The following sections examine computer anxiety, technophobia and technostress, in turn, defining them, reviewing the literature and theory on these constructs, and pointing out areas of agreement and discrepancy in the source literature.

## 2.3. Computer anxiety

Computer anxiety (CA) is the oldest construct used to capture negative reactions to technology implementation or use. The earliest recorded use of this term was in the Journal of Occupational and Environmental Medicine where it was suggested as a new diagnosis that captured the prolonged trauma that employees of a company undergoing computerisation tended to experience (Masters, 1967). In a world without computers there would not be computer anxiety – making the emergence of this concept correlate strongly with the massive explosion in the number of computing devices. The root word anxiety is an emotion, characterised by feelings of tension, worried thoughts and physical changes, like increased blood pressure, which may result in response to a situation or object perceived as threatening (Kazdin, 2000; Weiner & Craighead, 2010). Computers can stimulate CA in the same way social situations stimulate social anxiety and math problems stimulate math anxiety. Two common definitions of CA are: “the tendency of individuals to be uneasy, apprehensive, or fearful about current or future use of computers” (Igbaria & Parasuraman, 1989; Simonson, Maurer, Montag-Torardi, & Whitaker, 1987; Venkatesh, 2000) and “fears about the implications of computer use such as the loss of important data or fear of other possible mistakes” (Sievert & Others, 1988; Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002).

Two categories of theories, psychological and sociological, have been used as the foundation for the study of CA, providing slightly diferent contexts for understanding this concept. The psychological theory commonly applied is SCT (Compeau & Higgins, 1995a, 1995b; Compeau, Higgins, & Huf, 1999; Marakas, Yi, & Johnson, 1998). SCT views the diferent behaviour of individuals in similar situations as determined by their diferent appraisals of the environment (see Bandura, 1977 for a review). On the other hand, sociological theories focus on the changing role of the individual within a broader context in which the individual, technology, and the environment are intrinsically woven and interdependent. These sociological theories include Kurt Lewin’s field theory (Elie-Dit-Cosaque, Pallud, & Kalika, 2011) and Gidden’s social theory of transformation (Barrett & Walsham, 1999). Sociological theories look at CA as a result of technology implementations changing the work environment, while psychological theories view CA as the individual reacting directly to technology use.

## 2.3.1. Multidimensionality and measurement

CA was initially conceptualised as a multidimensional construct with dimensions including self-eficacy, com puter literacy, arousal, positive beliefs, and negative beliefs (e.g., Beckers & Schmidt, 2001; Chua et al., 1999; Heinssen, Glass, & Knight, 1987). As several dimen sions emerged as distinct constructs (e.g., Beckers & Schmidt, 2001), CA was increasingly conceptualised as unidimensional and commonly measured with Likerttype scales using items such as working with IT makes me feel nervous, uncomfortable, uneasy or scared (e.g., Brown, Fuller, & Vician, 2004; Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011; Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002). Physiologic methods have also been used to measure CA, given that anxiety responses are associated with physiological changes. For instance, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and electro dermal response (EDR) have been linked with CA (Powers, 1973), but are not commonly used.

The antecedents and consequences of CA, as documented in the existing literature, are depicted in the nomological network shown in Figure 1, and are listed in Appendix A1, Table A1.1 As noted in Figure 1, some relationships between antecedents and CA have been proposed but not empirically tested. Relationships that have been tested were examined in a piece meal manner with no comprehensive examination of the nomological network.

2.3.2.1. Individual characteristics (antecedent). From a psychological perspective, CA is conceptualised as a dynamic, IT-specific individual diference (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002), which can be influenced by other individual diferences (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011). CA is positively related to other forms of negative afect, including trait anxiety and negative afectivity (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002). Forms of positive afect have negative relationships with CA, including computer playfulness (Webster & Martocchio, 1992) and personal innovativeness with technology (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011; Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002). Cognitive-processing attributes such as cognitive style (Igbaria & Parasuraman, 1989) and computer self-eficacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999; Thatcher, Zimmer, Gundlach, & McKnight, 2008) are known to negatively influence CA. Demographic variables such as gender and age may also influence CA with females and older users reporting greater CA (Chua et al., 1999; Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011; Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990; Igbaria & Parasuraman, 1989). Other individual characteristics tested as antecedents of CA include education level, experience, locus of control, math anxiety, and use (Hackbarth, Grover, & Yi, 2003; Howard & Mendelow, 1991; Igbaria & Parasuraman, 1989; Igbaria, Pavri, & Huf, 1989).

2.3.2.2. Technology characteristics (antecedent). Characteristics of the technology may also influence the CA felt by an individual. For example, interface or website quality has been examined as a determinant of specific CA towards particular IT (Hofman & Novak, 1996; Hwang & Kim, 2007; Krasonikolakis, Vrechopoulos, & Pouloudi, 2014). While the relationship between technology attributes and CA may seem intuitive, there has been little research on the topic. The limited research may be attributed to CA being more commonly viewed as anxiety toward computer use in general, rather than towards a specific technology or characteristics such as interface quality, usability, security, etc.

2.3.2.3. Organisational/Environment conditions (antecedent). From a sociological perspective, CA can be influenced by the organisational environment in which an individual works, often referred to as external forces (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011) or macro stressors (Saleh & Desai, 1986). These external stressors include an individual’s role, assigned work tasks, managerial support, autonomy, and overload (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011), and can contribute to, or diminish, the general tendency of an individual to be anxious or fearful of using technology.

2.3.2.4. Use or use-related behaviour (outcome). Computer aversion, an extreme form of non-usage also referred to as computer avoidance (Rosen & Maguire, 1990), is the earliest and most commonly mentioned outcome of CA. Early research cites aversive behaviour, such as avoidance of computers and the general vicinity of computers, and attempts to reduce the necessary use of computers, as sure signs of CA (Brosnan, 2002). CA is believed to have a negative efect on computer-based performance, however, no empirical tests of this relationship were found in the literature.

2.3.2.5. Cognitive evaluations or expectancies (outcome). Another category of CA outcomes is cognitive evaluations or expectancies such as computer self-eficacy (CSE) (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002) and the broader related concept, perceived behavioural control (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011). A reciprocal relationship exists between CA and CSE, in line with the source psychological theory (Bandura, 1977). A priori CSE, an individual characteristic, influences CA (Compeau & Higgins, 1995a; Compeau et al., 1999; Marakas et al., 1998), while the experience of CA may then impact future CSE (Marakas et al., 1998). Perceived behavioural control (Trafimow, Sheeran, Conner, & Finlay, 2002) includes both internal and external constraints on behaviour, encompassing controllability and self-eficacy (Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003), but there are no known tests of the relationship with CA.

## 2.4. Technophobia

Technophobia is described as a more severe form of anxiety towards computers, and a composite of behavioural, emotional and attitudinal responses to computers i.e., a resistance to talking about computers or even thinking about computers, fear or anxiety towards computers, or hostile or aggressive thoughts about computers (Brosnan, 2002; Jay, 1981). It is also called computerphobia, to refer specifically to computers, rather than all technologies. Technophobia has been minimally studied in the IS literature (only 10 articles out of 1512 retrieved mentioned the term), and only a handful of psychologists have written about the construct, conceptualising it as a composite of computer anxiety and negative computer attitudes (Brosnan, 2002).

Technophobia is considered a specific phobia, a psychological disorder characterised by excessive, irrational fear of a specific object or situation, to be avoided or endured with great distress. Specific phobia is one of the most common psychiatric disorders in the US with one in five cases considered to be severe (Kessler, Chiu, Demler, & Walters, 2005). There are four subtypes of specific phobias: animal (e.g., spiders), natural environment (e.g., heights), situational (e.g., closed spaces), blood-injection-injury (e.g., dentist), and an “other” category for phobias that do not fit into the designated subtypes (Choy, Fyer, & Lipsitz, 2007; Thorpe & Brosnan, 2007). Technophobia falls under the “other” subtype. While there is limited empirical work ascribing clinical-disorder status to technophobia, technophobia reduction programmes have been designed with reductions in negative feelings observed (Brosnan & Thorpe, 2006; Weil et al., 1987).

## 2.4.1. Multidimensionality and measurement

Technophobia has been conceptualised as a composite of several factors including (1) computer anxiety, (2) negative attitudes to computers, (3) style of thought (or cognitive styles) (Brosnan, 2002), and individual factors which influence the incidence of other specific phobias such as (4) cultural/environmental factors, and (5) genetic factors (Maj, Akiskal, López-Ibor, & Okasha, 2004). One study investigated whether technophobia might reach clinical levels similar to other specific phobias (such as a spider phobia), and found tentative support, but measured technophobia with a CA scale (Thorpe & Brosnan, 2007). Researchers suggest that technophobia could be measured directly, or indirectly by the severity of outcomes (e.g., computer avoidance) or as a composite of other constructs, (e.g., computer anxiety, negative attitudes) (Brosnan, 2002), but there are no known direct measures, and no real validation eforts with indirect measures.

## 2.4.2. Antecedents and outcomes

The documented antecedents and outcomes of technophobia are depicted within a nomological network in Figure 2, and in Table A1.2. Given the paucity of literature studying technophobia as a construct, there are no empirically tested antecedents or outcomes. However, observations from the available literature suggest that antecedents of CA may be antecedents of technophobia, with the primary outcome discussed being computer aversion or avoidance. Physiological reactions (e.g., sweaty palms, heart palpitations, high blood pressure) and psychosomatic responses (e.g., headaches, nausea, dizziness) are also likely outcomes from technophobia (Brod, 1984; Brosnan, 2002), but have not been empirically tested.

## 2.5. Technostress

Technostress is commonly defined as “a modern disease of adaptation caused by an inability to cope with new computer technologies in a healthy manner which may manifest as a struggle to accept computer technology, or by over-identification with computer technology” (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Brod, 1984; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Riedl et al., 2012). It is also defined more generally as “any negative impact on attitudes, thoughts, behaviours, or body physiology that is caused either directly or indirectly by technology” (Rosen & Weil, 1997; Tarafdar, Tu, Ragu-Nathan, & Ragu-Nathan, 2007). Research on technostress tends to focus on business users of technology, and particularly mandatory use of technology. Technostress was first written about by psychologist Craig Brod who viewed technostress as emerging from work environments changed out of recognition by the technological revolution of the eighties (Brod, 1984). His book describes how work and daily life may be irreversibly changed by the proliferation of technology.

Technostress is based on the root concept of stress, which has several diferent definitions, including (1) the internal state of the organism (or strain); (2) an external event (or stressor); or (3) an experience that arises from an ongoing transaction between a person and the environment (Mason, 1975). It is further derived from general stress in the workplace, which is defined as the harmful physical and emotional responses that occur when job requirements do not match the worker’s capabilities, resources, and needs (National Institute for Occupational Safety & Health, 1999). Technostress is commonly described in association with an individual’s role in the workplace and the tasks the individual is assigned to perform with the technology as part of that role (Tu, Tarafdar, Ragu-Nathan, & Ragu-Nathan, 2007). Specifically, technostress occurs when an individual has a negative evaluation of their experience carrying out tasks using technology at any work, which is distinct from studies of general work stress among IS workers in particular (e.g., Ahuja, Chudoba, Kacmar, McKnight, & George, 2007; Lee, 2000; Moore, 2000).

In the IS field, there are two sub-streams of research investigating the phenomenon of technostress. The earlier stream is the series of papers by Tarafdar and collaborators (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar, Pullins, & Ragu-Nathan, 2015; Tarafdar et al., 2007, 2010) which employs sociotechnical theory (Trist & Bamforth, 2000) and role theory (Gross, Mceachern, & Mason, 1996) as well as transaction-based models of stress (McGrath, 1976). Their work takes the perspective that the implementation of technology within organisations has an influence on the individual’s role and technology-related work tasks, and thus this stream focuses on technostress creators as antecedents in the work environment. The later stream (Ayyagari et al., 2011) adopts the person–environment fit model of stress (Edwards & Cooper, 1990), a popular model of work stress (Jones & Bright, 2001). The person–environment fit model takes the perspective that work stress occurs because a misfit exists between the abilities of the employee and the demands of the job. This substream of technostress research focuses on a subset of established work stressors which have a significant technology component as a means for operationalising technostress.

## 2.5.1. Multidimensionality and measurement

In both sub-streams of technostress research, technostress is measured as the external events or stressors that lead to an internal state of stress, with no direct measure of the resulting internal state. Tarafdar and collaborators (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2007, 2010) developed scales to measure five technostress creators which serve as a proxy for technostress. These technostress creators have been operationalised as reflective sub-dimensions of a second-order construct, although only four of the five dimensions are often used (Tarafdar et al., 2015). Alternatively, Ayyagari et al. (2011) adapted and developed new scales to measure five work stressors as a proxy for technostress, but modelled the stressors independently of each other (i.e., not as a part of a second-order construct). In both sub-streams, the lack of a direct measure of technostress, conceptualised as a user’s internal state and operationalised as a unidimensional construct, has limited the validation of the stressors (i.e., antecedents), which serve as a proxy for technostress. Ayyagari et al. (2011) comes closest by measuring strain using an adapted scale of work exhaustion or burnout (Moore, 2000), but the items describe end-stage exhaustion rather than technostress. Users can experience strain or technostress without reaching the end-stage state of burnout (Schaufeli & Maslach, 1993), as burnout is marked by exhaustion and cynicism (King & Sethi, 1997), and is a condition of severe strain resulting from prolonged exposure to stressors (Pawlowski, Kaganer, & Cater, 2007). Riedl echoes this measurement concern, noting that the literature has examined antecedent, moderating and outcome variables, but has yet to look at direct measures of IT stress, or the activation of relevant biological stress systems (2012).

## 2.5.2. Antecedents and outcomes

The antecedents and outcomes for technostress documented in the literature are depicted in a nomological network in Figure 3, and are listed in Table A1.3. Antecedents are first discussed followed by outcomes.

2.5.2.1. Technology characteristics (antecedent). Ayyagari et al. (2011) identified a range of technology-related antecedents to technostress based on the existing literature on work stress. These antecedents include usefulness, complexity (or ease of use), reliability, pace of change, presenteeism, and perceived anonymity, and are all operationalised as perceptions of technology features. System breakdown has also been examined (Riedl et al., 2012).

2.5.2.2. Organisational/Environment characteristics (antecedent). Two groups of organisational (environment) characteristics have been identified in the literature as being antecedents, or components, of technostress: (1) technostress creators by Tarafdar et al. (2007), and stressors by Ayyagari et al. (2011).

Ayyagari et al. (2011) also identified five work stressors relevant to IT work based on Moore (2000): overload, job insecurity, privacy invasion, role ambiguity, and work–home conflict. 2.5.2.3. Individual characteristics (antecedent). Several individual characteristics are described as control variables in the technostress literature, including age, gender, and negative afectivity (Ayyagari et al., 2011). While relationships were not proposed, the literature suggests that older users, female users, and users with greater negative afectivity are more likely to experience technostress.

2.5.2.4. Behaviours and physiological reactions (outcomes). Given that psychologists Brod (1984) and Rosen and Weil (1997) conceptualise technostress as a psychological disorder with direct and psychosomatic health responses, technostress could be associated with reactions and behaviour such as irritability, headaches, nightmares, resistance to or avoidance of computers, or outright refusal to use computers (Brod, 1984). However, these relationships have not been empirically tested. Research on the physiological changes associated with technostress has measured levels of neuroendocrine markers such as salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase as a proxy for technostress (Galluch, 2009; Galluch, Grover, & Thatcher, 2015; Riedl et al., 2012; Tams, 2011).

2.5.2.5. Perceptions of evaluations (outcomes). While there has been limited study of direct behaviours in response to technostress, outcomes such as user perceptions of evaluations have been studied. Stress from the use of technology is believed to influence work-related conflict and overload (Tarafdar et al., 2007). When technology-related stress persists, users may approach an extreme end point of burnout or exhaustion (Ayyagari et al., 2011) as well as a loss of job satisfaction, performance (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2015) and productivity (Tarafdar et al., 2007). These forms of strain are undesirable because they are also known drivers of negative organisational outcomes such as turnover intentions (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2013; Moore, 2000) and individual conditions such as poor mental health (Faragher, Cass, & Cooper, 2005), although IS research is yet to explore these longer term efects of technostress.

## 2.6. Research gaps and integrated nomological network

Based on the existing literature, as documented in Figures 1–3, an integrated nomological net of computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress is depicted in Figure 4. Despite the commonalities among these constructs, there is little overlap in the studied antecedents and outcomes of these concepts, and there is also a large number of proposed but untested relationships. Other gaps identified in the literature include definitional problems, measurement issues, and diferent streams of research that need to be merged and synthesised, as further described below.

Definitional problems with the negative afective responses depicted in Figure 4 include the overlap between CA and technophobia, the diferent multi-dimensional representations of technostress, and the lack of a consistent, comprehensive representation of the three concepts as distinct but related. The IS literature generally describes CA as normal levels of nervousness and concern when interacting with computers while technophobia or computerphobia is described as the more extreme and neurotic fear of using computers (Chua et al., 1999; Tarafdar et al., 2010). Yet, technophobia is also defined as the combination of CA and negative attitudes towards technology, which are not typically defined as extreme or neurotic. Technostress is defined diferently by two diferent streams of IS research (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2007), and its relationship with CA and technophobia has not been addressed.

Given these definitional problems, measurement problems also exist. Technophobia is often conceptualised as CA and negative attitudes toward computers, which do not address the extreme and neurotic aspect of the construct, and no direct measure of technophobia exists. Technostress is commonly measured by two diferent multidimensional scales from the two diferent research streams on technostress (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2007), which assess the antecedents (stressors) to technostress. No direct, reflective scale exists for this important construct. A unidimensional, reflective scale is needed to directly measure technostress and to validate the two sets of independent antecedents to technostress that are being used in the literature (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2007). Measurement guidelines (i.e., Petter, Straub, & Rai, 2007), strongly support the need for a reflective measure of technostress (a second order construct), with the existing, multi-dimensional antecedents serving as formative, first order dimensions.

As shown in Figure 4, the potential relationships between the three negative afective constructs are not well-documented, as no conceptual or empirical relationships have been proposed with technostress and the other two constructs. Given that all three concepts are negative afective responses to technology, we would expect some overlap in antecedents and outcomes, but this overlap is also not documented in the existing IS literature. For example, individual characteristics are described as antecedents to all three constructs, but Tables A1.1–A1.3 show that diferent sets of individual characteristics have been studied as determinants CA, technophobia, and technostress. Technology and organisational characteristics have been discussed as antecedents to technostress but not for technophobia. Similarly, performance has been discussed as an outcome of technostress but not CA and technophobia, and no relationship has been proposed between technophobia and perceptions of evaluations. CA has been operationalised as anxiety to computers in general, and to specific applications. Last, there has been limited exploration of objective outcomes (e.g., physiological responses, time spent and accuracy) that may result from these negative afective responses.

These gaps in the literature are not surprising given that no common theoretical framework has been applied to guide our understanding of these negative responses to technology. A theoretical foundation is needed to better define and operationalise these concepts and relate them to one another, and to other afective responses to technology. ## 3. Applying the afective response model (ARM)

Given the lack of a solid theoretical framework for understanding afective response to technology, Zhang (2013) produced the ARM, based on recent literature from the field of psychology, which provides consensus on the meanings and structures of afect-related phenomena (Barrett & Russell, 1999; Rosenberg, 1998; Russell, 2003, 2009; Watson & Clark, 1984; Watson, Wiese, Vaidya, & Tellegen, 1999; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). ARM is a comprehensive model of afective concepts that was selected as a suitable theoretical framework based on the three reasons below.

(1) ARM provides a helpful taxonomy that diferentiates afective concepts along five dimensions: residing, temporal nature, particular vs. general stimulus, object vs. behaviour stimulus, and process-based vs. outcome based evaluations.

(2) ARM applies to situations where direct experience with an ICT is the stimulus, which aligns with the focus of this research, excluding cases where the stimulus was the announcement of an ICT implementation.

(3) ARM provides propositions to support the relationships among afective constructs based on the dimensions or characteristics of the constructs.

The five afective dimensions of ARM (residing, temporal nature, particular vs. general stimulus, object vs. behaviour stimulus, and process- vs. outcome-based evaluations) (Zhang, 2013), serve as a taxonomy for categorising and comparing afective responses to technology. These five afective dimensions are described and then are applied to better define and diferentiate CA, technophobia, and technostress. The residing dimension describes the referent object of an afective response, with the options being a person (e.g., the mood of person), a technology stimulus (afective attribute of technology), or the intersection of person and technology stimulus (an afective evaluation of technology). The temporal dimension describes whether the afective response is more permanent or fleeting (trait vs. state), while the object/behaviour stimulus describes whether the response is directed towards the technology or towards a specific behaviour with the technology. The general vs. specific dimension describes whether the afective response is applicable to a specific technology or a general class of technologies, and the process vs. outcome based dimension diferentiates whether the response occurs during use of a technology or repre sents an overall outcome and extended exposure to a technology. Appendix A2, Table A2.1, adapted from Zhang (2013), provides more detailed descriptions and examples of these five dimensions.

## 3.1. Classifying negative afective responses with the ARM taxonomy

Given the rich taxonomy of afect-related concepts provided by ARM, this paper now describes how the three negative, afective concepts reviewed in this paper (i.e., computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress) can be diferentiated based on the categorisations provided by ARM. In Zhang (2013), CA was categorised within the taxonomy, but technophobia and technostress were not. In the sections below, all three negative afective responses are categorised and compared by the five ARM dimensions. Table 2 depicts the ARM categories based on the five dimensions, and places CA, technophobia, and technostress within their respective categories along with other known and potential, negative afective responses to technology. For additional background information on important afective concepts see Appendix A2, Table A2.2.

(1) Residing nature: All three negative, afective responses are categorised as residing between an individual and an object, as CA, technophobia and technostress each represent an emotional response or evaluation by a user of technology. These three responses are not isolated to the individual (e.g., mood), and are not objective attributes of the technology itself (e.g., an hedonic interface). Thus, all three constructs are found on the right side of the ARM classification table (Table 2), under the heading “residing between person and stimulus.”

(2) Temporal dimension (state or trait): The temporal dimension describes whether an afective response is state-like (e.g., temporary, transient), or trait-like (e.g., persistent, permanent). All three constructs can be classified similarly as trait-like, as these negative afective responses tend to persist over time, and can be found on the right side of the ARM classification table (Table 2), under the heading “temporally unconstrained”. For example, an individual who is technophobic will likely continue to be fearful and neurotic about computers. Users who exhibit technostress, have accumulated negative feelings about a technology over repeated use of the technology, and are likely to continue feeling and exhibiting signs of technostress. CA, on the other hand, has been classified as both statelike and trait-like in the literature. It has been conceptualised as an on-going feeling towards what might happen when one uses computers (Chua et al., 1999; Heinssen et al., 1987; Rosen & Maguire, 1990; Zhang, 2013), but also referred to as state-like (Bostrom, Olfman, & Sein, 1990; Brosnan, 2002; Chua et al., 1999; Zhang, 2013), based on evidence that CA levels may change in response to an anxiety reduction programme (Weil et al., 1987). However, explicit tests of whether CA is state or trait by Beckers, Wicherts, and Schmidt (2007) conclude that CA appears to harbour components of trait anxiety, and such afective feelings about computers are considered as cumulative over time (Beckers & Schmidt, 2001). Thus, this paper takes the perspective that CA is temporally unconstrained, i.e., trait-like, a position consistent with the original classification of CA in ARM. Further, both technophobia and technostress are also temporally unconstrained, suggesting the need for a temporally constrained concept that can further our understanding of how afective responses to technology develop over time.

(3) Object or behaviour stimulus: The object vs. stimulus dimension helps to diferentiate CA and technostress from technophobia, as CA and technostress appear in the “Behaviour Stimulus” row, while technophobia appears in the “Object Stimulus” row of Table 2. The IS literature commonly treats CA as an anxiety associated with using ICTs (i.e., behaviour stimulus) (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999; Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002), or the prospect of using ICTs (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Technostress is similar, in that the expe rience of actually using a technology is necessary for technostress to occur (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Thus, both CA and technostress are responses to a behaviour stimulus (using an ICT). However, the literature on technophobia suggests that the extremely reactive behaviour of individuals who often qualify as being “phobic” appears to be directed at the object itself, without requiring use or interaction with the object. This may be key to understanding why technophobia is much more extreme and less common than CA. Consider a policeman who “developed such a complex about the computer console in his police car that he shot it” (Howard, 1986, p. 17) as an example of extreme behaviour that may result from technophobia. Thus, this paper takes the perspective that technophobia best represents an extreme response to an object stimulus.

(4) General or specific stimulus: The general vs. specific dimension provides further diferentiation, with CA and technophobia referring to computers in general (General Stimulus column of Table 2), and technostress referring to a specific technology (Particular Stimulus column). CA is regarded as a response to the prospect of general computer use (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999), and is most often measured as an afective response to using technology in general (Agarwal & Venkatesh, 2002; Brosnan, 1999; Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al., 2011). Similarly, technophobia is regarded as a more extreme or phobic response to computers in general. On the other hand, technostress is an accumulated afective response to a specific technology. For example, Ayyagari et al. (2011) surveyed users on work-specific IT such as enterprise systems, while Tarafdar et al. (2010) referenced specific computer applications, or categories of applications used in the respondents’ jobs.

(5) Process vs. Outcome based afective evaluation: Finally, CA and technophobia are afective responses to general stimuli and thus cannot be assessed for whether the response occurs during the process of using a specific technology or as an outcome of cumulative responses to a specific technology, according to ARM. Therefore, the process vs. outcome-based afective evaluation dimension applies only to technostress. Outcome-based afective evaluations are more in-depth, based on “goals, relevance, consequences, or overall take-away messages” (Zhang, 2013). Therefore, this paper categorises technostress as an outcome-based response derived from accumulated experiences with a specific technology.

Based on the five dimensions of ARM, CA, technophobia and technostress were categorised into diferent cells of Table 2, highlighting the diferences and similarities among these constructs. Contributions of this categorisation include classifying technophobia and technostress within the taxonomy (Zhang, 2013), which were not previously defined from this perspective. The comparison of the constructs through ARM also enables us to better understand how these constructs are distinct, and should be measured diferently. For example, the items measuring afective responses to the use of general technology should include wording about general technology, such as computers rather than specific software like spreadsheets, and actual use of the technology.

Further, classifying these constructs and other negative afective responses within the ARM taxonomy illuminated cells in the taxonomy that were empty. Theoretically, there should be a negative afective response within each cell of the taxonomy and an empty cell suggests that a negative afective response may have been overlooked in IS research. Two such cells were identified during this classification process, cell 5.2 (outcome-based evaluations of a specific technology object) and cell 4.0 (temporally constrained episodic afective evaluation of a technology). These gaps, which are noted in Table 2, are addressed below.

## 3.1.1. Afective fit – ARM cell 5.2

As shown in Table 2, cell 5.2 references a temporally unconstrained outcome-based evaluation towards a specific ICT object, but an applicable afective construct had not been assigned to this cell. During the literature review, an applicable afective concept emerged. Afective fit is the positive feeling experienced by an individual based on evaluation of an ICT design and is distinct from cognitive and physical fit (Te’eni, 2006). If the design of a specific ICT matches with the goals of using it, an emotional feeling of afective fit may occur (Avital & Te’eni, 2009). For example, a user wishing to carry out a wide range of mathematical operations on tabulated data, may experience afective fit if the interface is designed to look like a spreadsheet, e.g., as found in Microsoft Excel. The evaluation of the object interface (i.e., afective fit), is distinct from behaviour using the object. For example, an individual’s afective evaluation of Microsoft Excel as an ICT object (e.g., interface quality) is distinct from the positive feelings that arise from the act of using Microsoft Excel for a specific task and being successful (e.g., due to intuitiveness, ease of use, etc.). Afective fit, measured as emotional evaluation of visual complexity and other design features of a website, has been shown to influence subsequent behaviour towards the ICT object (Deng & Poole, 2010). Similarly, other IS research has studied evaluations of the ICT object using scales measuring satisfaction with an IT object (Wixom & Todd, 2005) and a decision aid (Hess, Fuller, & Mathew, 2006).

## 3.1.2. Technology induced state anxiety (TISA) – ARM cell 4

As shown in Table 2, cell 4.0 references a temporary, state-like negative afective response residing between a person and a technology-related stimulus, and was previously empty with no applicable afective concepts identified during the literature review. We propose that a new IS construct, technology-induced state-based anxiety (TISA), is needed to fill this gap and represent state-like feelings of uneasiness and anxiety during exposure to technology, which subsequently disappears when the technology is no longer present. CA, technophobia and technostress are trait-like and persistent and thus distinct from the proposed construct, TISA. TISA characterises a negative episode or encounter with technology and could be described as episodic stress or strain, providing a complement to positive state-like concepts in the IS literature, such as enjoyment. The proposed construct would also be correlated with neuro-physiological measures of anxiety that occur during a negative encounter or episode with technology.

An emotional episode like TISA occurs when (1) an event stimulates a response from an individual; (2) the event is appraised or evaluated by the individual; and (3) emotions result based on the appraisal and relevance of the event (Russell, 2003; Scherer, 2005; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). In this research, an ICT serves as the event that stimulates an initial response; is appraised by the individual based on relevance; and results in experienced emotion (Zhang, 2013). TISA provides a means to capture the temporary, emotion felt during a negative episode with ICT.

## 3.2. Relationships among negative afective responses

The classification of negative afective antecedents and responses to technology shown in Table 2 also suggests relationships among these constructs. While the ARM taxonomy (Zhang, 2013) describes how the various categories of afective constructs may be related, most relationships have not been empirically tested. This paper now proposes how the four concepts of interest (computer anxiety, technophobia, technostress and TISA) are related based on emotional episode models (Russell, 2003; Scherer, 2005; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996) and ARM (Zhang, 2013).

Emotional episode models take a process or circular perspective, as the emotions that result from an evaluation of multiple emotional episodes can become outcome-based evaluations and learned dispositions, which may in turn afect the afective response of an individual to another event (Russell, 2003; Scherer, 2005; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). As learned dispositions (trait-like responses), CA and technophobia may influence an individual’s initial response to an ICT stimulus. These trait-like negative dispositions toward ICT in general, may also influence the level of TISA that an individual experiences while using an ICT. In addition, the afective attributes of an ICT may influence the TISA experienced by a user. In other words, if an individual experiences CA whenever they use technology (or worse, is technophobic), then the individual is more likely to experience anxiety while using a specific ICT (i.e., TISA). Thus, an individual may experience TISA, a state-like negative response to using a specific ICT, based on learned predispositions, the afective attributes of an ICT, and other afect-related antecedents such as mood. Repeated episodes of TISA with an ICT can result in a more traitlike afective response to the specific ICT, in the form of technostress. From a process perspective (and circling back to CA and technophobia), the technostress felt towards a specific ICT could over time become a learned disposition toward all ICT in general, resulting in the trait-like conditions of CA, and possibly the more extreme response, technophobia.

Based on ARM and emotional episode models, we suggest that TISA, CA, technophobia, and technostress are unique, but correlated constructs. Formal propositions, which are instantiations of the high level propositions in ARM, are provided below:

An individual’s degree of apprehension towards ICT objects (technophobia) and apprehension towards using ICTs (computer anxiety) influences the TISA they feel when using a particular ICT.

P1b: The degree to which an individual experiences TISA while using ICTs increases their apprehension towards ICT objects (technophobia) and apprehension towards using ICTs (computer anxiety).

P2a: The degree to which an individual experiences TISA while using a particular ICT increases the degree to which the ICT is an ongoing source of discomfort and pressure to the individual (technostress).

P2b: The degree to which an ICT is an ongoing source of discomfort and pressure to the individual (technostress) increases TISA experienced by the individual while using that particular ICT.

P3a: An individual’s degree of apprehension towards ICT objects (technophobia) and apprehension towards using ICTs (computer anxiety) influences the degree to which they feel an on-going sense of discomfort and pressure with particular ICTs (technostress)

P3b: The degree to which an individual feels an on-going sense of discomfort, pressure or inadequacy with a particular technology (technostress) influences his/her degree of apprehension towards ICT objects in general (technophobia) and apprehension towards using ICTs in general (computer anxiety).

P4a: People who are apprehensive towards exploring and trying out ICTs (computer anxiety) are more likely to have a high aversion for ICT objects (technophobia).

P4b: People with a high aversion for ICT objects (technophobia) are also apprehensive towards exploring and trying out ICTs (computer anxiety).

## 4. Research method

Given the gaps previously identified in the literature and the research propositions above, a preliminary methodological task was to identify or develop scale items to measure the four negative afective constructs. The literature review documented the lack of direct measures for technostress and technophobia, thus new scales were developed for technophobia, technostress, and the new construct TISA. Two empirical studies were conducted to validate the scales, establish the discriminant validity of the four constructs, and to better understand the relationships between the constructs. A cross-sectional survey was first conducted to establish the validity of technostress, technophobia and computer anxiety. Existing formative dimensions of technostress and other known antecedents for all three constructs were included to provide a more comprehensive validation. A second, experimental study was required to better measure the new, episodic (short-lived) state, TISA. In an experimental context, the specific technology episode could be controlled and measurement improved by administering a survey right after the technology experience. Thus, the second, experimental study enabled appropriate measurement of TISA and further validation of the improved measures for technostress, technophobia and CA. The following sections describe the scale development process and both empirical studies.

## 4.1. Scale development

The scales used to measure the four negative afective responses to ICT are shown in Table 3. In creating the new scales for technostress, technophobia, and TISA, care was taken to clearly state the referent object and to ensure that the items were valid to the construct definitions based on the ARM dimensions. A new five-item, direct measure of technostress was created based on the perceived stress scale (Cohen, Kamarck, & Mermelstein, 1983), a reflective measure of an individual’s perceived general stress used extensively in research, by adapting the scale to stressful situations which involved the use of ICT. A new measure of technophobia was created by adapting existing specific phobia scales such as the fear of spiders questionnaire (Szymanski & O’Donohue, 1995) and the phobic beliefs scale (Chambless, Caputo, Bright, & Gallagher, 1984; Thorpe & Brosnan, 2007), such that the referent object was general ICT. The measurement scale for TISA was adapted from the state sub-scale of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) (Marteau & Bekker, 1992; Spielberger, Gorsuch, & Lushene, 1970), a commonly used measure of state anxiety. This scale prompts the respondent to focus attention on a recent ICT episode and report how strongly they experienced certain feelings. Finally, CA was measured using the existing, short form of the Computer Anxiety Rating Scale (Heinssen et al., 1987) commonly used in IS research (Hardin, Looney, & Fuller, 2014; Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002; Webster & Martocchio, 1992).

A multi-staged approach was taken to refine and validate the new measurement scales (Boudreau, Gefen, & Straub, 2001; Straub, Boudreau, & Gefen, 2004). The initial pool of items was reviewed by several researchers in IS and psychology. Following this, two pre-tests were conducted with an undergraduate sample and the results were used to refine the wording of the measurement scales, after which a pilot was conducted with a sample of workers. Due to space constraints, these pre-tests and pilot tests are not reported. Through this process, the scales were refined and shortened to the form listed above in Table 3. ## 4.2. Study 1 – Cross-sectional survey

The purpose of the first study was to validate measures for the three negative afective responses, which were the original focus of this research – CA, technophobia, and technostress, and explore the relationships among these constructs. An online survey was conducted which included direct measures for the three constructs, and for the technostress creators which have been used as multidimensional, indirect measures of technostress in past research (see Appendix A3, Table A3.1). Demographic questions were also included, as was a marker variable scale for assessing the possible efects of common method bias.

Given the need to survey individuals who had experience using computers, and were more likely to have experienced technostress (i.e., technostress is an outcome-based evaluation of using an ICT), a general sample of working adults were invited to take the survey through Amazon Mechanical Turk, an increasingly popular source of data for social science and behavioural research (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling, 2011; Goodman, Cryder, & Cheema, 2012; Steelman, Hammer, & Limayem, 2014). Participants were asked early in the survey about their use of computers at work. While all participants were compensated for their participation, only those individuals who reported using some ICT, 191 participants, were included in the data-set for analysis. This filter was necessary, as otherwise some participants would not be able to realistically respond to questions about technostress. Participants were asked to specify which category of ICT they used most frequently (i.e., ofice productivity, enterprise and database systems, and other ICTs), and then respond to survey questions referencing this specific category of ICT.

## 4.3. Study 2 – 2 × 2 experiment

A second study was conducted in a controlled experimental context to (1) appropriately measure the proposed new construct, TISA; (2) further validate the afective constructs measured in the first study; and (3) examine the relationships between the negative afective constructs. A controlled experiment was needed as TISA is a negative, afective response that is temporally constrained to an episode of technology use. In order to assess participants’ afective response to a specific, recent use of technology, an experimental design was employed in which participants used an ICT immediately prior to completing a survey. $\textup { A } 2 \times 2$ between subjects experiment was conducted with ICT familiarity (less/more) and task requirements (lesser/greater). These treatments were designed to create variation in TISA, by having participants use a less (more) familiar ICT to complete a task with greater (lesser) requirements. Using a less familiar ICT to complete greater task requirements is presumed to result in more TISA, and thus could demonstrate the distinction between TISA and technostress. Both TISA and technostress were measured in reference to the ICT assigned to participants. The sample used in this second study were 351 undergraduates enrolled in an introductory IT course at a large public university in the US.

## 4.3.1. Experimental procedure

Participants were first asked to complete a pre-task survey in which CA, technophobia and technostress towards an assigned technology (Microsoft PowerPointmore familiar; Paint-less familiar) were measured. After completing the pre-task survey, participants completed a design task in which they were asked to create a flyer. Depending upon their assigned treatments, participants were instructed to either design a flyer of their choosing (lower requirements) or to design a flyer that met specific requirements (higher requirements). Right after completing the design task, participants responded to a post-task survey in which TISA towards the assigned technology (Microsoft PowerPoint or Paint) was measured.

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

In the light of recent guidelines on theoretical contributions in IS (e.g., Rivard, 2014), this paper has a number of contributions. First, it applies an IS framework to differentiate afective concepts that seemingly overlap. As a result, this paper clarifies existing constructs, documents the diferences in how they have been conceptually and empirically studied and provides clear pathways for future research in this area. Our examination highlights the need to better understand the nature of computer anxiety, technophobia and technostress in accordance with the five dimensions of ARM, and to appropriately operationalise and measure these constructs. For example, research on technostress has identified the stressors and the resulting strain or burnout that results from technostress (Ayyagari et al., 2011), often conceptualising technostress as a second order construct with first order dimensions (Tarafdar et al., 2007), or even a formative first order construct (Tarafdar et al., 2015). However, existing research did not measure the higher order factor reflectively or formally model this multi-level construct. This oversight is problematic, as it confounds the internal state of technostress with the stressors that create technostress. In this paper, the overlap was resolved conceptually and empirically as formative measures of technostress creators are shown to be strongly related to a new, direct, reflective measure of technostress as an internal state.

Second, a new construct, TISA, was proposed to address a noted gap in the ARM taxonomy of afect-related responses to technology. This construct represents episodic afective responses to technology use that are temporary and do not persist over time. Yet, several similar emotional responses to multiple episodes of technology use can accumulate and become a persistent, outcome-based afective evaluation of that same technology. As an individual experiences TISA repeatedly, technostress can develop. Similarly, repeated TISA experiences can become learned responses, resulting in increased CA, or perhaps even technophobia related to technology in general. The relationships between these categories of afective responses had not been advanced in the literature, but are supported by ARM. Further, the absence of an appropriate concept to instantiate these patterns of efects (i.e., TISA) was a gap in this re-search area. The introduction of TISA provides a strong theoretical contribution as this construct explains how statelike emotional responses to technology can evolve into more permanent negative dispositions that may hinder performance outcomes with technology and future interactions to technology. Further, TISA may be critical to connecting research on afective responses to existing streams. For example, consider that recent research on ICT-enabled interruption in the workplace appropriately focuses on episodic stress rather than lasting or chronic stress (e.g., Galluch et al., 2015). Also, the growth of neuroIS methods, which focus on episodic measurements, makes it critical for such a state/transient afective concept to be proposed. Future research in this area can benefit from including perceptual measures of TISA in studies that examine the operational level of using technology, and then further connect TISA to lasting afective dispositions and use outcomes.

Third, in response to calls for research in ARM (Zhang, 2013) and to address gaps identified in the literature review, this paper develops several new scales. A reflective, direct measure of technostress as an internal state was developed. This presents a parsimonious and psychometrically valid alternative to the current use of stressors, as formative factors of technostress, to represent an internal state. A measure of technophobia was developed, as previously a composite of two or more existing afective concepts was used to measure this construct. Further, a measure of the new construct, TISA, was developed. The development of these new scales and the use of existing scales for CA and technostress creators, enabled a comprehensive construct validation exercise through two empirical studies. The results suggest that these constructs are distinct, yet related, and clear up potential for confusion in the literature, laying the groundwork for future empirical studies on negative afective responses to technology.

Fourth, this paper presents an integrated nomological network which provides a pathway to understanding the associations between sociological and psychological approaches to studying computer anxiety, technophobia, and technostress. Sociological theory and psychological theory typically operate on diferent levels, as such, this kind of convergence is desirable for the advancement of our understanding of important concepts (Levinson, 1964). For example, studies that focus on how individual characteristics relate to organisation level outcomes such as job satisfaction or job performance (i.e., psychological perspective) will do well to consider how organisational conditions (i.e., sociological perspective) or task characteristics can influence those outcomes, or take them into account as control variables at the least. This integration of additional categories of afective antecedences contributes by extending ARM beyond the two afective antecedences it considers (individuals and technology).

This research also provides an extended foundation for the increased study of cyberpathologies and disorders such as computer and internet addiction, computer rage and other obsessive compulsive technology use behaviours. In the nomological network, these behaviours would be represented as outcomes of these afective concepts. For example, research on technology addiction can integrate afective evaluations to better understand what triggers overly high levels of engagement with technology that becomes compulsive and interferes with daily life (Turel, Serenko, & Giles, 2011). Addictions are motivated both from pleasure-seeking motivations and the desire to avoid the aversive symptoms of withdrawal (Robinson & Berridge, 2000), therefore these concepts may have interesting relationships with problematic patterns of technology use. Further, technology addictions are serious negative outcomes from technology use that are real problems in the work and personal lives of many, and the theoretical contributions of this research provide better insight into how these outcomes develop based on users’ negative responses to technology.

## 7.2. Practical implications

This paper also delivers strong practical implications for the design of technology interfaces and the related training and ongoing support within organisations. A comprehensive structure for the antecedents of the three dark side variables provides increased insight into the factors that could be modified to minimise the negative afective experience on three levels – individual, technology, and organisational/environmental. These can be combined in innovative ways to curb technostress in organisations. For example, managerial support could be enhanced and the interface could be modified to reduce TISA, which should in turn reduce technostress. Further, individual characteristics associated with these negative afective responses could also be assessed to determine which users might benefit from individual management or intervention with these dark side variables.

Similarly, a better understanding of the outcomes that may result from these negative afective responses can assist organisations in being more aware that such responses are occurring and in understanding the seriousness or intensity of these responses. For example, routine assessments could help organisations in diagnosing when negative afective responses have crossed the threshold from the more benign computer anxiety to a more extreme technophobia. Such measures could also enable organisations to monitor how the downstream efects of technology-related stress and anxieties threaten employee retention and organisational performance.

Third, a distinction between generalised and non-generalised (specific) negative evaluations of technology has not been commonly applied in the IS literature and might be more appropriate going forward. This diferentiation, empirically shown in this paper, points out that the context appropriate construct and measure should be used in empirical studies to provide more appropriate operationalisation and increased explanatory power.

## 7.3. Limitations

This paper describes a literature review and conceptual discussion of negative, afective responses to technology, with two empirical studies conducted to validate and diferentiate four negative afective responses to technology. While several positive, afective responses to technology were highlighted, an empirical examination of positive afective responses to technology was not conducted. Further, cognitive responses to technology were excluded from the review and empirical studies. An integration of negative and positive afective responses and cognitive responses is needed to fully understand the impact of afective responses to technology on important outcomes.

## 8. Conclusion and future research

This paper set out to identify the range of antecedents and outcomes for the concepts of technostress, computer anxiety and technophobia, and to diferentiate among the three constructs. An extensive literature search was carried out to identify research from which individual nomological networks and an integrated nomological network of antecedents and outcomes was created. ARM was then applied to explain the relationship between these focal constructs and other afective constructs, a process which led to the identification of a new construct with foundations in the existing literature (technology induced state anxiety; TISA), and shed more light on the range of afective responses to ICT stimulus. Brod (1984), a pioneer of technostress research, opined that stakeholders needed to carefully re-evaluate many elements of computer technology integration into our daily lives in order to make technology less machine-centred and more human-centred. Understanding exactly how humans react to technology is essential to this undertaking. This work represents an attempt to integrate forty years of research on negative emotional responses to technology and recent theoretical advances (ARM) into a clear path for future research on how to better understand and predict the responses of individuals to technology.

For future research, the untested relationships which were identified from past literature (Figure 4) may be tested to confirm our understanding of this area and clear up any areas of confusion. Similarly, propositions from ARM are yet to be empirically tested in the IS literature and such work is clearly needed for us to gain a more nuanced understanding of how these negative afective concepts relate to each other over time, and impact technology-related performance outcomes. In addition, a better understanding of the process by which IT attributes (both physical and system attributes) might lead to TISA can help designers think of dynamic ways of reducing TISA using design. For instance, it is known that more beautiful interfaces are considered easier to use (Tractinsky, Katz, & Ikar, 2000), thus perhaps the adoption of smart and dynamic use-session experiences can be used to improve the degree to which an individual feels positive afect. This is not completely futuristic as present day video game design is known to utilise dynamic dificulty adjustment (DDA) to change parameters of the game environment based on the player’s abilities to prevent them either becoming too bored or too frustrated.

Further, research is needed to theoretically and empirically connect afective responses to technology with behaviour and performance outcomes. IS research now acknowledges that emotions can play a critical role in system use, and models like ARM need to be connected to post-adoptive behaviour (Zhang, 2013). An important next step in IS research on afect is to examine which afective responses to technology most influence performance during episodes of system use, and which responses to technology are most influential on longterm behaviour such as continued use or avoidance.

A final important direction for future research is the consideration of positive, afective concepts that result from interaction with IT objects or from using IT, and an integration of positive and negative afective concepts. It has been widely documented that positive and negative concepts are distinct (Huppert & Whittington, 2003; Warr, Barter, & Brownbridge, 1983; Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988) and there is need for positive, afective concepts to be aforded a similar focused review and validation using ARM. Further, a comprehensive empirical study of both positive and negative afective responses to technology is needed to understand the distinction and the efects on performance outcomes. By examining negative afective concepts, this work seeks to lay a foundation for future research in this direction.

## Validating technostress scale with technostress-creators (sample of workers)

The new, six-item technostress measure was validated using the existing scales for the five technostress-creators, which are commonly represented as a formative second-order construct with five, first-order reflective dimensions (Tarafdar et al., 2007). An exploratory factor analysis was first performed on the technostress-creators using pilot data run and revealed weak loadings and some cross-loading of items among dimensions. In order to maintain the “spirit” of the initial technostress creators, which are widely adopted in the IS literature, these items were retained with only minor modifications to clearly specify a referent object.<sup>1</sup> The slightly modified measures of the technostress creators were then used with the workers sample described in study 1. Three progressively comprehensive models are introduced below and include (1) the five technostress creators, (2) the five technostress creators modelled as a second-order latent construct, and (3) the five technostress creators modelled as a second-order latent construct that was allowed to covary with the new reflective measure of technostress. The three respective models are described in more detail below.

## Appendix A5

## Common method bias evaluation

This appendix describes analysis conducted to test for the efect of common method variance (CMV) on the observed relationships between afective concepts. To achieve this, Harman’s one-factor test was first performed (Podsakof, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakof, 2003). The emergence of a single factor that accounts for the majority of variance from all variables is a sign that CMV exists. In addition, the marker-variable technique, a more conservative test, was also used (Lindell & Whitney, 2001; Malhotra, Sung, & Patil, 2006; Podsakof et al., 2003). Similar to Malhotra et al. (2006), the three-item “fashion involvement index” (Tigert, Ring, & King, 1976) was included in both studies to be used as the marker variable since no relationship is expected between this scale and measures of the focal constructs.

Workers sample: Harmon’s single-factor test (computer anxiety, technostress, technophobia, technostress-creators and marker variable) showed that 27% of the variance in all variables was explained by a single factor, suggesting that CMV is not a concern. Following this, the marker variable technique for identifying CMV was conducted. A CFA model in which items measuring computer anxiety, technophobia, technostress and the marker variable was allowed to load on distinct factors. The results indicate that this baseline model showed good fit $( \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 5 9 , \mathrm { T L I } = 0 . 9 4 9 , \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 6 7 , \mathrm { S R M R } = 0 . 0 5 3 , \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 8 2 . 9 1 , \mathrm { d } f = 9 8 )$ The correlations between the marker variable and these constructs was then assessed, with all of these correlations being statistically non-significant $( p \ge 0 . 1 9 3 )$ , and having an average correlation of 0.043. According to guidelines in Malhotra et al. (2006), the CMV-adjusted correlations were computed and the diferences between the original and CMV-adjusted correlations were seen to be small $( \Delta r \leq 0 . 0 2 9 )$ (shown in Table A4.1). This indicates a negligible impact of CMV on the relationships observed. Finally, a model in which items load on respective factors and also load on the marker-variable was fit. A significant improvement of this model over the baseline model would indicate significant CMV exists. The results $( \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 5 8 , \mathrm { T L I } = 0 . 9 4 8 $ , RM-SEA = 0.068, SRMR = 0.053, χ<sup>2</sup> = 182.905, df = 97) did not suggest any significant changes to model fit $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 5 , \Delta \mathrm { d } f = 1$ $p = 1 . 0 0 )$ . This supports the conclusion that common method bias is not substantial in this analysis. Therefore, the rest of the analysis in this paper is conducted without including the common method factor.

Student Sample: The Harmon’s single-factor test showed that 30% of the variance in all variables (computer anxiety, technos tress, technophobia, TISA, marker variable), was explained by a single factor, suggesting that CMB is not a concern. Similar to the above procedure, a measurement model with computer anxiety, technophobia, technostress, TISA and the marker variable loading uniquely was tested. The baseline model indicates good fit (CFI = 0.965, TLI = 0.958, RMSEA = 0.057, SRMR = 0.032, $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 \bar { 7 } 9 . 7 5 , \mathrm { d } f = 1 7 9 )$ . However, a weak correlation between the marker variable and two constructs, technophobia and computer anxiety, was observed $( p = 0 . 0 4 1$ and p = 0.006, respectively). The average absolute correlation of the variables with the method factor was 0.084, a value treated as the method correlation (rM) partialled out of the CMV-adjusted correlations shown in Table A5.1. The diference in relationships due to CMV-adjustment was also relatively small $( \Delta \dot { r } \le 0 . 0 6 9 )$ ), supporting the conclusion that CMB is not a major concern in this sample. Finally, to test changes to the model when items are allowed to load on the marker variable, an additional model was fit (CFI = 0.964, TLI = 0.958, RMSEA = 0.057, SRMR = 0.032, χ<sup>2</sup> = 379.69, df  =  178). A $\chi ^ { 2 }$ diference test confirms that this new model does not have statistically worse fit than the baseline mode $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 6 , \Delta \mathrm { d } f = 1 , p = 0 . 8 0 6 )$ , supporting the existing evidence that CMV is not substantial in this analysis. Therefore, the rest of the analysis using this sample is conducted without including the common method factor.
