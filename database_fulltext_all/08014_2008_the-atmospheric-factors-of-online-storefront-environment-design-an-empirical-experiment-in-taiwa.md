# The atmospheric factors of online storefront environment design: An empirical experiment in Taiwan

Chin-Shan Wu <sup>a</sup>, Fei-Fei Cheng <sup>b</sup>, David C. Yen <sup>c,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of E-Commerce, WuFeng Institute of Technology, Chiayi, Taiwan

<sup>b</sup> Department of MIS, Southern Taiwan University, Tainan, Taiwan

<sup>c</sup> Department of DSC/MIS, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 November 2006 Received in revised form 12 May 2008 Accepted 22 July 2008 Available online 11 September 2008

Keywords: Atmospheric factors E-commerce Experiment Online storefront Approach–avoidance behavior

## A B S T R A C T

The purpose of our study was to identify the effect of atmospheric factors such as music and color on participants’ emotional responses and subsequent shopping behavior in an online store setting. In this study, a laboratory experiment was conducted to test our hypotheses. The experiment was a 2 (music: fast/slow) - 2 (color: warm/cool) between-subjects factorial design with one control group. The results indicated that both music and color factors had a significant effect on participants’ emotional response, which in turn influenced their intention to purchase. Specifically, participants felt more aroused and experienced greater pleasure when they were exposed to fast-tempo music and a warm color website than those people who experienced slow-tempo music and cool colors. In addition, both pleasure and arousal emotions were significant predictors of approach–avoidance intention. We expected that a welldesigned website with adequate music and color attributes would create a desired environment and thus entice and retain target customers. Our findings should help in deciding on ambient factors in online website design. Although studies on atmospheric effects in traditional stores have been abundant, knowledge of their effect in the online retail context has been sparse.

## 1. Introduction

Kotler [9] suggested that people respond to a ‘‘total product’ perspective when making a purchase decision, rather than simply reacting to the product or service being offered. One significant features of this perception is the atmosphere of the salesroom. Thus the environment plays an important role in shaping a shopping experience and increasing earnings for a business. In traditional retail stores, the shopping atmosphere can influence consumers’ emotional responses, purchase intention, as well as their shopping behavior [2,3].

Although atmospheric effects in traditional stores have been thoroughly studied, for the effect of these factors in online retailing is limited, though designers of virtual settings will try to influence consumers’ shopping behavior through incorporating atmospheric factors [1], which, in physical stores, includes music, color, odor, and other sensory cues, but are constrained in an online store environment, where only visual and auditory cues can be manipulated.

We decided to examine the effect of atmospheric variables on virtual storefront shoppers. The specific focus of our study was the effects of music and color on participants’-induced emotions and subsequent behavioral intention to purchase a product.

## 2. Theoretical background

We developed a model which assumed that the environment influenced how people felt and how this, in turn, should drive their response (as significant mediators between environmental stimuli and people’s behavior).

## 2.1. Dimensions of the shopping environment

Shopping environments have many design elements that may be classified into three dimensions: ambient conditions, space and functionality, and signs, symbols, and artifacts. Sautter et al. [17] suggested four major categories for traditional servicescapes: ambient conditions, functional/spatial elements, signs/symbols, and social elements; four elements were added for online store environments: vividness, interactivity, symbolism, and social aspects.

IS literature about online shopping environments generally focuses on web site design covering multimedia use, navigability, promotional activities, delivery, and product support. In addition, several success factors of web site design have been suggested: information quality, learning capability, playfulness, system quality, system use, and service quality. Functional requirements for an estore design from a consumer-oriented perspective were given in [11]. Consumers are more likely to shop at well-designed websites.

A process model of web site success in an online shopping context was developed and empirically validated in Ref. [19] by identifying the role of pleasure as a key mediating variable. This work suggested that it partially mediated the evaluations–success relationship and that there was significant support for a direct relationship between web site evaluations and success.

In our study, we attempted to measure the impact of sensory ambient conditions of a virtual storefront on consumers’ feelings. Ambient conditions are characteristics of the environment pertaining to our five senses, including lighting and color, shape, and sound. Even when not consciously noted, these factors affect people’s emotional well-being, perceptions, attitudes, and behavior. Smart design of store environment with adequate atmospheric cues can elicit positive behavioral responses from consumers.

## 2.1.1. Music and emotional responses

Bitner [4] argued that music is a key ambient condition of the servicescape and that music influences people’s emotions and physiological feelings, and mood.

Various structure characteristics of music, such as tempo, volume, and harmony, influence consumer response and behavior. Bruner [5] identified three main factors: time (rhythm, tempo, and phrasing), pitch (melody, keys, mode, and harmony), and texture (timbre, orchestration, and volume). Each of these conveys some affective meanings; for example, with a fast-tempo, animation and happiness are expressed and loudness can suggest animation or proximity.

Fast-tempo music will increase arousal levels were found to lead to an increase in physiological arousal among consumers, leading them to walk faster and to talk and eat more quickly in restaurants. We therefore hypothesize that

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ Respondents exposed to an environment with fast-tempo music have higher level of arousal than those exposed to an environment with slow-tempo music.

Further, Watson [18] suggested that songs characterized as very exciting or very happy were perceived to be louder while soft pieces were perceived as peaceful or serious. Musical compositions composed in major modes played at a fast-tempo were generally rated as ‘‘happy’’; tunes written in minor modes played slowly were considered to be ‘‘sad’’. Thus, the following hypothesis was proposed:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ Respondents exposed to environment with fast-tempo music have higher level of pleasure than those exposed to environment with slow-tempo music.

## 2.1.2. Color and emotional responses

The de facto system of psychological research, the Munsell System, defines colors in three dimensions: hue, value, and chroma [15]. Hue is the pigment, normally understood as blue, red, yellow, etc. Value is the darkness or lightness of the color relative to a scale that extends from pure black to pure white. Chroma refers to saturation, where highly saturated colors contain more of the pigment. Consequently, low chroma colors are dull while high chroma colors are rich and deep.

In general, hues are classified as either warm with a high wavelength, such as red, orange, or yellow, or cool, such as green, or blue. It has been believed that high wavelength colors possess strong excitation and thus high arousal potential to induce elated mood states.

Prior empirical studies have investigated the relationship between color and people’ mood states. Warm color is associated with arousal and higher levels of anxiety, whereas cool colors have been found to reduce arousal levels and elicit such emotions as peacefulness, calmness, and love. Thus, we hypothesized that

H . Respondents exposed to environment with warm background color have higher level of arousal than those exposed to environment with cool background color.

Mehrabian and Russell [14] suggested a ranking of pleasantness of hues (at constant saturation and brightness) that includes blue, green, purple, red, and yellow colors, in a descending order. Moreover, prior research has suggested that blue objects should produce more positive outcomes than red while cool colors were associated with happiness. Thus, we proposed that

H . Respondents exposed to environment with cool background color have higher level of pleasure than those exposed to environment with warm background color.

## 2.2. The emotional states and behavioral intention

Mehrabian and Russell suggested that three basic emotional states mediated approach–avoidance behavioral intention in environmental situations. The three emotions were: pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD). This refers to the degree to which a person feels good, joyful, happy, or satisfied with a situation; arousal is the degree to which a person feels excited, stimulated, alert, or active and dominance is the extent to which the individual feels in control of a situation.

Moreover, Mehrabian and Russell suggested that individuals react to places with either approach or avoidance behavior. The former includes all positive behaviors that might be directed at a place, such as desire to stay, explore, work, and affiliatem while avoidance behavior reflects the opposite: a desire to leave, etc. It is difficult to test these ideas because stores would not wish to be seen as unpleasant. Therefore, we hypothesized and tested only the effect of pleasant environments.

H . Participants’ level of pleasure is positively related to thei behavioral intention.

$\mathbf { H _ { 6 } } .$ Participants’ level of arousal is positively related to their behavioral intention.

## 2.3. Atmosphere studies in virtual settings

A 2001 study [6] defined a model to describe how an online retailing store environment influenced shoppers’ emotional and cognitive states and thus determined shopping outcomes. The model was then empirically tested [7]; the results supported the propositions and showed a significant effect of site atmospherics on shopper attitudes, satisfaction, and various approach–avoidance behavior during a shopping episode. Atmospheric cues were manipulated as high or low task-relevant rather than specific atmospheric ones. Although they empirically proved that the online atmosphere qualities affected shopper response, the effect of particular cues was, however, unclear.

In addition, the atmospheric variables were external and internal, layout and design, point-of-purchase, and customer services expected to contribute to the satisfaction of Internet consumers [13]. However, the variables proposed for Internet environments did not reflect the ambient dimension of atmosphere originally suggested by Kotler.

## 3. Research methodology

The research framework of our study is shown in Fig. 1; in it, two independent variables (music and color) were manipulated and two dependent constructs were measured. Further, subjects’ emotional states were assumed to mediate the effect of the environmental stimuli on behavioral intent. The PC monitor is narrower and limited in e-commerce settings. Therefore, all environmental factors can influence Internet consumers’ emotional states only through an area in which Internet consumers perceive the online storefront mainly through their eyes and ears.

In addition, many studies have investigated the effect of these two independent variables. In order to choose appropriate substance to be studied, we had to consider another feature of the online environment: shoppers can control their shopping environment, both by altering the volume of the speaker and brightness of the monitor. Therefore, music tempo rather than volume, color hue rather than value or brightness were selected as the variables to be studied.

In understanding consumers’ response to the stimuli, two emotional dimensions were measured: a model that eliminated the dominance dimension had been suggested by [16], which found that pleasure and arousal were sufficient to represent people’s emotional response in many environments. These reactions, in turn, influenced the consumers’ shopping intention. Thus only pleasure and arousal dimensions were used to measure participants’ emotional state in our study.

Finally, the subjects’ behavioral intention was measured in terms of an approach–avoidance instrument of Mehrabian and Russell.

## 3.1. Definition and manipulation

The first independent variable to be selected was the music tempo. As we were studying its influence on the individuals emotion, music type, volume, and performing medium were held the same across fast and slow-tempo conditions; i.e., we controlled the effect of performing medium by using only instrumental music performed in Panpipe, while the music was popular broadcast material and by avoiding vocal music, we removed the effect of the song lyrics and controlled for any unintended effect due to a subjects’ preference. Two songs from the same music collection (performed by the same group) were used, one fast and the other slow. Music tempo was manipulated by using a metronome to vary the beats per minute (BPM). Listeners have been found to prefer tempos in a range from 68 to 178 BPM [8]. Therefore, we considered music tempo of 72 BPM or less as slow and 92 or more as fast.

The second independent variable was hue. Blue is the coldest type of the cool colors, and it is near the opposite end of the spectrum from red which is considered the hottest. Thus, red and blue were used to manage the warmth of the background of the store environment in our study.

## 3.2. Experimental design

The experiment used a 2 - 2, between-subject, factorial design. One control was included as a basis of comparison. The design of experimental and control groups are shown in Table 1.

With 30 undergraduate students in each condition, there were a total of 150 participants. They were assigned randomly into the groups. An online gift shop served as the context of our study.

## 3.3. Facilities and procedures

The experiment was conducted in a computer laboratory equipped with 40 PCs. In order to avoid participants unintentionally turning off the sound on the computer, the music was played through the laboratory sound system. For this reason, the experiment was divided into three major parts, depending on the manipulation of the music: the first was the fast-tempo music condition, in which the participants were assigned into the warm or cool color condition randomly after they entered into the laboratory; the second was the same as the first, except for the music tempo which was slow; the third, the control, which was conducted in the laboratory without music the color treatment was removed, resulting in a background that was white.

Upon entering the laboratory, subjects were given an introduction to the experiment. They were told that this is a study in understanding their purchase of various products. After this, participants were told to enter the experimental web site, which randomly assigned them to one of the hue conditions. After they finished browsing the web site, the participants were asked to answer a questionnaire with three sections dealing with: emotional response measurement; approach–avoidance behavior intention measurement; and their demographics. The personal information included age, gender, degree of experience in using the Internet, and online shopping experience.

## 3.4. Design

The content of the web site was identical in all treatments, except for the background color, the web site had 10 pages. The first had a brief description of the online store, which sold various kinds of purses, necklaces, pens, and watches. The second to the ninth gave a variety of product information, one product per page. An illustration of the second page in the cool color treatment is shown in Fig. 2. Finally, the questionnaire that measured participants’ emotional and behavioral intention was collected along with demographic information.

## 3.5. Measurement

We employed Mehrabian and Russell’s 12-item semantic differential scale modified for online shopping to measure participants’ emotional response to the surroundings in terms with a 7-point Likert scale. Six item pairs were used to measure the arousal dimension of emotions: happy–unhappy, pleased– annoyed, satisfied–unsatisfied, contented–melancholic, hopeful– despairing, and relax–bored. The other six items dealt with the pleasure dimensions: stimulated–relaxed, excited–calm, frenzied– sluggish, jittery–dull, wide awake–sleepy, and aroused–unaroused.

These measures had been employed [12] to test the effect of scent and music on a subject’s in-store evaluations and behavior; the result of that study displayed acceptable reliability of more than 0.70. The reported Cronbach alphas of arousal and pleasure were 0.83 and 0.89, respectively.

Participants were asked to rate their level of agreement with the 6 statements on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree and 7 = strongly agree). The statements in our study included:

(a) I enjoy shopping in this online store;

(b) I like this online store;

(c) I would like to recommend this online store to my friends;

(d) I intend to revisit this online store again in the future;

(e) I intend to buy something in this online store;

(f) I would like to explore more about this online store.

Higher scores on this measurement scale indicated a high level of environment-approaching intention.

Because our experiment was conducted in Taiwan, the instrument had to be translated into Chinese. In order to guarantee consistency after translation, a lecturer proficient in both Mandarin and English was asked to retranslate the Chinese version measurement into English. Another lecture whose native language is English was then asked to compare the original and translated questionnaire. In this operation, no semantic differences were found.

## 4. Data analysis and results

## 4.1. Sample characteristics

Out of the 150 participants, 77 (51%) were male and 73 (49%) were female. The majority (64%) of the respondents ranged in age between 20 and 21 years old, and the average age was 21.8. Participants’ average year of experience using the Internet was 5.5, and 54% had 4–6 years of experience. Finally, most of the participants (71%) had experience with Internet shopping, with their average years being 1.4. Ninety-nine participants (66%) had 1–3 years of Internet shopping experience.

## 4.2. Tests of reliability and validity

To test construct validity, a factor analysis with varimax rotation was carried out on the 12 emotional response items. Table 2 shows the factor pattern matrix with the loadings of each item, reliability, and variance explained by each construct. Our results suggest that all factors emerged with no crossconstruct loadings above 0.7, indicating good discriminant validity. The instrument also demonstrated convergent validity with factor loadings exceeding 0.6 for each construct. These results, therefore, confirm that each of the constructs was unidimensional and factorially distinct and that all items used to operationalize a particular construct was loaded onto a single factor.

In our study, factor 1 (pleasure) accounted for 63% and factor 2 (arousal) for 17% of the total variance. This result suggested that the two emotional dimensions adequately represent the human reaction to environments.

The internal consistency was assessed by computing Cronbach alpha. These coefficients are shown for each of the constructs. As indicated, all of the constructs in our study exhibited Cronbach alpha of 0.9 or higher, which surpasses the recommended reliability threshold of 0.70 or more. Thus, internal consistency of the constructs’ items was established satisfactorily.

## 4.4. The relationship between emotional states and behavioral intention

The result of regression analysis of the pleasure and arousal dimensions against approach/avoidance is shown in Table 5. Overall, the regression equation was significant at the $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ level and the two emotional responses accounted for 43.7% of the variation in intended approach–avoidance behavior for the online storefront. Moreover, both pleasure and arousal responses were the significant predictors of approach–avoidance intention. This supported hypotheses $\mathrm { H } _ { 5 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 6 } .$ Descriptive statistics information demonstrated that participants in a fast-tempo music condition had a higher level of approach behavioral intention than participants in a slow-tempo condition. Similarly, warm color resulted in a higher level of purchasing intention. These findings further confirmed our assumption of the relationship between respondents’ emotional states and their intention in approach– avoidance behavior.

## 5. Conclusions and discussions

Our results suggested that music and color changed a potential purchaser’s emotional state. Participants experience higher levels of arousal and pleasure when the online store plays fast-tempo music with a red website background color. Some prior studies have reported a negative effect of red in a physical retailing environment but, our study suggested that a red background color resulted a more pleasant feeling than blue one. However, it is known that people in different cultures have different color preferences. In the Chinese culture, red is a ‘‘happy’’ color and the participants in our study were Taiwanese students.

Results from our study provide interesting findings but the study has some limitations. First, participants were college students and thus one must question whether age plays a factor in the tempo of the music and color hue. In order to understand the relationship between tempo and age, the effect of tempo on music preferences of different age groups ranging from third grade to college seniors were examined in Ref. [10]. The results suggested a trend to report greater preference with increasingly rapid tempo across all age groups. Second, our study only investigated the effect of music tempo and color tone in one experimental website. Thus, caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to situations where product type is different from that in our study. Third, our study used red and blue color to represent warm and cool color tone. However, generalization of findings to warm and cool colors should be made with caution because other colors were not tested.

In summary, both background music and color are important elements of the e-business website; they that may provide an effective way of influencing customers’ moods and subsequent shopping behavioral intention. A well-designed web site environment with appropriate music and color may create a desired environment that entices and retains customers.
