Abstract. Despite the proliferation of studies on sales distributions in e-commerce, little is known about how such a distribution in online markets is affected by the presence of mobile channels, which have become a significant conduit for e-commerce. Using a large transaction data set from a leading e-marketplace in South Korea, this study empirically investigates (1) how the sales distribution in the mobile commerce channel is different from the sales distribution in the traditional personal computer (PC) channel and (2) how mobile commerce channel adoption (as a search and purchase channel) affects e-market users search intensity and their aggregate sales distribution. Our analysis in comparing the sales distributions between the PC and mobile channels shows that transactions in the mobile channel are more concentrated on “head” products compared with PC channel sales. The subsequent user-level analysis, based on a difference-in-differences approach, reveals that mobile channel adopters search more but are less (more) likely to choose “tail” (head) products. This finding is contrary to our previous belief that more search activities lead to more tail product sales. The relationship between search intensity and head (tail) product sales, however, largely depends on the product categories. In the case of preference goods such as books, CDs, toys, and fashion items, adoption increased e-market users’ search activities and resulted in more tail product sales. For quality goods such as PCs, phones, cameras, and digital appliances, however, adoption intensified the search activities bu resulted in more head product sales. Finally, for convenience goods such as home supplies and processed foods, adoption discouraged search activities and decreased the choice of tail products. We discuss the theoretical implications of our findings.

## 1. Introduction

Since the seminal works of Brynjolfsson et al. (2003, 2011), which introduced the concept of the “long tail” (Anderson 2004), the shape of the sales distribution in online markets has drawn a great deal of attention from the research community of information systems. Several follow-up empirical studies show that the proportion of niche product sales in various online markets has more mass compared with the corresponding brick-and-mortar market and provides a substantial contribution to the overall revenue for online retailers (e.g., Peltier and Moreau 2012, Kumar et al. 2014). E-marketplaces such as Amazon have built up effective selling strategies using the long tail (Hoffman 2017). The demand shift toward niche products in online markets also implies a selection advantage to consumers, which is associated with an increase in the overall consumer surplus (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003).

The explanation behind the long-tail phenomenon in online markets is twofold, encompassing the low inventory cost of online retailers (Brynjolfsson et al. 2010, Zhou and Duan 2012) and more search activities by e-market users powered by online search tools (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Hinz et al. 2011, Zhang 2018). Unlike brick-and-mortar retailers, online retailers are not subject to physical constraints such as product storage and display space, which enables them to carry more products and provide more options to their online consumers. These online consumers discover products that better suit their needs with powerful search tools available in online markets, which makes niche products more viable and draws out the long tail of sales distributions.

Because the demand shift toward niche products in online markets creates significant meanings in the digital economy, many studies have empirically investigated the long-tail phenomenon in various markets, including books (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Peltier and Moreau 2012), clothing (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011), music (Chellappa et al. 2007), videos (Elberse and Oberholzer-Gee 2007, Zentner et al. 2013), and DVDs (Kumar et al. 2014, Tan et al. 2017). Despite a plethora of research on sales distributions in e-commerce, however, our knowledge is limited to comparing sales distributions between the brick-and-mortar channel and the traditional online channel or between two online channels where the mobile channel is not available. Yet little is known how the demand shape in online markets is affected by the presence of mobile channels, which have become a significant conduit for e-commerce.

Unlike the brick-and-mortar channel, both personal computer (PC) and mobile channels are electronic channels that share product assortments and prices. Thus, there would be minimal impacts on the sales distribution from the supply side. With respect to the consumer side, however, adoption of the mobile channel by consumers can affect their search activities, which may reshape the sales distributions. Internet-enabled mobile devices such as smartphones allow consumers to access online markets and search for products at any time and in any place (Ghose et al. 2013). On the one hand, ubiquitous access and search possibilities afforded by mobile channels eliminate the transportation cost of finding a fixed place that has an internet connection and a PC for online shopping, which could promote more online market searches (search affordances of mobile channels; Bang et al. 2013). For example, consumers could continue a product search by switching from a PC to a smartphone when they need to go outside. On the other hand, small screens and the limited usability of mobile devices put constraints on extensive product searches in online markets, which could entice consumers toward featured products (search constraints of mobile channels; Ghose and Park 2013, Wang et al. 2016). Considering both the search affordances and constraints of mobile channels, it is not obvious whether the use of mobile channels promotes or discourages the overall search activities of consumers, as well as how it affects sales distributions in online markets.

Furthermore, most prior studies have focused on the sales distribution of a single product category, thereby providing little guidance on how the online sales distribution differs across the characteristics of product categories. More important, although these studies explain the phenomenon with the enhanced search abilities of e-market users, the relationship between e-market users’ search activities and the choice of “head” (or “tail”) products has not been formally investigated. Consumer heterogeneity in product evaluation is a prerequisite condition of the search activities that produce the long tail. In this regard, most prior studies revealing the long-tail phenomenon have focused only on preference goods, in which consumers have widely different product tastes (Table 1). Even within preference goods, the Gini index of sales distributions, which measures the concentration of head product shares, significantly depends on the product category, ranging from 0.35 to 0.98. We have limited empirical evidence of the long tail for products in which consumers have relatively similar preferences or a small choice set when they are provided with enough information about the alternatives. In these product categories, the long-tail phenomenon (which is driven by more offerings on the seller side) may be counteracted by the intensive search activities of users.

To fill this gap, we empirically examine how the mobile channel affects sales distributions in online markets across various product categories using largescale data from a leading e-marketplace in South Korea. More specifically, we address the following research questions: (1) How is the sales distribution created through the mobile channel different from the sales distribution created through the traditional PC channel? (2) How does mobile channel adoption as a search and purchase channel influence adopters’ search activities and their aggregate sales distribution? (3) How do adoption effects differ across product categories?

Our analysis reveals that transactions in the mobile channel are more concentrated toward head products compared with PC channel sales, indicating that the search constraints of the mobile channel are dominant in the mobile channel. This does not imply, however, that the proportion of head product sales in online markets is increased by the presence of the mobile channel, given that e-market users can search for products in both PC and mobile channels, which is followed by a purchase in the PC channel. From a user-level analysis, we find that mobile channel adoption as a search and purchase channel increases e-market users’ search intensity and affects their aggregate sales distribution for subsequent transactions.

Contrary to our previous belief that more search activities lead to tail product sales (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Hinz et al. 2011, Doosti et al. 2017), e-market users’ search intensities were, in fact, associated with head product sales, but these effects largely depended on the product categories. In the case of preference goods such as books, CDs, toys, and fashion items, mobile commerce channel adoption increased e-market users’ search activities and, as we expect from prior long-tail studies, resulted in more tail product sales. For quality goods such as PCs, phones, cameras, and digital appliances, however, adoption intensified the search activities but resulted in more (less) head (tail) product sales. Finally, for convenience goods such as home supplies (e.g., paper products, laundry detergent) and processed foods (e.g., canned food, instant noodles), adoption discouraged their search activities and increased (decreased) the choice of head (tail) products.

Our study reveals important implications for the stream of long-tail and mobile commerce research. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate how the mobile channel affects sales distributions in e-commerce. Despite the theoretical and practical significance of sales distributions in e-commerce, we still have a limited understanding of how the mobile channel (which has become a significant conduit for e-commerce) affects sales distributions. Because of the ubiquity of the mobile internet and the form factor of mobile devices, the mobile channel provides both search affordances and constraints to e-market users. Our analysis illustrates that the effects of these search affordances and constraints can dominate each other, depending on the product categories, which results in different sales distribution shapes. The search affordances of the mobile channel significantly expand our search capacity when purchasing preference and quality goods. However, in the case of convenience goods, the search con straints of the mobile channel dominate the search affordances, thus discouraging mobile channel users from conducting extensive searches and enticing them to choose featured products on the front page.

Second, we provide counterevidence to our previous belief that more search activities by e-market users lead to an increase in the market shares of tail products. The basic assumption behind this belief is that consumers have diverse tastes or product evaluations; thus, exploring alternatives enhances their chance of finding a better fit. The degree of consumer heterogeneity in a product evaluation can depend on the product categories, however, and consumers may be less heterogeneous within a certain product category (Park et al. 2017). In our analysis, we find a positive association between search intensities and tail product sales for convenience and preference goods but a negative association in the case of quality goods.

The rest of this study is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the search affordances and constraints of the mobile channel and explain how they can affect sales distributions. In Section 3, we empirically compare the sales distributions of the PC and mobile channels and examine how mobile channel adoption affects sales distributions in online markets. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of the findings and implications.

## 2. Search Affordances and Constraints of the Mobile Channel

The mobile channel provides both search affordances and constraints to e-market users. The ubiquity of the mobile internet and the form factor of mobile devices allow e-market users to carry their device, such as a smartphone, and search for products in e-markets outside, where the traditional PC channel is not available. Searching for products through the mobile channel, however, comes with a higher search cost compared with the PC channel. Despite recent innovations to enhance the user interfaces of mobile devices (e.g., easy log-in features or a voice keyboard), the limited user interfaces of mobile devices still put constraints on e-market users’ extensive searches in the mobile channel (Pantano and Priporas 2016, Hintze et al. 2017, Raphaeli et al. 2017). In summary, the mobile channel expands our possibility to access e-markets and search for products online, but it restricts our extensive searches within a single visit to e-markets.

Given the search affordances and constraints of the mobile channel, PC-mobile buyers, who use both PC and mobile channels for product purchases, can strategically choose PC and/or mobile channels for their search and purchase activities. Table 2 illustrates the possible combination of PC-mobile buyers’ choice of channels for achieving their shopping goals. They can continue to use their PC channel when intensive search activities are needed (scenario (1)). They can also employ both channels (i.e., PCs and mobile devices) for product searches, switching from one channel to the other depending on their search needs (scenarios (3) and (6)). In this case, the search affordances of the mobile channel can dominate the search constraints of the same channel because the PC and mobile channels would be selectively used for their search needs.

When consumers buy products such as televisions or refrigerators, which are not purchased very frequently, they need to spend a significant amount of effort in evaluating potential alternatives (Huang et al. 2009). Thus, they would most likely not continue to use the mobile channel, which limits their search depth. They will primarily choose the PC channel for such product searches, backed up by the mobile channel. However, in the case of repeated purchases or when they have enough preknowledge about a product, an extensive search is not required (Beatty and Smith 1987); thus, the mobile channel can be chosen (scenario (5)). Buying low-involvement products such as toilet paper, for example, does not require much search effort because these items are purchased repeatedly. Therefore, e-market users may prefer to use the mobile channel in their hands when buying such products. Considering that consumers are more likely to purchase products when they have enough information about them, we do not expect many cases of scenarios (2) and (4).

There are PC-only buyers who purchase a product only from the PC channel. Although they rely on the PC channel for purchases, they may also use the mobile channel as a search channel (scenario (3)). Particularly during the early introduction period of the mobile channel, e-market users may hesitate to use it because of the complexity and goal uncertainty of the new channel (Valentini et al. 2011). Because of channel usage inertia, prior familiarity, and risk aversion, consumers use a new channel as a supplementary channel rather than switch away from the existing channel from its beginning (Montoya-Weiss et al. 2003, Dholakia et al. 2005, Shankar et al. 2010, Yang et al 2013). Consumers would learn additional possibilities afforded by the new channel and would become familiarized with it before adopting it as a purchase channel. PC-only buyers are either those who never use the mobile channel for their online shopping or those who are yet to be familiarized with the mobile channel and fully appreciate its properties, eventually transitioning into PC-mobile buyers. Thus, we can rationally assume that PC-mobile buyers would conduct more product searches on the mobile channel than PC-only buyers.

The marketing literature provides various taxonomies of product categories based on consumers search needs, which can be affected by their product awareness, comprehension, standards of taste, and clarity concerning product characteristics (Murphy and Enis 1986). Typical classifications include convenience and shopping goods, which are located at both ends of the searching behavior continuum. Consumers repeatedly purchase and spend minimal search effort for convenience goods, whereas they engage in a serious process of selection based on the suitability, quality, price, and style for shopping goods (American Marketing Association 1948). We can further classify shopping goods into preference goods and quality goods, given that they have distinct value inference processes. Preference goods, such as clothing and shoes, are mostly evaluated on the basis of consumers tastes regarding style or color, whereas quality goods, such as PCs and digital appliances, are mainly evaluated by the objective features of the products, such as the processing power or capacity. Consumers have an accepted ranking-based standard for evaluation in regard to quality goods; by contrast, they tend to have largely different opinions toward preference goods, which depend on their individual tastes (Ratchford 1979, Vandenbosch and Weinberg 1995, Lee and Lee 2009).<sup>1</sup>

Prior long-tail studies argue that more search activities by e-market users lead to a longer distribution of their aggregate sales, given that they can find niche products with a better fit. The assumption behind this argument is that their product preferences (or evaluations) are heterogeneous enough to produce the long tail. In the case of preference goods such as clothing or shoes, where consumers have largely different tastes, their search effort is associated with a higher chance of finding niche products that better suit their needs, thus resulting in the long tail of sales distributions. Therefore, we provide a theoretical prediction that more search activities afforded by the mobile channel (scenarios (3) and (6)) can prolong the tail of the sales distributions of such products. For quality goods, by contrast, more search activities in the mobile channel do not necessarily lead to the long tail because consumers may have a small choice set when they are provided with enough information about the alternatives. E-market users may update their evaluations during the search process to seek Pareto-efficient alternatives (Green et al. 1988, Krieger and Green 1991). Throughout multiple trade-off decisions between product attributes (e.g., price, product warranty, quality, and capacity), consumers may reach a similar understanding with respect to which alternatives are better, given that quality goods are subject to the accepted ranking-based standard for evaluation (Lee and Lee 2009). This learning process will not happen when consumers have strong and distinct preferences for a single or a few attributes beforehand. Therefore, more search efforts can be positively associated with the choice of head products for quality goods. Finally, regarding convenience goods, which do not require consumers to conduct extensive searches, mobile channel adoption can allow them to choose the mobile channel as the primary means for their searches and purchases (scenario (5)), enticing them to buy featured products on the first or second mobile pages. This response would form a shorter tail of their aggregate sales distribution compared with e-market users who purchase only from a PC channel.

Although we provide our rough predictions, an empirical question remains as to how the mobile channel affects sales distributions in e-markets, considering the search affordances and constraints of the mobile channel, as well as consumers’ varying tastes. We empirically examine these effects in the next section.

## 3. Empirical Analysis 3.1. Data

We use a large transaction data set from the database of a leading e-marketplace in South Korea that had initially provided the PC channel only and launched the mobile channel later. This e-marketplace was ranked first in South Korea in terms of the number of users in 2017. The data set contains the random samples of 30,000 PC-mobile buyers (who made at least one transaction in the mobile channel during our observation time window) and 30,000 PC-only buyers (whodidnot make any transactionsin the mobile channel) along with the entire database of 2.7 million transactions (including 105,089 mobile transactions) made between March 2009 and May 2011. This e-marketplace launched its mobile channel on June 1, 2010, and our data period covers at least one year before and after the mobile channel introduction by the e-marketplace.

The data set contains the detailed order-level history for the users such as the price, quantity, time, display ranks of the orders, and their demographics (Table 3). The display rank denotes the display location of an ordered product in the search results.<sup>2</sup> If an ordered product, for example, is listed at the top of the first search results page, the display rank of the product is one. If the product is listed at the bottom or on the next search results page, the rank is higher. The average display rank of the orders can be a good behavioral proxy for search intensity because orders with a high display rank imply that users review products that are listed not only at the top of the first page but also at the bottom or on the following pages. Prior studies have also found that the display rank serves as a good proxy for search intensity in the PC channel (Animesh et al. 2011) or mobile channel (Ghose et al. 2012, Zheng et al. 2016).

We also measure the average sales rank of the orders and the proportion of head and tail product sales, which have typically been employed in prior long-tail studies (e.g., Xu et al. 2016, Tan et al. 2017). We regard popular (niche) products for each month (e.g., the top 5% (bottom 50%)) in terms of sales volume as the head (tail) products for the transactions during that month. We use the channel-specific sales rank for the comparison of the sales distributions between the PC and mobile channels (Section 3.2). When comparing the sales distributions of the two distinct channels, the sales rank (based on the aggregate sales of both channels) may lead to a biased result because there could be a huge difference in the set of popular products and sales volume between the channels (Soysal and Zentner 2014). When examining the effects of mobile channel adoption, however, we use head (tail) products and the sales rank based on the aggregate sales (Section 3.3) because our focus is to investigate the changes in the sales distribution of the e-market (regardless of the purchase channel) by the mobile channel.<sup>3</sup>

To compare the sales distributions between the PC and mobile channels, we exclude transactions by users who signed up for the e-marketplace during our observation period. These incoming new users can distort the tail length, given that their joining may be associated with the intention to purchase products at the time of signing up (Hinz et al. 2011). Our remaining sample contains 28,433 users and over 1.5 million associated transaction records, including 43,258 transactions conducted through the mobile channel. Mobile channel transactions account for 2.87% of the entire number of transactions.

## 3.2. Comparison of the Sales Distribution Between the PC and Mobile Channels

PC-only buyers and PC-mobile buyers may be heterogeneous regarding their demographic composition or purchasing habits, which may explain differences in the sales distributions of the PC and mobile channels (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011). For example, PC-mobile buyers tend to be younger and more innovative than PC-only buyers. Furthermore, PC-mobile buyers’ purchase channel preferences related to order-specific characteristics such as price, time, and product category could also affect sales distributions. To tease out the channel effect, we first employ propensity score matching to match samples in the PC and mobile channels (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). Specifically, we conduct one-to-one matching with replacement and a caliper size of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the propensity scores We adopt order characteristics (price and time), users demographic variables (gender and age), display rank, and product variety in users’ purchase histories<sup>5</sup> and order summary measures (recency, frequency, and monetary values) prior to the mobile channel introduction by the e-marketplace (i.e., before June 1, 2010) to generate the propensity score of each order. Studies in the direct marketing literature show that recency, frequency, and monetary values are sufficient statistics to summarize the purchase histories of each customer, which provides a theoretical justification for using these measures (Fader et al. 2005). We match orders in the PC and mobile channels after the mobile channel introduction, and we compare the sales distributions of the two channels based on the matched orders.

The PC and mobile samples are matched well, given that none of the order characteristics, demographic variables, or order summary measures are significantly different after matching (Table 4). This type of matching addresses the potential selection bias issue in comparing the sales distributions between channels.

We then generate the Lorenz curve and calculate the Gini coefficient of the sales distributions in the PC and mobile channels to compare the demand shape between the two channels (Figure 1). The Lorenz curve depicts the sales proportion of the bottom x% of products; a higher Lorenz curve (closer to the 45-degree equality line) represents a longer sales distribution tail. The Gini coefficient measures the ratio of the area in between the 45-degree equality line and the Lorenz curve to the area under the equality line, representing the concentration of sales on head products.

The Lorenz curve of mobile channel sales is located below the curve of the PC channel, meaning that more head products were sold in the mobile channel than in the PC channel. The Gini coefficient of sales distributions in the mobile channel (0.361) is also higher than that of the PC channel (0.277), confirming that the PC channel has a longer tail than the mo bile channel.

## 3.3. Impacts of Mobile Channel Adoption on the Search Intensity and Sales Distribution

Following the discussion in Section 2, compared with PC-only buyers, PC-mobile buyers (who have adopted the mobile channel as a search and purchase channel) are more likely to use the mobile channel for their online shopping, including product searches and purchases. In this section, we investigate the change in the mean display rank of the orders that the PC-mobile buyers and PC-only buyers made over time and their choice of head (tail) products after mobile channel adoption to determine how adoption affects e-market users’ search intensity and the aggregate sales distribution.

To address the selection issue mentioned in the preceding section, we first conduct user matching before our main analysis. We employ users’ demographic variables (gender and age), and display rank, sales rank, proportion of top and bottom products, and product variety in users’ purchase histories, and order summary measures (recency, frequency, and monetary value) prior to the mobile channel introduction by the e-marketplace as our matching variables. All differences between PC-mobile buyers and PC-only buyers on the matching variables are controlled for after the matching, suggesting that the matched samples are rather homogeneous prior to the mobile channel introduction (Table 6).

Using the matched samples, we conduct a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis to tease out the impact of mobile channel adoption (as a search and purchase channel) on subsequent transactions regarding consumers’ search intensity and the shape of the sales distribution. To avoid a possible endogeneity issue, we set the posttreatment period as the time after the mobile channel introduction by the e-marketplace rather than the time after the adoption of individual e-market users (Narang and Shankar 2019).<sup>6</sup>

Before we conduct the DID analysis, we depict our dependent variables over time by groups: PC-mobile buyers and PC-only buyers (Figure A.1 in Appendix A). The difference in our dependent variables between groups significantly changes after the mobile channel introduction (June 2010), which provides model-free evidence that the introduction may affect the dependent variables differently across groups.

Our equation for user i and month t is the following:

$$
\begin{array}{c} D V _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta \times A d o p t e r _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {t} + \gamma \times A d o p t e r _ {i} \\ + \delta \times A f t e r _ {t} + P _ {i t} + X _ {i t} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $D V _ { i t }$ represents the log-transformed mean display rank of orders (or the mean sales rank of orders, the proportion of head or tail product sales), Adopter<sub>i</sub> is a dummy variable indicating PC-mobile buyers (1 = PC-mobile buyers, 0 = PC-only buyers), $\dot { \boldsymbol { A } } f t e \boldsymbol { r } _ { t }$ is a dummy variable indicating the period after the mobile channel introduction by the focal e-marketplace (1 = after the mobile channel introduction, 0 = before the mobile channel introduction), $P _ { i t }$ represents the proportion variables of sales in each product category, $X _ { i t }$ represents the control variables, $\tau _ { t }$ represents the monthly dummy variables that control for the common long-term systematic changes across all users, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the error term clustered by users to allow for correlation over time (Moulton 1990, Xu et al. 2016) The coefficient $\beta$ for the interaction term represents the effects of adoption on the dependent variable.

Table 7 illustrates the results of our DID analysis. The coefficient of the interaction term for the mean display rank is positive and significant, suggesting that mobile channel adoption drives e-market users to seek out more alternatives for subsequent transactions. The coefficient of the interaction term for the mean sales rank is negative and significant, and the coefficient for the proportion of top products is positive and significant. These coefficients collectively indicate that e-market users’ demand is more concentrated on head products after adoption. In other words, mobile channel adoption (as a search and purchase channel) promotes search activities in the e-marketplace but increases the chance of purchasing head products overall. The coefficient for the proportion of bottom products is negative and significant, showing that adoption discourages consumers from purchasing tail products.<sup>7</sup>

Furthermore, we compare the size of the coefficient in our main result with the mean of the randomly calculated coefficients using the Z-score of the difference. The treatment effect in our main analysis is significantly larger than the insignificant pseudotreatment effect, which also shows that the difference between the treatment and control groups is significantly enlarged after our treatment. Similarly, we conduct a falsification test with random placebo treatments. We randomly assign e-market users into hypothetical treatment and control groups, and we estimate the effect of the pseudotreatment (Table 13). The pseudotreatment effect is not statistically significant, and the treatment effect in our main analysis is significantly larger than what would be expected by chance, which also supports our claim.

Why are more search activities positively (negatively) associated with the choice of head (tail) products? To find clues for this counterintuitive result, we conduct additional category-level analyses (Table 15).

From consumers’ search needs, we first classify convenience and shopping goods. Home supplies (e.g., paper products and laundry detergent) and processed foods $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , canned food and instant noodles) are classified into convenience goods, which are low in search needs, because we repeatedly purchase and have enough preknowledge about such products (Holton 1958, Kaish 1967, Murphy and Enis 1986). Among the remaining product categories, PCs, phones, cameras, and digital appliances are classified into quality goods because they are more subject to the accepted ranking-based standard for evaluation (Ratchford 1979, Vandenbosch and Weinberg 1995, Lee and Lee 2009). Product searches in these categories typically go through the trade-off evaluation process (e.g., price versus quality) and may lead to the Pareto-efficient alternative (Green et al. 1988, Krieger and Green 1991). As such, additional search efforts afforded by the mobile channel are not necessarily associated with the choice of niche products in these categories. Evaluations of books, CDs, toys, and fashion items, by contrast, rely largely on individual preferences or tastes, and more searches would be associated with the choice of niche products, as found in prior long-tail studies (Lee et al. 2011, Dewan and Ramaprasad 2012, Hervas-Drane 2015).

We match users again within the group of categories based on their demographics and order summary measures (recency, frequency, and monetary values) prior to the mobile channel introduction. First, the empirical results of the book, CD, toy, and fashion item markets show that adoption intensifies the search activities and the chance of purchasing tail products. Second, for PCs, phones, cameras, and digital appliances, mobile channel adoption promotes search activities in those categories but increases (decreases) head (tail) product sales. Finally, for home supplies (e.g., paper products and laundry detergent) and processed foods (e.g., canned food and instant noodles), adoption discourages search activities and increases (decreases) the choice of head (tail) products. For the remaining categories (e.g., skincare and baby supplies and fresh/healthy foods), adoption increased their mean display rank, but there was no significant adoption effect on the proportions of head or tail product sales.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

In this research, we investigate how the presence of the mobile channel affects sales distributions in the online market. More specifically, we compare the sales distributions of orders made through the PC and mobile channels; furthermore, we examine the causal effects of mobile channel adoption as a search and purchase channel on e-market users’ search intensity and the shape of their aggregate sales distribution. Our results reveal that orders made through the mobile channel are more concentrated on head products compared with the PC channel. However, the specific effects of the mobile channel on sales distributions are more complicated, given that emarket users’ search activities are affected by the mobile channel; additionally, these users could place orders on the PC channel.

Our individual-level analysis shows that mobile channel adoption as a search and purchase channel by e-market users encourages their search activities in the e-marketplace but decreases the chance of purchasing tail products overall for subsequent transactions. More specifically, the display rank of orders made by e-market users increased via their adoption of the mobile channel, suggesting that more alternatives are considered in placing orders. The share of head products in consumers’ aggregate sales distribution, however, was increased via adoption. We find similar results from the analysis on transactions in the PC channel, where PC-mobile buyers conduct more intensive searches for transactions in the PC channel after their adoption, but their sales distribution in the PC channel is shortened.

The follow-up category-level analysis provides a clue as to why we obtain a counterintuitive result. The results show that adoption effects on search activities and aggregate sales distributions largely depend on product categories. The underlying assumption behind the explanation of search activities producing the long-tail phenomenon is that consumers have different tastes and evaluation schemes for products. Intensive search activities powered by online search tools therefore can enhance the chance of finding products that better suit their needs. In the case where consumers have similar preferences or value assessments for a product, however, their extensive search activities would form a smaller choice set, which would fatten the head of the sales distribution.

For quality goods, more search activities afforded by the mobile channel allow users to find attractive alternatives displayed in the back, rather than on the front page, where users are enticed by featured products.<sup>9</sup> With respect to preference goods such as toys or clothing, by contrast, consumers’ preferred taste, style, or color matters much more than quality goods. Thus, adoption leads users to explore more alternatives and purchase tail products that fit their needs. For convenience goods, which are purchased repeatedly on a regular basis (and thus do not require users to collect additional information), adopters may choose the mobile channel as the primary means for searches and purchases, which provides affordances of anytime and anywhere access to the e-marketplace at the expense of usability. Consequently, adoption lowers the mean display rank and increases (decreases) the chance of choosing head (tail) products.

We articulate a few limitations and provide suggestions for future research. First, although we investigate the relationship between e-market users search intensity and their choice of head (tail) products, because of data availability, we could not examine how PC-mobile buyers (endogenously and strategically) switch between PC and mobile channels to search for and purchase products. Collecting search log data across the two channels and examining e-market users’ search behavior of hopping from one channel to another would provide more detailed evidence on the search affordances of the mobile channel. Second, mobile channels innovate the search activities of consumers in the brick-and-mortar channel as well. Although they visit a brick-andmortar shop, they may access online markets to collect product ratings or reviews, for example, which could reshape the sales distribution of the offline channel. Examining such mobile impacts on other channels would be an interesting avenue for future research.

To conclude, this study empirically examined how the mobile channel (which has become a significant conduit for e-commerce) affects the shape of sales distributions in online markets. Although orders made through the mobile channel were more concentrated on head products, the overall impacts driven by the mobile channel were not obvious, given that PC-mobile buyers could purchase a product in the PC channel after searching for it in both the PC and mobile channels. We found that the search affordances and constraints of the mobile commerce channel dominate each other depending on the product categories, thereby reshaping the sales distributions differently. Our finding gives us an opportunity to rethink the relationship between consumers’ search intensity and their choice of head (or tail) products.

## Endnotes

<sup>1</sup> This classification essentially captures Lancaster’s notion of product space (Lancaster 1971).

<sup>2</sup> Our focal e-marketplace provided the same display rank system for both the PC and mobile channels. As such, an order has the same display rank, regardless of the purchase channel, if the same search condition (i.e., keyword or filter) is applied.

<sup>3</sup> Our context is different from prior long-tail studies, where multichannel retailers manage the shelves and inventories of each channel differently. E-market sellers do not manage their inventories separately for the mobile channel. They merely post their deal on the e-marketplace, which provides channel options for consumers. As such, e-market sellers care about the aggregate sales of PC and mobile channels for their stocking or inventory decisions.

<sup>4</sup> The results are robust to using different matching techniques (e.g., one-to-one matching without replacement and the nearest three neighbors matching).

<sup>5</sup> The product variety is measured by the average number of distinct products in each month purchased prior to the mobile channel introduction.

<sup>6</sup> As a robustness check, we also operationalize the first mobile purchase as our treatment (see Appendix C).

<sup>7</sup> In our data, the display rank has the same value for the same product across channels if the same search condition (e.g., the same filter) is applied. Given that there is a difference in the usability between channels, e-market users’ choice of a search condition can depend on the channels, which may be the driver for the difference in the display ranks between the two channels. We investigate this possibility by focusing on the products, which are not subject to any filter or option, assuming that they are less affected by the usability. We find n qualitative difference in the result (see Appendix B).

<sup>8</sup> Thus, we do not expect many cases of scenarios (2) or (4) in Table 2, where all the search activities are conducted in a channel, but the purchase is made in another channel.

<sup>9</sup> Our focal e-marketplace has a bidding system for a few front slots. As such, higher bidders could display their products in the front. Other products were displayed based on the “late-come, first-dis play” rule. Upon e-market users’ accepted ranking value systems, sellers with attractive offers (high in quality but low in price, for example) may have fewer incentives to feature their products in front slots. Although this may provide one possible explanation behind the negative association, we need more data, such as sellers’ bidding prices for the front slots and their conversion rates across product categories, to formally investigate the relationship
