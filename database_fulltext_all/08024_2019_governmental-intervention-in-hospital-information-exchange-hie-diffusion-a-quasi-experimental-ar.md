# Governmental intervention in Hospital Information Exchange (HIE) di<sup>f</sup>usion: a quasi-experimental ARIMA interrupted time series analysis of monthly HIE patient penetration rates

David Gefen<sup>a</sup>, O<sup>fi</sup>r Ben-Assuli<sup>b</sup>, Mark Stehr<sup>c</sup>, Bruce Rosen<sup>d</sup> and Yaron Denekamp<sup>e,f</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Decision Sciences and MIS, Bennett S LeBow College of Business, Drexel University, Philadelphia, USA; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems Management Department, Faculty of Business Administration, Ono Academic College, Kiryat Ono, Israel; <sup>c</sup>Bennett S LeBow College of Business, Drexel University, Philadelphia, USA; <sup>d</sup>JDC - Brookdale Institute, Jerusalem, Israel; <sup>e</sup>Clalit Health Services, Tel Aviv, Israel; <sup>f</sup>School of Public Health, Haifa University, Israe

## ABSTRACT

This study examines changes in the monthly <sup>penetration</sup> rates of a Health Information Exchange (HIE) in a large Health Maintenance Organisation (HMO) in Israel after its successful adoption, and how those rates changed in anticipation of a government policy to turn this HIE into a national system. Penetration rate is the proportion of patients whose data have been accessed through the HIE. We apply the Bass model to the penetration data and estimate an ARIMA interrupted time series analysis on the resulting dependent variable. In the Bass model, the di<sup>f</sup>usion of new products or services over time follows an <sup>S</sup> curve, where the proportion of non-users who take up the new technology is assumed to be a linear function of the proportion who are already users. The results indicate (1) that also HIE <sup>penetration</sup> shows a Bass model pattern, thus extending previous research that indicated that the <sup>adoption</sup> rate of an HIE (i.e. its initial installation) shows a Bass model pattern, and (2) that there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant one-time increase in penetration in this HMO when hospitals in other HMOs started training towards adopting this HIE in preparation for it becoming the national system. Implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The adoption of Hospital Information Exchanges (HIE) is of crucial medical and <sup>fi</sup>nancial importance. HIE are IT that enable sharing patient information across healthcare providers (such as hospitals and laboratories), and as such have received much governmental attention (e.g., Angst and Agarwal (2009), NLC (2013), Rosen, Waitzberg, and Merkur (2015), Blumenthal (2009), and Blumenthal and Tavenner (2010)). HIE can substantially improve service and data quality, cut costs (Adjerid, Acquisti, Telang, Padman, & Adler-Milstein, 2016; Bower, 2005; Pinsonneault, Addas, Qian, Dakshinamoorthy, & Tamblyn, 2017; Rosen et al., 2015), and enable better and seamless medical data transfer among stakeholders, such as community-based providers, insurers, hospitals, and medical centres (Hillestad et al., 2005). Nonetheless, despite this recognised potential, current governmental reports show that many US hospitals still manage their patient information such that it cannot be accessed by other healthcare providers (HeathIT, 2014).<sup>1</sup>

Research into the adoption patterns of HIE over time (i.e., the di<sup>f</sup>usion rates of HIE installation) examined this as the increase over time in the number of medical sta<sup>f</sup> or medical centres which reported using an HIE (e.g., Veiga, Keupp, Floyd, and Kellermanns (2014), DesRoches et al. (2013), Agha (2014), and Mennemeyer, Menachemi, Rahurkar, and Ford (2016)). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the di<sup>f</sup>usion of HIE adoption coincides approximately with a Bass model (Bass, 1969) pattern. A Bass model predicts that the proportion of the population that adopts a new product or service exhibits an S curve behaviour over time based on two parameters: the innovation rate (initial rate of adoption) and the imitation rate (rate of adoption based on past adoption). Past applications of the Bass model to HIE di<sup>f</sup>usion assumed that those two parameters were constant and without external intervention during the di<sup>f</sup>usion process. Although research indicated that the pattern of di<sup>f</sup>usion visually resembled a Bass model, studies have not actually estimated the Bass parameters statistically or measured how those parameters change over time or due to policy.

The objective of this study is to investigate the phase after HIE adoption: its actual penetration. Adoption is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as whether the HIE is in place and ready for use, and penetration is the degree of actual use. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, penetration is the proportion of patients coming to the medical centre whose data are accessed through the HIE by the medical sta<sup>f</sup>.

Obviously, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of seamlessly sharing patient information depend on the number of patients accessed through the HIE system, as indeed found by Yaraghi, Du, Sharman, Gopal, and Ramesh (2015). We examined the penetration of a fully operational HIE in the context of Emergency Room (ER) patients in 8 hospitals of a large Health Maintenance Organisation (HMO) in Israel over a 72-month period. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that: (1) the Bass mode provides a reasonable description of HIE penetration, and (2) the consequences of an external intervention – namely governmental policy recommending, but not legally requiring, other HMOs to adopt this same HIE – can change the innovation rate parameter of the Bass model during the penetration process. That the one-time increase in the penetration rate occurred six months before the new policy came into e<sup>f</sup>ect shows the importance of timing to prepare for policy changes. After that one-time increase, HIE penetration increased at approximately the same rate as before. Although other hospitals joined the HIE during the sample period, the jump in the penetration rate cannot be explained by that process.

The governmental intervention studied here is a rare success; other countries that attempted the implementation of a national HIE had been less successful (e.g., in the UK (Anderson, Frogner, Johns, & Reinhardt, 2006; UK, 2011) and in Spain (Corbellini, Giest, Artmann, Heywood, & Dumortier, 2010)). This may also have rami<sup>fi</sup>cations beyond the realm of HIE di<sup>f</sup>usion because the Bass model, because it is primarily empirical rather than theoretical, is silent on how external intervention might change its parameters. Every empirical application of the model that we found assumed that the parameters remained constant, including in the context of an HIE. This scenario may be realistic in many cases, but in other cases, portraying a more realistic picture may require allowing the parameters to change to accommodate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of external interventions. In fact, assuming constant parameters throughout the product or service lifecycle and ignoring the e<sup>f</sup>ects of marketing (and by extension governmental policies) are among the critiques levied at the Bass model (Fornerino, 2003). This study demonstrates empirically the consequences of an external intervention, and how this intervention can be captured by allowing for a change in the parameters of the Bass model.

The ability to model, and therefore numerically forecast, the increase in HIE penetration rate over time – and knowing that that rate can be increased through intervention – could enable IT managers to better plan and allocate their scarce resources for essential support and training, as well as informing administrators running the HIE. Given the low penetration rates of HIE (e.g., HeathIT (2014)) and given increases in quality of care and reductions in costs provided by a better implementation of an HIE (Adjerid et al., 2016; Bower, 2005; Pinsonneault et al., 2017; Rosen et al., 2015), the enhanced allocation of resources achieved by managing the HIE penetration rate as a Bass model with changing parameters could be crucially important. Governmental intervention can be included in the model which would lead to even better resource allocation as well as advanced planning in response to such intervention. Moreover, that governmental intervention can increase HIE penetration rate is in itself reassuring in light of the failure to do so in other countries. Theoretically, linking these results to the Bass model suggests that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may extend beyond the realm of HIE.

## 2. Background and the importance of this study

## 2.1. Previous research on HIE adoption difusion

Prior to the $\mathrm { E H R } ^ { 2 }$ and HIE revolution, medical information sharing between providers was very limited, with the patients themselves serving as the primary conduit of information. The need for more e<sup>fi</sup>cient transfer of information has been the main driver for investment in HIE networks. The transition from paper-based medicine to the digital format enabled real-time transfers of large quantities of information for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time (Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010; Davis, Michelle McEvoy, Shea, & Stremikis, 2009). The introduction of EHR and HIE was heralded as a way to bring about tremendous changes for patients, health service providers, and other medical sta<sup>f</sup>, as well as managers and policy-makers in healthcare organisations. Sharing medical information between di<sup>f</sup>erent providers helps the healthcare sector function in a more coordinated fashion, thus improving quality and containing costs as well as avoiding double testing (Ayabakan, Bardhan, Zheng, & Kirksey, 2017; Claxton, Sculpher, & Drummond, 2002; Furukawa, Patel, Charles, Swain, & Mostashari, 2013; Kapoor & Kleinbart, 2012; Pinsonneault et al., 2017; Rudin, 2011; Sittig & Singh, 2010; Thorn, Carter, & Bailey, 2014; Walker et al., 2005), which is why policy makers and politicians have been allocating funds to promote HIE adoption by healthcare providers (Williams, Mostashari, Mertz, Hogin, & Atwal, 2012).

As promising as it may have appeared, the transition from paper-based medicine to computerised, digital formats, has been fraught with practical, legal, medical, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial di<sup>fi</sup>culties that dramatically reduced potential increases in e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality (Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010; Davis et al., 2009). Boonstra and Broekhuis (2010) characterised the main obstacles to successful EHR adoption which include technical barriers, time-related barriers (such as the time needed to learn the system, and move existing data to the new HIE), psychological barriers, social barriers (such as reluctance among physicians), organisational barriers, and barriers related to the change process (attitudes towards change may lead to resistance to the new tools). Boonstra and Broekhuis (2010) also noted that not all kinds of obstacles are within the reach of the implementers, and that support from other entities (e.g., the government) is needed to bring this complex process to fruition. Lack of training with the technology as well as poor system adaptation to the organisation (Gagnon et al., 2014) can also impede implementation of HIE at least in the case of EHR. Likewise, security concerns can also be a <sup>fi</sup>nancial and regulatory barrier (Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Fernández-Alemán, Señor, Lozoya, & Toval, 2013; Haas, Wohlgemuth, Echizen, Sonehara, & Müller, 2011; Singh et al., 2012), as are safety concerns both within and between organisations that require governmental policy stipulations (Sittig, Gonzalez, & Singh, 2014; Sittig & Singh, 2012; Wright et al., 2011). Despite these challenges, there have been successful implementation strategies, including, for example, highlighting the success of other organisations (Fattore, Frosini, Salvatore, & Tozzi, 2009).

Although they face privacy issues, EHR adoption rates have been rising (Royle, Hambleton, & Walduck, 2013) and HIE have gradually been implemented in the USA since the mid-1990s (Yoder, 2011). One successful example on a large scale is the American Veterans Health Information Systems and Technology Architecture (VISTA) (Shortli<sup>f</sup>e, 2005). The US government invested extensively in the implementation of VISTA, and created an HIE network of more than 1,000 governmental hospitals. In Australia, a recent application integrated the EHR and HIE functions into the existing system (Morrison, Robertson, Cresswell, Crowe, & Sheikh, 2011), including also personally controlled EHR for engagement with the patients (Pearce & Bainbridge, 2014). In Finland, regional HIE are well-adjusted and synchronised with each other in terms of documentation format (Hyppönen, Reponen, Lääveri, & Kaipio, 2014). However, there are also failed attempts to implement HIE, and the overall adoption processes have been slower than expected (Gans, Kralewski, Hammons, & Dowd, 2005; Jaana, Ward, & Bahensky, 2012).

One notorious failure was the British National Health Service (BNHS). In 2002, BNHS, launched a programme to digitise all patient records and make them accessible to the whole BNHS network for all authorised users in England. Despite the vast amounts of money invested in the BNHS, the project was abandoned in 2011, with very few hospitals nationwide meeting the completion benchmarks (Anderson et al., 2006; UK, 2011). The Spanish government also ran into di<sup>fi</sup>culties implementing their HIE Quality Plan for National Health System, which was focused on health promotion and HIE use, and Avanza, which centred on research promotion, development, and innovation in HIE (Corbellini et al., 2010). Simply because the government mandates the adoption of an HIE, does not necessary mean that hospitals and other medical organisations will adopt and implement the HIE e<sup>f</sup>ectively (Vest, 2010).

Given these concerns, the objectives of this study are (1) to show that HIE penetration can be modelled along the lines of a Bass model and (2) that governmental intervention, despite past failures, can be successful. Being able to model HIE penetration – and being able to adapt the modelling to also account for the impact of governmental intervention – can provide the basis for providing reasonably accurate numerical forecasting of the HIE penetration process. Knowing the numerical forecasted extent of HIE penetration over time through modelling means that better management of the HIE by allocating appropriate resources at the right time is possible. This type of modelling can lead to more e<sup>fi</sup>- cient management of an HIE and its penetration, and hence to it better achieving its goals.

## 2.2. The specific context of this study

In contrast to applications of the Bass model (Bass, 1969) that have dealt with the initial decision to adopt a new product/service, this study examines HIE penetration after its successful adoption. We do so in the context of visits to an ER where MDs and RNs access the HIE as they provide medical treatment which in many cases is urgent and critical, and where any delay can have severe medical consequences. Accessing information from the HIE in such circumstances is crucial but takes time that could instead be spent on the direct delivery of medical services. Given that the use of these HIE in the HMO we studied was voluntary, unlike some hospitals in the US and Europe where HIE use is compulsory, the MDs and RNs need to repeatedly decide whether their use of the HIE warrants spending less time with the ER patient. Hence, the role of $q ,$ the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation, in this context takes on a somewhat di<sup>f</sup>erent meaning than it does in other Bass studies. Advertising for example may be important in other contexts, but in the context of an HIE in an ER, the MDs and RNs will give precedence to their own and others’ experience with the HIE and its ability to improve care. In other words, q in this context represents the value the HIE provides, rather than the imitation of others. However, as shown below, the data analysis supports a Bass model pattern.

Another speci<sup>fi</sup>c contribution of this study is its dependent variable. Considerable research dealing with HIE di<sup>f</sup>usion has probed the adoption process with a dependent dichotomous variable indicating whether the HIE had been adopted or not. Measuring the penetration over time, as done in this study, allows for an examination of the next phase in the lifecycle of an HIE after its successful adoption. Moreover, our data, spanning many periods, enables a time series analysis to statistically verify the adoption pattern, whereas previous research has tended to rely solely on assessing whether the pattern of adoption visually resembled the expected Bass model pattern. Even in the broader context of IT di<sup>f</sup>usion, and not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally HIE, research has concentrated mostly on snapshot, often cross-sectional and survey based, data. Even the few time series analyses (Agha, 2014; DesRoches et al., 2013; Mennemeyer et al., 2016; Veiga et al., 2014) have used survey based selfreported data rather than studying how actual penetration changes over time. Likewise, studying EHR, Ford et al. (Ford, Menachemi, Peterson, & Huerta, 2009; Ford, Menachemi, & Phillips, 2006) relied on samples, and a meta-analysis, rather than raw data. Yaraghi et al. (2015) examined aspects of HIE penetration in a three-year longitudinal study on the adoption behaviour of 2,054 physicians, but did not run a time series, nor investigate how external events change the penetration rates. Yaraghi et al. (2014) examined HIE penetration among doctors, and concluded that HIE adoption and penetration (in terms of how many medical records were uploaded) by physicians over a 35-month period increased when they shared patients with other physicians who adopted the HIE, and noted the importance of professional and geographical proximity with other physicians. Although Yaraghi et al. (2014) discussed their data in the context of a Bass model, they did not run a time series analysis or look at external events.

The only study we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that applied the Bass model in the context of HIE is Mennemeyer et al. (2016) who used the Bass model to study HIE adoption by medical practices based on survey data. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they were interested in whether subsidies had any e<sup>f</sup>ect. They found that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of subsidies on adoption of HIE in medical practices was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Their study, however, did not address the penetration of the HIE as measured by how many patients were accessed. Crucially, that study did not examine how usage patterns change in preparation for a new governmental policy.

## 3. The <sup>Clalit</sup> HIE

In 2005 the largest HMO in Israel, Clalit, instituted an HIE for information exchange among its 8 regular hospitals and the thousands of community-based physicians it employs. The HIE, Ofek, retrieves data from many medical systems, providing an integrated real-time virtual patient record that is available at all points of care in the healthcare organisation. The system also gathers historical patient data from the other HIE at the health service organisation’s hospitals and clinics. The data in the HIE include demographics, medication, adverse reactions, detailed lab and imaging results, past diagnoses, and healthcare procedures, etc. Studies of the Clalit HIE reported that in both the hospital and the community, system use was associated with a reduction in the number of key imaging and laboratory tests (Nirel et al., 2010; Nirel, Rosen, Sharon, Samuel, & Cohen, 2011) as well as better admission decisions (Balicer, Shadmi, & Israeli, 2013; Ben-Assuli, Shabtai, & Leshno, 2013; Ben-Assuli, Shabtai, Leshno, & Hill, 2014; Shadmi et al., 2015). These improvements are in line with the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from an HIE in the US, where, citing the Brookings Institution,<sup>3</sup> an HIE has the “the potential to eliminate redundant and often harmful medical procedures,” and reduce lab tests and radiology examination by about 25% and, in some cases, reduce radiology examinations by 47%. Ofek has since been installed at the Permanente Medical Groups in the USA. The implementation of Ofek has been of continuing interest to The European Observatory on Health Systems and Politics in conjunction with WHO Regional Ofice for Europe (e.g., Rosen et al. (2015)).

Early in 2011, the Israeli Ministry of Health (MOH) initiated the creation of a national HIE. The MOH published an RFP (request for proposals) for the creation of a national HIE based on an existing proven HIE. The HIE was expected to provide services across all medical domains and become a unifying consistent transparent national information infrastructure for all medical providers in the country in a manner that would allow authorised clinicians to have access to patient data they are authorised to see across all medical centres in the country. Implementing this HIE was a big issue because very few countries had implemented a national medical data registry by 2011. Both national and international companies submitted proposals to this RFP. The plan was to implement this HIE gradually so that each hospital would implement the HIE when they were ready. The implementation process was required to be synergetic and transparent. The national ombudsman veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that this was the nature of the implementation process.

Early in 2012, the MOH chose the HIE run by Clalit. The objective was the expansion of Clalit’s HIE to include all the other Israeli hospitals (adding 25 hospitals to the current 8 in the HIE) and the other three HMOs. The MOH strongly encouraged, but did not require, the expansion of Clalit’s HIE to include all the other Israeli hospitals and the other three HMOs. The MOH encouraged the adoption of Ofek but did not require it because with Ofek already successfully serving 57% of Clalit patients by 2012, the MOH did not feel that there was a need to pressure the physicians into using the HIE. (Also in the <sup>fi</sup>rst installation of the HIE, when it was adopted only at Clalit in 2005, the management did not dictate HIE use yet clinicians nonetheless adopted it.) Despite this being a directive rather than a requirement anchored in law, all the hospitals agreed to participate. The MOH was highly involved in supervising this process. The CEO of the MOH sent formal o<sup>fi</sup>cial directives to all hospitals and relevant points of care, asking the hospital managers to ensure the implementation of Ofek and its use, to verify that their physicians’ notes and summaries were added to it so that that information could be shared with other institutions, and to ensure adequate instruction and training. The MOH team also explained the process, budget and resources requirements, technological demands, data security procedures, and other related issues. The MOH provided additional guidance as needed and delivered monthly HIE usage reports to all the hospitals.

After February 2013, the other hospitals and health plans actively began going-live with Ofek, turning it gradually into a national network. Figure 1 shows the gradual increase in the number of hospitals using this HIE along with the coverage rate in the original Clalit hospitals. The Y axis is the number of hospitals that adopted the HIE. The X axis is time. All the Clalit hospitals had long since been part of this HIE since 2005. The increase in the number of hospitals in the <sup>fi</sup>gure shows other hospitals (governmental, public, and private) that joined the HIE. Note that the hypotheses are not about this <sup>fi</sup>gure of adoption, but rather about the penetration of the HIE as measured by the proportion of patients whose data was accessed through the HIE.

Our interviews with hospital managers outside Clalit showed that the managers of those hospitals saw this as a national project. There was no indication of competition among the hospitals with regard to the implementation of this HIE. On the contrary, the hospital managers, after viewing demonstrations of the HIE and receiving the directives from the MOH to implement it, saw this HIE as an opportunity to improve medical services. We also know based on these interviews that the implementation of the HIE in those hospitals started by establishing telecommunication and data security procedures, a process that took 5–6 months. Only after telecommunication and data security issues were resolved, did the hospitals in the other HMOs start their own internal training, partly aided by Clalit. After training, pilot implementation projects were run in those hospitals. In the case of Clalit this meant additional training programmes in its own hospitals. This transition of Ofek from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c HMO HIE into the national HIE received press coverage (TV and newspapers) several months (\~5–6 months) before and after the go-live date.

## 4. Hypotheses

The hypotheses deal with the monthly change in the proportion of the ER patients whose data were accessed through the HIE (medically known as coverage rate) across the 8 Clalit hospitals, and how governmental directives a<sup>f</sup>ected it. Over this 7-year period from 2010 to 2016, the coverage rate increased from 0.2282 (= 22.82% of patients registered in the HIE) to 0.3096 with a mean of 0.2693 and a standard deviation of 0.0232. The underlying theoretical basis of the study is the Bass Model (Bass, 1969), adapted.

The Bass model was introduced by Bass (1969) as a “growth model for the timing of initial purchases of new products” (p. 215). The Bass model explicitly builds on the social processes suggested among others by Rogers’ Di<sup>f</sup>usion of Innovations model (Rogers, 1962). As Bass put it, “The probability of adopting by those who have not yet adopted is a linear function of those who had previously adopted.”<sup>4</sup> In this model, the di<sup>f</sup>usion of new products over time depends on an initial di<sup>f</sup>usion rate by what he called innovators and by subsequent adoption by imitators who are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by word-of-mouth and other social processes from satis<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier adopters (Bass, 1969). The model assumes, but no research we found has actually veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, that imitators learn about the product or service from earlier adopters. (In fact, Lawrence (1975) argued that there was no need to assume a learning function in di<sup>f</sup>usion models.)

The Bass model (Bass, 1969) has been applied to a variety of products (Bass, Krishnan, & Dipak, 1994; Mahajan, Muller, & Srivastava, 1990), across demographic segments, including age, education, income, and occupation (Mahajan et al., 1990), and countries (e.g., Takada and Jain (1991)). The model has been extended to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate mathematically between new adopters and those who switch to a newer version of the product (Jiang & Jain, 2012). The Bass model shows a repeating consistent pattern across products and services (Mahajan, Muller, & Bass, 1995). Bass (1969) and many others showed empirically that the innovator rate and the imitator rate parameters behave as if they are constants.

In the Bass model (Bass, 1969), the proportion of those in a market who have not yet adopted but choose to adopt at time t is a function of the proportion who have already adopted. Mathematically, the model speci<sup>fi</sup>es that this proportion, given as $f ( t ) / { }$ $\left( l - F ( t ) \right) .$ , depends on two parameters $\boldsymbol { p }$ and $q ,$ where $f ( t )$ is the proportion of the whole market that will adopt at time $t , ~ F ( t )$ is the proportion of that same market that has already adopted by time $t , ~ p$ is the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, and $q$ is the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation. See equation (1).

$$
\frac {f (t)}{1 - F (t)} = p + q * F (t)\tag{1}
$$

The coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ can be thought of as an initial di<sup>f</sup>usion rate by innovators at time $t = 0$

The coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation, $q ,$ is the degree that new adopters are a<sup>f</sup>ected by positive word-of-mouth from previous adopters. The value of $q$ may also depend on peer pressure and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Jiang & Sarkar, 2009). $F ( t )$ is the integral of $f ( t )$ from time 0 to time t (Bass, 1969). The graph of $F ( t )$ resembles an S with curvature at very low and very high levels of adoption, but with a middle portion that is nearly straight, indicating an almost constant degree of di<sup>f</sup>usion (see empirical cases (Colapinto, Sartori, & Tolotti, 2014; Ford et al., 2009, 2006; Teng, Grover, & Guttler, $2 0 0 2 ) ) . ^ { 5 } P$ and q are product-speci<sup>fi</sup>c parameters estimated from the data, and previous research assumed that they are time invariant (Wang, Li, Aguiar, Menasche, & Sha<sup>fi</sup>q, 2017). Originally, Bass (1969) estimated their values for a range of products up to 1961, with very high $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values. By assessing an HIE penetration rate over $^ { 7 2 }$ months as a Bass model (Bass, 1969), our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that this rate behaves as an autoregressive level 1 ARIMA model.

In line with the Bass model (Bass, 1969), the data were translated from their original coverage rate, $F ( t ) _ { \ast }$ the proportion of the population that adopted the system, to the dependent variable of the Bass model, termed here H, as: $H ( t ) = f ( t ) / ( { I } { - F ( t ) } )$ . The remainder of the time-series analysis deals with $H ( t ) . ^ { 6 }$ We assumed that the penetration of the HIE would follow the basic assumptions made in the Bass model: (1) once a patient is added into the HIE, the patient remains in the system, much as in the Bass model where once a person purchases a product or service they are assumed not to un-purchase $\mathrm { i t } ; ^ { 7 }$ (2) there is no repurchase, so once patients are added into the HIE, the patients can have their information updated but are not added again; (3) the dependent variable of interest is the rate at which patients are added to the system among those not yet added; and (4) as in a typical Bass model application, also in the case of the HIE studied, the market size is very large and changes very slowly, if at all. In other words, the nature of the penetration of this HIE is such that the proportion of patients accessed by the HIE continuously increases from one time-period to the next. That increase parallels the mathematical modelling in the Bass model of a continuous increase in the num ber of adopters of the product or service. However, rather than modelling the proportion of people at time t who adopt a service or product based on their own decision, it models the proportion of patients added to the HIE by the ER clinicians. This also means that rather than having the decision to access the HIE for that patient be based on imitation, the “imitation” coe<sup>fi</sup>cient represents learning from self-experience or the experience of others. The Bass model has been shown to apply empirically across a variety of products and services based on equivalent assumptions of a continuous increase from one timeperiod to the next in the number of adopters. This assumption has been made by research into EHR di<sup>f</sup>usion as well, and empirically supports the expected S shaped curve over time with an almost straight line after the initial introduction of the EHR (Ford et al., 2009, 2006). The Bass model has also been used to model the pattern of retrieving information by MDs from a healthcare provider HIE (Yaraghi et al., 2014) and in IT outsourcing contract growth patterns (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992).

Equation (2) shows the hypothesis that HIE penetration resembles a Bass model (Bass, 1969) based on equation (1). The proportion of patients whose data are accessed for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time through the HIE at time t, paralleling conceptually the proportion of adopters in equation (1) at time t, is the linear combination of ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ the innovation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, and $q ,$ the imitation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, multiplied by $F ( t )$ the proportion of patients who have already had their data accessed through the HIE at time $t . ^ { 8 }$ This model can be expressed in parallel terms as an ARIMA (1,0,0) model, i.e., as an autoregressive level 1 time series. In this ARIMA model penetration rate at time t, H(t), depends on a linear combination of ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ the innovation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient in Bass model terminology or $\mu$ in ARIMA terminology, and $q ,$ the imitation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient in Bass model terminology or the autoregressive level 1 parameter in ARIMA terminology, multiplied by $F ( t ) ,$ the proportion of the population (patients) who have already had their data accessed through the HIE.<sup>9</sup>

$$
H (t) = \frac {f (t)}{1 - F (t)} = \frac {F (t) - F (t - 1)}{1 - F (t)} = p + q * F (t)\tag{2}
$$

Accordingly, and consistent with the Bass model assumptions, $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 }$ assumes a constant level of both ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, and q, the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation. Importantly, once a patient has been accessed in any one of the hospitals, that patient is registered thereafter in all hospitals connected to this HIE. Consequently, the proportion of registered patients continuously increases over time. Seasonality is not assumed because the data deal with ER visits rather than family doctor visits. (There was no seasonality evident in the ARIMA models tested.) This hypothesis will be quali<sup>fi</sup>ed by $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ so that it refers to only the months excluding the response to the new government policy.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 1 : }$ The change over time in the penetration rate of this HIE can be described as a Bass Model, i.e., the proportion of patients who are currently unregistered and who will be registered at time t is a linear function of the proportion who have already been accessed in the past by time t.

The next hypothesis deals with the quasiexperiment. The HIE management, who were informed of the new MOH directive ahead of time, started their technical preparations as well as the internal training programs for their own clinicians and for hospitals in other HMO networks long before the go-live date of their HIE as a national system. These preparations are consistent with studies on related EHR systems, pointing to the need to account for technical, psychological, and organisational barriers (Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010) as well as to allow the organisation to adapt to the new system (Gagnon et al., 2014) and deal with regulatory issues (Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Fernández-Alemán et al., 2013; Haas et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2012). Starting early was especially pertinent in the case of an HIE such as Ofek because the HIE contains legally restricted access patient data (a major concern to the MOH), such that moving from an intra-organisational HIE to an inter-organisational (national) HIE raised new privacy concerns that would need to be addressed ahead of any transition.

This accrued attention on the part of the HIE by its management, Clalit hospitals and their management in this preparatory period, combined with the training of clinicians from other hospital networks, and interest from the press should have sent a strong message to the Clalit ER clinicians of just how good their HIE was. Such a promotional message should have encouraged the clinicians at Clalit to be even more eager to use the HIE based on the assumed social in<sup>fl</sup>uence at the core of the Bass model (Bass, 1969). In other words, based on the Bass model, one would assume that the ER clinicians in this HMO would be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by these activities over and above the increase caused by their positive previous experience with the HIE. This reasoning is consistent with research showing that the value of the imitation coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, $q ,$ may also depend on social in<sup>fl</sup>uences (Jiang & Sarkar, 2009). Suoranta and Mattila (2004) also found that imitators might be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by external factors; word-of-mouth in their case and not only by imitating innovators. This strong message should have encouraged the Clalit clinicians to use their HIE over and above their initial intention to adopt/use it based on their initial innovation rate, ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ and their self-learning imitating rate, $q .$ That is, the choice of Ofek as the national HIE and the activities to make it so should have reinforced the intrinsic value of Ofek. In Bass model terminology, such a promotion would increase people’s intention to adopt/use the product or service because the product or service now has increased intrinsic value, i.e., an increase in the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation $\textstyle { p . }$ But in contrast to the assumed change in the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ there was no reason to assume a change in the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation $q$ because the population of clinicians being studied was the same before and after this event (as validated in the interviews with Clalit managers). Thus, there was no reason to assume that their level of imitation q would change permanently.

## 5. Data analyses

## 5.1. The data

The data were provided by JDC – Brookdale Institute, Israel who obtained it from Clalit Health Services and the Israeli MOH. The data contained for each month between February 2010 (dealing with January 2010) and December 2015 (dealing with November 2015) the ER patient penetration rate (the proportion of patients visiting the ER whose data had been accessed through the HIE) across all 8 Clalit hospitals. Since only a relatively small number of patients go to an ER in any given month, each monthly data point was treated as an independent sample from the overall population, making it an approximation of the true rate in the population. Consequently, while the penetration rate increases continuously in the population, there will be noise around that rate in each sample. As a result, in some of the samples (monthly visits to the ER) the coverage rate was below and in some higher than that of the population, but in both cases insigni<sup>fi</sup>cantly so. Hence, even though F(t) cannot decrease, the monthly observation may <sup>fl</sup>uctuate around the true value in the population (i.e., be subject to sampling error). The dependent variable of the Bass model, f(t)/(1-F(t)), ranged from −.032 to .031 (mean = .001, std. = .012). The data also included how many hospitals from Clalit and from other HMOs were in the HIE. Patient di<sup>f</sup>usion rate relates only to the 8 Clalit regular hospitals. The data were compiled by the team in charge of the ongoing operation of the HIE directly from the HIE data.

## 5.2. Time series data analyses

The data were analysed with ARIMA in SAS 9.4. ARIMA is a standard Box and Jenkins method of applying time series analysis (Box & Jenkins, 1970). ARIMA has been used in the past also to estimate the Bass model (Bass, 1969) parameters (e.g., Wang et al. (2017), Darda, Guseo, and Mortarino (2012), and Guseo and Guidolin (2008)). As Wang et al. (2017) concluded empirically, the Bass model provides “comparable prediction accuracy” (p. 22) to an ARIMA model. Much as the Bass model, ARIMA too is data-driven. Research has applied other datadriven methods to estimate the parameters of the

Bass model. However, ARIMA is especially suitable for the purposes of this study because it can include the kind of transition hypothesised in $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ and because it allows for the comparison of alternative models and easily assesses seasonal trends. ARIMA also enables the assessment of white noise through standardised and visual tools.

The time series plot of the data is shown in the left panel of Figure 2 with an added superimposed regression line, and again on the right emphasising the two clusters. The <sup>fi</sup>gure shows $F ( T ) _ { \mathit { i } }$ , the proportion of patients whose data had already been accessed in the HIE by time t. Note that the hypothesis concerns $H ( t ) = f ( t ) / ( 1 { - } F ( t ) )$ , i.e., the proportion of patients whose data had not yet been accessed and whose data were <sup>fi</sup>rst accessed at time t. The superimposed vertical line at observation 37 corresponds to January 2013 when the new policy came into e<sup>f</sup>ect. ## 6. Subsequent interviews conducted after the data analysis

The results of the time series analysis were presented to two directors of the Clalit hospital division to corroborate the conclusions and obtain additional insights. The results were also presented to a former MOH department manager. The results were <sup>fi</sup>rst shown without specifying our interpretation of them. Only after the interviewees expressed their opinions did we present our understandings and asked the interviewees about them. The Clalit directors con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the government did not exert any pressure on Clalit to adopt the HIE any faster. The government chose the Clalit HIE as the national HIE because it was presumably the best system. Clalit did not need to make any direct technological changes to the HIE (except possible indirect and non-mandatory investments). Clalit helped other hospitals and HMOs through guidance, technical support, and other forms of assistance to deploy the HIE. The two directors were asked, based on their experience, to tell us why there was an overall monthly increase in di<sup>f</sup>usion rates, and what happened at the beginning of 2013 or perhaps a few months before that date that caused the noticeable upwards shift in the time series. The directors suggested that organisational culture might have contributed to the overall monthly increase in di<sup>f</sup>usion rates, and possibly because the other HMOs and the other hospitals started entering their patients’ data. The interviewees also con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed that a change in the number of Clalit’s HIE users was not the reason as the number of Clalit users was approximately constant over this time-period. The directors added that the choice of Ofek as the national HIE contributed to the increased enthusiasm with Ofek at Clalit and the subsequent increase in its di<sup>f</sup>usion rate. Beyond that, there was nothing speci<sup>fi</sup>c about the project management in the case of this HIE, except that Clalit helped the other hospitals and HMOs.

Asked about the increase in July 2012, the directors did not negate the possibility that there was an esprit de corps that may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the clinicians. The increase in implementation was not related to access to patient information from the other HMOs, since they only joined the HIE starting in April and some only in September and December 2013. Another major event that could have conceivably a<sup>f</sup>ected the shift in di<sup>f</sup>usion rates was the implementation of other IT systems and hardware. In fact, starting in 2012 Clalit gradually implemented a new EMR (Electronic Medical Record) system called Chameleon across its clinics. However, subsequent interviews with those involved with Chameleon clari<sup>fi</sup>ed that Clalit started implementing Chameleon in its ER units gradually, one hospital at a time, and only starting in September 2013, long after the July 2012 transition identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the data.

The next set of interviews sought to verify the role of the government in this process. A former department manager of MOH veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that it was the government who chose the HIE at Clalit for the national HIE, but also that the MOH did not pressure Clalit on the matter. Rather, this department manager said that Clalit wanted their HIE to be chosen, and, once it was, helped the other hospitals and health plans to integrate and implement the Clalit HIE through assistance with technical and security issues. For their part, the MOH, including their top management team, actively promoted the Clalit HIE to the other HMOs and hospitals in the country.

## 7. Discussion

7.1. Summary of the results as they relate to HIE penetration

Adding to the stream of research about HIE adoption, this study examined the penetration rate of HIE after its successful adoption. Importantly, the study shows that (1) the penetration of an HIE can be modelled as a Bass model (Bass, 1969), thereby enabling numeric forecasting and, accordingly, better resource allocation, and (2) that the innovation rate of the Bass model can be changed during this penetration process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the innovation rate exhibited a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant one-time increase in the penetration rate in the HIE in the 8 regular hospitals of the HMO as Clalit prepared to make its HIE into a national one. The results are especially important considering that no previous research on IT or HIE di<sup>f</sup>usion has addressed such a case of intervention from a Bass model perspective with its quantitative modelling, let alone measured it over an extensive period of time. Being able to precisely model HIE penetration over time including the e<sup>f</sup>ect of government intervention could provide managers with essential insight that allows them to improve planning and budgeting. Our adaptation of the Bass model to incorporate outside interventions may also be useful in other contexts in which the Bass model has been applied.

Shedding light on the reasons behind the change in the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, the interviews substantiated that the HIE project management took appropriate steps to prepare for that change starting 6 months before the go-live date and that the clinicians using the HIE were exposed to those activities by the HIE management and by their colleagues, and presumably also through news about their HIE being chosen to be the national one. All that positive attention to the HIE should have promoted the image of the HIE in their eyes. Viewed through the lens of the Bass model (Bass, 1969), such attention might, through social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, increase the intrinsic value of the HIE, thus explaining the increase in the coe<sup>fi</sup>- cient of innovation. Perhaps supporting that view, the interrupted autoregressive order 1 ARIMA time series model suggests a one-period change in the hazard rate, rather than a long-term lasting change. This is consistent with a one-time increase in the perceived intrinsic value of the HIE as a result of the positive attention it received from management during that period.

To put the results into context, to the best of our knowledge, there is no publication on ABI/INFORM that addresses a Bass model intervention about any other product or service. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings here can thus contribute on a practical level on how best to manage such a transition in the case of the di<sup>f</sup>usion of other HIE, an evident given the failed attempts to implement HIE in the UK (Anderson et al., 2006; UK, 2011) and Spain (Corbellini et al., 2010). This need is especially pertinent considering that governmental intervention is often a key to HIE di<sup>f</sup>usion success.

## 7.2. Implications for the Bass model in view of HIE penetration

In the Bass model (Bass, 1969) the adoption of a new product or service is a function of two parameters: ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, and $q ,$ the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of imitation. The model can be used to forecast adoption/di<sup>f</sup>usion rates at various points in time. As we showed here, the same applies also to HIE penetration rates. The model assumes that both parameters are constant, although subsequent research has also looked at temporary declines in the curve, dubbed saddles (Goldenberg, Libai, & Muller, 2002; Mahajan & Muller, 1998), and how new generations of the product may a<sup>f</sup>ect its di<sup>f</sup>usion (Norton & Bass, 1987). The Bass model also does not account for external governmental intervention that might change those two parameters in ways that markets cannot. As subsequent research has indicated, those assumptions are reasonable for many products and services, including IT, and the Bass model provides reasonable data-driven approximations of actual diffusion rates. However, there are cases in which portraying a more realistic picture may require acknowledging that signi<sup>fi</sup>cant external interventions do occur, possibly causing the model parameters to change. In fact, the assumption of constant parameters throughout the product or service lifecycle is one of the critiques levied against the Bass model (Fornerino, 2003). This study demonstrates that allowing the Bass model parameters to change as a result of policy implementation leads to a better description of HIE penetration.

Another contribution has to do with the addition into the Bass model of drawing positive attention to the product or service, attention that can be understood as a promotion, as formulated in $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } .$ The Bass model predicts product and service di<sup>f</sup>usion over time based on product- and service-speci<sup>fi</sup>c parameters, but does not address whether those parameters might change during the product lifecycle. The HIE penetration di<sup>f</sup>usion case studied here dealt with a change that was induced by the HIE management when preparing for a governmentintroduced change in policy. We expanded the Bass model by allowing its parameters to change in response to government policy, but the parameters might also change for other reasons such as marketing and advertising. Products and services are promoted, and sometimes discouraged, on a regular basis, so understanding how promotion can be added into the Bass model has both theoretical and practical importance. This study takes an initial step in that direction. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, HIE penetration was accelerated indirectly in response to new governmental policy by changing the penetration rate months before the new policy came into e<sup>f</sup>ect. This suggests that interventions can have a pronounced, but a onetime and indirect, e<sup>f</sup>ect in advance of their actual implementation.

## 7.3. The uniqueness of the data

Although the Bass model (Bass, 1969) has been widely studied since the 1970s, the application of time series analysis to the Bass model is rare (Christodoulos, Michalakelis, & Varoutas, 2010). In fact, we found no MIS journal article that applied an explicit statistical time series analysis to the Bass model. To put the data in this study into perspective, previous research on IT adoption has concentrated on snapshot data, often survey based, or less frequently survey based time series data (Agha, 2014; DesRoches et al., 2013; Veiga et al., 2014). The time series analysis presented here provides a glimpse into IT penetration as a process that evolves over time after its initial adoption. The time series data also show that the response to external promotion of the HIE is not in a long-term change in the hazard rate but rather in a one-time increase, suggesting that di<sup>f</sup>usion rates depend in the long run on other factors such as the characteristics of the IT, its contribution, and the population that uses it. The real-life data adds to the external validity of the results that could not have been achieved in a controlled experiment (Cook & Campbell, 1979; Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002), even if such controlled experiments were possible.

## 7.4. Contribution to practice

Being able to forecast the extent of IT penetration numerically, in this case HIE penetration, could have a substantial impact on IT management through better allocation of resources. Modelling the impact of external intervention could further improve such allocation of resources. Our results show that the announcement of a change in government policy can lead to a one-time increase in penetration in advance of the actual policy change, and that the rate of di<sup>f</sup>usion remains the same once this onetime increase has occurred. Considering that the speci<sup>fi</sup>c HIE studied is now implemented internationally, these conclusions could inform implementations in other countries where this speci<sup>fi</sup>c HIE is now being implemented, namely the USA and Australia. In fact, The European Observatory on Health Systems and Politics in conjunction with WHO Regional Ofice for Europe have been closely monitoring this HIE implementation process (e.g., Rosen et al. (2015)).

The results are also revealing because they put governmental intervention into perspective. Regulatory agencies may learn from this and emphasise the direct bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an HIE rather than dictate what needs to be done. The data suggest that the penetration rate of an HIE should be thought of as a long-term process that can be advanced by intervention but remains a typical di<sup>f</sup>usion process. As HIE become increasingly available in other countries (Ben-Assuli, 2015; Hyppönen et al., 2014; Morrison et al., 2011; Shortli<sup>f</sup>e, 2005; Yoder, 2011), these conclusions could be of even greater importance.

The interviews provide additional insights into the background. The successful penetration of this HIE was by no means obvious. Its success demonstrates the importance of preparing in advance and making sure all the parties, including the other hospitals and HMOs, are on board to achieve a unifying national HIE. Such preparation and the subsequent cooperation it created is one of the reasons for the one-time increase in the di<sup>f</sup>usion rate, but it is also the reason the higher penetration rate continued thereafter. These steps may have created a necessary sense of increased buy-in on the part of both the clinicians at Clalit and the clinicians at the other hospitals and HMOs. Starting early, 6 months before the go-live date, was apparently crucial in this process of not only preparing the technology, but, more crucially, of preparing user psychology. Even more importantly, Clalit helped other hospitals and especially the other HMOs despite being in competition with them, and this assistance ultimately served all the parties involved.

## 7.5. Limitations

No two systems are alike, no two national HIE networks face the same challenges, and there are always unique organisational constraints. The results of this study should be read with that caveat in mind. Another limitation relates to the nature of the data. Clearly more might be at play in the hospitals than these data alone reveal, although the white noise analysis in the ARIMA models indicates that there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pattern in the residuals over time that would suggest any missing variables.

## 8. Conclusion

HIE can lead to better and cheaper patient care. Its adoption and subsequent penetration, however, can be challenging. The results of this study, examining a successful penetration of an HIE, showed that HIE penetration approximates a Bass model (1969) and that external promotion can contribute to this process. The positive impact that can emerge ahead of such external promotion may come in advance of the go-live date of its expansion, but its impact may only result in a onetime increase in penetration rather than a permanent rise in the rate at which new users are added.

## Appendix 1. Previous applications of the bass model to IT and related applications

The Bass model (Bass, 1969) initially dealt with durables, and has been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed for a variety of products and services. It has since been applied also to software (e.g., Teng et al. (2002)). The model is highly cited in marketing research, but, with a few exceptions (e.g., Jiang and Sarkar (2009), Altinkemer and Shen (2008), and Loh and Venkatraman (1992)) has been mostly ignored in MIS journals. Related to our study, marketing studies exploring high tech di<sup>f</sup>usion and using the Bass model (such as Goldenberg, Han, Lehmann, and Hong (2009), Suoranta and Mattila (2004), Akçura and Altınkemer (2002), and Lee, Trimi, and Kim (2013)) showed that adoption by imitators relies heavily on having a large base of innovators, rather than mass media or advertising. Hence, the tendency to imitate innovators, giving out free trial samples (Arora, Hofstede, & Mahajan, 2017; Han & Zhang, 2018), and even software piracy (Givon, Mahajan, & Muller, 1995), can increase di<sup>f</sup>usion rates substantially. Building a large user base thus emerges as the most important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing imitators. Advertising, at least in the case of telephonic banking adoption, can also shift the Bass curve forward in time (Horsky & Simon, 1993).

One of the few core MIS articles that has applied the Bass model to actual IT adoption is Jiang and Sarkar (2009). That study di<sup>f</sup>erentiated between high-valuation and low-valuation customers, and discussed the impact of varying p and q coe<sup>fi</sup>cients as well as the e<sup>f</sup>ect of giving out free software on company pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. The authors concluded that as the value of p in that model increases, i.e., there is a higher initial di<sup>f</sup>usion rate, giving out new software free of charge has less of an impact in enhancing the adoption of new software. The e<sup>f</sup>ect of q, the degree of word-of-mouth, emerged as more complex. If the value of q is very low or very high, o<sup>f</sup>ering new software for free had little bene<sup>fi</sup>t. However, at intermediate levels of q, a higher level of q increased the di<sup>f</sup>usion rate. Likewise, in the Teng et al. (2002) study of the di<sup>f</sup>usion of 20 types of IT hardware and software products in a cross-sectional model of 313 large American <sup>fi</sup>rms, the Bass model provided a consistent representation of the di<sup>f</sup>usion pattern across IT types. They also reported that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of innovation, p, was very small relative to imitation, q.

The Bass model has also been applied to explore for correlations between free and paid app adoption, and showed that free apps are adopted quickly but that their adoption has a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on paid app adoption (Arora et al., 2017). The Bass model has also been applied to the di<sup>f</sup>usion of B2B, B2C and P2P exchanges in e-commerce where studies showed that the imitation e<sup>f</sup>ect is stronger than the innovation e<sup>f</sup>ect, i.e., imitation attracts more new users than innovation and additional features do (Akçura & Altınkemer, 2002). This may be why giving free samples is a widespread marketing tool to increase product penetration, especially in the context of software and games (Han & Zhang, 2018). The Bass model also applies to the pattern of major IT outsourcing contracts as noted in the press after Kodak initiated the process of signing IT outsourcing contracts (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992).

Other related research includes Fornerino (2003) who explored Internet di<sup>f</sup>usion in France based on data from 24,000 households from 1996 to 2000 and found that whereas innovators were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by external socia factors, imitators were impacted by innovators. Suoranta and Mattila (2004) applied the Bass model to explore mobile banking di<sup>f</sup>usion. Surveying 1,253 users in Finland, Suoranta and Mattila found that potentia adopters were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by mass media and word-ofmouth. Likewise, smart-phone di<sup>f</sup>usion followed a Bass model pattern (Danaher, Hardie, & Putsis, 2001), as did that of CD players in France, and DVD player/recorders as well as digital cameras in Germany (Decker & Gnibba-Yukawa, 2010). For game console di<sup>f</sup>usion, Altinkemer and Shen (2008) added repeat adopters to the Bass model in the context of competing technologies, and tested their extension on data on the di<sup>f</sup>usion of various kinds of game consoles. Here as well, the Bass model provided a good <sup>fi</sup>t to the data. Kim, Chang, and Shocker (2000) simulated market behaviour and calibrated their model on wireless telecommunication services in Asia to assess inter-product category and substitution e<sup>f</sup>ects.

To summarise, research suggests that the Bass model provides a good estimate of the proportion of adopters in the market at time t as a function of previous adoptions by individual consumers at time t-1. None of the studies assumed external governmental intervention or a corresponding preparational organisational IT management reaction.
