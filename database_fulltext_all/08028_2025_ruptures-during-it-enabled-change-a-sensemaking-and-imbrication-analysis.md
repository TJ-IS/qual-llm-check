# RUPTURES DURING IT-ENABLED CHANGE: A SENSEMAKING AND IMBRICATION ANALYSIS<sup>1</sup>

Nicole Haggerty Ivey Business School, Western University London, Ontario, Canada {nhaggerty@ivey.ca}

Deborah Compeau

Carson College of Business, Washington State University Pullman, WA U.S.A. {deborah.compeau@wsu.edu}

Focusing on imbrication change as conducted by designers during design and development, we undertook a 2.5-year long case study to examine how 10 hospitals configured technology and planned routine changes as they sought to transform their organizations. Our observations revealed a phenomenon we call ruptures, defined as situations that occur due to breakdowns in the process of IT-enabled change. Ruptures allow us to unpack the temporal process of identifying and resolving instances of disruptive problems during IT-enabled change. Our analysis of ruptures in this context expands the theory of imbrication to account for the role of designers. We demonstrate that three imbrication types—historical, envisioned, and realized—exist cognitively during design and influence the design process. Our results demonstrate how problems arise, how dynamic change unfolds through sensemaking, and how fractal, embedded, imbrications complicate the IT-enabled change process.

Keywords: IT-enabled change, sociotechnical systems, sensemaking, imbrications, affordance

## Introduction

Organizations often struggle with IT-enabled change initiatives, despite their prevalence in the drive towards digitalization. Surveys, such as the one by de la Boutetiere et al. (2018), indicate that while a vast majority of organizations are engaged in such initiatives, only a minority see performance improvements. This issue is not new; the challenges of IT projects have been discussed for decades (Standish Group, 1995), yet a significant proportion of these projects still fail or face substantial challenges (Johnson, 2020). The Phoenix payroll project in Canada (Office of the Auditor General of Canada, 2018) serves as a stark example. This project was promoted as a transformational payroll initiative, requiring extensive collaboration between diverse stakeholders from 101 departments paying almost 300,000 public servants. Initiated in 2010 and rolled out over 2016, nearly 82,000 employees experienced pay errors with the new system. The outcome highlights the difficulty of managing complex IT projects despite the application of standard project management and governance practices. More specifically, the Phoenix project failed to navigate the differing priorities of its cross-disciplinary stakeholders, whose differing payroll requirements demanded different routines within the new technology.

Existing literature provides various perspectives on ITenabled change, emphasizing the interplay between human and material elements. Models like the punctuated sociotechnical change model (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008) and theories on emergent change through improvisation (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) highlight the importance of aligning organizational components and facilitating learning among users. Concepts such as embedding (Volkoff et al., 2007) and imbrication (Holeman & Barrett, 2017; Leonardi, 2011) further elaborate how change is enacted through the integration of new technologies and routines. These perspectives acknowledge that IT-enabled change unfolds as social and technological elements interact to produce or inhibit change, with varying emphasis on the social or technical drivers. They further suggest that successful ITenabled change hinges on the dynamic interactions between design and use and the resolution of conflicts and disagreements during the design process. This can happen as actors collaborate to align the build and work systems (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008), through the social influence of project designers on users (Boudreau & Robey, 2005), or through designer efforts to accommodate the emergence of novel practices (Holeman & Barrett, 2017).

Yet these perspectives diverge in how they consider the interactions between social and material agencies, especially at the granular level of features, capabilities, and routines—a domain we term “microdynamics.” While aiming for generalizability, this often results in a “black boxing” of crucial details (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). Moreover, concepts like critical incidents (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008) and practice breakdowns (Holeman & Barrett, 2017) suggest that disagreements or problematic incidents between designers and users pose significant alignment challenges. However, the existing literature largely overlooks the intricacies of these disagreements and breakdowns during the design phase, including how they emerge and are resolved and their eventual impact on project outcomes.

Our research seeks to address these limitations by focusing on the early phases of design and development, which are crucial for understanding the outcomes of IT-enabled change initiatives. We used the lens of imbrication as a toolkit for understanding how challenges to IT-enabled change emerge with new sociotechnical practices (Holeman & Barrett, 2017; Kim et al., 2012; Stampe & Müller, 2018). Imbrications emphasize the microdynamics of reciprocal interactions between material and human agency that set change in motion and span design and use time frames and can shed light on early experiences of conflicts and disagreements. Though mostly used to understand user-led change, in this research, we seek to expand on the theoretical understanding of imbrication change by focusing on the sensemaking processes of multidisciplinary actors during the design and development phases of IT-enabled organizational change.

We conducted a 2.5-year case study with a group of 10 hospitals to understand how they configured technology and planned routine changes based on intended human and material agency during design through implementation and early adoption. We followed two leadership teams composed of senior management, senior medical professionals, senior IT leaders, and vendor partners tasked with oversight of what they called a transformation program, the design and implementation of the technology, and the work changes required. We noted how they navigated collaboration, provided input, and recursively determined the design of technology and routines to set the stage for the intended change. We paid attention to difficulties that arose to detect the early emergence of problems that could interfere with the effectiveness of the changes.

While observing participants making choices about the design of routines and technologies underpinned by interwoven human/material agency, we uncovered naturally occurring episodes we call ruptures. Ruptures became a central contribution of our theorizing since they encapsulate issues that prior research has identified as emerging in use. The concept of ruptures captures how these emerge during design. Ruptures encompass surprise, disagreement, or misunderstanding among participants and actions taken to interpret and plan fixes to new routines and technologies as they are tested and implemented.

By bracketing ruptures as they unfold during design, development, and implementation we were able to frame the change process as instances of sensemaking and imbrication. This provided a way to analyze how change unfolded dynamically during difficulties and disagreements. It required an expanded vocabulary for imbrications in the work of designers and their position between users’ past and future use. The rupture concept also allowed us to observe that imbrications are not linear but can be characterized as fractal and multilevel. This insight offers a more precise lens for detecting, evaluating, and acting on these change processes.

Our findings contribute to studies of IT-enabled organizational change by illustrating how dynamic sensemaking and imbrication occur within ruptures and how they influence change outcomes. Ruptures provide an opening for participants to address emerging issues and resolve them. We also contribute to the literature on imbrication by extending it to account for the time frame when material and human agencies are interwoven between materiality and action (Holeman & Barrett, 2017; Leonardi, 2013), even as the materiality aspect is in flux, i.e., being designed and tested. This extension shows imbrication theory’s potential for insight into the simultaneous, interwoven aspects of design, development, and use (Leonardi & Rodriguez-Lluesma, 2012) and, owing to the fractal nature of imbrication, how its multiple levels expose design and implementation challenges that require collective work.

## Conceptual Foundations

## Imbrications Lens

The concept of imbrication has drawn attention as a way to understand the interweaving of human and material agencies and how this interweaving influences human action in realms as varied as flying a plane (Berthod & Müller-Seitz, 2018), plagiarism detection (Introna & Hayes, 2011), and automotive design (Leonardi, 2011). Leonardi’s explication of imbrication provides a seminal view of IT-enabled change as sociotechnical changes to routines and technologies (Leonardi, 2009, 2011). Routines are aspects of work that people undertake as sequential patterns of social action (Leonardi, 2011; Pentland & Rueter, 1994); technologies comprise the software-based information systems deployed to help people do their work and achieve objectives. From an imbrication perspective, a routine or technology is the visible infrastructure produced when human and material agencies overlap in particular ways. Human agency refers to the ability to set and realize goals (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). Material agency is the capacity of nonhuman entities to act on their own without human intervention (Holeman & Barrett, 2017; Leonardi, 2011), though human agency remains distinct because it is intentional and goal directed. Materiality itself comprises the properties of the material object—in this case, its feature set—that bound its interpretability and scope of action (Pinch & Bijker, 1987).

In Leonardi’s imbrication theory, materiality exists in the realm of structure, whereas through social interaction and interpretation, humans exist in a realm of action. Imbrication is an intermediate process where structure and action merge. It is the process by which material (Leonardi, 2013) and human agency interweave to create a routine or technology, each of which, ontologically speaking, is made of the same human and material agencies in different figurations. An imbrication-based account of change shows it emerging from shifting figurations of agencies as users act on them (Leonardi, 2011; Saadatmand et al., 2017).

Theoretical analyses using the imbrication lens focus on the distinction between the sociotechnical interweaving of human and material agency of the imbrication lens (Kautz & Jensen, 2012; Leonardi, 2012; Putnam, 2015) and the entanglement of human and material agencies in sociomaterial perspectives (Niemimaa, 2016; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). Imbrications also relate to the perspective of affordances and constraints, where imbrications are likened to underlying rock structures that influence currents that affordances correspond to as action potentials. Organizational scholars have used the imbrication lens to describe the complexities of human and material agency across levels of analysis. At the highest level of abstraction, such as a society, Ciborra (2006) theorized how dangerous consequences emerge from the imbrication of managerial risk representations when intertwined with the underlying technologies. Introna and Hayes (2011) theorized on the imbrication of plagiarism detection software with Greek university students in the context of the U.K. higher education system to yield a cultural perspective that these students are deficient and plagiaristic. At a more granular level, studies have shed light on how, over time, imbrication shifts led to the evolution of a shared platform in the Swedish Road Haulage industry (Saadatmand et al., 2017), how the implementation of Google Glass technology altered work practices and generated innovation in the Danish agricultural sector (Stampe & Müller, 2018), and how routines of lowlevel bureaucrats are imbricated with new digital government systems to empower discretionary decisionmaking or curtail street-level actions (Alshallaqi, 2022). Contrasting with this higher-abstraction level of imbricated material and human agency, other scholars have used the imbrication lens to untangle human and material agencies present in individual, event-based analyses of phenomena as varied as pilot interactions with an automated flying system during an airplane accident (Berthod & Müller-Seitz, 2018) and designer/user interactions during the deployment of a temperature gauge to improve vaccine efficacy in Kenyan health facilities (Holeman & Barrett, 2017). Each study detailed the temporal intertwining of material and human agency to influence action and produce change.

Empirical accounts of imbrication change have shown how novelty, learning, and experimentation are implicated in the change process (e.g., Holeman & Barrett, 2017; Stampe & Müller, 2018). Observing that transformational routines and technologies require a cognitive and learning orientation amongst participants (Jenkin et al., 2019), we added sensemaking to our imbrication lens to understand the mechanism at work.

## Sensemaking

Sensemaking (Weick, 1995) informs the study of organizational, group, and individual behavior during turbulence and novelty. Rather than expecting individuals to act rationally for effective decision-making, the sensemaking perspective holds that sensemaking begins when participants encounter the unexpected. Defined as “a process, prompted by violated expectations that involves attending to and bracketing cues in the environment, creating intersubjective meaning through cycles of interpretation and action, and thereby enacting a more ordered environment from which further cues can be drawn” (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014, p. 67), sensemaking allows researchers to interpret processes associated with organizational change. The sensemaking lens has been applied to technology because it is equivocal (Berente et al., 2011), with meanings and interpretations, placing demands on the ability to reason about its effects. It is thus subject to uncertainty and demands sensemaking because it addresses tacit functions of the organization that individuals have only limited access to (Weick, 1990).

Although Weick et al. (2005) argued that sensemaking is inherently retrospective, it can explain generative acts such as strategy formation (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). Like strategy formation, IT-enabled organizational change is guided by generative thinking. It is prospective and involves envisioning new routines and technologies built on the foundation of prior ones. Sensemaking is an appropriate lens because it allows us to seek explanations for actions during the IT-enabled change process that are rooted in sensemaking about how to achieve novel goals.

We use this lens to support our analysis by attending to how designers and users make sense of new technology by imbricating it with routines. In analyzing ruptures observed in the data set, we show how they illustrate processes of sensemaking: noticing and bracketing that trigger the sensemaking process, creating intersubjective meaning through individual learning and efforts to create shared understanding, and enactment or taking actions to restore order.

## Research Methodology

We conducted a longitudinal case study of the implementation of new health information technologies in a multihospital setting. This single-case holistic design worked (Yin, 2018) for the longitudinal nature of the study. Access granted by the organization allowed us to observe the project from design initiation through to implementation and stabilization. Our unit of analysis was the project, a clinical transformation program (CTP) to implement an electronic medical record (EMR) system. While multiple hospitals were involved in the project, we covered them as a unit because the project was to be integrated across the sites. The following sections describe the research context (the organization, technology, and project) and the data collection and analysis methodologies.

## Research Site

Ten hospital organizations (with 12,000 employees) were involved in CTP: three large urban teaching hospitals and seven regional hospitals in surrounding communities. The program involved the implementation of four new capabilities within an EMR system purchased from a leading vendor. These capabilities were expected to change the work routines of most clinical staff. For example, with computerized provider order entry (CPOE), physicians<sup>2</sup> would be required to enter medication, lab, and diagnostic imaging orders directly into the system rather than writing them on paper and having data entry performed by nonregulated clerical personnel. With electronic medication reconciliation (e-MedRec), the process of transitioning patients to/from their athome medications to those in-hospital would be computerized, requiring direct physician entry. With electronic medication administration record (eMAR) and closed-loop medication administration (CLMA), the process by which nurses administered medications would also change, requiring workstations on wheels to transport medications and record their administration and handheld devices to read wristbands and barcodes for tracking.

CTP was conceived in 2009, with a project charter formally approved in 2011; the steering committee (SC) first met in April 2012, and implementation was completed by mid-2014. The charter governed transformation objectives, success metrics, in-scope/out-of-scope elements, budget, stage/gate timing, project structure, and reporting relationships.

It followed a largely sequential, structured design methodology. Workflow sessions were held with potential users to clarify requirements and map the needed degree of change. A design review explained the system logic with several hundred key users and managers. Following workflow sessions and design review, a design team of hospital and vendor personnel worked to configure the vendor software for the organization. Implementation was conducted in phases, with different hospitals going live over an eight-month period from late 2013 to mid-2014. The EMR vendor was engaged as an implementation consultant, providing services related to system building and configuration and assistance in project and change management. The CTP elements were stabilized by January 2015, when the project moved to an optimization phase. Despite challenges, leadership considered the transformation effort a success: new systems were in place, some objectives were achieved according to key metrics (usage and medication error detection and reduction), and, other than a cost overrun on devices, most budget criteria were met.

## Data Collection

The primary source of data was nonparticipant observation of two committees that met monthly for the life of the project. The first committee was a multidisciplinary steering committee (SC) responsible for the overall conduct of the program, including budget, schedule, and benefits realization. The 25- member SC included senior hospital leadership, other senior clinical staff such as chief of pharmacy, chief nursing officer, and chief medical officer, the CIO of the hospital leading the program, other senior technical staff, and the vendor’s project liaison. We also observed the joint advisory committee (JAC) of 44 members, including physicians and nurse practitioners, pharmacists, nurses, allied health professionals, administrative staff, and members of the CTP team, including the program lead, track leads, a vendor representative, the technical lead, the regional program lead, the training lead, and the change management lead. The program leads were healthcare professionals assigned as boundary spanners for the duration (Gasson, 2005; Levina & Vaast, 2005) of the development process. The JAC made decisions about solution design, workflow changes, implementation, and change management to support the transformation.

Following the initial SC meeting in April 2012, the JAC first met in September 2012. Direct observation ran from November 2012 to the project’s conclusion (excluding optimization) in January 2015.<sup>3</sup> During the monthly meetings, each research team member took separate notes, creating 57 individual detailed observation documents (27 from the SC and 30 from the JAC). Because of our initial interest in shared understanding in information system design and development (Jenkin et al., 2019), we paid particular attention to discussion that reflected participant understandings of the CTP, routines to be altered, and the technology required to achieve goals. After the meetings, we shared perspectives on what we observed and amended our notes accordingly. Field notes were transcribed and stored in an NVivo database.

For each meeting, including those we did not personally attend, we had access to the PowerPoint slide deck used for discussion and meeting minutes. To check on the completeness and accuracy of the notes, we coded slides and minutes in NVivo alongside our field notes. Reviewing field notes with meeting documents ensured the capture of all discussion topics and allowed us to link our interpretations to the specific content of the discussions. The meeting documents were also a way to follow the work from meetings we did not attend. We also had the opportunity to observe go-live activities where we (1) saw actual work rather than just talk about work, and (2) saw how the issues/themes we had been observing played out at go-live. At least one member of the research team was on-site to observe the activities of team members for all go-live dates at the main command center. Since ethics requirements excluded visiting the floors, we used command center discussions of user experiences to track progress.

To supplement our observations, we conducted 16 interviews with project stakeholders selected based on meeting observations, occasionally following up with debate participants about controversial decisions. Other semistructured interviews were conducted as part of a project debrief we led for the organization. Participants were selected based on their project team roles. Each interview began with questions about the participant’s role in the organization and the CTP project, followed by how the participant understood the overall project and the technology and how they would be affected once it launched. We then probed the current state of the project, including how successful they believed it was/would be and what concerns they had. Interviews were transcribed in NVivo. We also observed two hospital-wide events with hundreds of participants: a vendor design review of system functionality in November 2012 and a preimplementation briefing day in September 2013. Throughout the project, we captured informal conversations with participants in field notes. Finally, we had access to project documents, including the charter, pre-reads for the SC and JAC, readiness assessments conducted at various points, and a closeout report. Table 1 above summarizes the data sources.

Data sources emerged over the course of the project, with new sources added to “enrich the emerging concept[s]” (Birks et al., 2013, p. 3). Data collection ended with project conclusion, but coding and follow-ups continued until we felt we had reached theoretical saturation.

## Data Analysis

Data analysis was iterative, beginning with the first observation of the design review. Figure 1 summarizes the coding process, and how it evolved. Throughout the project, we coded data and documented our emerging understanding of the unfolding process. The first phase of coding involved reviewing and recoding field notes and other sources on a range of themes. Coding related primarily to project activities, challenges the team faced in completing the work, and aspects of CTP under discussion. Activities included design decisions like how to configure forms and reports, change management planning in preparation for go-live, regular project status reporting, reviews of current workflow for the actors, and plans for revised workflow under CTP. These activities are shown in the first pane in Figure 1, along with samples and comments on how these are represented in the data. Figure 1 also shows salient challenges identified in the data with examples of the CTP components.

Further analysis of coding revealed patterns where particular activities (design decisions) for specific components of CTP (first coded as “problem areas”) produced challenges to shared understanding and collaboration. Exploring these challenges showed that while some issues were presented, discussed, and resolved with little controversy, others provoked tensions visible through body-language or emotions expressed in the debate. Each one of these situations reflected a misunderstanding or loss of meaning between players or from one meeting to the next. These were breakdowns that required action (Taylor et al., 2001) and led to our concept of ruptures. We identified 12 such ruptures in our data, which we explored in more detail during second-level coding, for every instance where a rupture was discussed in our meeting documents, field notes, project documentation, or interviews. We then compiled chronological descriptions of each rupture (ranging from 4-24 months long) to document its history and characteristics. One author wrote the description and the other checked it against the data. For simplicity of presentation, in this paper, we focus on three exemplar ruptures (Table 2). Additional information on the remaining ruptures is available in the Appendix.

We then returned to the literature to frame the findings. Iterating between the literature and the data led to a focus on two core aspects of the ruptures. Theories of sensemaking (Berente et al., 2011; Gasson, 2005; Glynn & Watkiss, 2020; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Weick, 1995) clarified how ruptures emerge and are resolved, and theories of imbrication provided the basis for identifying what required sensemaking and the underlying reasons for the ruptures. Using these theoretical tools to guide second-level coding, we reconstructed each rupture account into a sensemaking narrative, paying particular attention to the imbricating process that occurred. The nature of imbricating as a process that begins during design is a key contribution of our work. In our Analysis of Ruptures section, we demonstrate that imbricating is not only undertaken during the use of IT but is also undertaken by designers as they create and test new routines and technologies to produce shifts in how human and material agencies will intertwine once enacted. Sensemaking afforded the opportunity to disentangle what was happening and how. As we analyzed these sensemaking narratives and how they played out over time for each of the ruptures, the phases of noticing/bracketing, social interaction, and enactment made the recursive, evolving nature of this sensemaking and imbricating work visible. For example, the 12-page narrative of the Diagnostic Imaging Form (DIF) rupture detailed three rounds of sensemaking that occurred over a four-month time period. The MC rupture (12-page narrative) played out over 18 months and three sensemaking cycles, and the ED rupture (19-page narrative) lasted 24 months and involved four cycles. The last stage involved a review of sensemaking accounts for explanations of how to imbricate the changes from design through use and coding them for evidence of how human and material agencies shifted and intertwined.

The sensemaking narratives and the human and material analysis for each rupture led to potential models of how the transformation was unfolding, resulting in the third-level coding. This level identified key characteristics of sensemaking around imbrications that resulted in ruptures and shaped how they developed. These characteristics are presented in the Results section.

## Results

We present a detailed analysis of three of our 12 ruptures. We selected these three because they vary in scope (how much of the organization’s workflow was impacted) and complexity (how many related elements made up the rupture). The simplest exemplar (DIF), for example, involved the single process of ordering diagnostic images and revolved around a single element, the design of a form. The Message Center and Emergency Department exemplars showed increasing scope and complexity. Collectively, these three ruptures provide insight into the nature of ruptures and demonstrate the process through which they are uncovered and, if possible, resolved. We begin with the simplest rupture and move to the most complex. ## The Exemplar Ruptures

## The Diagnostic Image Form Rupture

The first rupture related to the design of the ordering form for diagnostic images (e.g. X-rays, CT scans, MRI). An imaging order is placed by an “ordering physician” and goes to a diagnostic imaging physician (“radiologists,” for simplicity).

The rupture unfolded over 4 months during the meeting of the JAC, with debates among ordering physicians and radiologists. IT members of the project team such as the track leads attempted to mediate the dispute to allow the form to be finalized.

This electronic form (e-form) was first presented at a joint advisory committee meeting (JAC) in April 2013 as a means of speeded data entry for outpatients in diagnostic imaging. It was a six-question, yes/no form. The form prepopulated from the patient’s history for new visits; there was a red text warning to verify prepopulated answers indicating information carried forward from the patient’s history regarding potential obstacles in performing the diagnostic procedure.

The reaction from the ordering physicians was less positive. They questioned the need to enter the information since this was already being done by nurses or clerks; further, because patients could be harmed by an error in the information, radiology would need to recheck the information at the time of the visit. However, The track lead for the radiologists insisted that the form was necessary because some information could affect the scheduling of imaging appointments—for example, the current creatinine levels for patients with kidney dysfunction. One radiologist said “it will cripple them”<sup>4</sup> if this information is not captured for each appointment.

![](/api/attachments/Y8YH2GGY/fulltext/images/8acddf3fa1278ae465919451a518ef1c41a5ca1aa3886142beb7fd8a62390599.jpg)

There was contention as to who was right. One participant asked whether physicians ordering tests had been consulted in the e-form development—since radiologists rarely place orders, they only receive them. Ordering physicians challenged the need to enter this form every time. Another track lead “made fun of the docs for their complaints about a five-question form,” suggesting that many participants felt the ordering physicians were being unreasonable. There was little resolution in the discussion, but there was a plan to review it and find a way to avoid entering the data each time. The discussion concluded with the meeting chair saying, “Let’s kill this before it kills us.”

The issue returned in the next meeting in May 2013. Ordering physicians continued to resist the proposed form, with their objections becoming increasingly heated, suggesting that they felt radiology was being “unreasonable.” They had met and seemed to have made progress, but radiology insisted that the information was required for the professional standards of their licensing. This was corroborated in an interview with a radiologist who argued that the issue was about ordering physicians trying to avoid accountability.

“[Physicians think they don’t] need to comply to processes, right? What don’t you get? I have a stupid system. It will not let us run if you don’t put in all the data. It’s like your [airline]. The airline won’t sell you a ticket if you don’t actually put your name down, your address and … your [credit card], right? You can’t get on the plane without a boarding pass. You can jump up and down and say I don’t need a boarding pass if you recognize my [credit card] number. I don’t care, boarding pass.”

Radiologists contended that the information requested had not changed; it was part of physician accountability. But in the paper system, ordering physicians may have signed blank requisitions to be completed by a nurse, clerk, or medical secretary. While this common workaround violated College of Physician guidelines, it was invisible in the prior system. The Clinical Transformation Project, specifically the component related to CPOE, required compliance.

The discussion/debate continued until the meeting chair intervened: “we are circling the point.” The chair then sought a person each from the radiologists and ordering physicians to decide what would happen at launch. The ordering physicians were encouraged to “try it for a few months and see how it works” and adapt the process further. Our field notes indicate:

“They are trying to sell the physicians on trying it out. The physicians are questioning whether radiologists will ever be willing to change, and the team is saying ‘they will’ even though the recent meetings suggest that they won’t at all! They are trying to work on some technical solutions (can they flow through information from [the charting module of the EMR system]) but they don’t have this now.

A project leader suggested that the issue should go to SC; the meeting chair proposed sending it to the executive sponsor and the Medical Advisory Council. The SC affirmed that the physicians were responsible for completing the form, and DIF was implemented according to the original design.

When the issue returned two months later, in July 2013, a bypass option had been added to the form, specifically to accommodate emergency/trauma situations in the Emergency Department where the information could not be acquired. The change seems to have been made because radiologists realized during their own testing of DIF that it was more burdensome for ordering physicians than they had realized. There was a discussion about auditing the bypass to make sure physicians were not abusing the system to avoid extra work. We considered the rupture to have ended there as emotions subsided, the issue was resolved, and relations were restored.

## The Message Center Rupture

Message Center (MC) is the proprietary email system within the CTP tool. All orders placed in the system are delivered to the destination (e.g., radiology or pharmacy) through MC and test results are returned in MC. The MC rupture unfolded slowly in a layered fashion over a period of 18 months (February 2013 – July 2014). Beginning with a request for guidance on a single routine/technology workflow, it developed into a unique problem as more routine/ technology workflows were added and implementation unfolded. Unlike the DIF rupture, which was resolved, the MC rupture solution was moved to the optimization phase post-field work. As with the DIF rupture, the MC rupture involved physicians and the track leads on the JAC. Vendor representatives on the JAC (vendor leads) were also involved as the team sought to understand the issues and possible solutions. The development team housed within the hospitals’ IT organization and responsible for the actual coding of the system was also involved.

In February 2013, there was a request for guidance on notifying physicians about system-detected outdating of medication orders. The system had two options for stopping medications: a physician-determined option (physician stop) or a system-generated option (system stop). If a physician set a stop date when ordering a medication, there was no further action from the system. If the physician accepted the system stop, the system would generate a reminder when the order had expired through a messaging system, the Message Center.

The system-generated messaging raised concerns at the JAC. One was ambiguous terminology; a system stop was a ‘hard stop’ but if a physician created it, it was a ‘physician stop.’ Another issue was confusion over the message recipient, with a preference for nurses, who were not official users. This distinction also revealed the complexity of shared care situations like the ICU, where patients were treated in a physician pool and it was not possible to ensure a physician who could act on it would be alerted, regardless of who ordered the medication.

According to our meeting field notes a member of the JAC asked: “Do we need this (renewal notification)—does the current system work?” The alternative was to revert to the existing notifications process where expiring medications were communicated by paper, telephone, post-it notes, or chart notes, despite the report of a JAC member that “we’ve had some medication errors based on this—one problem area is where meds stopped too soon.” The JAC revised the system terminology to “renewal notification required” to reflect the workflow need. They also became aware of the need to create a pool system in the Message Center. The issue of physician pools in shared care settings was discussed but not resolved. Our field notes indicate “Pools—concern is too many messages going to wrong people and worry that messages will be irrelevant.” At this meeting, the vendor lead was “asked about other sites” to see how other organizations had handled these issues with MC; there was no further discussion.

In March 2013, the project and vendor leads formally introduced MC to the JAC explaining its full function. The system would send messages to physicians about their medication, imaging, lab orders, results, and other action items. This would run in parallel within the current email system. Two issues emerged at this stage—the configuration of proxy access, a means by which physicians could give others access to their own inbox in shared care situations, and the management of a new routine called “proposed orders.” The proposed orders routine involved nonlicensed health professionals (nurses, medical students) entering proposed orders on a patient chart, and the system would send a request for approval to the physician. Prior concerns over the complexity of MC and the scope of messaging became clearer to physicians. A key concern was: “A lot of terminology, we need to ensure clearly specified and defined so we all know— need to understand downstream impact and ensure (coverage) in training. 2

In September 2013, the rupture resumed when the development team requested input from the JAC on another new routine. If MC were to mistakenly send messages to the wrong physician about the wrong order or the wrong patient, a routine was needed to account for this. Physicians would have the option to reject messages not meant for them, but that required the creation of a new routine and software item— refused orders—with a refused order mailbox to be monitored to redirect messages to the correct individual. JAC discussed that they would:

‘need a process to deal with orders that are rejected by a physician (usually phone/verbal orders)—plan is for doc to correct but rejected orders will also go to a central inbox for triage by a patient care committee. … Lots of debate about whether the doc has to do the correcting, particularly when the rejection is for Wrong Doctor.’

The concern was the extra workload to determine the proper physician. Eventually a project leader called on a member of the vendor advisory team who explained “there is usually someone assigned each shift to monitor the refused items mailbox (this is one mailbox per hospital)—[it] can be an unlicensed person monitoring but a licensed physician has to take action.” Field notes reflect piqued emotions regarding the new work, as the development team tried to diffuse the situation: “don’t freak out—this is not a big deal in the sense it’s not much different from what happens today.”

By March 2014, some locations had gone live with the new system, and issues around MC were being brought back to the JAC. Issues with the ‘volume and duplication of proposed orders’ (e.g., those generated by medical students and requiring physician approval) were creating extensive new work, and the JAC learned the magnitude of incoming messaging at the live locations. Physicians using MC began to report serious concerns and resistance. Field notes indicate two requests from active users, specifically the ability to “limit/modify the results they see—want to modify because they are getting duplicates” and the ability to turn off MC. The JAC deliberated on the two issues, especially turning off MC: ‘(1) creates some risk because they can turn off too much, (2) rule is that it is a physician accountability if they screw up.’ At this point, no changes were made to the MC or how it functioned. By May of 2014, complaints about the functionality of MC peaked, and this issue was brought forward to JAC and SC. Physicians complained that it demanded so much time and attention that their patient care work had devolved to tending to MC messages. Our field notes captured this sentiment; it was as if a new, overarching routine—caretake MC—had emerged from the accumulation of individual decisions on the various routines and technology-based messages discussed previously. While some aspects of the technology could be improved (recreating pools of physicians and creating a dummy email for some groups), the overarching problems demanding physicians to work in new ways could not be altered. Efforts to remake MC were postponed to the optimization phase, and users had to work within the designed constraints.

## The Emergency Department Rupture

The ED rupture was one of the most complex that we observed, lasting 24 months (November 2012 – November 2014) and covering several issues caused by the reconfiguration of practices intended to alter who did what, when, where, and how in the emergency department. While the MC rupture consisted of a series of small issues that gradually accumulated, with an understanding of their interrelationships emerging over time, the ED rupture consisted of three distinct issues related to different aspects of the relationship between emergency services in the ED and CTP that were bound together over time, each one informing the next and all reflecting on the macroroutine of supplying emergency services.

Even though the ED would implement the same technologies and routines as other parts of the hospital, the JAC and SC treated it as distinctive because of the unique demands of ED work. The ED is considered the ‘front door of the hospital’ since many hospital admissions start there. Physicians in the ED bear the burden of taking initial histories and physical exams, and populating information into paper-based patient charts.<sup>5</sup> In this hospital region, ED wait times were a key performance metric and influenced hospital funding. This meant that adding work to the ED that might slow it down had significant overall implications for the hospitals.

In January 2013, the JAC became aware of vague ED concerns from users and within the JAC that had been circulating since the design review in November 2012. These were later described at an SC meeting as the ED physicians’ “need to understand how CPOE will function in ED without affecting wait times.” To mitigate this, the JAC and SC assigned a subgroup to research ED workflows and CTP in other hospital EDs, including a visit to one of the vendor’s other hospital clients to “see how a mature ED organization does this.” The goal was to educate physicians by providing information and concrete examples of how ED services would be efficiently conducted. While the site visit was of limited use because of the different resources deployed there, the subgroup was able to observe how the complexity of new routines and technologies of CTP might work and how physicians had adapted to the new process there: “[there is] no negativity now. [In the] beginning there was grumbling but [after] three months [it was] ok.” In March 2013, lingering concerns expressed within the teams and by ED physicians began to be seen through the lens of “physician resistance” with a plan to “meet with affected groups to create solutions.” For example, in response to reported physician anxiety over ED order sets (preestablished workflow based on presenting symptoms), the team discussed the “need to lower skepticism and anxiety by showing progress,” implying the concerns reflected irrational fear over new processes. A senior hospital executive spoke later about the importance of including the ED in the implementation, characterizing it as a “tough decision” requiring “important work.”

In April 2013, when the decision was made to proceed with CTP in the ED, the selected pilot hospital withdrew, sending a shockwave through both the SC and the JAC. A senior executive described an emerging “epidemic” of hospitals expressing concern that threatened the entire program. The pilot hospital felt they were already assuming too much risk by being the first to implement, and taking on the ED implementation was more than they could absorb. For the JAC and SC, the major problem became that the pilot site would now implement only a subset of the functionality in one part of the context, as they also planned to implement the system for inpatients but not ambulatory clinics. As a result, a new pilot site was selected.

The next aspect of the ED rupture occurred during unit testing in August 2013. Prior to CTP, the EDs of the largest hospitals had been using the EmergSys (ESys) module of the EMR system as a stand-alone package. That meant many ED physicians had experience using the tool but not as it was integrated with the CTP modules. As a stand-alone package, ESys had been customized in a number of ways specific to the work. Not surprisingly, this past customization resulted in challenges as the project unfolded. As one of the project leaders said in a post-implementation interview: “We can’t even do the normal upgrades and different things to it so that we can benefit from ESys because we’ve MacGyvered it<sup>6</sup> so bad.”

The customizations meant the vendor’s standard coding was not used, compromising the ability to create the new functionality, like legally necessary reports on patients who left without being seen or who left with tests completed but not reported. The vendor developers would say something could be done, but internal IT people would say it couldn’t because “they weren’t using that field that way.” The unexpected complexity and customizations in ESys threatened the deadlines for integration testing and the golive date for the first site with knock-on effects for the entire project. Project status went from yellow to red, signifying a major threat and alerting the SC that immediate action was required. After deliberation, the teams enacted an emergency ‘SWAT team’ response, which one physician referred to as being sent to “ESys Jail,” effectively locking experts in a room to fix the technical problems so that ESys could be developed in time, with compressed time for testing but the ability to implement on schedule.

The appearance of this problem shocked SC members. It was discussed openly in the group, but to the rest of the organization, the reaction was concern, not panic. The SC expressed appreciation to the project team and JAC for clarity of communication on the issue and focused on a “communication plan for [staff] to maintain confidence in project.” The SC decided to:

‘Wait to tell physicians until end of August (since it’s only then that we’ll know the go-live date is compromised)—there is some chatter in the organization that CTP is going to fail, and they didn’t want to add to it.’

The issues were resolved in time<sup>7</sup> and the final aspect of the ED rupture unfolded after launch (July-November 2014), when despite earlier efforts to reduce the concerns and fix the integration of CTP with ESys, it proved disruptive. The volume of patients and the pace of ED work resulted in errors in commonplace routines like medication orders and diagnostic testing entered on the wrong patient record because physicians were sharing devices. ED work was slowed because physicians had to log in to the system 70-100 times per shift due to automatic logouts after 2 minutes and were forced to deal with technology-related errors caused either by the technology or the physician. While some of this had been foreseen, the actual experience, including the new DIF and MC, created exceptional frustration for users, resulting in active efforts by ED physicians to organize and force a shutdown of the new technologies. The CIO met with the ED physicians, who reported:

“25% reduction in our productivity…given volume of patients it is a safety issue ... Not uncommon to place orders on the wrong patient. Finding it hard to maintain a sense of what is going on with a patient. Before you could see everything easily on one page. You could see the clinical history as well as the orders. But now the orders are gone.”

While the steering committee was skeptical, to assuage concerns and avoid a revolt, the JAC and SC created resources like at-the-elbow support and data entry support, and investigated opportunities to rework CTP workflows for the ED. The rupture subsided following efforts to support ED physicians and take them seriously rather than assume resistance to change.

## Analysis of Ruptures

Analysis of the ruptures observed during the investigation led to formal conceptualization, which we now present. We begin with our definition of ruptures. Next, we elaborate on the nature of ruptures by exploring imbrications where sensemaking was required and the ensuing sensemaking process. We conclude by exploring the fractal nature of imbrication revealed in the analysis.

## Definition of Ruptures

Based on our analysis, we conceptualize ruptures as situations that occur due to breakdowns in the process of ITenabled change. Taylor et al. (2001) describe situations as composed of a focus of attention arising from a breakdown of some type, along with resources to draw upon to understand and resolve the breakdown, and a set of constraints appropriate to the circumstances that created the situation. As a specific type of situation, ruptures are triggered by a breakdown in the expected process of design and/or implementation and they unfold through a process of sensemaking (Mesgari & Okoli, 2019). The most important resource to draw on is the knowledge of the actors and the results of rounds of sensemaking and imbricating that feed into later rounds. The constraints include the original goals of the IT-enabled change, the limits of the technological solution, and the limits imposed by the organizational structure and systems.

We chose the term ruptures purposefully, reflecting the multiple meanings of the word, each of which helps us elaborate an aspect of the concept. Synonyms for the word rupture fall into three broad groups. One group relates to disagreement, where ruptures are a breach of a harmonious relationship. Each of the three ruptures showed elements of disagreement, as participants wrestled with their lack of shared understanding about issues like who should bear the new workload of CTP use or even what new workload would be created. The second group relates to the notion of sudden change or bursting—some surprising knowledge or event. Surprise was a feature of the DIF when the radiologists experienced the work of ordering physicians and were surprised by how their requirement bogged down work, leading to a bypass feature. In MC, it occurred at go-live when the volume of work to caretake MC surprised users and led to complaints. In ED, it occurred when it became clear that ED physicians were threatening to abandon the system. It is important to note that in all these cases, actual experience with the system was where the surprise occurred, whether in training, as in DIF, or post-implementation as in the other two ruptures. The third group can be thought of in a physical sense as cracks or openings in a surface—in this context, making underlying issues visible to participants. In each of the ruptures, the disagreements, surprises, and openings that were experienced created a need to start or continue sensemaking and take action. Ruptures provided us with the segment of action that demanded further exploration of the issues and challenges that ultimately led to either resolutions of the rupture (DIF, ED) or at least a plan for its future resolution (MC).

The breakdowns observed in the study were all due to a lack of shared understanding about the routines and technologies to be implemented as part of the CTP. We identified three drivers underlying the lack of shared understanding: (1) differences between people’s points of view (ambiguity), (2) inability of actors individually and collectively to form a clear vision due to the newness of technology and the required changes (novelty), and (3) historical routines and technologies entailing deeply embedded imbrications of human and material agency that are not visible or understood as new ones are put in place (complexity).

The DIF rupture primarily reflects ambiguity: ordering physicians and radiologists had competing views of the importance of the form for patient care. The radiologists viewed the information on the form as essential to providing safe diagnostic images while the ordering physicians saw the additional work of populating the form as a barrier to patient care because of the time it took. These competing perspectives made it difficult for them to reach an agreement on the design of the form. Initially, the organization sided with the radiologists’ view, but once the latter had experienced the work of the ordering physician, a compromise was developed that allowed bypassing the form when needed.

The MC rupture reflects novelty and complexity. In this rupture, the issue was not competing understandings between groups but a lack of initial understanding from anyone regarding the new routine for caretaking of MC that would be needed once CTP was launched. Individual design decisions were being made and implications for existing routines were considered, but the possibility of a higherlevel routine emerging did not become apparent until the system was implemented.

The ED rupture reveals both ambiguity and complexity. Hospital leadership did not grasp the implications of the overall design for physicians in the busy ED, like the automatic logout times and data entry expectations at admission, so they could not appreciate the burden placed on them. Rather, they attributed complaints of ED physicians as resistance to change that needed to be overcome (ambiguity). Also, the vendor team did not understand the customizations made to the ESys module, which meant that the solutions they proposed for some design decisions were unworkable. At the same time, the hospital IT team did not recognize the need to revisit those customizations to facilitate effective integration of the CPOE components (complexity).

The ruptures stood out among thousands of activities involved in the design, configuration, testing, and implementation of CTP over two and a half years. The teams dealt with many things that were not ruptures and we coded many discussions of mundane problems and actions agreed to following discussions. We present two brief examples.

The initial coding identified a challenge to the team regarding total parenteral nutrition (TPN), or intravenous delivery of nutrients. Forms had to be designed to order TPN for adult, pediatric, and neonatal patients. While the CTP method for ordering was new, the process was not novel or complex. Between February 2013 and 2014, questions arose about the terminology, the calculations, and the ways the form would be used. While this issue took time to resolve, with some frustration over the pace, it never generated debate about its appropriateness to the project goals or ambiguity about whose responsibility it was. Rather, at each meeting where it was discussed, the team presented their progress on the form. JAC members raised questions and change requests were sent back for further development. The adult form was developed first as a model, followed by the pediatric and neonatal forms; all the forms were tested to ensure they worked. No aspects of ruptures were observed nor was any disruption of harmony other than mild frustration. There was no bursting or opening up to reveal hidden assumptions about the underlying issue. It progressed by a series of decisions to achieve the desired result.

The second example relates to login times, a common dimension of system performance. The technical advisory committee tasked with improving performance on login presented their preliminary analysis at the SC meeting in February 2013. The current time to turn on a device, log in to the network, and connect to the EMR was 120-210 seconds; the team set a target of 35-62 seconds. At each subsequent meeting, the TAC lead presented a dashboard showing progress. The issue was challenging, and it was not clear that the login time goal would be met by the first golive. Yet it did not become a rupture because the reports never provoked disagreements or emotions; they did not require sustained effort to understand and act on, and for participants, this was not an ambiguous or complex routine or technology, it was simply a difficult problem that had to be solved during the design and development process.

## The What of Ruptures: Imbrications

Each rupture involved activities that imagined and enacted the new technology, new routines, or both. Thus, the ruptures contained acts of imbrication during design, development, and implementation. Each rupture captured the activities of change and learning about a thing that our context defined. It could be a new technology feature or routine like DIF or some routines in MC, or more complex routine/technology combinations of embedded imbrications of smaller routines. These features can be understood in terms of the imbrication of human and material agency that underlies the application of any organizational IT.

For example, in the DIF rupture (Figure 3), the initial material agency of the form (M1) was paper and allowed human agency where anyone could fill out the form, as long as the physician’s signature was on it. Not all information had to be filled out, since it is easy to skip questions on paper (H1); thus, the past imbrication of the routine is in the first circle. The new material agency required physicians to fill in five questions before proceeding with the order (M2) and the imbrication of technology was created by H1:M2. Physicians had to change their human agency to conform, resulting in a new routine created by M2:H2. They resisted, but this imbrication remained during design. At testing, the radiologists altered the technology to create a bypass option (M3) which allowed users to adapt their routine and exert more human agency on how and when to complete required information before ordering a diagnostic test (M3:H3).

But while prior research (Leonardi, 2011) has examined the ways that human and material agency become imbricated in use, our study (like that of Holeman & Barrett, 2017) shows how the design process is based on attempts to understand and shape the way those imbrications will occur in the future. The configuration of technologies and routines designed by the CTP team set up the material and human agencies likely to be enacted. While design does not guarantee the shape imbrications will take, it reflects the intent of those involved. For example, in the MC rupture, each participant tried to envision how the technologies and routines would come together in use; that mental model influenced their interpretations and actions. Thus, building on research that shows how imbrications emerge during use, we demonstrate that they exist cognitively during design and influence the design proces

This focus therefore demanded an elaborated conceptualization of imbrications. Leonardi (2011) recognized that past imbrications inform how users will imbricate current human and material agencies to achieve their goals. For actors who are designers and not users, and are engaged in the generative act of designing and implementing new routines and technologies for the whole organization, we theorize that there are three states of imbrication: those that exist in the past (historical), those that are called into action and imagined from goals (envisioned), and those that are acted upon to make them “real” (realized) to be tested and launched to users, though they might remain precarious and need to be reconstructed until users accept them.

Historical imbrications provide the starting structures to be transformed. They exist in the human and material agency of routines and technologies prior to IT-enabled organizational change, the past imbrications used by Leonardi (2011). During design, past imbrications become present-at-hand (Heidegger, 1954/1977), and accessible to designers who must envision imbrications that will translate the organizational goals and vendor promises into broadly defined configurations of routines and technologies. Realized imbrications occur when the envisioned imbrication reaches a level of concreteness (tested or launched technology or routine). They are agreed upon by participants, enacted during development and configuration, tested, and released to users. Eventually, users adopt realized imbrications from the development process, actualizing their affordances, and continuing the change process. From design work, realized imbrications may still be contested and may change as users experience affordances or constraints. Realized imbrications are open to alteration and may be redesigned. Eventually, realized imbrications meet user needs and become the new imbrications (Leonardi, 2011) that stabilize, recede from notice, and become ready-to-hand as the taken-for-granted historical imbrications of the underlying human and material agencies of routines and technologies (Taylor et al., 2001). These imbrication types are summarized in Table 3.

## The How of Ruptures: Sensemaking to Achieve Shared Understanding

During the imbrication process, knowledge, specifically the distributed, incomplete, and developing knowledge of the various actors in CTP, was the key resource needed to understand and, if possible, resolve ruptures. We saw instances of confusion over terminology and language about, for example, whether CTP was the name of the organizational program or the EMR software (from the first meeting of the steering committee we attended) and the difference between a prescription and an order (from JAC February 2013). Lacking common language made it difficult for participants to discuss complex issues because they were often referring to different things. We also saw problems in understanding based on a lack of shared knowledge among different groups of participants.

For example, operational practices differed between the regional hospitals and the large teaching hospitals. Similarly, the vendor and the project team members had different levels of knowledge about the capabilities of the EMR software and the clinical practices being supported:

Our feeling was that many of the [vendor project team members] were way too green, not experienced enough, didn’t know the system enough, didn’t—half of them weren’t clinicians. So, they didn’t actually understand the clinical workflow. (Interview with project team member)

Finally, even with similar language and knowledge, there were cases where participants constructed different meanings about what had transpired. For example, physicians placing orders and those receiving orders (discussed in the DIF rupture) had similar training and knowledge but different ideas about quality patient care, based on differences in their work.

Because ruptures emerge from a lack of shared understanding, it is only by drawing on distributed knowledge that breakdowns can be addressed. For example, in the case of DIF, it was necessary for radiologists to experience the ordering process to gain the knowledge of ordering physicians and understand their concerns; once that happened, a solution could be designed. In the case of MC, rooted in problems of novelty and unfolding complexity, it was through the accumulation of knowledge in the design process and the knowledge gained from direct experience at go-live allowed the team to understand the nature of the MC rupture. At that point, solutions were not available and action had to be deferred.

The process whereby this knowledge can be developed and shared is best framed as sensemaking. Each rupture had cycles of noticing/bracketing, attempts to develop intersubjective meaning through collective interpretation, and enactments based on those. Each rupture began with the formation of envisioned imbrications, based on the transformation goals and historical imbrications. In each case, the envisioned imbrications resulted in some challenge that launched a sensemaking process. The DIF rupture occurred because of differing imbrications envisioned by the physicians and radiologists on how to accomplish the organizational goal of safety and efficiency. The ordering routine for imaging involved a historical imbrication with paper technology, a material agency that permitted partial completion and/or completion by employees other than the ordering physician. Before CTP, the human agency of the routine prevailed, in that ordering physicians could choose what information to complete on the paper form; the order required the physician’s signature only. In the radiologists’ envisioned imbrication, the initial design presented to JAC, it was the material agency of the e-form that dominated and demanded full completion of the form by the physician.

In the case of MC, the historical modes of communication to physicians about orders, results, and medications were a mix of formal and informal routines and diverse technologies like paper notes on charts, verbal orders, post-it notes, and telephone messages. These routines and technologies were so ubiquitous that they were not understood as a single process. Yet the new MC routine and technology would consolidate and automate this work and add new routines within the technology. We noted that while the concept of MC as a coordination and quality mechanism was accepted as a transformation goal, and the envisioned communication routine was broadly understood in the abstract as the envisioned imbrication in Figure 5, the demands of the collective routines and technologies comprising the ruptures were perceived more gradually.

In the ED (Figure 6), a key transformation goal was care efficiency. This was necessary because of the importance of managing wait times and because the ED is the front door of the hospital; seamless integration would aid the organizations in several ways. The historical imbrication of the ED was a process largely governed by the human agency of the emergency physicians, embedded in their familiar routines. Technology’s underlying human/material imbrications were in service to this human agency and directed at ensuring speed, effectiveness, and low wait times. The imbrication envisioned for this goal was not universally shared; ED physicians were concerned that the change needed to achieve the goals would result in increased workload and decreased efficiency in the ED. This is part of what led to the first sensemaking cycle (Figure 6, Sensemaking Round 1). The expression ED concerns encapsulated the unease among those who would be affected and permeated thinking about new ED imbrications.

Each rupture included multiple rounds of sensemaking, as participants sought to gain a shared understanding of the underlying issues and an envisioned imbrication—a proposed set of routines and technologies—to reach transformation goals while working within the conflicting constraints of stakeholders. During sensemaking cycles, different imbrications were realized as the technology was configured and provided more concrete information about the routines needed to conform to it. Over time, as the design became more complete, ambiguity was resolved and understanding became shared. This is where the rupture showed itself as a crack in the surface and understanding emerged by exploring beneath it. New issues sometimes arose, like the problems that occurred from this emerging understanding in the ED in Sensemaking Cycles 2 and 3, and these had to be resolved. At other times (e.g., MC) the emergent understanding concerned a different issue than the one that provoked the rupture. In this case, the initial rupture emerged from a set of seemingly unrelated routines that coalesced into the understanding of caretaking MC as a novel routine brought about by the material agency of the MC technology.

Each cycle of sensemaking (Figure 7) began when a departure from expectations was noticed. This launched activities aimed at collective interpretation and resulted in an enactment. The enactments in each rupture involved an evolution of the envisioned imbrication and/or the development of a realized imbrication based on the collective interpretation of the participants.

To illustrate the sensemaking cycles, we provide a detailed explanation of the sensemaking process in the DIF rupture. In the case of DIF, the physician reaction to the proposed form (“why do we even have to do that”) initiated the first round of sensemaking because it contradicted the envisioned imbrication of the radiologists and the initial design of the form. The process of achieving a collective interpretation unfolded in debate where it emerged that current practice deviated from formal professional obligations because of the limited material agency of the paper form. During this discussion, those siding with the radiologists minimized the concerns of the ordering physicians (“they are complaining about a five-question form”). Both groups invoked the goal of quality of care, yet they did so from distinct perspectives. The enactment of the collective interpretation was a decision—but not an agreement—to move forward in development and revisit the issue later. The project team expressed frustration with the lack of agreement but pushed to move on and accept the design.

The second round of sensemaking began with physicians noticing that the form remained problematic to them. This triggered their explanations of why the proposed design was unworkable for ordering physician routines and why the radiologists’ routines did not actually require the completion of all aspects of the form since each question was asked again when the patient arrived. The final round of sensemaking began when participants noticed the form design change with the bypass option, a surprise for the ordering physicians. The resulting explanation showed the emergence of a new understanding of the limits of universal enforcement of form completion. At the same time, recognition of the form’s auditability provided a rationale to work towards the enforcement of form completion. This entailed a routine for reviewing the form and moving enforcement back to human rather than material agency. By the end of our time in the field, it was unclear whether this enforcement would happen. The MC and ED ruptures evolved similarly. We provide more detail on these ruptures as we explain the next key findings.

## Embedded Imbrications as Fractals

The DIF rupture represented a straightforward situation of resolving disagreements between key actors. Figure 4 accords with the linear intertwining of human and material agency of Leonardi’s (2011) early representation of imbrication changes. However, while the MC and ED ruptures also involved this type of disagreement, the imbrication change could not be shown as linear and single layered. Instead, what was revealed throughout these ruptures was empirical evidence for the fractal nature of imbrications—something that has only been theorized about by other scholars (Taylor et al. 2001; Ciborra, 2006).

Mandelbrot (1989, p. 4) described a fractal as an object where “each part is a linear geometric reduction of the whole, with the same reduction ratios in all directions.” The structure at each level replicates the whole structure. Taylor et al. (2001) concurred, describing how agency relationships become interleaved to form imbrications with the fractal characteristics, a repeating “structure at finer and finer degrees of scale” (Taylor et al., 2001, p. 87). Our analysis demonstrates this by showing how some imbrications can be broken down into more deeply embedded imbrications, demonstrating the implications of embeddedness for resolving ruptures.

The MC rupture provides a first explanation of the embedded nature of imbrications. As shown in Figure 5, the first cycle of sensemaking for the MC rupture concerned a specific routine (expired orders). At this stage, there was no realization of MC as a thing in itself. The second cycle of sensemaking focusing on three additional unrelated routines surfaced the idea of MC as a thing in itself. While each routine was common in the ebb and flow of work pre-CTP as informal, paper-based, or verbal, their instantiation in the technology with its reconfiguration and formalization of routines required the teams to detail how each routine would work and how the technology would send messages, depicted as realized imbrication . By this time, it became apparent to the JAC that there was an emerging accumulation of new routines in MC that led to concerns over the introduction of a second email system. As new information was gathered to support collective interpretation and enable implementation, the group began to appreciate the scope of the human and material agencies of the new routines demanded of physicians in addition to their normal practices of reviewing results and checking orders.

Through this, the fractal nature of imbrications within the MC rupture became apparent as lower-level routines and technology features became the more complex, broader ones at a higher level. Once the full CTP was launched and MC use was live, it became clear that the MC technology had created a new unanticipated routine—caretaking MC—that did not exist before CTP, causing frustration. MC as a high-level imbrication of underlying individual routine/technology imbrications became manifest through the accretion of individual routines and technology features that had not existed in this combination. Imbricating individual routines/technologies—renewing medications, refused orders, pools, orders to approve—was sensible individually. Yet the totality of MC work was not easily detected in these individual design decisions. Each decision imbricated a pattern of human and material agency layered on top of others, becoming the overarching message center imbrication of human and material agency that demanded user time and attention and affected the ability to perform patient work.

The ED rupture also reflects a high-level imbrication of lower-order imbricated routines and technologies that had existed in the pre-CTP ED. The historical imbrication of human and material agencies underpinning these high-level emergency routines and technologies was dominated by the human agency of ED physicians to determine how and when to provide emergency care (Figure 6). Underlying technological human/material imbrications were in service to this human agency and directed at ensuring speed, effectiveness, and low wait times. Within the ED, prior imbrications of the DIF as a paper-based technology with weak material agency were present, as was the MC historical imbrication of mixed formal and informal communications with strong human agency to organize these routines and the weak material agency of mixed technologies and tools. Many more routines and technologies were also present. Low-level imbrications of agencies creating routine and technology structures like DIF and MC were encapsulated by higher-level imbrications of coordinating emergency care, triaging patients, admitting or discharging patients, and limiting wait times.

Figure 8 depicts the ED imbrication as a high-level imbrication of human and material agencies to form the routines and technologies needed to operate an emergency department.

At the level of the ED, we can view these as interacting to produce emergency care. This high-level imbrication can also be decomposed into meaningful parts, any one of which might interrupt the production of emergency care. Here we highlight two lower-level imbrications that formed part of emergency care, ordering—since labs and other tests must be ordered to care for a patient in the ED and Message Center, where the results of orders are returned to the physician. The decomposition of MC into lower-level routines and technologies that surfaced while examining that rupture are illustrated, as is the decomposition of ordering that includes the diagnostic imaging order form present in the DIF rupture. Figure 8 elucidates how lower-order imbrications reflect similar yet smaller instances of higher-order imbrications. It shows that DIF is the simplest rupture, because the imbrications involved in this rupture exist at the lowest level (the smallest rectangle). The MC rupture is shown to exist at a moderate level of complexity (the middle-sized rectangle), involving embedded imbrications that combined to produce the new routine of caretaking MC. ED is even more complex than MC, since it includes both the smallestlevel imbrication, the DIF, a higher-order imbrication like MC, and other higher- and lower-order imbrications comprising the provision of emergency services.

## Ruptures: A Synthesis of Findings

To synthesize our results, we compare an idealized view of design to the fractal model of imbrications during observed ruptures (Figure 9). Figure 9a shows the idealized view of design, where the combined change instantiates new imbrications of human and material agency intended to be experienced as affordances. It shows that the historical imbrications of human and material agency combine with transformation goals to produce envisioned imbrications (Path 1). Technologies and routines are implemented through design, and imbrications are realized in some fashion (Path 2). As the organization reaches go-live, these realized imbrications emerge as affordances and constraints for users (Path 3). Some aspects of the design process proceeded in this fashion, as described in examples of work on TPN and login time.

Focusing on the microdynamics of design over the project’s life revealed that ruptures are a core aspect of design, as depicted in Figure 9b. In contrast to Figure 9a, this shows the need for sensemaking through the recursive loops that drive designers to revisit aspects of the design (represented as Paths

The model further explains how ruptures arise from breakdowns in understanding that are due to ambiguity, complexity, and novelty. Ambiguity and the perspectives arising from it are central to sensemaking. It is through sensemaking that a collective interpretation can occur, allowing design to move forward. Ambiguity exists for many reasons, including differing perspectives between user groups as documented in prior research on IT project conflicts (Jenkin et al., 2019; McLeod & Doolin, 2012). But as we observed in our ruptures, it also depends on novelty and complexity. Novelty is inherent in new technologies; it leads to participant difficulty imagining what is being developed and so increases ambiguity. Complexity is inherent in embedded imbrications; it drives ambiguity when different understandings arise as participants contemplate imbrications of agencies that create new routines and technologies at higher and/or lower levels of granularity.

## Discussion

The primary purpose of the research was to gain insight and explain the persistence of issues and challenges in realizing value from IT-enabled change efforts. We specifically sought to investigate microdynamics of change in an environment where a multidisciplinary design group created a context for the emergence of new sociotechnical practices, following it through implementation and use to detect the origin and outcomes of challenges and issues. Ruptures encapsulated the sensemaking and imbricating cycles that unfolded as designers addressed problematic situations. Not all the work involved ruptures, yet ruptures occupied the attention of designers and users alike as they signaled something awry in the IT-enabled change—a problematic routine or technology or a bundle of both that required reworking. Below we discuss three principal contributions made.

The first contribution is to articulate the phenomenon of ruptures, and their usefulness in anticipating challenges during IT-enabled change from design to implementation. This is meaningful for several reasons. Ruptures are signals of something specific to attend to. This is important because a central question in process-based analysis of IT-enabled change is which events or activities in the flow of development are important to consider in order to understand the difficulties and challenges that emerge. Prior research has recognized that moment-to-moment analysis of situated action is infeasible (McLeod & Doolin, 2012) and that what to attend to could either be perceived by the designer as (1) emerging from design activities (McLeod & Doolin, 2012, p. 181) or (2) detected by emerging gaps in sociotechnical elements as revealed by a critical incident (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). Ruptures are early signals of problems. By attending to their origin and resolution, designers can respond promptly to problems as they arise.

Understanding ruptures also offers insight into the early emergence of issues that trigger resistance during use, complementing Lapointe and Rivard’s (2005) resistance model. In the DIF rupture, power dynamics between physicians and radiologists surfaced during design and testing but were resolved when the team recognized the functional importance of accommodating physicians’ requests. The MC rupture demonstrated how gradual accumulation of complexities can lead to significant concerns, although early attention allowed designers to work to appease users. In contrast, the ED rupture persisted through design and implementation, requiring postimplementation resolution when the ED physicians began actively resisting CTP. Nonetheless, early detection during design and implementation allowed designers to detect escalating ED concerns and prevent CTP shutdown by adding clerical support.

Our conceptualization of ruptures is similar to concepts put forth in other change literature, including jolts, social movement ruptures, and sensemaking triggers, but is distinct (Meyer, 1982). Unlike jolts, which involve adaptive responses to surprising environmental events for organizational survival and growth (Meyer et al., 1990; Munir, 2016), the ruptures we explored stemmed from internal participants driving strategic change within an organization rather than from external surprises. Ruptures in the social movement literature are defined as a surge in activities within the movement that leads to changes and periods of great transformation (della Porta, 2020; Leong et al., 2018). Yet the notion of rupture we observed was distinct in that the ruptures did not stem from a sudden change brought about by collective action like a protest (della Porta, 2020). Finally, ruptures in this context are related yet distinct from phenomena like triggers or critical incidents. A trigger is an event or technology feature (Griffiths, 1999) that activates sensemaking for an individual or group (Mesgari & Okoli, 2019). Our concept of ruptures includes a trigger and a sensemaking effort but incorporates the recursive practices of sensemaking cycles and imbricating followed through to resolution. Ruptures are also distinct from critical incidents that generate a gap in the elements of a sociotechnical system during IT-enabled change (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). As an event, a critical incident demands action but does not include actions taken or the form of that action. Ruptures encapsulate the series of triggers, sensemaking, enactments, and events that comprise an unexpected or contentious situation that designers and users can act on to influence the success of the project.

Our second contribution is to expand the theory of imbrication by articulating the role of designers and how their imbrications contribute to organizational change and by expanding the vocabulary of imbrication to reflect design work. When IT-enabled change theorists talk about the role of users in enacting change (Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Taylor et al., 2001; Volkoff et al., 2007) they typically focus on the response of users and their interpretation of how to act with new technologies and routines. But users are not the only actors who can enact change. Designers influence IT-enabled change in concert with users. By focusing on the actions of designers with users, our research extends the insights of

Holeman and Barrett (2017), going beyond the idea that designers design for the emergence of new sociotechnical practices to explore how they do it. Our findings demonstrate the fluctuating nature of the imbricated human and material agency that designers orient to at different stages of the process before new technologies and routines have taken hold. This variation demands a new vocabulary for the status of the imbrication at different points in time. Designers influence the trajectory of change through the effectiveness with which they design for emergence; in our research, this was undertaken through collaborative practices. The designers’ role is alluded to in the imbrication study of Saadatmand et al. (2017), where they are part of the aggregate of human agency that brings about a platform within an industry. Holeman and Barrett’s research (2017) focuses on the actions and interactions of designers in a successful ICT4D intervention within a local context where their artifacts and new routines are intended to create value.

Our research illuminates imbrication by elaborating on the states of imbrications in play during design and implementation. Early work on the metaphor of imbrication focused on user choices. As described by Leonardi, users encounter technology in practice, and based on their perceptions make choices that become the sources of change. We found that a focus on designers illuminated the shifting nature of imbrication as it is undertaken. Our research expands the metaphor of imbrication to account for the sensemaking, foresight, and anticipation used to design for imbrications intended to emerge as affordances to users and realize change goals. Historical, envisioned, and realized imbrications reside in the cognition of designers as they further emergence of new practices and technologies. This insight extends imbrication theory.

Our in-depth field data and longitudinal research design allowed us to observe and document the microdynamics of change and fill in the black box present in other high-level theories of change and complements them. In the PSIC model of change, even with elaborate theorizing of levels of analysis about sociotechnical elements over a sequence of events, the authors acknowledge a black box of explanatory mechanisms for gaps that emerge between system elements to be aligned (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). The theory does not explain how the gap emerges, for whom, and how to bring the elements into alignment. The sensemaking and imbricating that occurs during ruptures illuminate microactivities in human and technical agencies enacted during design and development. Holeman and Barrett (2017) suggest two mechanisms that alert designers and demand action: material backtalk and practice breakdowns. These arise from idiosyncrasies in local contexts and must be imbricated into technologies and routines. Our research goes further by explicating how imbrication happens during design and continues post-implementation. Our account of microdynamics of imbricating and sensemaking allowed us to elaborate on imbrication work during design and early implementation, showing activities that intertwine design and use and depicting how change unfolds.

Our third contribution is to illuminate the fractal nature of imbrications in greater detail. In contrast to the linear, singlelayered concept of imbrication provided in Leonardi’s (2011) seminal exposition on routine and technology change, our research demonstrates that imbrications are complex, layered, and fractal. Leonardi characterizes imbrication as temporal and nonrecursive, using the imagery of cross-tiling a roof with imbrex and tegula in an ongoing chain. But this imagery is static, in contrast to the notion of imbrication others have characterized as fractal, complex, and multilevel, like a branching tree (Taylor et al., 2001) that replicates the structure at every stage. While the tiling metaphor helps portray interlocking human and material agency, it does not represent its fractal nature because it doesn’t show patterns that repeat at higher levels of granularity. We believe a fractal model captures the temporal interweaving of human and material agency and the multilevel recursive processes that occur during ruptures. In this model, IT change thus unfolds fractally from the work of designing for the emergence of sociotechnical practices. This perspective aligns with Ciborra’s (2006) representation of risk with digital technologies in banking and insurance, where he describes imbrication as a “reciprocal, self-reinforcing, often nonlinear” (p. 1340) layering of representations upon each other as “a dynamic unfolding process” (p. 1344) for interlocking human and material agency.

Fractal imbrication is also consonant with the notion of vertical structures of affordances presented in Volkoff and Strong (2013), where the authors suggest that lower-order affordances must be actualized so that higher-order structures and groups can intertwine and produce higher-order affordances. We concur with the descriptions of these scholars that imbrications exist in the realm of the empirical. In the case we examine, they provide ways of theorizing about real events, since the situations of rupture reveal sensemaking/imbricating about events that can disrupt project success. Future research might investigate ruptures as generative mechanisms of change since affordance actualization has already been theorized as a generative mechanism.

## Implications for Practice

Our findings suggest two key implications for practitioners involved in IT-enabled change. First, the study points to the urgent need to add mechanisms of learning and adaptation to traditional models of project management for large-scale systems. Sensemaking among designers is a key feature of their work and the learning that they experience. We believe that improved documentation of learning outcomes, with opportunities to recast project documents to account for realtime changes, can improve outcomes. This would fit well with agile development practices but applies beyond technology to new routines, work practices, and projects as a whole. Second, as revealed in our sample ruptures, it is at the point of use, either during testing or after implementation, that ruptures occur and their importance becomes known. Therefore, organizational change processes can benefit from test periods of concrete experiences with routines and technologies interwoven early in the design and development process. These might include sandboxing new routines and technology iterations so changes can be made when they are still possible and affordable. Rather than focus on each feature, transformation practice should engage with larger-scale interwoven applications that constitute the work of roles and departments and reflect the ebb and flow of work life. Creating mechanisms to do this productively and acting early to materialize abstract ideas generated by prospective sensemaking would reveal better configurations for eventual usage.

## Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations of our work. First, as a single case study, we sought to develop theory that would contribute to future research and practice. However, the single setting, though intensive and offering many data sources, limits our ability to generalize. Second, we sought to detect sensemaking and imbrication events as places where change would stand out during ruptures. But ruptures are not the only pathway for change. Other work occurred outside the observed activities and contributed to the organizational change goals. Therefore, rather than offering a full account of IT-enabled change here, we elaborate on how it functions during times of ambiguity, complexity, and novelty with a focus on the origin of challenges to the process. Future research could expand on the interaction of decision-making and sensemaking processes during transformation.

## Conclusion

Our theoretical exploration of the phenomenon of ruptures reveals the important role of designers and their work to design for the emergence of new sociotechnical practices. Through careful construction of sensemaking and imbricating of routines and technologies, we build an account that is sensitive to the origin of problems that may later create resistance and/or lead to poor outcomes of ITenabled change efforts. We offer designers, developers, and business leaders new insights into navigating organizational change as it unfolds.
