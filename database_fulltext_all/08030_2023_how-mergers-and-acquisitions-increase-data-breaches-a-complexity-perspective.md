# HOW MERGERS AND ACQUISITIONS INCREASE DATA BREACHES: A COMPLEXITY PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Huigang Liang Department of Management Information Systems, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, U.S.A. {hliang1@memphis.edu}

Santhosh Srinivas

Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {ssrinivas@vt.edu}

Yajiong Xue Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business, East Carolina University, Greenville, NC, U.S.A. {xuey@ecu.edu}

This study examines how the mergers and acquisitions (M&As) of a firm influence its number of data breaches. Drawing from complexity theory and matching theory, we propose that M&As increase data breaches and this effect is contingent on M&A diversity (dissimilarity between the parent and target firms). Based on the 18-year panel data (2004-2021) of 5,072 public firms in the United States, we find that more M&As by a firm lead to more data breaches. Moreover, we reveal that the number of data breaches increases even more when the parent and target firms operate in different business domains. This finding is confirmed when we operationalize M&A diversity as size discrepancy or a strategic type difference between the parent firm and the target firm (vertical vs. horizontal M&A). Furthermore, based on routine activity theory, we explore two additional moderating mechanisms through which M&As influence data breaches: M&A media publicity and target vulnerability. Our analyses demonstrate that M&As with higher media publicity experience more data breaches than those with lower media publicity, but counterintuitively, M&As involving a more vulnerable target firm lead to fewer data breaches than those involving a less vulnerable target firm. This study not only confirms the impact of M&As on data breach recurrence but also unveils various underlying mechanisms of this impact, thus contributing to both research and practice in cybersecurity and information systems.

Keywords: Merger & acquisition, data breach, diversity, complexity theory, matching theory, routine activity theory, media publicity, target vulnerability

The worst enemy of security is complexity. (Schneier, 1999)

## Introduction

As a direct way to achieve inorganic growth for organizations, mergers and acquisitions (M&As) are intended to create synergy; eliminate competition; absorb unique resources;

capture new products, technology, and innovation; and increase stock returns (Coffey et al., 2002). However, in addition to benefits, M&As can also lead to unexpected negative consequences (Cartwright & Schoenberg, 2006), particularly in the cybersecurity domain. In many high-profile data breaches, such as those affecting JPMorgan Chase, T-Mobile, and Under Armor, M&As are suspected to have played a culprit role.<sup>2</sup> Understandably, according to a Deloitte report (2021), around 62% of business executives cite cybersecurity risk as their biggest concern following an M&A deal (Deloitte, 2021). Given the high cost of cyber incidents and the significant value of the M&A market, cybersecurity has become a major challenge for firms involved in M&As. According to IBM, the average cost of a cybersecurity incident for a firm is \$3.86 million.<sup>3</sup> At the same time, the global M&A market has seen over 200,000 deals since 2020, with a total value of over \$5 trillion in 2021 alone.<sup>4</sup> These figures suggest that firms involved in M&A face significant financial, operational, and reputational risks if cybersecurity events occur. Given the magnitude of the potential consequences, it is crucial to explore the cybersecurity implications of M&As.

Therefore, this study seeks to answer the research question: How do M&As affect the cybersecurity risk, in terms of the number of data breaches, for the parent firm? From both academic and practical perspectives, it is imperative to address the data breach risk involved in M&As. Despite the importance of the linkage between M&As and data breaches and the mounting attention to this topic, as indicated by numerous media articles and industry reports, there has been surprisingly little academic research on this subject. The existing M&A literature has mostly focused on financial and strategic performance (Cartwright & Schoenberg, 2006; Yaghoubi et al., 2016). Due to the rapid development of information technology (IT) and its increasingly indispensable role in organizations, IT integration is deemed one of the most critical and challenging aspects of M&As (Alaranta & Mathiassen, 2013; Brown et al., 2008; Johnston & Yetton, 1996). M&As will inevitably affect the technological balance of a parent firm after acquiring a new firm, which could cause negative consequences such as data breaches. However, to date, only one study (Tanriverdi et al., 2019) has explored the relationship between M&As and data breach risk from this perspective.

To bridge this gap, we draw on complexity theory to understand the cybersecurity ramifications of M&As. According to cybersecurity technologist Bruce Schneier, “complexity is the worst enemy of security” (Schneier, 1999). Complexity theory suggests that complexity contributes to uncertainties and errors (Benbya et al., 2020). Its logic is that in a system including many parts, the interaction among the parts becomes nonlinear and unpredictable (Simon, 1962; Kauffman, 1992), creating opportunities for data breaches. Hence, we believe that examining the effects of M&As on data breaches from a complexity perspective can provide valuable insights. When information systems become more complex, they also become more vulnerable to cybersecurity risks, because the number of coding bugs and modules also grows with complexity, and it is more difficult to test, understand, and analyze such complex systems to prevent cybersecurity risks (Schneier, 2000). During an M&A, not only do information systems become more complex, so does the change process. An M&A requires technology integration involving major changes such as procuring new equipment, implementing a new IT architecture, migrating or divesting systems, and decommissioning obsolete equipment (Henningsson et al., 2018).

Organizational complexity can also pose a risk to cybersecurity, as illustrated by a study showing that about 83% of firms identify organizational complexity as their biggest cybersecurity risk factor (Ponemon, 2015). To this point, M&As may also require a variety of changes to organizational structure, business processes, security policies, and user roles, which can significantly alter how the information systems operate (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020; Tanriverdi et al., 2024). These complex technological and organizational changes are tangled together to create an even higher level of complexity, yielding reduced efficiency, broken applications, augmented vulnerabilities, and weakened security (Lohrke et al., 2016). Although a complex integration process requires careful planning, it is lacking in over 60% of M&A deals (Kengelbach et al., 2015). As a result, M&As boost system complexity on multiple fronts, often creating increased cybersecurity vulnerabilities in the merged firm.

Based on complexity theory, we first identify the main effect of M&As on the frequency of data breaches. To achieve a holistic understanding, we apply matching theory and routine activity theory to supplement complexity theory and examine how the effect of M&As is contingent on M&A diversity, media publicity, and target vulnerability. Our findings show that more M&A events by a firm lead to more data breaches, and the number of data breaches is amplified if the parent and target firms are more dissimilar. Furthermore, we reveal that M&As that have attracted higher media publicity suffer from more data breaches, but counterintuitively, M&As involving a more vulnerable target firm result in fewer data breaches. Our in-depth investigation of M&As’ heterogeneous impacts on firms’ data breach risk is the first of its kind, contributing to both research and practice in information systems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first describe the research context, review existing work on M&A and data breaches, and discuss the theoretical foundation. We then present the research model and hypotheses. Following that, we report the methodology and empirical results. Finally, we conclude the paper by discussing the implications for research and practice and presenting limitations and potential future research avenues.

## Background, Theory, and Hypotheses

## Technology Integration Related to M&A

Information technology integration (ITI) is recognized as one of the most challenging tasks in M&As (Harrell & Higgins, 2002; Wijnhoven et al., 2006). Failure of ITI often leads to poor M&A outcomes (McKiernan & Merali, 1995). While a smooth ITI can contribute to M&A success (Lohrke et al., 2016; Sarrazin & West, 2011), it is difficult to achieve and about 75% of participating firms have faced ITI problems during M&As (Harrell & Higgins, 2002).

An M&A requires major changes and updates in both the parent firm and the target firm. It impacts the technological architecture of a firm immensely and breaks the technological status quo, thereby creating a complex operating environment. Complexity arises due to differences in infrastructure, processes, and policies (Alaranta & Mathiassen, 2013). Technological complexity results from the migration and customization of old and new systems, which is both caused by and contributes back to the overall M&A complexity. Prior research shows that standardizing information systems from both firms involved in an M&A is a huge challenge (Chang et al., 2014). Poor ITI can lead to detrimental effects such as business disruption, inefficiency, and overspending (Henningsson & Kettinger, 2016). Moreover, the complexity of M&As can lead to technological glitches and breakdowns. Among all the undesired outcomes associated with M&As, data breaches likely cause the most devastating damage and thus demand serious scrutiny.

## Cybersecurity Impact of M&As

M&A has received ample attention from researchers who have investigated its impact on business outcomes such as employment (Conyon et al., 2002), stock returns (Alexandridis et al., 2010), corporate culture (Stahl & Voigt, 2008), and firm performance (Akben-Selcuk & Altiok-Yilmaz, 2011). Most studies on M&A are conducted from the strategic and economic perspective. Some scholars have realized the importance of ITI in M&A and studied its impacts on M&A outcomes (Brown et al., 2008; Johnston & Yetton, 1996). ITI has been found to play a critical role in M&A success (Benitez-Amado & Ray, 2012; McKiernan & Merali, 1995), and the benefits from successful ITI are welldocumented (Lohrke et al., 2016; Sarrazin & West, 2011). While ITI influences M&A success, its implementation is tedious. Henningsson et al. (2018) argue that ITI is one of the prominent problems in M&A and should be a major research topic in the IS discipline. However, few studies have examined the relationship between ITI and data security in the M&A context.

In today’s digital era, firms consider data breaches to be one of the most serious threats. Prior research has identified many antecedents of data breaches such as human or software incompetence (Cheng et al., 2017), theft (Wikina, 2014), human vulnerabilities (Hong & Linden, 2012), security measures (Romanosky, 2016), corporate governance and social responsibility (Lending et al., 2018), miscellaneous error, and insider misuse (Cheng et al., 2017). However, the influence of strategic moves like M&As on data breaches has received little attention. Our comprehensive literature review found only three papers on this topic. In an opinion paper, Lohrke et al. (2016) warned managers about the increased cybersecurity threat from ITI in M&As but did not provide empirical evidence to support their arguments. In a study on IT control effectiveness, Tanriverdi and Du (2020) found that M&As as a special type of corporate strategic change can disrupt a firm’s IT controls over technology, processes, and people and lead to IT material weaknesses, but they did not directly examine the impact of M&As on data breaches. In another paper, Tanriverdi et al. (2019) showed that M&As can increase firms’ risk of data breaches. This is the only empirical study that has specifically examined the relationship between M&As and data breaches.

Although Tanriverdi et al. (2019) reported useful findings about the impact of M&As on data breaches, much remains unknown. Our study goes beyond their study in three aspects—research focus, theory, and empirical rigor. First, we focus on the heterogeneous treatment effects of M&As on the number of data breaches, whereas Tanriverdi et al. examined the main effect of M&A presence on data breach risk. We explore how the effect of M&As is contingent on three moderators, whereas Tanriverdi et al. did not test moderation effects. Thus, our study has the potential to unveil the underlying mechanisms through which M&As affect data breaches. Second, in terms of theory, Tanriverdi et al. primarily drew on structural complexity, arguing that M&As make the organizational structure more complex and thus increase the risk of data breaches. We also draw on complexity theory but take a broader approach, positing that the general system complexity involving structure, people, process, and technology contributes to cybersecurity vulnerability. In addition, we integrate matching theory and routine activity theory with complexity theory to develop an integrative theoretical framework for the heterogeneous treatment effects. Thus, our study offers a richer and more comprehensive explanation of M&As’ impact on data breaches.

Finally, we conducted thorough endogeneity and robustness tests to support causal inferences, which provide strong empirical evidence for our findings. Taken together, our study extends and complements Tanriverdi et al.’s study with a sharpened research focus, a strong theoretical foundation, and rigorous analysis, thus offering a deeper understanding of the relationship between M&As and data breaches.

## Complexity Theory

In line with Schneier (2000), we view data breaches as a product of M&A complexity and disentangle this phenomenon by applying complexity theory. While there is no single complexity theory, several theories concerned with complex systems can be gathered under the umbrella of complexity science (Burnes, 2005). In general, complexity theories explain how order, pattern, and structure can arise or dissipate in complex systems (Manson, 2001). A complex system consists of many parts interacting with each other in a complex way such that the whole is more than the sum of the parts (Simon, 1962). Contemporary firms are complex adaptive systems made up of various business functions, units, and divisions that dynamically cooperate by exchanging tangible and intangible resources. The nonlinear and emergent nature of their exchanges makes it difficult to understand and predict their future behavior and performance (Kauffman, 1992). An M&A triggers numerous changes to a parent firm at all levels, especially in the ITI phase, which involves coupling different IT systems. These changes result in more complexity for the firm, which is already a complex system.

Complexity reflects the degree of uncertainty in understanding a phenomenon or in achieving a functional requirement and consists of three key facets: scale, diversity, and connectivity (Suh & Ralph, 2005). The ITI phase of an M&A embodies all three facets: the M&A summons many systems to be integrated and changed (scale), creates an environment filled with diverse systems and actors (diversity), and requires connecting numerous subsystems to act as one (connectivity). These changes lead to an unstable and unpredictable environment in which negative events such as data breaches are likely to occur. Complexity theory has been utilized to examine post-merger integration in prior research and is conducive to a better understanding of post-M&A outcomes (Turner & Baker, 2019).

The complexity of the M&A environment can be exacerbated by the dynamic nature of the changes. An M&A setup often requires changes and upgrades to numerous digital technologies, causing tension in the environment and actors responsible for such changes (Benbya et al., 2020). Complexity may result from ITI because “endogenous changes in the process lead to nonlinear bursts of complexity and after the burst of complexity the processes look much different” (Pentland et al., 2020). Consequently, M&As cause an eruption in complexity, and the system state is never the same again. M&As require a variety of systems to be interconnected based on different logic, and the merged system will exhibit unpredictable behaviors. The changes involved in M&As are nonlinear in the sense that the new system is not simply the sum of individual systems (parts) and a small change in some variable of a subsystem could trigger chain reactions, ripple through the entire system, and lead to huge consequences (Rickles et al., 2007). The post-M&A environment is a sensitive state where a small change could continuously escalate and eventually cause critical impacts such as cybersecurity breaches (Benbya et al., 2020), exemplifying the butterfly effect. For example, In the JPMorgan Chase breach in 2014, a testing server used in an M&A was neglected, and this seemingly harmless inaction created an entry point for hackers to breach the bank’s information systems, leading to catastrophic losses (Silver-Greenberg, 2014).

In the context of M&A, merged technology systems can induce aggregate complexity (Manson, 2001) because an M&A requires the interaction of otherwise disjointed systems from different firms, and these subsystems need to work together as parts in a new system. As the number of acquisitions increases, so does the complexity of the new system. Thus, the number of acquisitions of a firm affects the magnitude of complexity, as with every M&A, more new components are integrated, and the intercomponent relationships become increasingly complex.

## Integrative Theoretical Framework

Complexity theory in itself suggests that all M&As lead to complexity and are equally vulnerable to data breaches. It cannot explain why data breaches happen to some M&As but not others. We integrate matching theory and routine activity theory (RAT) with complexity theory to achieve a more holistic understanding of this phenomenon. RAT is a criminology theory that explains why crimes occur (Cohen & Felson, 1979). It has increasingly been used by IS researchers to understand organizational cybersecurity incidents (Li et al., 2021; Pang & Tanriverdi, 2022; Wang et al., 2019). RAT posits that the occurrence of a crime depends on three conditions: (1) motivated offenders; (2) a valuable, visible, and accessible target; and (3) insufficient guardianship of the target (Cohen & Felson, 1979). This theory is particularly relevant to the M&A context because much information related to an M&A can become visible to threat agents through media coverage of the M&A deal, and the parent firm can become accessible to threat agents through the vulnerable target firm, which lowers the parent firm’s guardianship.

Our integration of matching theory and RAT is based on the following logic. First, all M&As do not lead to the same level of complexity. Matching theory (Mitsuhashi & Greve, 2009) suggests that the more dissimilar two firms are, the more complex their M&A will be and the more data breaches they will have. Second, M&A complexity does not automatically lead to data breaches. For a data breach to occur, it has to be known to the threat agents, and they must be motivated to attack. RAT (Cohen & Felson, 1979) suggests that the more visible an M&A is, the more attention it gains from threat agents and the more likely these agents will exploit its complexity to initiate an attack. Finally, if the parent firm inherits cybersecurity vulnerabilities from a target firm during an M&A, complexity may not be the direct cause of subsequent data breaches, although it could still indirectly cause data breaches by making it difficult to detect vulnerabilities. As a competing argument, we draw on RAT (Cohen & Felson, 1979) to posit that the more vulnerable the target firm is, the more likely it is that the parent firm will experience data breaches in the M&A context. Altogether, the integrative theoretical framework explains not only how data breaches can arise indigenously from the heterogeneous complexity of the M&A transaction but also how they can be contracted like an infectious disease from the target firm.

Matching theory, developed by strategic management scholars, explains why firms form strategic alliances based on dyadic interfirm attributes, such as market complementarity and resource compatibility (Mitsuhashi & Greve, 2009). According to this theory, mutually compatible resources can help firms access new knowledge, new markets, or new technologies, leading to greater success (Wang & Zajac, 2007). Compatible or similar resources enable efficient collaboration between firms by lowering coordination costs so that the firms can satisfy consumer demands, reduce competition, achieve scale effects, and avoid internal conflicts (Mitsuhashi & Greve, 2009). In the context of M&A, resource compatibility has also been found to be positively related to M&A success (Bauer & Matzler, 2014). Conversely, matching theory posits that incompatible resources will increase the difficulty and complexity of business operations, causing higher transaction costs and inefficiency (Mitsuhashi & Greve, 2009). It has long been recognized that similar management styles, organizational structures, and business processes enable two firms to effectively cooperate (Palich et al., 2000).

Two firms have a better match fit if they are similar in terms of the nature of their businesses and/or the organizational attributes (process, people, and technology). Even for two matched firms with compatible resources, the integration of similar systems requiring homogeneous changes would be complex. For two unmatched firms with incompatible resources, integrating dissimilar systems requiring heterogeneous changes will be even more complex and could easily result in chaos (Bauer & Matzler, 2014). The technological misfit is particularly concerning because firms in different business domains operate with different technological systems to meet their unique business requirements, and their IT applications exhibit different levels of standardization, customization, automation, scalability, and compatibility (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). Such diversity poses tough challenges to technology integration in M&As and could exponentially increase the complexity. We contend that an excessive level of complexity will become chaotic and weaken the system’s security. As Tanriverdi and Du (2020) demonstrate, M&As can alter a firm’s IT control environment by modifying the cross-business IT linkages, thereby affecting the complexity structure of the firm. These changes can lead to IT control material weaknesses, making the firm vulnerable to cybersecurity incidents.

## Hypothesis Development

Integrating complexity theory, matching theory, and RAT, we develop a research model to examine how M&As lead to data breaches (Figure 1). Specifically, we examine if the number of M&As increases the number of data breaches. We further hypothesize that the impact of M&As on data breach recurrence depends on three moderators: M&A diversity, M&A media publicity, and target vulnerability.

Main effect: An M&A demands numerous technological adaptations and changes from both the parent and target firms so that they can integrate with each other. Human or system errors in such a complex environment abound. Such an errorprone and complex environment is vulnerable to security flaws and may be exploited by malicious actors to induce data breaches. Too often, M&A announcements are followed by waves of attacks from malicious actors to take advantage of the overwhelmingly complex changes involved in an M&A (Jelen, 2019; Lim, 2019). According to an IBM cyber risk report, it is common for malicious actors to target M&A activities by finding loopholes in operations in transition (Meyrick et al., 2020).

When firms join hands through M&A, the complex web of changes and updates creates heightened tensions that demand endogenous changes, which in turn lead to nonlinear bursts of complexity and create a transformative phase of change (Pentland et al., 2020). Post-M&A environments often leave themselves open to attacks from malicious actors, and M&As have become a major target of hackers in vulnerability scans, phishing attempts, and other cyberattacks (Paterson, 2017). Lohrke et al. (2016) indicated that hackers may attempt to steal credentials from employees, mainly those involved in the M&A because more employees gain access to the organization’s critical information assets during the due diligence phase and firewalls are often lowered when information systems are being integrated.

Due to the complexity induced by M&As, technology integration is often accompanied by increased IT control weaknesses in the transition process (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Even a mundane task could be potentially high-stakes if errors are overlooked. According to a Deloitte report (2021), over 53% of IT decision makers reported that devices are often left unattended during integration, leaving precious resources vulnerable to being breached. The same report also found configuration errors and human errors to be among the top concerns in post-M&A periods. As a Verizon report documented, about 20% of 761 major IT breaches involved companies going through an M&A (Lohrke et al., 2016). Such anecdotal evidence suggests that an M&A can trigger or augment the likelihood of a security breach. While a single M&A event can increase opportunities for malicious attacks, a firm going through multiple M&As induces more complexity in every iteration, causing a snowball effect and hence a more risky environment every time a new firm is acquired (Paterson, 2017). As a result, not only will the firm be more likely to experience a data breach, but it will tend to be repeatedly breached.

## H1: The number of data breaches experienced by a firm increases with its number of M&As.

M&A diversity: We draw on matching theory (Mitsuhashi & Greve, 2009) to propose that the dissimilarity between the two firms involved in an M&A can amplify complexity and consequently lead to more data breaches. If the two firms in an M&A have dissimilar resources, regardless of their impact on M&A outcomes, their integration will tend to require more effort and higher costs and the firms will thus be more likely to be vulnerable to cyberthreats. We use M&A diversity, defined as the degree to which the business domains of the parent firm and the target firm differ, to capture interfirm mismatch in the M&A context. M&As can occur between two similar firms (e.g., horizontal merger) or two dissimilar firms (e.g., crossindustry merger or vertical merger) based on their core businesses. When M&A diversity is high, the parent and target firms are divided by a large institutional distance that indicates their distinct cognitive, regulatory, and normative institutions (Kostova et al., 2020). While M&A diversity may sometimes improve the likelihood of M&A success (Fan & Goyal, 2006), it can also cause unintended consequences due to the complexity it brings with it (Hwang, 2004). The tedious and error-prone task of integrating diverse business processes and systems is likely to disrupt the smooth operation of even the best-managed systems (Robbins & Stylianou, 1999). As M&A diversity increases, firms will be more likely to suffer from unintended consequences such as data breaches.

Prior research has highlighted the crucial role of complexity in the ITI process of M&As between mismatched firms. Tanriverdi and Uysal (2011) summarize the integration complexities into five categories: IT infrastructures, IT applications and data, IT human resource management practices, IT vendor management practices, and IT strategymaking practices. They suggest that dissimilar firms introduce increased tension regarding diverse IT applications, human resources practices, IT vendors, and vendor management practices, which can significantly complicate decisions related to the integration of technology, people, and processes. A recent study confirmed that M&As can result in IT control material weaknesses because existing IT controls are disrupted by the addition of new connections and dependencies (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020).

For example, when a retail firm acquires an IT firm, there is often a significant mismatch in the firms’ strategic goals and institutional environments. The retail firm may prioritize customer engagement, while the IT firm may focus on developing new applications. Their organizational cultures, norms, and policies may also differ, and their employees will be likely to possess different domain knowledge, skills, and cognitive styles. As a result, there will be a significant institutional distance between them (Kostova et al., 2020) and integrating the two organizations will undoubtedly add complexity that may lead to unpredictable problems. Several failed vertical mergers have confirmed the profound impact of diversity on M&A. For example, Amazon’s acquisition of Whole Foods in 2017 resulted in suboptimal outcomes due to cultural differences (Gelfand et al., 2018). AOL’s merger with Time Warner, valued at \$183 billion, is considered one of the worst M&A deals, with its failure attributed to cultural and other incompatibilities (Bodie, 2006). The 2018 M&A between AT&T and Time Warner, another blockbuster failure, caused AT&T to lose \$47 billion (Stewart, 2022), with the failure once again attributed to cultural clashes (Glass, 2021). These failed cross-industry M&As corroborate that the integration process is much more chaotic in M&As between dissimilar firms compared to those between similar firms.

Particularly when considering the ITI of an M&A, the additional complexity stemming from the dissimilarity between the parent firm and the target firm tends to make the integration process more challenging. Because the two firms operate in different business domains, their information systems tend to be customized to serve their unique business purposes and are unlikely to be compatible with each other (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). The greater the difference between the two firms, the more complex and chaotic the integration may become. The integration requires business and IT professionals from both firms to work together, but miscommunication and conflicts are likely to take place because of differences in their domain knowledge, cognitive schema, and work styles developed from previous work experiences (Emmanouilides & Giovanis, 2006; Marks & Mirvis, 2001). As posited by complexity theory, a tiny error could lead to a seemingly unrelated huge event in a complex system (Thietart & Forgues, 1995), and matching theory suggests that such tiny errors will happen more frequently when two dissimilar firms are integrated. The interaction between professionals in a highly complex environment can become chaotic due to the lack of shared language and knowledge as well as political struggles (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020), potentially leading to unanticipated cybersecurity disasters.

In general, instability in an organization can arise from the diversity of organizational activities (Thietart & Forgues, 1995). In an M&A, the more different the two firms are, the more diverse their organizational activities will be and the more effort will be needed to merge them—

which will often create vulnerabilities and provide opportunities to hackers who are particularly interested in attacking firms undergoing M&As. Hence, we contend that the diversity of the firms involved in M&As could worsen the negative consequences of M&As, causing parent firms to have even more data breaches when they acquire firms from different business domains.

H2: M&A diversity amplifies the effect of M&As such that the number of M&As has a stronger effect on the number of data breaches of the parent firm when the M&A diversity is greater.

Media publicity: An M&A often makes headlines well in advance of its formal announcement, and such media publicity makes both firms involved in the M&A targets for hackers scanning for susceptible victims to infiltrate. From the RAT perspective (Cohen & Felson, 1979), media publicity directly contributes to the visibility of the firms associated with an M&A. News articles about an M&A may only reveal bits and pieces of information about the M&A plan, timeline, key personnel, and information systems. But the isolated information that appears on multiple web channels can be stitched together to provide significantly more information than the firm may intend to disclose. This logic has been explained in another line of research on IT strategy. Grover and Kohli (2013) cautioned that firms should publish information strategically and carefully because system visibility carries the risk of essential company information disclosure to competitors. This warning is readily applicable in the cybersecurity context, as hackers are constantly monitoring their environment to identify potential targets, and the media exposure of M&As is likely to draw their attention, increasing the probability of attacks.

H3: Media publicity amplifies the effect of M&As such that the number of M&As has a stronger effect on the number of data breaches of the parent firm when M&As have greater media publicity.

Target vulnerability: Drawing on RAT, prior research has shown that the lack of capable guardians can increase the likelihood of data breaches (Pang & Tanriverdi, 2022). If hackers want to infiltrate a large firm in the wake of an M&A, a typical strategy for them is to breach the smaller target firm’s cybersecurity defense first and then access the large parent firm’s systems. This strategy can be effective because large firms usually invest tremendous resources to fortify their IT security and thus provide a stronger guardianship, whereas small firms have a much weaker guardianship due to limited resources or attention to IT security. Prior research has pointed out that breaching the target firm may not be the ultimate goal of hackers but can serve as a stepping stone to break into the parent firm (Meyrick et al., 2020). For example, Marriott acquired Starwood Group and then found that 133.7 million<sup>5</sup> customer records were stolen because of breached servers at Starwood (Jelen, 2019).<sup>6</sup> Thus, it is possible that the relationship between M&As and data breaches is not directly caused by the complexity of M&As but instead by the ex ante vulnerabilities of the target firm. In this case, M&A complexity becomes a secondary determinant of data breaches, although it could still make an indirect contribution.

H4: Target vulnerability amplifies the effect of M&As such that the number of M&As has a stronger effect on the number of data breaches of the parent firm when the target firm has a higher vulnerability to data breaches.

## Methodology

## Data and Variables

To test the hypotheses, we developed a unique dataset by merging multiple sources of archival data based on ticker IDs. We collected data on M&As, data breaches, and firm attributes for all the U.S.-based public companies whose data were available at Crunchbase (a popular information provider to support investment decisions). The firm size varied from 1 to 2.2 million employees, with revenues ranging from a few million dollars to \$556 billion. The firms operated in a variety of business sectors (25 GIC groups). Overall, our data covered 5,072 firms over an 18-year period (2004-2021), yielding a total of 61,209 firm-year observations on 19,175 M&As. The average number of acquisitions made by each firm was 3.78. A large corporation could have had hundreds of M&As over the 18 years. For example, Google had 242 M&As, and Gallagher had 343. The first breach occurred in 2004, which is why we chose to start the observations in 2004. Overall, the firms in our sample had 1,704 data breaches, and 537 firms experienced at least one data breach during our observation period. All the sources and measures of the variables are summarized in Table 1. Table 2 presents descriptive statistics of all the variables. Appendix A shows the pairwise correlations between the variables.

Dependent variable—data breach: A data breach is the intentional or unintentional release of secure, private, or confidential information to an untrusted environment without appropriate authorization. It occurs when sensitive, protected, or private information is copied, communicated, viewed, stolen, or utilized by unauthorized agents. We obtained data breach information for all firms from three sources: the Identity Theft Resource Center (IDTRC), a nonprofit institution aimed at enabling and guiding consumers, victims, businesses, and government to reduce risk and alleviate the consequences of identity compromise and crime; the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC), a non-profit organization dedicated to engaging, educating, and empowering individuals to protect their privacy; and Verizon’s Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR) (Verizon, 2021), which offers yearly assessments of security incidents and data breaches classified by sector. Many studies have quantified data breaches using these sources (Jiang et al., 2022; Seh et al., 2020; Tanriverdi et al., 2019). Overall, we collected 638 breaches from ITRC, 461 from PRC, and 605 from DBIR, adding up to 1,704 breaches in total. The number of data breaches is a simple tally of the data breaches experienced by a parent company in a given year.<sup>7</sup>

Independent variable—M&A: The number of M&As and other associated information were gathered from crunchbase.com and aggregated annually for each firm. Crunchbase is a business information platform that provides information on investment and financing, leader biographies, mergers and acquisitions, events, and industry developments. Crunchbase data has been increasingly used in firm-level research in recent years (den Besten, 2020). Detailed information on Crunchbase's data is available elsewhere (Dalle et al., 2017).

Moderator—M&A diversity: M&A diversity was calculated based on business domains between the parent firm and the target firm using two indexes: the Shannon Diversity Index and the Simpson Diversity Index (hereafter Shannon Index and Simpson Index). These two indexes have been widely applied in various disciplines such as ecology, economics, information science, and physics to measure the degree to which members of a group or community differ from each other (Jost, 2006; Whittaker, 1965).

Shannon Index: The Shannon Index has been used for decades to assess the diversity of elements (e.g., groups or species) in a set (Spellerberg & Fedor, 2003). Originally designed to quantify the entropy of text strings, it is related to the scaled geometric mean of the relative abundance of types. The logic is that the more types that exist in a set, the higher the entropy will be and the more difficult it will be to predict the type of a randomly chosen item from the set. The equation of the Shannon Index is:

$$
H = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {R} p _ {i} * \ln p _ {i},\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { i }$ is the proportion of a specific element belonging to type i in the set. In our research context, the set is the union of the unique business domains of both the parent and target firms.<sup>8</sup>

Simpson Index: We used the Simpson Index as an alternative to the Shannon Index. When individual items are grouped into categories, the Simpson Index is often employed to measure the degree of concentration. It reflects the likelihood that two randomly picked individuals from a given sample will belong to the same category (Keylock, 2005). The Simpson Index is denoted as:

$$
D = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {R} \frac {n _ {i} * (n _ {i} - 1)}{N (N - 1)},\tag{2}
$$

where $n _ { i }$ is the number of items belonging to the category i, and N is the total number of items of all categories in the sample. The Simpson Index can be created by taking the reciprocal of the Simpson Index (Jost, 2006).<sup>9</sup>

The Shannon and Simpson Diversity Indices are calculated based on a single M&A event. If a firm has multiple M&As in a year, the diversity of each M&A is computed, and their average is employed to represent the overall M&A diversity for the firm by using the following equation:

$$
D i v e r s i t y _ {i, t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \frac {S _ {i , j , t}}{n},\tag{3}
$$

where $S _ { i , j , t }$ is either the Shannon or Simpson Index for the j<sup>th</sup> M&A of firm i in year t.

M&A media publicity: Media publicity is operationalized as the number of online news articles related to an M&A event. Since search engines are the primary tool for seeking information about any topic of interest (Jansen & Spink, 2006), we employed Microsoft Bing, one of the most popular search engines (Dillahunt et al., 2015), to identify media articles related to each M&A deal 1 year before (1-year pre) or 3 months after (3-month post) the announcement date.<sup>10</sup> Using the Bing Web Search API, we searched for all online articles based on the search terms: “[Parent company name]” AND (acquires OR purchases OR buys OR “merges with”) AND “[Target firm name].” The number of search results was used to represent M&A media publicity. If a parent firm had multiple M&As in a single year, the sum of all search results was calculated to measure media publicity.

Target vulnerability: Target vulnerability is operationalized as a binomial variable, target breach history, to indicate whether the target firm had data breaches before the M&A (1=breached before, 0=not breached before).

M&A transaction type: There are five common types of transactions: (1) a simple acquisition in which a parent firm acquires ownership of the target firm, (2) a merger in which two equal or tenable firms merge to form a new larger firm, (3) a leveraged buyout in which the parent firm acquires the target firm using borrowed funds, (4) an acquihire buyout in which the parent firm acquires the target solely for the resources and skills the target firm possesses, and (5) a management buyout in which the management team of a firm acquires the assets and operations of the company they are overseeing. We coded M&A transaction type as a categorical variable.

M&A strategic type: We measured two popular M&A strategic types because of their sharp contrast: horizontal M&A, in which a parent firm acquires a competitor in the same industry, and vertical M&A, in which a parent firm acquires a business partner (supplier or customer) from a different industry along the supply chain (Rozen-Bakher, 2018). Our CrunchBase data included all business domains of firms. We determined the M&A strategic type based on these domains: if none of the target firm’s businesses is distinct from those of the parent firm, it is horizontal; otherwise, it is vertical. Since a firm-year observation could have multiple M&As, we coded all vertical M&As as 1, all horizontal M&As as 2, a mixture of both vertical and horizontal M&As as 3, and no M&A as 0.

Size discrepancy: We computed the difference in the number of employees between each parent-target firm pair and used the average of all the size differences of all M&As in a given year to represent the overall size diversity for a given parent firm. The natural log of size discrepancy was used in data analysis.

Control variables: Firm variables, including firm size (number of employees), revenue, goodwill, R&D expenses, cash, and assets were collected from Compustat. We controlled for revenue, as hackers usually prefer to attack profitable and reputable firms and because these firms are always the bigger target (Dahmash et al., 2009). We controlled for the goodwill score of the firm, which reflects the firm’s intangible value. The total reported goodwill has been found to be positively associated with firm value and the valuation of M&As (Bugeja & Gallery, 2006). We also controlled for other firm attributes that have the potential to attract malicious actors, such as R&D expenses, assets, cash flow, dividends, and total inventory. Finally, we controlled for two additional factors related to M&A diversity: cumulative M&As and cumulative data breaches that a firm has experienced as of the end of each year.

## Empirical Analysis

We used the following fixed effects model to test the hypothesis that M&As affect the number of data breaches for a firm:

$$
\begin{array}{l} y _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} X _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} M _ {i, t} + \beta_ {3} (X _ {i, t} \cdot M _ {i, t}) + \sum \beta_ {k} C _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {i} + \\ \gamma_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The dependent variable $y _ { i , t }$ is the number of data breaches for firm i in year t. $X _ { i , t }$ is the independent variable—the number of M&As for firm i in year t. $M _ { i , t }$ is the moderator—the diversity of all M&As, media publicity, and the target vulnerability for firm i in year $t . \ \sum \beta _ { k } C _ { i , t }$ is a collection of time-variant control variables. $\alpha _ { i }$ denotes the firm fixed effects. $\gamma _ { t }$ denotes the year fixed effects. $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the idiosyncratic error term. We controlled for both firm and year fixed effects to alleviate endogeneity concerns regarding omitted variables related to firm attributes and temporal shocks, as fixed effects (FE) regression is especially useful for deriving causal inference (Gangl, 2010). Additionally, to establish causal identification, we created matched samples and applied difference-in-difference (DiD) analysis to show that firms with exactly one M&A have more data breaches than firms without any M&As.

Therefore, we reran the analysis based on the sample of firms that had at least one M&A. As Table 3 shows, the results are highly similar with only minor differences.

Media publicity: To test the moderating role of media publicity (H3), a search 1 year prior to the Bing search was included as a moderator. The significant interaction term (β = 0.250, p < 0.01) suggests that M&As with high media exposure are more likely to be associated with data breaches, compared to those with low media publicity (see Table 4). Additionally, we also tested using a search 3 months after the Bing search, and the results were consistent with the 1-yearprior results. Thus, H3 is supported.

Target vulnerability: To test the moderating role of target vulnerability (H4), we included target breach history as a moderating variable in the analysis. Surprisingly, the coefficient of the interaction term was negative (β = -0.966, p < 0.05), suggesting that if the target firm has a record of data breaches, more M&As can reduce the number of data breaches to the parent firm. A plausible explanation is that the breach history of the target firm sounds the alarm to the parent firm so that it pays more attention to securing information assets during the M&A, thus reducing future data breaches. Conversely, a target firm that has not been previously breached can create a false impression of its security. During the M&A transaction, the parent firm may be misled by this false impression and fail to take enough caution in taking cybersecurity measures. Consequently, the parent firm inherits the target firm’s hidden vulnerabilities and may suffer from more future data breaches.

We also conducted subgroup analysis by splitting the data based on target breach history. As Table 4 shows, for the group where target firms have no breach history, M&As significantly increased data breaches $( \beta = 0 . 8 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ whereas for the group where target firms had a breach history, M&As had a nonsignificant negative effect on data breaches $( \beta = . 6 9 8 , p = 0 . 0 8 )$ . These results suggest that the target firm’s vulnerability did not provide a facilitating mechanism by which M&As increase data breaches. Instead, it may have served as a warning signal for the parent firm to enforce cybersecurity measures during the M&A and therefore impeded data breaches.

Finally, we included all three moderators in a single model to check if their moderation effects still existed. The results in Table 4 show that all three interaction terms remained significant, suggesting that these moderation mechanisms work in parallel and do not compete.

## Causal Identification

Many confounding factors could interfere with our causal inference that M&A leads to data breaches. To address this concern, we conducted DiD analysis based on matched samples to achieve causal identification (Athey & Imbens, 2017). In our dataset, 2131 (42%) firms had no M&As, 970 (19%) firms had exactly one M&A, and 1971 (39%) firms had multiple M&As during the 18-year period. The first two groups provided a quasi-experiment-like scenario where we could examine whether a single M&A, treated as an exogenous shock, can cause data breach recurrence. Since we had panel data and since each firm’s attributes change from year to year, we adopted a hybrid matching approach to match firms without M&A and firms with just one M&A, following Valentini (2012). We used coarsened exact matching (CEM) to match firms based on two variables (year and industry) and propensity score matching (PSM) to match firms based on other firm attributes (employees, revenue, cash flow, assets, goodwill, R&D expense, dividends, and inventory). This ensured that firms from the same industry and same year were matched based on the firm attributes. Using this approach, we matched 642 firms with one M&A (treatment group) with 1975 firms without M&As (control group). The common support assumption was satisfied since the density plot showed a large overlap between the propensity scores of the treatment and control groups. After matching, the standardized mean differences of all covariates between the treatment and control groups were below 0.1, and the density plot showed an improved overlap between the two groups (see Appendix C). We then conducted DiD analysis using a twoway fixed effects (TWFE) model:

$$
y _ {i, t} = \sum \beta_ {k} \mathbb {1} [ t - E _ {i} = k ] + \sum \delta_ {k} C _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t},\tag{5}
$$

where $E _ { i }$ is the year when a M&A occurred, $\mathbb { 1 } [ t - E _ { i } = k ]$ indicates being k years from the M&A year, $\alpha _ { i }$ denotes the firm fixed effects, $\gamma _ { t }$ denotes the year fixed effects, and $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the idiosyncratic error term. As the M&A year for each firm was different, this model allowed us to test the staggered treatment effect. As Table 5 shows, the average treatment effect is significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting that an M&A event can lead to more data breaches. We repeated the analysis by replacing PSM with Mahalanobis distance matching (MDM) and keeping all the other procedures the same. The treatment effect was highly consistent with only minor variations (β = 0.171, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , confirming the causal effect of M&A.

The matching methods so far created unbalanced samples in which one treatment firm was matched with one or more control firms (1:M). While this approach increases precision, it may also increase bias. Hence, we applied one-to-one (1:1) matching using the same approach. This time, we only assigned one best-matching control firm to a treatment firm. With the new sample, we reran the DiD analysis. As Table 5 shows, the treatment effects were slightly larger, but still significant at the 0.05 level, confirming the findings from the 1:M matched samples.

While the TWFE model has been widely used to identify causal effects of staggered events, recent econometrics methodology research shows that it can yield biased results because the estimation of treatment effects includes a problematic comparison between the treatment group and the already treated group (Goodman-Bacon, 2021). To correct the bias, we followed an approach proposed by Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) to further validate the TWFE results. The analysis on the matched sample (PSM+CEM 1:1 matching) showed that the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) was still significant (ATT = 0.937, p < 0.01, firms = 310, observations = 20,432),<sup>12</sup> confirming the effect of M&A on data breaches.

Overall, these results indicate that there is likely a causal relationship between a single M&A and the number of data breaches. While it is infeasible to perform matching and DiD for firms with multiple M&As,<sup>13</sup> it can be reasonably deduced from the current results that when a firm has multiple M&As it will encounter even more data breaches.

## Alternative Conceptualization of M&A Diversity

We hitherto conceptualized M&A diversity from the business domain perspective. Alternatively, M&A diversity can be reflected by the difference between the parent firm and the target firm in terms of size and industry. In this section, we demonstrate that size diversity and industry diversity of M&As may also lead to more data breaches.

Size diversity: Size diversity refers to the size discrepancy between the parent firm and the target firm. It may also boost the complexity level of the M&A, thereby influencing data breaches. Large firms and small firms typically have different business processes, management approaches, and IT systems. The mismatch between the two firms makes it difficult to smoothly integrate these elements without conflicts or errors during the M&A. As a result, IT control weaknesses can take place and create opportunities for cyberthreat agents (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Moreover, when there is a large size discrepancy, a large parent firm is typically acquiring a small target firm. The small target firm usually has weaker cybersecurity capabilities and can be more easily breached. A strategy often used by cybercriminals is to use the small firm as a stepping stone to sneak into the large parent firm. They breach the small firm’s system but remain silent until the M&A occurs and then start harvesting valuable data from the parent firm. The larger the size discrepancy, the more lucrative this strategy will be to cybercriminals and the more likely it is that data breaches will occur.

To test the moderating effect of size discrepancy, we first estimated the interaction between size discrepancy and the number of M&As and found it to be significant (β = 0.173, p < 0.05). We then split the data into two subgroups based on the mean of size discrepancy and conducted subgroup analysis. As Table 6 shows, for M&As with a large size discrepancy, the effect of the M&A count is significant, but for M&As with a small size discrepancy, the effect is not significant.

Industry diversity: Industry diversity between the parent firm and the target firm is embodied by the strategic type of M&A. Based on its strategic objective, an M&A can be categorized as being vertical or horizontal (Yasuda & Iijima, 2005). A horizontal M&A seeks to reduce competition by acquiring competitors in the same industry, whereas a vertical M&A aims to increase market power and efficiency by integrating with suppliers or distributors in different industries along the supply chain (Rozen-Bakher, 2018). The integration tends to be more complex for vertical M&As than for horizontal M&As because two firms from different industries generally have a large institutional distance between them (Kostova et al., 2020). As the two firms merge, new business processes, new functional units, and new strategies will be created to accomplish synergy. Meanwhile, IT strategy, IT processes, IT infrastructure, IT policies, and IT personnel will also need to be modified to reflect the business change. This creates a highly complex adaptive system in which IT control weaknesses may become prevalent (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Consequently, firms going through vertical M&As will likely be more vulnerable to cybersecurity attacks, and data breaches may be more likely to occur. In contrast, for horizontal M&As, the parent and target firms are competitors in the same industry and generally manufacture the same products and provide the same services. Since their business strategy and structure are similar, they do not need to change much after integration, and their IT arrangements will typically require only minor changes. As a result, their IT control will likely remain effective for enforcing cybersecurity protection and data breaches will be less likely to occur.

We tested the moderating role of M&A strategic type by conducting a subgroup analysis. We split the data based on M&A strategic type (vertical vs. horizontal). If a firm had both vertical and horizontal M&As in the same year, the observation was excluded to avoid cross-contamination. The effect of M&As on data breaches was estimated separately for pure vertical M&As or pure horizontal M&As. As Table 6 shows, the effect on data breaches was significant for vertical M&As but not significant for horizontal M&As.

## Robustness Check

We conducted a series of checks to further confirm that the effect of M&As on data breaches was robust and not sensitive to choices of model estimators and operationalization of variables.

Placebo test: We ran two placebo (or falsification) tests to provide additional support for the causal identification of our findings (Athey & Imbens, 2017). The first test was based on the M&A’s transactional type. According to our prior theorization, M&As with low complexity are unlikely to lead to data breaches. The transactional type of an M&A determines how ownership changes, how funds are mobilized, and eventually how the parent firm’s structures are modified. The complexity of integration varies based on the transaction type. For example, an acquihire transaction usually does not require structural change and technological integration between two organizations and hence introduces a lower level of complexity. In a management buyout, because the existing management is assuming control of the business, integration complications are largely absent. In comparison, an M&A adds considerable complexity during the integration process because business processes, organizational structures, and information systems need to be adjusted and sometimes revamped to achieve integration (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). Thus, the acquihire and management buyout types of M&As are not expected to increase data breaches. To confirm the falsification prediction, we split our data into two subgroups—the placebo group, involving a low level of structural changes (acquihires and management buyouts), and the treatment group, involving a high level of structural changes (mergers, acquisitions, and leveraged buyouts)—and conducted the analysis on each group separately. The results show that the effect of M&As on data breaches was significant for the treatment group, but not for the placebo group (See Appendix D, Table D1).

The second placebo test was based on randomized M&A assignment. Instead of the actual number of M&As, we created a random number for each firm-year observation to represent the firm’s number of M&As. Clearly, this random M&A number would not be expected to influence the firm’s data breaches. With random M&A numbers, we reran the tests of all hypotheses. As Table D1 in Appendix D shows, none of the hypotheses were supported.

Dummy M&A test: To reduce concerns that outliers may have impacted the effect of M&As on data breaches, we created a dummy variable to represent whether a firm had M&As or not. The dummy variable was coded as 1 regardless of the number of M&As. The results (Table D1 in Appendix D) confirm that the M&A dummy had a positive effect on data breaches. The interaction between the M&A dummy and the Shannon Index could not be estimated because the dummy variable was a constant, whereas the Shannon Index varied.

Big firm effect: It is possible that there was no causality between M&As and data breach recurrence and their apparent relationship resulted from their respective relationship with firm size. Because large firms tend to have both more M&As and more data breaches, it could be falsely concluded that M&As cause data breaches based on their co-occurrence. This issue is addressable because our dataset included 5,072 firms with a large size variation (mean number of employees = 8,777, median = 385, std. = 49,915). Among these firms,

3,480 firms had fewer than 1,000 employees, which is the cutoff between small and midsize enterprises (SMEs) and large enterprises (according to Gartner).<sup>14</sup> Our analysis already controlled for firm size (number of employees), suggesting that the impact of M&As on data breaches was not specific to big companies. To further mitigate this concern, we ran analysis based on both big and small firms (small = less than 1000 employees, big = at least 1000 employees). The results (see Appendix D, Table D2) confirm that for both subgroups, there was a significant positive effect of M&As as well as a significant positive interaction between M&As and diversity. In addition, we split the sample based on the medial employee number (n = 385) and obtained consistent results (also in Table D2). These results suggest that the effects of M&As were not dependent on firm size.

Alternative diversity measure: We used the Shannon Index in the main analysis. We replicated the analysis by using the Simpson Index, which is moderately correlated with the Shannon Index (r = 0.773), as an alternative measure of M&A complexity. As shown in Table D3 in Appendix D, the Simpson Index had a significant interaction with the M&A count, suggesting an increase in the number of data breaches.

Controlling for past M&A experience and data breach experience: We included cumulative acquisitions and cumulative data breaches as two additional control variables. A firm’s cumulative M&As reflect its experience in managing the M&A process. It is likely that a more experienced firm would do a better job of carrying out the necessary integrations and adapting to the associated complexities. The cumulative data breaches of a firm suggest how vulnerable the firm has historically been, and more breaches in the past would be expected to either attract more attacks in the future or alert the firm to enhance security measures and thus reduce attacks in the future. Therefore, it is essential to control for these two variables to eliminate their potential confounding effects. As shown in Table D3 in Appendix D, the results confirm our main findings. As expected, the effect of cumulative M&As is significantly negative and the effect of cumulative breaches is significantly positive. These results verify that in a given year, immediate past M&As have a causal effect on data breaches independent of M&As or data breaches in the distant past.

Lag model: Although it is unlikely that reverse causality was a concern for this study (i.e., data breaches lead to M&As), we estimated a lag model with all independent variables lagged by one year to minimize the concern. As shown in Table D3 in Appendix D, the results of the lag model also support all our main and interaction hypotheses, confirming that the number of M&As increases the number of data breaches and this effect is strengthened by M&A diversity.

Heckman model: Since M&As are major business deals that require tremendous financial capacity, not all firms are able to engage in M&As. This means that the firms that had M&As were not randomly selected, leading to a potential selection bias in our sample. We used the Heckman two-stage regression model (Heckman, 1976) to address this endogeneity concern. We split the firms into two groups based on whether they had M&As in given year. In the first stage, we estimated a Probit model with all the control variables as predictors. To generate unbiased estimates, Heckman models require at least one exogenous variable as an exclusion restriction in the first stage but not the second stage (Certo et al., 2016). We thus included two exogenous variables in the Probit model: the industry average revenue and industry average R&D input. Industry attributes are generally considered exogenous to a firm’s M&A decision (Valentini, 2012). The inverse Mills ratio (IMR) was created from the Probit model. In the second stage, data breach was estimated as a function of the original regressors with the addition of the IMR to account for any sample-induced endogeneity. As shown in Table D3 in Appendix D, the significant main and interaction effects confirm our main findings.

Count model: The number of data breaches is essentially a count variable with Poisson or negative binomial distribution. We applied several count data estimators to perform additional data analysis. First, we estimated fixed-effect Poisson models (xtpoisson) and an overdispersion model (xtnbreg). We then applied a different analytical approach, the generalized estimating equation (GEE) regression model (Liang & Zeger, 1986). The distribution family was set to either Poisson or negative binomial, and the log link function was used. The results are consistent with our main analysis (see Appendix D, Table D4).

Logit model: Our main analysis focused on the number of data breaches because this maximized the informational value of the panel data. Alternatively, the risk of having data breaches can be studied as a binomial variable with only two possible outcomes. We quantified the data breach risk as 1 if the parent company had any data breach in a given year and 0 if there was no breach. Employing logit models, we evaluated the effect of M&As on data breach risk. This is a reductionist approach, but it may have practical value. We ran both FE and RE models because many observations were excluded from the FE model due to the time invariance of the risk dummy (most firms had no data breaches) and the RE model could include these observations for cross-firm comparison. As Table D4 in Appendix D shows, there is a significant positive interaction between the number of M&As and M&A diversity in both the FE and RE model results, suggesting that when M&A diversity is high, more M&As lead to more data breaches.

Rolling window tests: We analyzed how our main findings change over time by performing rolling window regressions. Rolling regression is often employed to characterize the changing relationships over time and examine the stability of the focal relationship (Cai & Juhl, 2023). We estimated the regression parameters using a subset of data based on a fixed time window and then “rolled” this fraction through the entire sample. To make the tests more manageable, we chose to include 11 rolling windows with a size of 8 years spanning from 2004 to 2021. As Table D5 shows, the moderation effect of the Shannon Index is significantly positive for all the rolling windows except one window (2012-2019), suggesting consistent and stable patterns over time.

Overall, the above tests verify the robustness of our results, confirming that the M&As of a parent firm increase the number of data breaches, and the diversity between the parent firm and the target firm amplifies this relationship such that when the two firms are dissimilar, the M&A count has a stronger effect on data breaches.

## Exploration of Nonlinear Effect

To explore potential nonlinear relationships between M&As and data breaches, we included the quadratic term of the number of M&As (M&A-squared) in the analysis and found its regression coefficient to be negatively significant (Table 7). This indicates an inverted-U-shaped relationship between M&As and data breaches, with the turning point around nine M&As. This suggests that firms with nine M&As suffer the most data breaches. While this empirical finding is interesting, there is no theory to explain why such a curvilinear effect exists. A possible mechanism is that if firms learn naturally from their mistakes, it takes nine M&As for them to accumulate enough knowledge to reduce data breaches. However, future research would be needed to shed light on this interesting curvilinear effect.

## Discussion

This study examines how M&As influence the number of data breach occurrences (recurrence) of the parent firm. Based on complexity theory, we propose that M&As increase data breach recurrence and that this effect is strengthened by M&A diversity or by the dissimilarity between the parent firm and the target firm, which may thus lead to more data breaches. Our empirical analysis based on an 18-year panel dataset (2004-2021) of a large quantity of American public companies lends strong support to our hypotheses. First, we found that more M&As lead to more data breaches. We ascribe this effect to the complex environment that an M&A event creates. Every M&A deal introduces considerable complexity and exacerbates the technical vulnerabilities existing in either of the two firms or emerging during the M&A lifecycle; thus, it is not surprising that the increased M&A count heightens data breach events. Second, we show that if the target firm is dissimilar to the parent firm in terms of business domains, the amount of data breaches is amplified. This moderation effect can be explicated through the lens of matching theory. Given that two firms operating in different business domains likely have incompatible strategies, structures, human resources, and technology architecture, the coupling of such two distinct organizational entities could aggravate the already complex environments by adding another layer of complexity, in which tensions, conflicts, miscommunications, and human errors will further increase the risk of data breaches. Third, we demonstrate that M&As lead to even more data breaches when they attract high media publicity or when the target firm has no data breach records. These findings are consistent with the visibility proposition of RAT but contradict the “lack of guardianship” proposition of RAT.

## Implications for Research

Our study makes an important contribution to both the IS and M&A literature by highlighting the dark side of M&As. In this paper, we present strong evidence that M&As heighten the number of data breaches. Prior research has mostly focused on the positive impacts of M&As, while the undesired effects of M&As have rarely been investigated. Only one study has empirically tested whether the presence of M&As increases the risk of data breach occurrence (Tanriverdi et al., 2019). While that study draws attention to the complexity phenomenon, more research is needed to generate further insights. Our study echoes this call to examine the heterogeneous treatment effects of M&As and offers a nuanced understanding of the causal relationship between M&As and data breaches.

Our study also contributes to the application and extension of complexity theory in explaining IS phenomena in the contemporary business environment. Resonating with Benbya et al. (2020) who call for more in-depth research using the lens of complexity theory, we posit that the M&A process introduces a great deal of complexity in many dimensions of the involved firms and that such increased complexity can both create and expose vulnerabilities to be exploited by cyberthreat actors. This logic is intuitive and similar to what Tanriverdi et al. (2019) propose in their research. However, we take this one step further by theoretically articulating the connection between complexity and M&A diversity. Based on matching theory, we argue that M&As can increase complexity by adding new components from the acquired firm such as structure, people, technology, processes, cultures, and so on. Such complexities are relatively manageable if the new components are similar to their counterparts in the parent firm. However, if they are drastically different, the situation could become chaotic due to the challenge of reconciling their differences and integrating them. Based on this logic derived from matching theory, we propose the moderation effect of M&A diversity. Our results confirm the moderation, highlighting the heterogeneous effects of M&As on data breaches contingent on M&A diversity. The empirical evidence supports our theoretical proposition that when complexity is amplified by organizational diversity, firms become more vulnerable to data breaches. Thus, our study makes a significant contribution, both theoretically and empirically, to IS research in terms of how M&As lead to data breaches.

Moreover, our attempt to identify alternative mechanisms underlying the effect of M&As on data breaches sheds more light on this understudied phenomenon. Drawing from RAT, we show that the media publicity associated with M&As provides another route for M&As to influence data breaches because it makes the firms more visible as an attack target for cyberthreat agents. We failed to support the plausible theory that M&As increase data breaches because of target firms’ vulnerability. Surprisingly, we found that M&As involving vulnerable target firms have fewer instead of more data breaches. This counterintuitive finding suggests that when theories such as RAT from criminology are applied to explain cybersecurity phenomena at the firm level, the original propositions from RAT may not hold. This should be viewed not as a problem but as an opportunity to generate new knowledge. Taken together, our work offers some clarity regarding how M&As influence data breaches and will hopefully inspire new research ideas in this nascent area of scientific inquiry.

## Implications for Practice

This research highlights the importance of information security management in the M&A context. Firms face serious cybersecurity threats before and after M&A deals (Information Age, 2020; West Monroe, 2016). Indeed, one in three executives surveyed in a recent IBM report said they had experienced data breaches attributable to M&A activities, and one in five had experienced such breaches post-integration (Meyrick et al., 2020). While M&As aid firms to attain growth goals, they also create plenty of opportunities for cyberthreat actors who target firms that are going through or have just gone through an M&A (Paterson, 2017). Surprisingly, despite data breach threats, firms often fail to take appropriate actions to deal with them. One common problem is the lack of IT personnel participation in M&A planning. In fact, only 24% of firms involve IT executives in pre-M&A planning (Curtis & Chanmugam, 2005). This could be due to a lack of understanding of the complex M&A lifecycle and the severe consequences of excluding IT and security experts. It is also possible that some firms may choose to keep the pending M&A as a business secret among a small group of people even if they understand the potential risks of doing so. However, excluding IT and security experts from the planning process can prove to be costly when preventable data breaches occur.

The results of our study can help raise cybersecurity awareness among executives and consultants planning M&A deals. Greater attention should be given to risk management in the due diligence phase and IT integration. The fact that dissimilar companies are more likely to experience breaches than similar companies calls for more effort to manage integration complexities when a firm acquires another firm operating in a completely different business domain or industry. A dedicated team should be assembled to oversee IT integration to address resource mismatch, reduce complexity, and prevent possible data breaches. Key personnel from both the parent and target firms, including business, IT, risk, and security experts such as chief information officers (CIOs), chief information security officers (CISOs), and chief risk officers (CROs), should participate in the team to make sure comprehensive knowledge from both sides is represented. In addition, compliance with IT security policies has been identified as a crucial factor in preventing security breaches. Firms involved in M&As are advised to align their disparate IT security policies and disseminate the new rules among all employees to avoid inadvertent policy violations due to confusion and misunderstandings during the transition period (Lohrke et al., 2016). Ultimately, the complexity issues involved in M&As require a systems thinking approach (Senge, 2006) that mindfully considers every part of the system to prevent unexpected data breaches.

## Limitations and Future Research

Like every study, ours is not without limitations. First, we only focus on U.S. public companies as our research sample. Although they are a significant contributor to the global economy, they may have unique attributes that firms from other countries may not possess. A study with firms from other parts of the world, with diverse cultural, political, technological, and economic backgrounds, would probably yield different insights. Second, due to the lack of detailed data, we cannot differentiate the types and scopes of data breaches. It is possible that M&As have different impacts on various types of breaches (e.g., insider breaches vs. outsider breaches) or on breaches with different scopes (e.g., major breaches of billions of records vs. minor breaches of hundreds of records). Future research investigating how each type of breach is triggered would extend our findings and help us understand how to prevent data breaches from the beginning. If firms can predict when an insider breach versus an outsider breach is more likely to occur following an M&A, they can prioritize their risk management efforts towards the most critical areas during the M&A lifecycle. Third, we have not considered the composition of the M&A planning team as a factor in our analysis. M&As are generally perceived to be strategic transactions, and the involvement of technological experts such as CIOs/CISOs is often limited (DeTullio, 2017). If data on the M&A planning team were available, it would be interesting to examine whether the presence of IT executives on the team roster would reduce the risk of data breaches in the post-M&A period.

## Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals that M&As can be detrimental because they can increase the number of data breaches, and this effect is amplified when the parent and target firms are dissimilar, when the M&A attracts high media publicity, or when the target firm has had no previous data breaches. Our findings establish a strong empirical link between M&As and data breaches, which will hopefully motivate IS scholars and practitioners to recognize the important role played by M&As in fostering data breaches. In an era when firms are facing serious cybersecurity threats, it is essential that business and IT practitioners pay close attention to the integration between parent and target firms during M&As to prevent potential data breaches.

## Marginal Effect Plots

To further investigate how the number of M&As influences the number of data breaches at each level of the Shannon Index, we developed marginal effect plots. We only considered firms that have M&As, because the Shannon Index cannot be calculated if there is no target firm. Specifically, we created three plots. In the first plot, we created a binary variable group, based on whether the firms had more than one M&A. In the second plot, we changed the cutoff value to 2, and in the third plot, we changed it to 3. This helps show levels at which the interaction between M&As and diversity becomes significant.

Figure B1. Marginal Effects of M&As on Data Breaches at Different Values of Shannon Diversity

Across all three plots, a common pattern is that the Shannon Index always has a positive relationship with data breaches, regardless of the level of M&As. At each specific value of the Shannon Index, we can compare how firms with different numbers of M&As differ in terms of data breaches. When the M&A cutoff is 1 or 2, the confidence intervals of data breaches for both groups overlap with each other, suggesting no significant differences (Figures B1a and B1b). When the M&A cutoff is 3, the confidence intervals of data breaches for both groups intersect with each other when the Shannon Index is below 1.8, suggesting no significant differences (Figures B1c). But when the Shannon Index is 1.8 or greater, the confidence interval of the high M&A group (>3) is separated from that of the low M&A group (≤3), suggesting a significant difference. This indicates that comparing a firm with three or fewer M&As and another firm with more than three M&As, the latter will have significantly more data breaches than the former when its Shannon Index is no less than 1.8. In addition, at a very low Shannon Index level, regardless of the M&A cutoff value, the number of data breaches of the low M&A group does appear to be greater than that of the high M&A group. However, the confidence intervals of the high M&A group and the low M&A group always overlap, suggesting no statistically significant difference between them. In other words, even when the Shannon Index is very low, more M&As do not lead to fewer data breaches.

## Matching Assumptions and Outcomes

To test if the common support assumption of matching was satisfied, we created density plots. As Figure C1 shows, there is a large overlap between the propensity scores of the treatment group (with M&A) and the control group (without M&A), suggesting that it was appropriate to conduct matching on our data. In addition, following Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020), we found that the pretreatment average treatment effect of the treated (ATT) did not differ from zero $( \chi ^ { \bar { 2 } } = 3 8 . 3 6 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , supporting the parallel trend assumption for DiD. In addition, a relative time model based on Callaway and Sant’Anna (2020) showed that the treatment effects before the M&A event did not differ from zero, and several treatment effects after the event differed from zero, thus supporting the parallel trend assumption (Figure C2).

After propensity score matching, we tested matching balance. We did not use a t-test because it has been criticized as a fallacy that can generate misleading inferences (Imai et al. 2008). As Figure C3 shows, the SMD (and variances) for each variable of the matched data became much smaller than that of the raw data.
