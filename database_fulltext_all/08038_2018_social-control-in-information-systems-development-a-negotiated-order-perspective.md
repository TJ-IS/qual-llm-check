## Abstract

Control is vital for IS projects to be delivered on time and within budget. Control theory helps us better understand and explain control in information systems operations and development. However, simplistic notions about control do not correspond to how control actually works. We believe that a social perspective that sees controls as negotiated orders, and not just things imposed by controllers on controlees, better explains control of IS projects and operations. Through an interpretive case study of client–vendor control in the IS department of a large agricultural distributor, we show that: (1) control enactment involves the controller’s consideration of the social and organizational context as well as the controller’s intention. (2) Control enactment involves a socially constructed meaning and unintended consequences. (3) The control enactment also involves controlee intent either because of negotiation with the controller, or indirectly by way of alternative structures. Finally, (4) the control itself influences the controller and controlee in unanticipated ways. Seeing control as the result of negotiated orders between controllers and controlees contributes to control theory by suggesting that, in practice, negotiation, shaping and re-appropriation are necessary for successful control enactment. ## Introduction

F or IS development projects and operations to be delivered on time and within budget, control is vital. Control theory has given us a better understanding of control in IS contexts. Specifically, IS research explains that controller knowledge of the transformation process helps determine the mode of control (Kirsch, 1996); identifies the function of control and modes of control in information systems development (ISD) projects (Kirsch, 1997); demonstrates the importance of a portfolio of control modes; shows that relationships may involve a network of peers with multiple controllers, controlees and control modes (Kirsch, 1997); and highlights the critical roles of users in controlling ISD projects (Kirsch, 1997). Recent work has extended control theory to a multi-stakeholder context (Soh et al.,

2011), to complex IT projects (Chua et al., 2012) and to internal and outsourced software projects (Gregory et al., 2013; Tiwana and Keil, 2007, 2009).

However, one enduring puzzle is that controls often do not produce anticipated behaviours. Both controller and controlee behaviour often deviates from expectations. This paper shows how a social perspective on control (Kling, 1980; Maines and Charlton, 1985; Strauss, 1978; Strauss et al., 1963) helps explain this puzzle. Specifically, we argue for viewing controls as social structures that result from negotiated orders, where the enactment of control is shaped by the controller’s rational decisions and by context, the controlee, and the control itself.

As social structures are tied to the social context, social forces affect both controller and controlee. The contribution of this paper is to provide a more nuanced understanding of the social forces that affect control in information systems. We do this by adopting a negotiated order perspective on control in a case study of IS client–vendor control at one of the world’s largest agricultural distributors.

Our case demonstrates a number of social influences on control enactments not hitherto discussed. Specifically, (1) control enactment involves the controller’s consideration of the social and organizational context as well as the controller’s intention. (2) Control enactment involves a socially constructed meaning and unintended consequences. (3) Control enactment also involves controlee intent either because of negotiation with the controller or indirectly by way of alternative structures. Finally, (4) the control itself influences the controller and controlee in unanticipated ways by imposing costs on both controllers and controlees. These costs are driven by the social and organizational context as negotiated orders.

The paper proceeds as follows. First is a review of the research literature on control theory and negotiated order theory. We then present our research method, the case study, and our analysis of the findings. The final section is the conclusion.

## Literature review

## Control theory

Control theory focuses on motivating individuals to perform in accordance with organizational objectives. Control is usually defined as the mechanisms designed to motivate individuals to work so that desired objectives are achieved (Kirsch, 1996). Fundamental to an IS manager’s job is aligning stakeholders with managerial goals, including encouraging subordinates to complete work on time and within budget, and to the required standard. Control theory (Kirsch, 1996, 1997) identifies mechanisms that can ensure that others perform assigned tasks correctly. It is through control that managers ‘‘motivate IS project leaders to deliver applications that meet specific needs and requirements and that are of value to their firms’’ (Kirsch et al., 2002: 485). One unresolved question is how social forces and negotiated orders affect how clients ensure that vendors perform to assigned levels of satisfaction. Negotiated order theory helps illuminate the dynamics of IS control in practice and helps explain unintended consequences.

Control theory explains that vendor performance can be managed through enacted controls (Kirsch, 1996, 1997). Four modes of control are generally explored: outcome, behaviour (process), clan and self-control. Outcome and behaviour control are formal controls (Henderson and Lee, 1992; Jaworski et al., 1993; Kirsch, 1997) that are enacted by the client (controller) using institutional power to encourage the vendor (controlee) to behave in a particular way. In outcome control, the vendor is rewarded or punished based on output. For example, the vendor may be penalized for not adhering to service level agreements (Goo et al., 2009). Behaviour control refers to management of the work process. Rules and procedures are prescribed, and the agent is monitored to ensure compliance (Kirsch, 1997). Negotiated order theory highlights the importance of social context in any exercise of control and the process by which controls are shaped and re-appropriated within this context.

Clan control employs social capital to encourage vendor performance (Kirsch et al., 2010). There are two steps in the use of social capital to achieve clan control: (1) first, controllers build social capital by creating structure, shared cognition and relationships, and then (2) controllers leverage social capital to achieve specific tasks (Chua et al., 2012). Trust and social relationships are associated with clan control, because it is the interaction between community members that proscribe an agent’s behaviour (Adler, 2001). Negotiated order theory highlights that the structure, shared cognition and relationships that controllers build (the social capital) constitute the social context within which the social structures of controls are enacted.

Self-control occurs when the controlee performs goals desired or valued by the controller because the controlee selfmonitors, or self-rewards (Kirsch, 1996; Kirsch and Cummings, 1996). The key distinction between self and clan control is the origin of the monitoring, and/or reward/ punishment. For clan control, these derive from individuals external to the controlee; for self-control they derive from the controlee (Kirsch, 1997). Negotiated order theory adds to control theory by highlighting the importance of ascribed meaning and its role in influencing whether the controlee performs as the controller desires.

In control theory, the appropriate portfolio of control is determinable based on known or knowable factors such as the situational task, role, knowledge and skill (Kirsch, 1997). However, the empirical evidence often shows that both controllers and controlees behave in unexpected ways (Kraemer and Danziger, 1984). For example, Mullings (1999) demonstrated the limited effectiveness of outcome controls on female Jamaican data entry operators. In theory, but not in practice, outcome controls such as salary adjustments should motivate data entry operators to better performance, as their work (outcome) is highly observable (Eisenhardt, 1989; Govindarajan and Fisher, 1990; Kirsch, 1996). Similarly, Lim et al. (2011) describe a case where behaviour controls were applied to improve a co-located vendor’s performance, but instead caused the revolt of vendor staff. Another conundrum is why controllers in practice do not enact the portfolio of controls recommended by theory. For example, Crisp (2003) found distinct virtual project groups assigned the same task would enact unique portfolios of control. Negotiated order theory helps explain these anomalies by shifting attention to the social context within which controls are enacted.

Recent research has suggested that understanding how controls are enacted (i.e. shaped and re-appropriated) within a social context may help close the gap between theory and practice (Wiener et al., 2016). However, the elements of control enactment that facilitate this understanding are not well understood.

## Controls as negotiated orders

We suggest that the gap between theory and practice may be partly addressed by exploring controls as negotiated social structures using negotiated order theory. It has long been known that social control is seldom, if ever, a purely rational enterprise. This was the essence of Weber’s ‘‘iron cage’’ (Weber, 1930) and the heart of Simon’s concept of bounded rationality (Simon, 1957).

Negotiated order theory is one of several interpretivist theories that argue social structures are not stable (Kling, 1980). Instead, social structures, such as control, are constantly negotiated by interested parties (Maines and Charlton, 1985; Strauss, 1978; Strauss et al., 1963). The theory explores how work processes both constitute and are situated within organizational dynamics and structures (Bechky, 2011). A negotiated order perspective views control not as a permanent enactment by the controller, but as something created through negotiation between the controlee and controller within the social and organizational context (Maines and Charlton, 1985; Strauss, 1978; Strauss et al., 1963). There are four elements negotiated order theory would suggest one should examine: context, controlee, controller and negotiation.

## Context

One cannot enact controls in a vacuum. Control theory recognizes that controls are enacted based on ‘‘pre-existing mechanisms’’ (Kirsch, 1997). However, we argue that such mechanisms both serve as controls the controller can select from, but also constrain controller choice. Context interacts with controls in three ways. First, context shapes controlee behaviour (Danziger and Kraemer, 1985; Kling, 1977) and hence the enactment of controls. Many controls persist across time. Employment contracts focused on outcome and behaviour control cannot be revised the next day. The rewriting of contracts is often expensive and requires the mutual consent of all parties involved. Cultures that underlie social control cannot be changed overnight (Kotter, 1996; Kotter and Cohen, 2002). Thus, controllers cannot enact or repeal controls as they see fit. A ‘‘legacy’’ control (and therefore part of the organizational context) may influence controlee behaviour long after it has lost its usefulness.

Second, in contexts with high ambiguity (e.g. many IS projects), tradition provides a foundation for selecting an initial portfolio of controls (Heimer, 1985; Kirsch, 1997). Thus, the social and historical context of organizational controls is crucial for understanding control formation. These traditions are the manifestations of the social capital that has been built over time through structure, shared cognition and social relationships that form the basis for clan controls.

Finally, control (or indeed any enactment) can change the organizational and social context (Bechky, 2011). Barker (1993), for example, documented a situation where clan controls successfully controlled employees but simultaneously created a family-unfriendly atmosphere, the opposite of what was intended.

## Controlee

Purposeful action, including controls, often leads to unintended consequences (Merton, 1936). Controls may malfunction because the controlees have agency and because the controlee’s interpretation may not match that of the controller. For example, fines enacted at daycare centres to deter parents from picking up children late resulted in parents picking up children even later because of the meanings that parents ascribed to the fines (Levitt and Dubner, 2004).

One cause of unpredicted behaviour is agency – the ability of humans to make choices despite the existence of constraining structures, including control structures (Giddens, 1976, 1979, 1984).

Agency, in turn, is impacted by the controlee’s interpretation of control. In the daycare example above (Levitt and Dubner, 2004), parents interpreted fines as compensation for the cost the daycare centre incurred for taking care of children after hours, rather than as a deterrent. This ascribed meaning led to parents choosing to delay child pickup and pay the fine.

## Controller

As with the controlee, the controller has agency and interprets the organizational context and controls. However, controller agency and interpretation are shaped by two additional factors. First, the controller is as much a controlee as a controller because both are subject to social and contextual factors such as the existing structures, shared cognitions and relationships (Strauss, 1978). Blau (1955), for example, describes how law enforcement officials developed a culture where bribes were refused. However, while police officers could use the fact a bribe attempt was made to pressure businesses to change, a police officer who reported bribes would be ostracized by co-workers.

Second, pre-existing mechanisms serve as possible controls and constrain choices. The controller typically possesses institutional power, which not only gives the controller the ability to enact controls, but likewise constrains the controller’s ability to enact controls (Fine, 1984). For example, a controller cannot punish a controlee for not doing what the controlee is not expected to do. However, because not all work tasks can be specified, sometimes controlees use ‘‘work to rule’’ behaviours – following rules even when they are organizationally detrimental – to ‘‘punish’’ controllers for being overbearing (Strauss, 1978). The controller cannot punish controlees for working to rule, because they are doing ‘‘what they are supposed to do.’

## Negotiation

Because controls are social structures negotiated within a social context and are subject to interpretation, they are inherently unstable. Hence, there is an ongoing dialog between controllers and controlees about what controls are, and how they should work (subject to the social and organizational context within which controls are shaped and re-appropriated). The controlee must not only agree to be controlled, but must agree on how the control works, with the degree of controlee agreement always subject to some coercion (Li and Edwards, 2008; Ram et al., 2007).

Thus, while control theory focuses on controls unilaterally enacted by controllers, in reality such controls represent a negotiated order (Maines and Charlton, 1985; Strauss, 1978; Strauss et al., 1963). Table 1 summarizes the complementary nature of control theory and negotiated order theory.

## Research method

Given our use of negotiated order theory as a theoretical lens (Maines and Charlton, 1985; Strauss, 1978; Strauss et al., 1963), we used interpretive case study research (Klein and Myers, 1999) to study control. We conducted our research in a large New Zealand agricultural distributor’s IT department (client) and one of its IT vendors, an Asian vendor with a New Zealand subsidiary.

## Site description

The client is involved in all stages of the supply chain, from collection at farms to transformation of the raw product to a commodity and trade on commodity markets. The company produces more than 1/4th of the world’s international trade in this product.

IT systems are instrumental to the client’s operations and include systems for planning vehicle routes for farm collection, testing product quality, warehousing and managing global trade. Although the client manages most new IS projects in-house, the company employs a multivendor outsourcing model, with particular vendors dedicated to handling separate aspects of client operations such as infrastructure, network and applications.

The main vendor, a large multinational CMM-5 certified IT service provider, earns more than USD 5 billion globally. The New Zealand subsidiary manages the client’s ongoing operations and applications including custom applications and large enterprise systems. The vendor is the preferred manpower supplier for client projects, but the client may approach other vendors if the vendor cannot provide personnel. From henceforth, we use ‘‘vendor’’ to refer to the vendor team in New Zealand (vendor NZ subsidiary)

plus vendor elements in Asia supporting the New Zealand team. We use ‘‘vendor HQ’’ to refer to the vendor headquarters located in Asia.

The vendor is located in the same office building as the client. The vendor services the client and other New Zealand clients. Work is done both by onshore and offshore consultants, although onshore consultants provide clients with their primary source of contact. The vendor/client engagement began in 2007. When we entered the field site in March 2011, the client opted to renew the vendor’s 5-year contract and the client and vendor were renegotiating contract details. Governance and control were thus important issues in both parties’ minds. Our research project was seen as being of potential practical relevance to the company.

## Data collection

Consistent with principles of interpretive case study research (Klein and Myers, 1999), we obtained data from multiple sources including the client and vendor. We were given access to meetings, meeting structures, reporting relations and portions of the contract relating to vendor governance. Also, as the client has significant impact on the New Zealand economy, it is regularly featured in public documents such as newspapers. While we had access to the minutes of meetings, we discovered several contractually mandated meetings had not taken place.

We interviewed individuals involved with vendor governance including the Client CIO and his direct reports up to two levels down. These included the client’s contract expert, vendor executive sponsor, and quality assurance head. We also interviewed the vendor’s client engagement manager, his subordinates, and the leads in charge of each vendor functional silo. We ceased soliciting interviews after we were not generating new findings, controls, or insights, i.e. when theoretical saturation was achieved (Myers and Newman, 2007). Table 2 summarizes the interviews conducted.

Most interviews were at least 1 h long, and most were tape recorded; some interviewees refused permission to record interviews, in which case we took notes. There were certain general questions asked of all interviewees, including their role in the client/vendor governance relationship and a description of the relationship strengths and weaknesses.

## Data analysis

We reviewed collected data to identify enacted controls, both formal and informal. After identifying controls, we attempted to identify deviant controls. A control was defined as deviant if:

• It did not produce the desired result, or the result expected from control theory.

• It produced unanticipated side-effects.

• It had been abandoned and hence worked contrary to expectations.

Given the current contract revision and governance review, interviewees were quite willing to discuss deviant controls. We subjected such to a deeper analysis, identifying from interviews why controls produced the effects they did and what subsequent remedies arose. In keeping with negotiated order theory, each control was analysed as follows:

• Initial context We explored the broader social, organizational and interorganizational context that surrounded the control. This included the history of each organization and their joint histories, and the prior experiences of controllers and controlees.

• Controlee perspective We attempted to understand why controlees did not behave as expected.

• Controller perspective We attempted to identify the controller’s intent. In keeping with our recognition that modern work is complex and dynamic, we were sensitized to the idea that the controlee need not be solely controlled by a single, hierarchical controller.

• Negotiation We explored how controller and controlee interacted to change the initial context and enact the following context. We also observed the outcome of the negotiation.

We present four vignettes that illustrate the importance of integrating control theory and negotiated order theory and identify the social forces that affect controllers and controlees when enacting control.

## Findings

In 2007, the client opened a tender for vendor support of its code-base (application support). These included hundreds of custom applications for managing such diverse tasks as the client’s transport fleet, factory systems, and quality assurance to three separate enterprise systems. Consistent with control theory, an Asian vendor with an international customer base was selected, mainly because of client access to vendor management which would enable shared cognition and clan control.

We had better relationships and access to the executive in [vendor] than we did with the other companies. We had access all the way up to the chairman. (Client)

The client encouraged internal IT staff to transfer to the vendor. The client recognized that cultural differences might create difficulties in the outsourcing arrangement and this transfer of staff might ameliorate some of these difficulties.

The vendor now employs more than 200 full-time employees specifically to support the client both in New Zealand and elsewhere. In addition, the vendor draws upon personnel in the wider vendor talent pool to supplement on an as-need basis. Within New Zealand, vendor employees are spread across the client’s multiple IT centres and work side by side with client IT staff, which control theory suggests would strengthen relationships and social capital which should further enable clan control. Vendor leadership at the engagement level spends considerable time travelling between various New Zealand centres and the vendor headquarters in Asia.

The client hired a third-party specialist to draft the contract. This specialist drafted a ‘‘tight’’ contract mandating governance structures and adherence to particular performance guidelines, a form of behavioural control.

The contract was originally very complicated and very one sided. The [Client] had a lot of rights – too much. (Client)

The contract has two main parts. In the first, the vendor receives a fixed sum to maintain the existing code-base. On paper, this sum diminishes each year – supposedly incentivizing the vendor to be efficient through outcome controls. In practice, the amount has increased, because more appli cations and enhancements are added each year.

In the second, the vendor has ‘‘first offer’’ privileges to supply manpower (both onshore and remote) for new IT projects. Manpower must be supplied at an agreed rate card. Project management and leadership are principally performed in-house by the client.

Today, the client is generally happy with the vendor. However, although the agreement reflected practices that are consistent with those outlined in control theory, some did not work as intended and, consistent with negotiated order theory, were later shaped and re-appropriated within the social and organizational context. Four vignettes illustrate this negotiated order process.

## Vignette 1: rate cards

## Initial context

The contract for vendor project personnel benefited the client at four levels. First, the vendor’s rates were substantially lower than equivalent New Zealand rates. For rare skill sets (e.g. SAP expert in Global Trade Management), the rates were lower than equivalent rates elsewhere in the world. Second, because vendor personnel were involved, it was relatively easy to transition a new application or project to the vendor support team. Third, the vendor possessed consultants with very rare expertise the client needed (e.g. SAP expert in Global Trade Management). Finally, interacting with the vendor’s skilled personnel across projects improved the client’s IT skill.

We’ve got very good capability… You know there are maybe 200 people in the world that have got [specific enterprise system skill] … We’ve got 6 people … But they are very difficult to find… And when you start competing with the UK and US market, why would you come to New Zealand? (Vendor)

Unfortunately, the vendor consistently refused requests for anyone other than low-skilled people for two reasons. First, vendor headquarters had to approve the deployment of individuals to New Zealand and it allocated individuals based on profitability based on a US dollar standard.

This pre-existing outcome control by the vendor headquarters proved problematic because the New Zealand dollar floats freely and the central bank has intervened in the exchange rate only three times since the currency floated in 1985. It is also one of the ten most traded currencies in the world. The New Zealand dollar exchange rate fluctuates significantly.

At contract inception, the New Zealand dollar was in one of its low cycles. Thus, on paper the vendor’s New Zealand operations were underperforming.

What they were doing was they were revaluing their book every month on a spot basis so offshore they were becoming very concerned that they weren’t making any money, but it was all actually about exchange rate, and not about whether the core service was good or bad. (Client)

Second, especially for senior individuals, the defined rates were too low.

The rate difference [between junior and senior] was less than 10 dollars. If you have a guy with 1 year experience, 5 years, experience, 8 years experience, 12 years experience, 20 years experience. [The] rate card was not reflecting this. So we always try to find junior people. And we’re not able to get the right outcome. (Vendor)

This created a problem for both organizations. For the vendor, this meant many unfilled revenue generating opportunities. For the client, this meant they were sourcing for skills at a rate above what they could get from the vendor.

There were periods where that was as high as several hundred thousand dollars a month in lost opportunity. (Client)

## Controlee perspective

The controlee obviously wanted to deploy more skilled persons on client projects. However, the pricing model was not working for the vendor.

For example, we require 5 people, but we’re only funding 0.5… If they had 5 people in place, they’d be losing money. So, they couldn’t do that, so eventually we get really poor performance. (Client)

## Controller perspective

The client wanted more skilled persons from the vendor working on their projects. Beyond price, the client found that vendor personnel did a very good job.

I think [vendor] by their nature are very hard workers, and can churn out a lot of work. They actually do it a lot better than we ever did when we had it in-house. (Client)

## Negotiation

Because they had developed shared cognition and relationships, the client and the vendors in New Zealand pointed out the currency issue to vendor headquarters, resulting in the New Zealand operations being evaluated in local currency. The elimination of accounting problems meant headquarters was more willing to provide resources to support New Zealand operations.

And now we can invest in other areas. We used to get kicked. We were always bleeding. Now [headquarters is] more open to investment. (Vendor)

Also, the rate cards have been revised to be more generous, so outcome controls are still in place, but they have been adapted to better reflect the local context.

The rate card has been revised and the rates have been increased by 50 percent. (Client)

Note that while expert consultants from the vendor became 50% more expensive, they remained cheaper than equivalent consultants in New Zealand. We had some access to financial information in this research project. One puzzle we discovered was that the vendor filled approximately 70% of the client requests, but only received 30% of the revenues. This was explained to us:

We are an [Asian]-based company and we provide a lot of our resources from [Asia]. And they are at a lower cost structure to our competitors in the New Zealand market. And we’re talking the difference is we might put someone in for \$1000 a day, and our competitors would put the same skilled resource into the client at \$3000 a day. (Vendor)

Finally, reflecting strong social capital and clan control, the client has been active in promoting the vendor to other New Zealand businesses to improve vendor performance and hence make it more attractive to its Asian HQ.

The more successful they become, the stronger their footprint is in New Zealand, which gives them a better delivery machine, which we win from anyway. So there’s a strategic reason for us wanting them to be successful. (Client)

## Mini-analysis

Our vignette illustrates a number of control issues. First, it illustrates the complex network interactions of modern control enactments. Here, the controlee is beholden to two controllers, the client, and vendor headquarters in Asia. Both controllers enact controls that together create a dysfunctional situation. Mindlessly adopting pre-existing outcome controls, the vendor headquarters rewards or punishes based on revenue in US dollars. Unfortunately, because the vendor earns New Zealand dollars, this is subject to exchange rate risk. The client provides a reward that is too low for the vendor to claim. For the vendor to provide skilled experts to the client, both controllers had to revise their control portfolio.

In control theory, this idea of revising the control portfolio is called the ‘‘dynamics’’ of control (Cardinal et al., 2004; Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 2004). Control dynamics research has mainly focused on how modes of control change over an engagement. However, this vignette demonstrates changes in control independent of the mode of control. The outcome control mode of the rate card is appropriate given the highly observable outcome (i.e. whether a person with a given skill level is given work). Similarly, the vendor headquarter’s evaluation of revenue is likewise appropriate. The problem is in the detailed elements of the specific control enactment.

This vignette also reveals how controllers not only enact controls, but also negotiate with controlees to manage controls enacted by others. The client helps the vendor profit from other clients which makes the vendor HQ more willing to deploy rare expert skill sets to New Zealand, and hence the client – an unusual outcome of the vendor’s profitability controls is that they ‘‘control’’ the client. This kind of negotiated order was enabled by the existing trust and social capital that had been developed between controller and controlee.

## Vignette 2: helpdesk process controls

## Organizational context

The client performed the IT application support function for many years. When outsourcing began, vendor employees replaced client employees. Control theory suggests that when controlee behaviour is observable and the controller understands the work, the controller should enact behaviour/ process controls – even when outcomes are simultaneously highly observable (Kirsch, 1996). The client’s behaviour controls included procedural instructions on how to perform the task, an application of a pre-existing control mechanism.

[We made them] run the relationship and do the work the way we used to do the work, as opposed to the way they could most efficiently do the work… What it did is actually build a lot of inefficiency into the relationship. (Client)

For example, the client suggested routine problems like resetting user passwords was best done with tools.

We should be focused on telling them what they should be doing and measuring how well they’re doing it, [but], we were diving into how they should do it as well. We were kind of ‘‘Oh no, you should have these tools to do this, and you should be monitoring this and doing this.’’ (Client)

Some might argue IT vendor research suggests outcome control rather than behaviour control is best applied on vendors. However, this is because vendor behaviour is difficult to observe and/or interpret (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana and Keil, 2007, 2009). In this case, the vendor helpdesk person sits alongside the client who knows the business.

## Controlee perspective

The vendor was reluctant to point out flaws in client governance or work processes because of concerns that this might offend the client, suggesting that the vendor’s behaviour reflected ascribed meanings. Thus, for a time, the vendor was willing to use client processes, but did not adopt self-monitoring and self-rewarding control mechanisms.

Cause [when they say yes] we think they’re saying yes they agree with us, but actually all they’re saying is ‘I hear what you’re saying.’ (Client)

However, the vendor was also unhappy, mainly because of process inefficiency. High-value vendor employees were performing mundane support tasks like re-setting passwords and creating user accounts. They were not free to perform high-value tasks like developing new systems. Also, highvalue employees were dissatisfied with the work they were doing because it did not provide them with a challenge.

People with 8–10 years of experience were doing a repetitive job. For this kind of work, you don’t need that kind of capability. (Vendor)

## Controller perspective

Because the vendor did things the way the client wanted, rather than leveraging off their distinct capabilities, the level of support achieved was comparable to when helpdesk support was done in-house, i.e. not satisfactory. Tickets were not resolved quickly enough, and important (but not critical) issues were not resolved in a timely fashion.

However, the client could not perceive a way to improve the process, because they were locked into a New Zealand perspective – a context where labour is expensive, there is a minimum wage and trained people are relatively scarce. Maximizing productivity meant maximizing productivity per individual labour unit, hence the client’s insistence on tools.

## Negotiation

As the relationship developed, the vendor suggested they had better ways of solving many problems and they could improve client processes. The client relaxed the process controls. To solve the helpdesk problem, the vendor identified and documented issues in a knowledgebase. Understanding the Asian context, they then hired large numbers of low and semiskilled people in the Asian country. Their job was to receive customer calls forwarded from New Zealandbased vendor employees, look up issues in the knowledgebase, and follow knowledgebase instructions to resolve them. Only when issues cannot be solved in this way are more highly skilled individuals involved.

[Asian country] is very different from New Zealand in how they work… They can go out and get 20 new staff within an hour, because just the employee base there is just so huge. We … just can’t understand that, but it might be the most cost-effective or best way for them to do things. (Client)

The process controls were ineffective because the client was used to the New Zealand context where labour is expensive. In this Asian country, labour is cheap. It was more economical and efficient to hire massive numbers of people to solve low-level issues, thereby freeing up skilled labour.

## Mini-analysis

In control theory, the appropriate control to employ is based on task, role, knowledge and skill (Kirsch, 1997). The vignette illustrates how each of these three factors is socially constructed. From a task and role perspective, the client conceived of helpdesk support as a single person performing helpdesk work with tools. The vendor considered the work distributed, requiring multiple people in a variety of roles.

The IS department of the agricultural distributor had the knowledge and skill to perform the helpdesk task – they had performed it for many years. However, they did not have the knowledge and skill to perform that task for a high demand volume situation. In addition, the agricultural distributor came from a context where each worker was individually expensive. This meant work was best addressed by incorporating technology to multiply individual work capability. The vendor, in contrast, came from a context where workers were plentiful and cheap. The solution to volume problems was hiring massive numbers of unskilled personnel.

In this vignette, knowledge and skill relate not so much to task, but access to distinct labour markets. This raises the question of how one can ascertain what task, role, knowledge and skill are independent of context.

It could be argued the initial control failure arose because outcome controls should have been chosen in-lieu of behaviour controls. After all, outcome controls generally work better with vendors (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Nidumolu and Subramani, 2003/2004). However, such an argument produces another dilemma: why did the supposedly rational client deliberately select a non-optimal portfolio of controls? One answer could be in the way the client perceived the vendor. Prior research on vendor control explores vendors as providers of projects, including project management capability (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003; Nidumolu and Subramani, 2003/2004). In this relationship, the vendor was a supplier of expert human resources. Controls were enacted based on this worldview and were only changed when client and vendor renegotiated the control.

## Vignette 3: contract terms

## Organizational context

First time contracts generally favour the vendor, as vendors engage in many more contracts than clients (Chua et al., 2012; Saunders et al., 1997). To ensure it received a good deal, the client commissioned a contract from a specialist in IT outsourcing contracts. The contract included a strong set of controls and governance structures drawn from the specialist’s set of pre-existing controls, but previously not used by the client. Also, the vendor viewed the client as a gateway into the New Zealand market and was eager to sign the contract, even though the governance structures were so strong.

It was the classic scenario, where obviously the sales staff were involved in putting the contract together and would do anything to get the contract, agree to everything. (Client)

## Controlee perspective

The vendor believed that the contract provisions, if taken literally, created distractions and diverted energy away from critical things the client and vendor could work on to improve operations. This ascribed meaning had important implications.

So it became quite an operational type contract… What I’d like to see more is [client], where are you going and what intent and behaviors are you looking for… And to try and remove a lot of that small noise and a lot of the small operational and administration task. (Vendor)

## Controller perspective

The contracted provisions were so onerous that both parties agreed the standards were unachievable. Also, the contract included numerous governance mechanisms and meetings the client was unable or unwilling to enforce, because they required personnel and resources the client was unwilling to expend. The client is an agricultural cooperative owned and operated by farmers. Its primary business requires client personnel (including IS staff) to deal with farmers, including family farms. Thus, the client relies heavily on clan control for governance.

We work through people. Our process discipline is not high. We’re better at reaching out. … Formal processes and control description not so good at. (Client)

## Negotiation

On reviewing the contract terms, the client found them highly bureaucratic so the controls were shaped and reappropriated within the social and organizational context. Drawing on their social capital and clan control, the client and vendor reached a gentleman’s agreement not to enforce them.

Some of those service levels we actually decided between [vendor] and [client], ‘‘Hey, that’s not something we would report on now. That might be a later day, or it’s not a monthly report, it’s actually quarterly report, or it’s more operational, not service level.’’ (Client)

In addition, both parties agreed not to enforce many contractual governance mechanisms and procedures. When we first entered the field site, we requested minutes of meetings specified in the contract only to discover:

Due to internal changes … the Management Committee and Service Delivery Committee meetings have not been taking place therefore there is no minutes. (Client)

## One client representative admitted:

This is [points to book] half of the contract, and there’s another book like this… So to follow every single process in a document that large to the T is never going to happen. There’s intent and then there’s reality. (Client).

While neither party followed the prescribed contractual governance clauses, these clauses remained, primarily because of the cost that would be associated with changing them.

You don’t really want to do a contract variation… That’s quite onerous, and quite expensive actually… If you’ve got [law firm] at 500 bucks an hour. (Client)

When we left the case site, the two organizations were negotiating a new contract with a very different mindset. The client and vendor were drafting it with legal support from a third-party law firm. The new contract orientation has less emphasis on formal control and tries to strike a balance between the needs of the client and vendor.

If it’s not win–win, then it’s really lose–lose… The new contract will not involve [a contract expert]. (Client)

## Mini-analysis

This vignette illustrates the danger of taking a control mechanism (e.g., the contract) at face value. Many contract clauses were included without careful consideration of their implications on the client and vendor. Instead, clauses originated from a boilerplate provided by a third party. While the contract had numerous standard control mechanisms, these mechanisms were not actually enforced as written, but were adapted over time to better fit the context. This adaptation process was enabled by social capital and shared cognition.

The vignette also highlights four critical elements about control not well explored in the literature. First, the vignette emphasizes that beyond notions of task, role, knowledge and skills, the control is a social structure negotiated within a social context and the organizational makeup of the client shapes controls that can be enacted. As an agricultural collective, the client is more comfortable with social relationships than it is with contracts so the enacted controls were more consistent with clan mechanism of control than with outcome or behavioural mechanisms.

Also, the vignette demonstrates that control mechanisms are expensive to enforce, and controllers can choose not to enforce them if they are viewed as not worth the cost. The client was not prepared to read multiple reports and hold formal meetings.

Second, the vignette illustrates that controls that are not mindfully or purposefully enacted may lead to unintended consequences or spur later negotiations to provide a better fit with the context. Many controls were established from a standard legal boilerplate developed to manage other client/ vendor relationships. This is especially relevant because contracts are often a proxy for formal control/governance (Goo and Huang, 2008; Goo et al., 2009; Gopal and Koka, 2012; Gopal et al., 2003; Lee and Cavusgil, 2006; Poppo and Zenger, 2002; Ryall and Sampson, 2009; Zheng et al., 2008). Our research highlights that when contracts are used as proxies, one must determine whether contractual terms are actually considered and used by the parties and how the parties interpret the meaning of the contractual terms.

Third, existing research has generally focused on a control’s ability to affect the controlee. The vignette highlights that otherwise effective controls may not be enacted, because the controller has no capability to enact them. Capability may refer to competence. For example, the controller had little competence in bureaucratic (behaviour) controls, but had competence in social (clan) controls. It can also refer to available controller resources. For example, the reports and meetings specified in the contract were desirable and ‘‘best practice.’’ However, the controller found attending meetings and reviewing reports costly, and thus decreased the periods of review. Thus, when controls are purposefully enacted, the controller weighs two concerns – the benefit of the control and the cost the controller must bear.

Finally, the vignette demonstrates how controlees influence the controller decision to enforce a control. The last quote demonstrates that controllers have to strike a careful ‘‘win–win’’ balance. Onerous controls that punish the controlee excessively do not work.

## Vignette 4: crisis reporting

## Initial context

The governance structure between client and vendor was not 1:1. A number of client employees could request status updates of the vendor, especially during a crisis.

In a rightsized organization, there’s a lot of people in governance and oversight roles. [Here] there’s no 1-to-1 faceoffs. 10 people claim [vendor manager] is their [faceoff]. … It will always be a M-to-M mapping. (Client)

This created problems for the vendor, especially during crises. For example, when critical applications went down, vendor operations staff would be inundated with client requests for a status update. Operations staff would be distracted from solving the problem by the need to update. Also, when client executives talked directly to operations staff, they would give a factually and technically correct update, i.e., the problem was being investigated, and they were uncertain how long the issue would take to be resolved. That client executives did not receive information they could use to structure operations (e.g. how long the system would be down) increased executive anxiety, and hence, monitoring. Finally, in the absence of a solution, executives enacted a behavioural control mechanism and demanded that the vendor employ a defined, structured process to investigate faults. Unfortunately, true fault rectification often requires an iterative process of testing and observation, which is not amenable to a structured process.

There is a severity 1 incident. The delivery team is working to fix the issue. Multiple stakeholders want to know. Poor chap the delivery guy: his mind is on how to fix the issue. But multiple guys want to know. It’s not quite working. (Vendor)

## Controlee perspective

The vendor, of course, wanted to fix the problem as quickly as possible. They were penalized if faults were not rectified in a certain amount of time, a commonly used outcome control mechanism. As a result, having to spend time fielding multiple calls was unhelpful.

## Controller perspective

When a critical application went down, it impacted multiple groups across the client. Some people would be unable to do work, while others would be activated to work around the problem. To the client, it was important to know how long the critical application would be down to continue business as usual.

## Negotiation

To handle this issue, the vendor instituted a change in structure to maintain social capital and created a new relationship manager role that was not specified in the original contract and was not chargeable to the client. One duty of the relationship manager was to field client executive requests for updates. The operations group would update the relationship manager as and when changes occurred.

Say one of [client’s] biggest applications goes down… Then we’ll create two different groups. There’s a technical group… that will focus on the fastest resolution, and restoration of service… And my role will focus on the communication to [client] … directly to management and that sort of stuff (vendor).

We found this aspect of the job interesting because the relationship manager existed to block monitoring of vendor operations staff and resist the client’s behaviour control while operations staff was rectifying a problem.

In a [critical situation], the typical way of working of Asian people… If you don’t find this is concrete, you will not go and say this is the problem. What we learned was, still tell them it will be ready in 30 min. This is something the [relationship manager] model helps there. See this kind of concern. [The relationship manager says] we will email during the incident. [He] says I will set up a conference call. [But to the operations person] If you have not really looked into it, what are you going to tell? (Vendor)

## Mini-analysis

During crises or when existing controls do not appear to work, a common action on the part of the controller is to enact greater control (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003), in this case, increased oversight. However, the greater control by the client created a drop in overall process efficiency.

All controls rely on the dissemination of information because all controls require observation of the controlee by the controller. In many cases, disseminating the information is costly. Furthermore, in this case, the information required (e.g. time to resolution) was beyond the capability of the controlee to produce. Thus, enacted controls are not only costly on controllers (as illustrated in the previous vignette), but also the controlee. Controlee cost can potentially impact the task the controlee performs.

Also, the increased observation by the controller was not organizationally rational. It was less efficient to have several executives independently request updates than to have one. Unfortunately, the existing situation resulted in a tragedy of the commons effect.

To resolve this issue, the controlee enacted what we call an alternative structure, i.e. the controlee enacted a mechanism designed to overcome deficiencies in the malfunctioning control. The controlee created a role that, during emergencies, was designed to be monitored and to produce the information the controller wanted, even if the information had little basis in reality, to maintain the relationship and preserve social capital. It should be emphasized that an alternative structure need not be about organizational resistance (Ago´ cs, 1997; Prasad and Prasad, 2000). The one described in the vignette existed explicitly to help work get done.

The control literature has spent very little effort examining the cost of controls on controlees or on alternate structures, and yet, they are omnipresent. In many organizations, workarounds and shadow systems combine with other common behaviours to bypass or overcome controls that impede our ability to perform work (e.g., red tape). That alternate structures exist partly explains why control enactments do not lead to expected results – control enactments lead to alternate structures that alter the control’s desired effects.

## Discussion

This article has provided an interpretive case study of control between a client and one of its IT vendors. We especially focused on deviant controls, analysing why controls produced unexpected effects and what subsequent remedies were implemented. Our theorization on social structures suggests that beyond the controller’s intent, other forces shape control enactments and behaviour. These forces are the context, the controller’s and controlee’s interpretation, a negotiation between the controller and controlee, and finally the control itself. Our findings support our theorizing, but also extend them with new insights. Table 3 summarizes our findings and extensions to control theory.

## Context

We postulated one reason behaviours did not align with control theory was because the nuanced impact of context was not initially taken into account. Specifically, (1) controls persist across time, (2) tradition will often be employed in environments with high uncertainty, and (3) controls can reshape the context. Vignette 1 presents an example of controls persisting across time. The default control applied by the vendor’s HQ was to evaluate financial performance in US dollars. This control was wholly inappropriate because of uncontrolled fluctuation in the New Zealand Dollar.

Vignettes 2 and 3 similarly highlight how controls are often applied as a result of tradition, rather than because the controller mindfully considers the control beneficial. In vignette 2, the client hired the vendor to improve its helpdesk performance. To the client’s surprise, the vendor performed at the same level as the client because the client enacted the same (principally behavioural) controls on the vendor as it enacted on its own workers. Once controls were relaxed and the vendor was allowed to manage the helpdesk its own way, performance improved. In vignette 3, entire suites of controls, including various meetings, reports and key performance indices were included into the contract even though neither the client nor the vendor thought those were good ideas – simply because the company recruited to draft the contract had those controls in its standard boilerplate.

## Controlee interpretation

We theorized that controls are not solely enacted by controllers. Instead, consistent with negotiated order theory, controlees have a hand in shaping controls. An interesting related finding is how negotiations between controller and controlee actually play out. Vignettes 2 and 3 present examples of negotiations expected by theory. In vignette 2, the vendor negotiates with the client to drop the behaviour controls because the vendor uses an alternate procedure leveraging workers from its home country. In vignette 3, the vendor negotiates with the client to eliminate meetings and reports that both parties feel are dysfunctional. Vignettes 1 and 4 demonstrate unusual negotiation – the vendor negotiates with the client to help it change the vendor HQ’s way of controlling the vendor – the controlee negotiates with one controller to help it influence another controller.

Vignette 4 especially presents an unusual form of negotiation we call ‘‘alternative structures.’’ The relationship manager position serves to deliberately block monitoring (i.e. control) by the client. The vendor developed an alternative structure to work around the dysfunctional elements of an enacted control, especially ones that cannot be easily rescinded. The client’s organizational structure did not permit it to reduce the number of monitoring personnel. The vendor created a new structure to work around the client’s limitations. Other vignettes contain alternate structures. In vignette 1, the client could not directly get the vendor HQ to stop evaluating its New Zealand subsidiary. Consequently, the client helps the vendor obtain new business to boost its bottom line. In vignette 2, changing the contract terms was felt to be an unnecessary expense, so a tacit agreement was developed to ignore contract provisions. These alternative structures provide yet another piece in the puzzle – control may be enacted, but not result in the expected performance level because controllers and/or controlees enact alternate structures to make it so.

## Controller interpretation

We theorized that controllers did not enact controls in a deterministic way, but rather based on ascribed meaning and on their interpretation of the context. We indeed observed how the controller interpretation shaped the enactment of control. However, more importantly, we observed how constraints in the controller’s perspective led to suboptimal control enactments. Vignettes 1 and 2 both illustrate how knowledge in one control context is often dangerous when applied to another. The vendor HQ had previously successfully controlled its international subsidiaries via measuring revenues and profits in US dollar terms, but contextual factors shaped the enacted control. Given its varied subsidiaries (including in the US, UK, and Singapore), the vendor HQ felt this control would work in its New Zealand subsidiary. Similarly, consistent with control theory, in vignette 2, the client’s prior experience managing its service desk was employed to develop behaviour controls over the vendor. Both vignettes illustrate how these controls prevented the vendor from functioning in an optimal way.

Vignette 4 illustrates a different kind of controller limitation – the ‘‘tragedy of the commons.’’ The vignette highlights how controllers are often simultaneously controlees of others. When IT systems failed, various stakeholder groups put pressure on their IT representatives who in turn, contacted the vendor to get (frequent) status updates. However, the need for frequent reporting prevented the vendor from rectifying the problem. Furthermore, structural constraints in the client’s organization prevented the client from fixing this problem. Instead, the vendor addressed it by creating the relationship manager role.

Control theory has recognized that controls change over the course of a controller-controlee relationship, but has generally argued that the amount of control enacted should increase when controls fail (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003). Consistent with negotiated order theory, this research demonstrates the opposite effect. The client relaxed the portfolio of controls because it impeded both client and vendor performance. In vignette 1, the vendor HQ changed the control to reflect the different context and no longer compared the New Zealand subsidiary against others in US dollars. Also, the client recognized that paying the vendor a higher personnel rate would better achieve the client’s outcomes. In vignette 2, behaviour controls only allowed the vendor to reach the client’s former level of performance. Finally, in vignette 3, drawing on social capital and shared cognition, the client relaxed the portfolio of controls because the portfolio impeded client performance – the client had to spend time attending meetings and reviewing vendor performance rather than getting its own work done.

## Control

Finally, our vignettes present interesting empirical examples of how the enactment of controls can impact the controller and controlee in ways behaviour control theory does not expect but that are consistent with negotiated order theory. Vignette 1 demonstrates how controls can interact with each other and with the context to produce unanticipated and unwanted consequences – the combination of the vendor HQ’s revenue control combined with the strict rates encouraged the vendor to not staff client projects. Vignette 2 illustrates how enacting a behaviour control based on common mechanisms can cause performance to decrease; the vendor followed a process that was not optimally efficient because the vendor did not want to offend the client

The control literature has also focused on the selection of controls from multiple modes. Our research demonstrates control failure despite appropriate modes of control: in vignette 1, the nature of the exchange rate and rate card controls, in vignette 3, the number and diversity of meetings, and in vignette 4, the multitude of controllers made those controls dysfunctional. A social perspective on control helps identify the lure of these pre-existing common mechanisms and highlight the importance of negotiation in shaping and re-appropriating these controls within the social context.

Furthermore, the control literature argues for the purposeful application of controls based on behaviour and outcome observability and task programmability (Kirsch, 1996, 2004; Kirsch et al., 2002, 2010). While Kirsch (1997) recognizes that controls are sometimes enacted based on a controller’s previous roles and knowledge, Kirsch does not explicitly suggest that the controller’s roles and knowledge can be mindlessly applied. Our vignettes empirically document the mindless application of controls (Butler and Gray, 2006; Swanson and Ramiller, 2004). Vignette 1 illustrates the application of an exchange rate control that did not make sense in a country where the exchange rate fluctuated wildly. Similarly, vignette 3 demonstrates how controls from a boilerplate contract can be enacted without consideration of the client’s own situation.

Thus, our research highlights the socially negotiated nature of controls and that factors that influence controls are themselves tied to the social context. Our research illustrates that task, role, knowledge and skill are not anchored in an objective reality, but instead are socially constructed.

We also argue that beyond the objective criteria that influence the control decision articulated by extant control theory, the controller and controlee socially negotiate the appropriate controls. Consistent with negotiated order theory, each vignette demonstrates that the specific, nuanced nature of the controller-controlee relationship must be understood to identify and shape the enactment of the appropriate control mechanism. In vignette 1, issues with rate differentials of IT experts in New Zealand and around the world, and the unique characteristics of the New Zealand dollar, influenced which controls were appropriate. In vignette 2, the New Zealand vs. Asian nature of the client/ vendor suggested the appropriate control portfolio. In vignette 3, it was the client’s background as an agricultural cooperative based on trust and relationships that caused it to reject the formal meetings in the contract. We thus suggest that an appreciation of how controls are shaped by human agents and the social context contributes to our understanding of control .

In the traditional perspective on control, increasing control leads to improved performance (Henderson and Lee, 1992). Consistent with negotiated order theory, our research demonstrates that increased control may be costly or harmful especially if it is inconsistent with social structures and context. First, the controller may be unwilling or unable to bear the cost of control and all controls are costly to enact. Behaviour and outcome controls require performance monitoring. Clan control requires the controller to spend time engaging the clan to build and maintain shared cognition and strong relationships. In client/vendor relationships, selfcontrol enacted by the client depends upon the client’s ascribed meaning and requires the client to evaluate the vendor and suggest structures for improvement (Choudhury and Sabherwal, 2003). However, the controller has finite resources, which may already be pre-allocated. Reallocating resources to control means controller performance in other areas may decline, such that the drop in controller performance can outweigh the benefits of improved controlee performance. This happened in vignette 3, where the client highlighted its lack of competence and unwillingness to spend effort to enact bureaucratic (i.e. behaviour) controls.

We have also seen that enacted controls create costs on the controlee. Outcome controls and behaviour controls are normally enacted through meetings between controller and controlee, which take time. Clan controls require the controlee to engage other members of the clan (which may include the controller). Like the controller, the controlee has fixed resources such as time. In vignette 3, the vendor could either meet with the client, or do work. In vignette 4, the vendor could either tell the client when systems would be up, or spend time fixing the system.

## Conclusion

If human behaviour cannot be controlled very well by applying control theory in its current form, that theory has limited applicability for information systems development and operations. We have shown that controls result from negotiations between controllers and controlees within a particular context. Hence, we have suggested that negotiated order theory adds to control theory by providing a more nuanced understanding of social control in IS development. A contribution of this paper is to see controls as social structures tied to the social context within which they are enacted. Controls are shaped and re-appropriated within this context. Controls have intended and unintended consequences, and other forces not included in control theory shape a controller’s enactment of control.

We have identified some of the social forces that affect both controller and controlee when enacting controls. These forces help explain why controls result in different outcomes than expected by control theory. Controllers might enact controls mindlessly, producing unintended consequences. Controlees can ascribe the wrong meanings to controls or respond in ways that change the efficacy of controls. Controls shape both controlee and controller behaviour. The negotiated order perspective recognizes that social forces operate in a social context that shapes both.

Recent work on control theory has called us to better understand how controls are enacted (Wiener et al., 2016). Our research contributes to this by identifying key elements of the enactment to consider, including the context, controlee interpretation, controller interpretation and negotiation (see Figure 1; Table 3).

A negotiated order perspective on control theory has important implications for practice. Specifically, it suggests a controller cannot unilaterally enact control. Instead, both the controller and controlee must negotiate control. If no explicit negotiation takes place, there will either be an implicit negotiation (e.g., the creation of alternative structures), or some form of control dysfunction will arise. Such a dysfunction could include controlee resistance or an inability of the controlee to perform work.

## Further research

Our research suggests a number of possible avenues for further research. First, organizational structure and culture influence the kinds of control that can be enacted, but little research has explored this link (see Cardinal et al., 2004 as a notable exception). Second, most research on the issue of governance has been positivist with a rationalist view of organizations; our research suggests that an interpretive perspective on IT governance will yield some valuable insights. Third, IS-enabled innovation increasingly takes place in networks or ecosystems outside of corporate IT departments; this shift has a substantial impact on strategy, governance, and control. Traditional logics do not work when linear, firm-centric, and hierarchical models of development and innovation are disassembled. How controls can be enacted in such an environment remains an open question.
