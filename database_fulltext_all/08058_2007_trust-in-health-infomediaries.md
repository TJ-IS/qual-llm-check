# Trust in health infomediaries

Jaeki Song <sup>a,1</sup>, Fatemeh “Mariam” Zahedi <sup>b,⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409, USA <sup>b</sup> School of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201, USA

Received 4 November 2005; received in revised form 23 October 2006; accepted 1 November 2006 Available online 2 January 2007

## Abstract

Health infomediaries play an increasingly critical role in providing support for people's health and wellness decisions. Effectiveness of health infomediaries depends on people's trust in them. In this paper, we conceptualize a comprehensive synthesis of trust antecedents that are relevant to health infomediaries based on trust research and the actor-network theory. The model is constructed to highlight the factors that play a significant role in trust formation in health infomediaries. The empirical test of the model indicates that web users' beliefs about the ability and benevolence of the health infomediary critically affect their behavior intentions. Moreover, testing has identified the dimensions of information and system quality as well as the trust signs that enhance each trust belief. Environmental factors also play a significant role in enhancing beliefs about the ability and integrity of the health infomediary. Our empirical results further show that an individual user's propensity to trust has a significant relationship with riskrelated beliefs. Such trust and risk beliefs positively influence web users' behaviors. Our study shows the importance of context specific modeling of trust in health infomediaries. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Health infomediary; Trust; Trust signs; Information quality; System quality; Trust beliefs; Risk belief

## 1. Introduction

One of the important principles of having a healthy life style is to be knowledgeable about health and fitness issues. Individuals need timely and affordable access to information in order to be aware of health issues, to evaluate their health and fitness risk factors, to recognize warning signals and symptoms, and to be informed of the latest discoveries in fitness, nutrition, and healthy living. Although medical professionals constitute the best sources of one-to-one health information and assessment, the cost and time required to visit health providers prevent many people from seeking their advice in non-urgent as well as critical conditions. The Internet offers the best opportunity to provide people with free 24/7 access to health particularly in conditions when there is no ready access to health providers as well as health and fitness assessments.

The critical role of the web in health delivery has already been documented. In a survey of 1000 adults in the U.S., www.clickz.com reports that 74% have used the web for health information. The federal government has recognized the important role of online healthcare services as evidenced by the Department of Health and Human Services' declaration of the present decade as the decade of health IT, in which the web is to play a critical role [23,41]. Patients and health/fitness consumers have become more active and involved in decisions related to their health and wellness and at the same time overwhelmed by the complexity and diversity of health-related information. “Consumers turned to the World Wide Web in search of information that would help them make more informed decisions about their healthcare” [15]. “People can become overwhelmed by available information, possibly because of its jargon, organization, or overall volume, and experience degradation in the quality of their personal decision making” [57]. Health infomediaries play a critical role in making health information accessible to the general public. However, web users need to trust the health infomediary in order to use its information for health decisions.

There are many studies in IS that have investigated trust as an important factor [19,20,29,36,46,54,74]. Liang et al. [37] investigated trust in online prescription filling, and Leimeister et al. [36] have explored the role of trust in online healthcare communities. However, none of these papers focuses on the special nature of online health information providers. In e-commerce, customers' goal is to obtain products, for which websites serve as store fronts; whereas with health infomediaries, web users' goal is to obtain information from the website, hence the web contents are the purpose of users' visits. Hence, the health infomediary needs to attract web users and cultivate their trust solely by its contents. In this research, we argue that web users trust properties are particularly critical in the success of health infomediaries because web customers' use of the information depends to a large extent on their beliefs that health infomediaries provide unbiased, undistorted, and valuable information. It is critical to understand how various features of health infomediaries influence webusers beliefs and subsequent intentions to use the information in their decision-making process.

Furthermore, health infomediaries are actor-delegates that may need to generate a higher level of trust on the part of their users, which makes the study of trust formation in infomediaries more compelling and illuminating. As Pavlou and Gefen observe, “[a] trusted intermediary can also be expected to take steps to reduce buyer risk.” [54, p. 44]. This is particularly true for health infomediaries, which are expected to provide reliable health information and health-related recommendations.

In this paper, we attempt to address the trust formation using websites that provide health and wellness information and provider referrals (hereafter health infomediaries). The main research question is: what factors may impact various trust beliefs in health infomediaries? What are the natures and roles of different trust beliefs in web-users' intention to rely on the information provided by a health infomediary in making health decisions? Answers to these questions could provide an insight about how trust in health infomediaries is created and how different trust beliefs impact web-users' decisions. Our results could be insightful for infomediaries in developing design strategies that could enhance their customers' trust — a prerequisite for their success and longevity in the online market. Government organizations and healthcare providers can benefit from our findings to increase effective dissemination of their online health information. Furthermore, our findings could add to field knowledge about the factors that promote or inhibit trust formation in web-based information providers.

In what follows, we present the theoretical background and research model conceptualizing the antecedents of trust formation in health infomediaries. We test the model with a sample of 494 observations using structural equations modeling. We then discuss our findings and their implications. Concluding remarks and future directions of research are presented last.

## 2. Theoretical background and research model

We develop a conceptual model for exploring the trust formation in health infomediaries as the delegates of web users. In order to conceptualize the trust formation in health infomediaries, the following sections discuss the nature health infomediaries and webusers' trust in their agency.

## 2.1. Health infomediary

Infomediary is “a new Internet business model that applies to firms that help customers deal more efficiently and effectively with online vendors. In e-commerce, it functions as a third-party provider of unbiased information and as a business matchmaker” [30]. However, through health infomediaries, web users can get unbiased information about a variety of medical, health, and wellness topics for their health-related decisionmaking as well as information about various health providers. The use of information (the main fare of infomediaries) for decision-making relies to a large extent on users' trust in the health infomediary. Health infomediaries become successful mainly on repeated and habitual use of their information (as opposed to the one-time sale of a product). They need to build a loyal following by fostering trust in the quality of the information they provide. Gaining trust is critical to becoming a favorite information source, as is the case for physicians, lawyers, disaster warning channels or other sources of information for vital decisions and actions. Hence, the important role of trust in using infomediaries makes them an appropriate business type for exploring the antecedents of online trust formation. At present, we lack insight to factors that impact various trust properties of health infomediaries, lead to their use in web users' decision-making process.

## 2.2. Anthropomorphism in trust relationships with health infomediaries

Conceptualizing the trust relationship between web users and a health infomediary website raises the issue of anthropomorphism, which involves ascribing human attributes to nonhuman entities [40]. In the parlance of Latour's actor-network theory (ANT), in such relationships infomediaries are “actors” who “stand in” and act on behalf of web customers to reduce their risk in making health choices and in dealing with providers [33,72]. By accumulating health information, infomediaries act as “delegates” whose points of view are those of web users. These delegates are online entities, which are an amalgamation of humans, social and business standards, and information technology, interconnected like a chain. Latour [33] argues that at each link, humans and objects are intertwined in order to transform matter to form. For example, web designers, web technology, health information, health experts are intertwined to use IT and other resources to create a design for the website. At the end of the chain a phenomenon is created (for example, the health infomediary). This chain could be traversed in either direction from one end to the other. The actor-network theory makes it possible to investigate this chain in more detail [48]. Furthermore, ANT makes it possible to combine the technology determinism – IT artifact forms beliefs or beliefs shapes IT artifacts – with social constructivism that emphasizes human social interactions in using the technology. ANT makes it possible to synthesize the two perspectives in exploring how technology is developed and used [17].

Applied to our analysis, we argue that trust in health infomediaries (as a phenomenon) is the result of a network of objects and humans that at one end starts with a group of humans (creators and owners of the health infomediary website) who collect, store, and transfer information through their designs and software applications to their servers, which could be accessed by a web of interrelated computers created by a large group of other humans (web users) through their own hardware, software, and communication capabilities and capacities. There are humans at each end of this chain, interacting and intertwined with a host of nonhumans that are in turn created or facilitated by other humans (such as network owners and Internet providers). The question is whether the feeling of trust by web users is trust in the website (non-human), the creators of the website (human), or the organization paying for the website creators' salaries (non-human). Following Latour [33], we argue that the object or reference of trust is the “entire” chain, in which the human and non-human are linked in a chain and cannot be isolated. Any attempt to strip the network from its intermediate chains would obscure the exploration of trust. This point of view is further supported by Wang and Benbasat [76], who argue that consumers perceive online agents as “social actors” and ascribe to them human characteristics and trust properties.

## 2.3. Definition and nature of trust beliefs

There are different views about trust as belief, attitude, intention, and behavior [42,44]. Trust-related beliefs are complex with many components. The theories of trust have identified a number of general trustworthiness beliefs that apply to many circumstances. These beliefs include integrity, benevolence, competence, consistency, loyalty, openness, and predictability [7,42,44,69].

In the context of online information agents, Demolombe [14] argues that trust is context dependent in that “we do not trust an agent for everything and we have to define the property which is implicitly referred to” (p. 112). Whitener et al. [77] and Bhattacherjee [5] also argue trust is a domain-specific psychological state, a view that supports Demolombe's assertion of the context-dependency of trust. Therefore, it is important to carefully define trust properties in the health and fitness domain and within the context of information provided by health infomediaries.

Pavlou and Gefen [54] define trust in an intermediary as “subjective belief…that the intermediary will institute and enforce fair rules, procedures, and outcomes… competently, reliably, and with integrity” (p. 44). Adapting from [46,76], we identify ability, benevolence, and integrity as the relevant properties in the context of online recommendation agents. Although trust studies use ability, benevolence and integrity as properties implied in trusting behavior [53,54], they should be defined in the context of health infomediaries. Adapting from [27,42,46] to the context of health infomediaries, we define the ability belief as the web-users' belief that the health infomediary has the ability, knowledge, skills, and competence required to provide the best healthrelated information and referrals. The benevolence belief refers to web users' belief that the health infomediary will act in their best interest and care for them by providing reliable and useful health-related information and referrals. The integrity belief is defined as the belief that the health infomediary adheres to a set of ethical principles and values by providing unbiased and comprehensive information and referrals. Furthermore, relying on a given health advice in vital circumstances for making health decisions (such as the decision to go to emergency room) could have life-anddeath consequences. Hence, there is risk involved in relying on the information provided by health infomediaries. Risk belief is specific to possible undesired consequences associated with using a health infomediary's information to make vital health-related decisions.

## 2.4. Behavior intention and trust beliefs

In the actor-network theory (ANT), design is a process of “translation” of users' and other stakeholders needs into specific categories of needs, and later to a general set of needs [1,8,33,48]. These general needs are then “inscribed” into a uniform solution (the design) [48]. Inscription in this context means that the IT artifact reflects and embodies the needs and use patterns of its users [1,48]. In the context of health infomediaries, the design of a health infomediary is the result of inscription of various needs into the network (the health infomediary and its users and other stakeholders). Alignment is defined as “the extent to which the agendas and interests inscribing into the practice, institutions, and strategies of the network pull in the same direction, and serve the same purpose” [6]. When the system is running, the alignment of the networks' constitutive parts – including users' beliefs and experiences – will determine the patterns of use [48]. In the conceptual model presented in Fig. 1, we discuss how the constitutive parts of the network – including users' beliefs and experiences as well as the contents of the health infomediary and the environmental factors – will determine the pattern of intention to use in the information provided by the health infomediaries.

In ANT, one of the major constituents of a network is the perceptions of its users. Trust beliefs are critical in the network to which the health infomediary belongs.

Fig. 1. The conceptual model for trust in health infomediaries.

We argue that web users will form positive intention to use a health infomediary when their trust beliefs are positive with respect to the network (the health infomediary, the environment it operates in, and the predispositions of the users within the network). One can argue that trust beliefs and trust attitude are two distinct sets of constructs. Trust beliefs form the cognitive aspects of trust, whereas trust attitude is the affective state or feeling of trust. This point of view is in line with the work of McKnight et al. [45], which distinguishes between trusting beliefs and trusting intention. On the other hand, adapting from the theory of reasoned action (TRA), the technology acceptance model (TAM) [11] postulates that behavioral intention to use the technology has perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use beliefs that form the antecedents of intention to use [11,12]. Many TAM studies exclude attitude as a mediating construct [74,75]. In our work, we view trust beliefs as critical mediating constructs and argue that the impact of beliefs, such as ease-of-use and perceived usefulness on behavioral intention to use health infomediaries is mediated by trust beliefs. Furthermore, due to the informational nature of the domain, trust attitude and trust beliefs are arguably indistinguishably related. Hence, following ANT and TAM's adaptation of TRA, and based on ample evidence of the importance of trust in online research outcomes, we argue that trust beliefs are associated with web-users' intention to use the health infomediary, as depicted in Fig. 1 and postulated below:

Hypothesis 1. Web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary are positively associated with their intention to use the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 2. Web users' beliefs about the benevolence of the health infomediary are positively associated with their intention to use the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 3. Web users' beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary are positively associated with their intention to use the health infomediary.

## 2.5. Web users' risk beliefs

In ANT, web users' experiences and social make-up are part of the network components. We argue that web users' risk beliefs are part of the network. Perceived risk is a critical component of building interpersonal, social, and economic relationships [10,31]. In the relationship between web users and health infomediaries, the latter could be considered by web users as their agents, who collect and process health-related information. Services rendered by these agents are needed to reduce the transaction cost associated with the uncertainty involved in making decisions about health issues, health services, and health-related products. However, there is a perception of risk in using an agent. Agency theorists analyze economic relationships between principals and agents. “A principal-agent relationship exists when one party – the principal – contracts with another party – the agent – to perform a task involving delegation of decision making in exchange for compensation” [77]. In this relationship, both parties try to maximize their utility and minimize risks associated with the relationship.

In the context of e-commerce, Pavlou [53] argues that “the distant and impersonal nature of the on-line environment and the implicit uncertainty of using a global open infrastructure for transactions have rendered risk an inevitable element of e-commerce” (p. 109). Tan and Theon [70] make a similar observation about the risk of opportunistic behavior due to information asymmetry on the web. Health information consumers attempt to get the best information from health infomediaries while minimizing the risk of misinformation. Hence, we define risk beliefs as users' perceptions of negative consequences if the health infomediary's information is incorrect. Therefore, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4. Web users' perception of risk in using the health infomediary is negatively associated with their intention to use the health infomediary.

As shown in Fig. 1, we identify five categories of antecedents of trust beliefs: (1) web users' propensity to trust, (2) the health infomediary's trust signs, (3) the design and system quality, (4) the informational contents health infomediary, and (5) the health infomediary's environment, which includes the health infomediary's reputation, web users' previous experiences with infomediaries, and in general the reliability and dependability of the Internet.

## 2.6. Web users' propensity to trust

From a personality-based trust perspective, it has been argued that “trust develops during childhood as an infant seeks and receives help from his or her benevolent caregiver” [46]. Previous studies have found that propensity to trust positively influences trusting beliefs or trust, which slightly differ due to the theoretical background [27,41,42]. In this study, we argue that the impact of personal propensity to trust on behavior intention is mediated through risk beliefs, in that web users with propensities to trust have a lower perception of specific perceived risk when using health infomediaries, and so have a more trusting behavior in using the information. We, therefore, posit that:

Hypothesis 5. Web users' propensity to trust is negatively associated with their perception of risk in using the health infomediary.

## 2.7. Health infomediary's trust signs

Luo and Najdawi [39] review web elements that may influence trust. These elements include self-regulating policies such as statements about privacy and security, source disclosure such as providing references for information posted on the website, ownership disclosure such as acknowledging that the health infomediary is owned by a pharmaceutical company or HMO, and third-party seals such as the HON Code that guarantees that the health infomediary follows basic ethical codes in presenting information on its site. Additional signs include customer communities that reflect users feedback [35,65,72]. Moores [49] and Ray et al. [58] also have studied the effect of third-party seals and protocols. As observed by Tan and Theon [70], the trusting cues are control mechanisms for reassuring web users.

Based on ANT, one can argue that trust signs are the inscription of needs to enhance web users' trusts. This case parallels Latour's hotel example, in which the management of a hotel wished to have the customers return the hotel key at check-out time. After trying and failing various ways to inscribe the desired behavior (returning the room key), the management attached a metal knob to the key and gradually increased the weight of the knob until the desired behavior was achieved [48]. The metal knob inscribed the need of the management to the design of the key in order to attain the desired behavior by hotel customers. Similarly, trust signs inscribe the needs of designers of the health infomediary to convince web users that they can trust the information it offers.

Furthermore, in ANT signs are an integral part of the network: “at every stage, each element belongs to matter by its origin and to form by its destination; it is abstracted from a too-concrete domain before it becomes, at the next stage, too concrete again… We see only an unbroken series of well-nested elements, each of which plays the role of sign for the previous one and of thing for the succeeding one” [34, p. 56]. In the chain of trust formation, trust signs constitute part of the input for assessing the risk associated with the trust context as well as the integrity of the health infomediary.

This view is supported by “signaling theory,” in which properties related to trust are divided into observable and non-observable [3]. In face-to-face interactions between two humans, verbal cues are observable properties and non-verbal cues such as an individual's clothes, grooming, and mannerisms are non-observable properties. In the case of health information providers, information quality could be considered as the observable property, which is the focus of attention, whereas trust signs serve as nonobservable properties intended to reassure web users that there is no risk associated with using the health infomediary and to reinforce their beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary [55]. We argue that trust signs are designed to alleviate fears related to accessing and using the health infomediary by negatively influencing the perception of risk involved in using the health infomediary. Furthermore, trust signs signal web users that that the health infomediary follows ethical principles in presenting information on its site and subscribes to certain values and standards of conduct. These signals could bolster web users' beliefs regarding the integrity of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 6. The health infomediary's use of trust signs is negatively associated with web users' risk beliefs in using the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 7. The health infomediary's use of trust signs is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary.

## 2.8. Health infomediary's system quality

System quality of websites has received attention as an important factor in customer satisfaction [19,43]. The relevant aspects of the health infomediary's system quality are interactivity and ease-of-use.

## 2.8.1. Interactivity

Yi and Straub [78] argue that interactivity in websites provides new opportunities for vendors to build customer loyalty. Web customers normally prefer to complete the purchase process as quickly as possible, whereas web users spend more time exploring and interacting with health infomediaries in their information search. In the context of such websites, interactivity entails web elements that facilitate users' search of the website for the information they need, and may provide personalization features, such as sorting the information based on users' decision criteria (e.g., sorting physicians' locations by the distance from the user's house), providing advice for a given problem (e.g., recommending a suitable course of action), or making assessments based on users' circumstances (e.g., fitness assessment). Making the health infomediary interactive is relatively costly since it requires additional resources (technology and manpower) to achieve interactivity. Since accessing the needed information from the health infomediary may require interactions, the lack of quality in interactivity could cause web users to conclude that the health infomediary lacks the benevolence required to make the needed investment to address their issues and questions. In such a case, trust beliefs regarding benevolence of the health infomediary could suffer.

Hypothesis 8. The health infomediary's system interactivity is positively associated with web users' beliefs about its benevolence.

## 2.8.2. Ease-of-use

Ease-of-use has been one of the two most important constructs in the application of TAM to the study of trust (see, for example, [19,53]). In a study of the pattern of use in a health infomediary, Zhang et al. [79] report that 52% of information-seeking attempts were undirected, as opposed to 22% directed and 23% semi-directed. This means that users spend a great of time in the search process. Since health infomediaries provide quite varied and at times large volumes of information, ease-of-use is both difficult to achieve and crucial to have, since it profoundly impacts the delivery of information to web users.

With web users increasing experience with using the Internet, the difficulty of using a website signals the lack of skills and ability. Lack of ease-of-use may not necessarily indicate lack of benevolence or integrity since even with benevolence and integrity, the designers of the health infomediary may not have technical skills to achieve ease-of-use. For example, if web users have to click many times before accessing the needed information, if the fonts are difficult to read, or if navigating through the page is confusing and difficult, web users will not be able to find their needed information with ease. They may conclude that the health infomediary and its creators lack the skill needed for creating a user friendly design. Furthermore, the lack of ease-of-use could be equated with lack of ability in other aspects of contents.

Hypothesis 9. The health infomediary's ease-of-use is positively associated with web users' beliefs about its ability.

## 2.9. Health infomediary's information quality

Trust is “situational and context-specific, and should be investigated under specific contextual and situational parameters” [35] (p. 78). Health infomediaries need to provide evidence or proxy for the verification of their goals and actions in order to reduce web users' perception of risk. In any risk-taking behavior, the context in which the risk is to be taken plays an important part [42]. In the case of health infomediaries, the information provided by an infomediary is the evidence of its goal to operate as web users' delegate actors and constitutes the context for trusting behavior, an argument that is in line with the concept of alignment in ANT. Information quality (IQ) is the single most important attribute for users of health information. Therefore, one can argue that web users may view the high quality of information supplied by a health infomediary as a proxy for its goal alignment and proper actions as the web users' agent/delegate. The health infomediary's high information quality is the evidence for the presence of the shared goal with web users.

Information quality has a number of dimensions, including understandability, relevance, usefulness, reliability and relevance [43]. Although one can argue that all dimensions of information quality could impact all trust beliefs as an overall yet ambiguous approach, teasing out how each aspect of information could influence a given belief goes a long way in explaining how web users form their beliefs when they are exposed to the information provided by the health infomediary. Since little is known about the specific impact of information contents on each trust belief, we base our approach on the way Demolombe [14] relied on the definition of each trust property in order to logically derive the extent of trust in specific circumstances. More specifically, we rely on the exact definition of each trust belief to identify which information dimension could logically influence a given trust belief.

Adequacy of information relates to the completeness and extensive coverage of necessary topics and referrals of interest to web users. Web users may use the adequacy of information as a sign that the health infomediary is committed to the principle of providing comprehensive and unbiased information and referrals, which corresponds with the definition of integrity beliefs. Hence we posit that

Hypothesis 10. The adequacy of information provided by the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about integrity.

Relevance concerns the applicability of information to the web users' needs. Usefulness is the application of “perceived usefulness” in the technology acceptance model (TAM) to the health infomediary's information. Reliability concerns the accuracy and credibility of the information.

Of the three trust beliefs, the definition of benevolence makes it most susceptible to the relevance, reliability, and usefulness of information and referrals provided by the health infomediary. We argue that since web users do not have adequate knowledge about the caring and benevolent intentions of the health infomediary, they have to deduce benevolence from the nature of information and referral that the health infomediary provides. Hence, the dimensions that could closely be related to benevolence are usefulness, relevance, and reliability. Although there is little investigation of the details of information quality dimensions on trust beliefs, there are some hints of support in the literature for our argument. Pavlou and Gefen [54] identify reliability as a substitute for benevolence. Delgado-Ballester [13] has defined trust as “confident expectations about the brand's reliability and intentions in situations entailing risk to the consumer” (p. 586). Also, Smith and Manna [66] observe that reliable information influences trust in e-medication. In addition, Oliver and Montgomery [51] argue that the information and its relevance to trustor's cognition influence trustworthiness in social phenomenon. We posit that reliable, useful, and relevant information signal the benevolence of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 11. The reliability of information provided by the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about its benevolence.

Hypothesis 12. The usefulness of information provided by the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about its benevolence.

Hypothesis 13. The relevance of information provided by the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about its benevolence.

Understandability refers to the clarity of meaning that makes the information easy to read and comprehend. This is particularly important in the health domain, whose medical terms and scientific language should be made accessible to web users and patients seeking to use the information in making important health decisions. Understandable health information could signal the ability of the health infomediary to digest and simplify difficult and complex health information.

Hypothesis 14. The understandability of information provided by the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary.

## 2.10. The environment of trust

Environmental factors provide a context within which a given web user connects to the network represented by the health infomediary. These sources together create a favorable or unfavorable pre-disposition towards the health infomediary. Based on ANT, these factors represent additional links in the chain that connects the health infomediary with other actors (the Internet as a whole, other infomediaries, web users) in past time periods and circumstances. Hence, these factors summarize the historical context of the network within which the health infomediary operates from the perspective of the web user who is visiting it. Three levels of granularity are presented in these factors: the health infomediary (its reputation), infomediaries as a group (positive experiences with infomediaries in general), and the institution in which infomediaries operate (the web).

## 2.10.1. Reputation of the infomediary

The reputation of a health infomediary is the socialknowledge source of trust. It reflects the social influence of trust, which is consistent with the point of view promoted by Schoorman et al. [60] that trust-related studies need to start with a social influence point of view. Trust can be conditionally built upon social relations or social networks among actors [25,63]. The social exchange theory posits that social structure can shape one party's reputation based on a third party's ability to tell stories about its trustworthiness [52]. In the context of the Internet, previous studies found that reputation significantly influences trust in online retail stores [16,28,46,59]. Favorable reputation of a health infomediary is the evidence for the repeated exchange of benefits, and therefore would be the factor that biases web users in favor of the health infomediary's trustworthiness. Furthermore, reputation is a measure of social presence, which Gefen and Straub have found to be a significant antecedent of trust [22]. Hence, we posit that prior positive experience with a given health infomediary impacts web users' trust beliefs.

Hypothesis 15. The reputation of the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 16. The reputation of the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the benevolence of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 17. The reputation of the health infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary.

## 2.10.2. Prior positive experience with web infomediaries

The second factor of the trust environment is cognition-based, which produces the first impression of trust prior to engaging in interaction [47]. The trustor may rely on categorizations such as group identification (or unit grouping) and stereotyping [44]. In the context of health infomediaries, a web user who has regularly relied on infomediaries to find information may be more likely to have a favorable impression when visiting a health infomediary. Therefore, the depth and breadth of general experiences in using infomediaries increase a web user's ability to form stronger initial beliefs about the health infomediary. From the ANT perspective, infomediaries together create the network that a web user can connect to in order to access information. Positive prior experiences with this network alter web users' disposition by transferring the same positive perception to a particular health infomediary. Hence we argue that prior positive experience with infomediaries enhances web users' trust beliefs.

Hypothesis 18. Web users' prior positive experience with infomediaries is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 19. Web users' prior positive experience with infomediaries is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the benevolence of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 20. Web users' prior positive experience with the infomediary is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary.

2.10.3 Structural assurance of the Internet: specific to the Internet as an institution

Institution-based trust requires the presence of an impersonal social infrastructure for enabling the establishment of trusting interactions [80], and is divided into structural assurance and situational normality [19,46]. By structural assurance, we mean the presence of a wellestablished social infrastructure with necessary safeguards such as the existence of regulatory agencies, enforceable laws, guarantees, and well-established norms [64]. Situational normality implies the presence of a normal working structure. Of the two types of institution-based sources of trust, we posit that structural assurance plays a significant role in forming web users' prior beliefs. Since health infomediaries operate on the Internet environment, web users' perceptions about information reliability on the Internet color their beliefs regarding health infomediaries. This is particularly important in using health infomediaries, since consultation with a health infomediary may involve exchange of web users' private health information.

Hypothesis 21. Web users' perception about the structural assurance of the web is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 22. Web users' perception about the structural assurance of the web is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the benevolence of the health infomediary.

Hypothesis 23. Web users' perception about the structural assurance of the web is positively associated with web users' beliefs about the integrity of the health infomediary.

## 3. Research method and data analysis

The instrument needed for testing the model was developed and used in a questionnaire-based experiment.

## 3.1. Instrument development

The scales for measuring these constructs were developed based on a review of the literature to ensure the content validity of the instrument, as reported in Table 1. A pilot test was carried out prior to administering the survey. The objective of the pilot test was to ensure the clarity of the wording and instruction in the survey. Twenty-three subjects participated in the pilot test. They were asked to comment on the clarity of the survey questionnaire. Based on the feedback from participants in the first pilot test, two questions were modified in the questionnaire.

The research method was a questionnaire-based experimental study. We selected two successful health infomediaries: WedMD.com and MedPlus.com, which provide comprehensive health and wellness information. WebMD.com provides a range of health-related information, transactions and technology solutions that help consumers, physicians, providers and health plans. Its mission is to provide clinical information and educational tools. MedlinePlus.com provides health information from the medical library, the National Library of Medicine, the National Institute of Health and other sources on diseases, conditions, hospitals, physicians, fitness, and drugs.

The participants were asked to choose one of the two health infomediaries. Self-selection helped increase participants' interest in exploring the health infomediary. Participants were graduate or undergraduate students at two large U.S. business schools, one located in the Midwest and one in the South.

External validity has been considered as an important aspect in survey research [31]. External validity refers to “the approximate validity with which we can infer that the presumed causal relationship can be generalized” [11]. Locke [38] advocates applying an inductive approach of accumulating evidence for establishing generalizeability. In addition, Campbell [9] writes that “Perhaps college students are real people…why their disguise fools observers into thinking otherwise is not clear” (p. 276). He emphasizes that “A statement to the effect that the research method is valid because it takes place in a real organization or invalid because it used in a lab study is no argument at all” (p. 277). In addition, Ashton and Kramer [2] argued that student subjects in the studies of human information processing and decision making are surrogates of a general population, which is the case for this study. Furthermore, a study by the U.S. Department of Commerce reported that in September 2001, 54% of males and 54% of females in the United States used the Internet, and that 53% of the population in the 25–34 year age group used the Internet for online shopping [73]. The average age of our participants (26 in pilot and 21.1 in the main experiment) was relatively close to the lower end of this range. Furthermore, the GUV WWW Survey [24] characterizes web users as highly educated (88% had at least some college experience), which matches the educational levels of our participants (all college students) with a relatively high level of web activities. Hence the participants in this study do not present a significant threat to external validity and could be considered as representative of the younger group of online shoppers.

## 3.3. Reliability and validity

We carried out exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess initial convergent and discriminant validity. Of 19 first-order factors in the study (listed in Table 1), seven factors had one or two items that did not load well. Items that indicated cross-loading or low-loading values were removed and the analysis was performed again. The results show that items for each factor loaded well and there was no cross-loading above 0.40 [46].

We have taken a number of measures to reduce the common method variance, as recommended by Podsakoff et al. [56]. “Method variance refers to variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than the construct of interest” (p. 879). We used neutral wording of the items, semantic differential measures and multiple items for constructs, all of which help reduce the occurrence of common method variance. We have carried out three statistical analyses for checking and correcting for common method variance. The first analysis was carried out by applying Harman's single factor test. This test involves an exploratory factor analysis of all items to “determine whether the majority of the variance can be accounted for by one general factor” [56]. Harman's test showed that the first factor accounts for 45% of variance, which indicated the presence of common method variance. The second analysis for common method variance was carried out to remove the common method variance. Podsakoff et al. [56] recommend two major approaches in removing common method variance. One is to incorporate a single factor consisting of all items in the analysis. The second approach is to use a marker variable to purify the data. We tried both approaches. The first approach, as predicted by Podsakoff et al. [56], led to overidentification and the SEM solution did not converge. In the second approach, we used a general item of one of the constructs that was not used in the analysis. We regressed every item used in the model as the dependent variable and the marker item as the independent variable. We captured the residual for each item. Another round of Harman's test indicated a 30% reduction variance explained by the single factor. Hence, we used the purified data in place of the original data in the estimation of the structural equations method (SEM), including the estimation of the measurement model, as recommended by Podsakoff et al. [56].

The reliability of constructs was measured using Cronbach alpha, composite factor reliability (CFR) and average variance extracted (AVE). Results are reported in Table 2 (for both original data and purified data). All Cronbach alpha values are well above the threshold of 0.80. As expected, the Cronbach alpha values are lower for the purified data as compared with those for the original data, since the purified data do not have the inflation due to the common method variance. Similarly, all CFR values are well above the cut-off value of 0.70, and all AVE values are well above the cut-off value of 0.50 [61], together providing support for the reliability of constructs. (Both original data and purified data showed a high degree of reliability.) The results indicate high reliability of constructs.

We also computed the correlations of items within each construct with the general question for that construct. The very high and statistically significant values of these correlations support the convergent validity of constructs. The general question for each construct was included neither in reliability checks nor in the model estimation. Moreover, we carried out confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for establishing the convergent and discriminant validity by estimating the measurement model. The software for the estimation of the measurement model and the SEM estimation of the research model was Mplus, which is developed by Muthén and Muthén [50].<sup>2</sup> The high values for factor loadings (above 0.70) support convergent validity for the constructs. Furthermore, the t-values for factor loadings of manifest variables were well above 2, supporting the statistical significance of factor loadings [50]. Moreover, the high ${ \bf \bar { \boldsymbol { R } } } ^ { 2 }$ values for the indicators support the assertion that the indicators are good measures for the construct [19].

## 4. Discussions, implications, and conclusion

Our empirical evidence showed the influence of various factors representing contents, environment and web users' beliefs and predispositions that influence their intended behavior in using the health infomediary. In particular, the results indicate that web users' trust in the ability and benevolence of the health infomediary plays a stronger role in their behaviors, whereas trust in integrity and risk beliefs, though significant, plays a lesser role in users' behavior intention. Furthermore, we found that to enhance the trust in the health infomediary's ability, the understandability of the information and ease-of-use of the site should be improved. By increasing the relevance, usefulness, and reliability of the information as well as the interactivity of the health infomediary, web users' beliefs in the benevolence of the health infomediary could be enhanced. The perception of integrity will improve with adequacy of information and the trust signs imbedded in the health infomediary. There is little the designers of the health infomediary could do to enhance the perception of risk, since this belief is impacted only by web users' propensity to trust. Of the environmental factors, ability beliefs are most influenced by the environmental factors (reputation, experience with infomediaries and structural assurance), whereas benevolence beliefs are influenced only by web users' experience. Hence, we conclude that the impacts of environmental factors on user's behavior intention are mostly mediated by web users' beliefs about the ability of the health infomediary. The environmental factors influence integrity as well. However, the relatively small coefficient of integrity beliefs in web users' behavior makes the integrity-mediated influence of environmental factors of less importance in influencing web users behavior intention.

Our findings have important managerial implications. In this work, we have differentiated among various trust beliefs and their roles in users' behavior intention. Trust in the ability of health infomediaries is of great importance. It is critical for managers to take actions to bolster the perception of ability. Doing so requires making the health infomediary understandable and easy to use. Since medical and health information is complex, varied, and full of jargon, our results indicate that designers should invest adequate resources to help web users understand the jargon and plow through the mass of health information in their decision process. Managers should also pay attention to the perception of benevolence, which could be bolstered by increasing the reliability, relevance, usefulness and interactivity of the health infomediary. Trust signs, while important in forming the perception of integrity, play a lesser role in this context. Furthermore, health infomediaries have a potentially large market in individuals experienced in using infomediaries and the Internet. This is a market niche that could be tapped through marketing strategies that reach such users, such as advertising in other similar infomediaries, encouraging the exchange of information among users by creating chat rooms and cyber communities, and actively sending users personalized health-related information on topics of interest to such users. Moreover, the significant role of reputation on ability and integrity shows that health infomediaries should make an effort to enhance their reputation and visibility through innovative initiatives in both cyberspace and informal channels of communication.

Our finding also point how important it is for health infomediaries to understanding the nature and profile of their target audience. While standard and run-of-themill system design may work well for a webexperienced audience, health infomediaries may need to experiment with alternative system design for a novice population. This is particularly true if government agencies use the Web to provide health-related information to their citizens. Designing understandability for the general public is different from designing understandability for health professionals. In the first case, design should involve simple explanations of health issues and have limited technical or scientific language. However, health professionals need more specific and scientific language for understanding a health-related description. Similarly, the general public may not need detailed discussions, whereas a health professional may need a complete explanation. Hence, adequacy may differ for different target audiences. A possible solution for the dilemma of reaching multiple groups is to provide the option of personalizing or customizing the website based on web users' preferences. The dimensions of information quality are appropriate candidates for personalization of content delivery for health infomediaries.

The research implications of this work are the identification of the detailed impact of the contents, design, and environment of the health infomediary on web users' individual trust beliefs. We also showed how the actor-network theory could be used as the over-arching theory for conceptualizing the model, which shows in the relevance of ANT in the investigation of human–IT networks. We teased out the role of trust beliefs in web users' behavior intentions, showing the importance of ability and benevolence, and theorized how understandability and ease-of-use could influence ability whereas relevance, usefulness, and reliability of information and the interactivity could influence benevolence. This is, to our knowledge, the first time that such differentiated impacts have been identified in the context of online infomediaries.

In addition to trust-related issues, this study provides a significant theoretical contribution to our field. Many trust studies in the e-business area have adopted the theories from social and personal psychologies. However, the contexts in such studies are human relationships. In e-business areas including health infomediary, trust issues deal with the relationships between human and non-human (website) entities. We applied actornetwork theory (ANT) in addressing the relationship between human and non-human entities, which may resolve the issue of anthropomorphism. Furthermore, our results highlight the importance of context in modeling web trust. In the delivery of products such as music and entertainment videos, both content and system design may influence web customers' beliefs about the vendor's ability.

Our study has a number of limitations. The type of participants in our lab experiment limits the generalizeability of our findings to the younger web customer age group. Our experiments should be repeated with additional health infomediaries. There is a need to explore the role of system design and trust signs in other settings, and to delve deeper into the study of risk beliefs associated with using health websites.
