# Media, affect, concession, and agreement in negotiation: IM versus telephone

Norman A. Johnson ⁎, Randolph B. Cooper <sup>1</sup>

C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, Houston, TX 77204-6282, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 8 April 2008 Received in revised form 31 July 2008 Accepted 27 October 2008 Available online 5 November 2008

Keywords: Communication media Affect Concession and negotiation Instant messaging Telephone

## a b s t r a c t

The communication of affect, the exchange of offers, and the goal of reaching agreement are factors that play key roles in negotiation processes. Although instant messaging (IM) and phone are used for these processes, not much is known about how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence these key factors. The present study is focused on this issue. By examining natural communication between negotiators who use these media, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that computer mediation reduces both the amount of affect communicated and concession, which in turn decreases the likelihood of agreement. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of affect communicated is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduced by computer mediation.

## 1. Introduction

A recent trend, being driven by the increase in online transactions enabled by the Internet is for negotiations to occur over computer media [43,59], such as email and instant messaging [27,43,88]. For example, in December 2007 over 6 million listings were added per day and over 2 thousand dollars worth of goods were traded every second on eBay [26]. Many of these transactions took place between buyers and sellers with con<sup>fl</sup>icting interests, and as result there was a need for negotiations [88]. Online dispute resolution services, such as Square Trade, handle a large number of these negotiations in over 120 countries [82]. By allowing buyers and sellers to engage in “direct negotiation” via computer media, this type of service can lower the cost of negotiation, increase its speed, make it more informal, and reduce the need for third parties [43]. Indeed, researchers have long suggested that, in electronic commerce, most negotiators would use such a service [51].

However, computer-mediated negotiation is not without challenge [20], because, for example, it can make agreement more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to reach [85,89] Given the potential gains from conducting negotiations over computer media, it would be helpful to have a better understanding of the associated challenges in order to apply this understanding to inform potential negotiation strategies [61,62]. We therefore investigate the research question “What in<sup>fl</sup>uences do media have on the negotiation process?” by speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examining the kind of relationships that might exist among different media (instant messaging versus telephone), communicated affect, concession making, and agreement. Our interest in these relationships is motivated by the following reasons. (1) Agreement is the primary goal of negotiation [31]. (2) Concession making is the process by which agreement is reached. (3) The likelihood of concession making increases when negotiators are cooperative [69], and cooperation is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the communication of positive affect [10,38]. (4) The different media we employ enables a comparison of a medium (instant messaging) in which negotiators begin with a less cooperative orientation [62,94] and which makes the communication of positive affect more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult [89] to a medium (telephone) in which negotiators' orientations are more cooperative and positive affect is easier to communicate. Our work extends negotiation research in the following ways.

Current <sup>fi</sup>ndings associated with affect in computer mediated negotiation typically come from communication allowed prior to, but not during, negotiation. In addition, the communication is typically structured such that topics discussed are unrelated to the negotiation, such as participants relating positive past experiences to each other (e.g., [2,59]). But during negotiation many unexpected things, such as surprisingly low offers, can occur that dramatically change the situation [83]. Also, there are moments in the negotiation when parties reach new understandings of their situation, such that the nature of the con<sup>fl</sup>ict and the relationship among the parties is rede<sup>fi</sup>ned [47,70]. Our work therefore extends negotiation research by looking beyond the strict give-and-take exchanges of offers upon which most scholars have focused (e.g., [50,55,92]), and including an examination of the unstructured communication process. This is in contrast to much negotiation research, and enables natural interactive dynamics, which makes our study more relevant to real-world negotiations [25].

Our work also extends research on media and negotiation by enhancing our understanding of how communication media in<sup>fl</sup>uence the negotiation process and associated outcomes.<sup>2</sup> For example, past studies typically assume that different media vary in their abilities to convey certain cues, such as affect, and they then examine the impacts that different media have on negotiation outcomes (e.g., [33,77]). In contrast, we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine the degree to which affect is conveyed by different media, and then the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that these differences in affect have on negotiation outcomes. Also, past studies have examined group process variables, such as the cognitive limitation, the degree of structure, and the use of anonymity, as well as outcome variables, such as satisfaction and negotiation time (e.g., [21,33]). Our work adds to the scope of these studies by including the process variable – concession, as it relates to the outcome variable – agreement. In addition, though not typically examined, our measurement of concession includes that associated with <sup>fi</sup>rst offers. This can be important, for example, when a seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer is relatively low because he/she wants to increase the likelihood that the buyer will reciprocate with a concession of his/her own, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a relatively high counter offer. If the buyer does in fact come back with a high counter offer, then the initial offers for both the seller and buyer would include concession that should be measured.

Research suggests that, compared to telephone communication, computer-mediation will reduce the level of positive communicated affect. However, prior research has not addressed whether this mediation will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of that affect actually communicated. Because computer mediation increases depersonalization, provides fewer cues on which to evaluate their opponents' trustworthiness, and increases the potential for sinister attributions [85], individuals negotiating over computer media may interpret positive communicated affect as part of a manipulation strategy, rather than, e.g., a sign of cooperation. We therefore speci<sup>fi</sup>cally investigate whether computer mediation decreases the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of communicated affect. This investigation is important because, if true, it suggests that efforts to facilitate the communication of affect over computer media may be wasted.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The theoretical background is <sup>fi</sup>rst offered and the research model described. Construct operationalization and a description of the experiment then follow. Experimental results and a discussion, which include implications for research and practice, are provided last.

## 2. Theory and research model

In this section, we start with a description of negotiation and the context that are the focus of our study. Our concern is with negotiation that occurs between two people who are strangers to each other. We also describe constructs that are the foundation for our research model. The constructs of interest are: (1) concession, (2), communicated affect and (3) agreement. The media of interest are instant messaging (IM) versus telephone. We propose several hypotheses that explain the ways in which these media and constructs are linked together as depicted by our research model in Fig. 1.

Negotiation. Negotiation is a process that involves group decisionmaking, and that has speci<sup>fi</sup>c kinds of goals and relationships [58]. Negotiating parties perceive each other's goals to con<sup>fl</sup>ict with their own [84]. However, the parties also believe that they can reach an agreement which makes each better off, and that they must cooperate to reach that agreement [40,60]. This results in a mixed-motive relationship in which the parties cooperate as well as compete for divergent ends [71]. We focus on distributive negotiations, in which parties negotiate over a <sup>fi</sup>xed resource, with each party competing to claim a larger portion of the resource for himself/herself.<sup>3</sup> However, this competition is tempered by the realization that as one party claims more of the resource, the likelihood of the other party cooperating (i.e., agreeing to the resource distribution) decreases; and lack of agreement (or impasse) decreases the potential utility of the negotiation for both parties [63].

Dyadic negotiation between strangers. Our speci<sup>fi</sup>c concern is with two-party (dyadic) negotiation. This case of negotiation is the subject of vastly more empirical research than the multiparty case [69]. In addition, information about two-party negotiation can be useful, for example, to mediators in their attempts to help with the negotiation process [5]. Two-party negotiations are also common in online settings today. For example, Cybersettle, an online dispute resolution service that facilitates two-party negotiations regarding a single issue, has completed more than \$1.2 billion in settlements and has more than a 100,000 registered attorneys as users.<sup>4</sup>

We focus on negotiating parties who are strangers (i.e., unfamiliar with each other), since many parties who transact online do not know their opponents, at least for initial encounters and associated agreements [97]. This is especially true in online environments, such as eBay, when online resolutions take place as a result of disputes arising between buyers and sellers [88,97]. An important implication of this focus on strangers is that the potential effects of media on negotiation are heightened. This can occur because when individuals are not strangers they may be able to overcome many communication restrictions imposed by various media [8,13,94].

Concession. Negotiation differs from the general notion of group decision-making in terms of concession. Concession is said to occur when one person's offer supports another person's interests such that there is a reduction of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the person who makes the offer [69]. For example, when negotiating the sale of a <sup>fi</sup>xed resource, the seller offers a price that he/she is willing to accept as payment. The buyer may then make a counter offer lower than that of the seller. The seller may then make a counter offer that is lower than his/her <sup>fi</sup>rst offer but higher than the buyer's offer. The difference between the seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst and second offers represents a concession by the seller. In this way, concession is ritualized as an exchange of offers and counteroffers [98].

Past research has typically examined concession in two ways: (1) the frequency of changes in offers and counter-offers over time, termed concession rate and (2) the magnitude of the difference between an individual's offers (e.g., [55,92]). In this study, we are interested in comparing concession as represented by the size of negotiators' offers, and not by the rate at which these offers are made. Therefore, when we hereafter discuss concessions or concession, we are focusing on concession magnitudes. Details regarding how concession is measured are described later.

Agreement. Though negotiators set out to reach an agreement, negotiations can result in no agreement. Agreement occurs when negotiators accept a mutually suitable alternative [69]. Sometimes such an alternative exists at the start of negotiation, and negotiators may <sup>fi</sup>nd and accept it, thereby quickly reaching agreement. Alternatively, an agreement may be reached over time through a process of discussion and alternating concessions [69].

Communication of affect. In negotiation, each party employs in<sup>fl</sup>uence strategies aimed at getting the other party to concede more of the resource of value and to reach agreement [60]. These strategies can involve threats and coercion [24]. But one of the more effective ways to encourage concession and agreement is through the development of positive bonds, which include promoting positive feelings between negotiators [59,69]. Positive feeling is an affective state that can be developed through positive affective communication between individuals [10,38]. In general, affective communication frequently occurs in negotiations because negotiators usually begin their interaction with strongly felt emotions [12].

IM versus telephone mediation. Communication is the essence of negotiation [71], and the type of communication media used can affect the ability of negotiators to share viewpoints, engage in compromise, etc. [67]. Clark and Brennan [16] describe the notion of grounding during communication, which is the process by which individuals use social and other communicated cues to develop a shared sense of understanding and participation. Clark and Brennan [16] identify eight attributes that media may have which can facilitate grounding. Six of these attributes are provided by face-to-face communication. As a result, this channel can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly assist in the transfer of cues, thereby providing for more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective grounding [16,37]. The six attributes to which we refer are the following. (1) Co-presence exists when parties share the same physical environment and allows each to see what the other is looking at and to see and hear what each other are doing. (2) Visibility allows each party to see the other, but parties do not know what each other is doing or looking at. (3) Audibility allows each party to hear the timing and intonation of speech. (4) Simultaneity enables both parties to send and receive messages at the same time (such as when a party who is speaking sees the other smiling and nodding). (5) Sequentiality enables turn taking to stay in sequence by, for example, allowing each party to know the exact moment when the other has <sup>fi</sup>nished communicating. (6) Cotemporality makes it possible for a message to be produced just about when it is received and understood, without delay.

Two attributes are not included in face-to-face communication, but rather can exist in textual media such as email and letters [16]. These attributes are (7) Reviewability, which allows anyone to review a message at later time and (8) Revisability, which allows a person to be able to change a message privately before sending it to another person.

Of speci<sup>fi</sup>c interest here is that telephone communication provides four grounding attributes: audibility, simultaneity, sequentiality, and co-temporality [37]. And, as described in detail later, these attributes facilitate the transfer of relational and social cues. In contrast, textbased instant messaging (IM)<sup>5</sup> has the ability to support only two grounding attributes: reviewability and revisability [16,37]. Though these attributes can aid grounding by providing a better understanding of message content (reviewability) and by preventing mistakes from being sent (revisability), they do little to aid in the transfer of relational and social cues. Thus, in contrast to telephone, IM communication results in reduced relational and social cues, such as communicated affect [62]. In addition, the fact that communication is “typically received and written while the writer is in isolation, staring at a computer screen” makes IM “more profoundly asocial” ([37] p. 1329).<sup>6</sup>

Social presence theory [78], information richness theory [17], and the social identity model of deindividuation effects (SIDE:[68]) are in accord with this view, suggesting that IM reduces individuals' abilities to convey nonverbal cues (visibility and audibility) and to provide quick feedback (simultaneity), which results in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reductions in relationship and socially-oriented communication, such as communicated affect [41]. For the same reasons, this impact is also supported by cuelessness theory [76] and reduced social cues theory [81], both of which suggest that such computer communication can decrease personalization and increase the psychological distance among communicators. Empirical evidence supports these claims. For example, Kahai and Cooper [42], Moore et al. [59], Morris et al. [61], and Valley et al. [89] found decreases in social and relationshiporiented communication with different forms of text-based computermediated communication when compared with telephone or face-toface communication. We are concerned here with IM, which, when compared to telephone communication, restricts individuals' abilities to communicate affect as well as other socially-oriented information.

Not all researchers agree with the notion that text-based computer communication signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly restricts the transfer of relationally and socially-oriented information. Some researchers suggest that the degree of relationship-oriented communication that can be transmitted in a medium may be greater than what would be expected by an objective evaluation of the medium's richness [22]. For example, employing emoticons as well as variations in font type, color, and case in order to convey voice in<sup>fl</sup>ection and pitch can enhance the ability of text-based media to communicate emotion [8,74]. Though this is true, such communication conveys “far fewer …clues about emotional states” than do nonverbal cues via face-to-face or telephone ([37], p. 1334). In addition, the ability of a text-based medium to convey relationally and socially-oriented information may depend on the relative familiarity of communicating individuals with each other, thereby enabling them to employ cues based on their shared experiences [13]. Since the focus of this paper is on agreements among strangers, individuals' abilities to convey such information over text-based media are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly diminished [62].

Walther [94] and Tidwell and Walther [87] also suggest that individuals can adapt their behavior to the medium to a degree that as much social and relational information can be communicated via text as via face-to-face, though such social and relational communication may be at a slower rate. Their examples of individuals' adaptation to the medium include instances of email romances, online social support communities, and discussions about sex, love, family, and religion. However, their arguments may not apply to negotiations [37], since the goals of such instances are to build relationships and provide support, not to manage con<sup>fl</sup>ict; i.e., not to “assert one's needs or wishes through differences of opinion” ([37], p. 1334).

In summary, negotiation is a special type of group decision-making in which interested parties have con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals but also have a common cooperative interest in reaching agreement. In such mixedmotive circumstances, concession plays a key role in reaching agreement; and employing different communication media for negotiation may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the concession process as well as the outcome of negotiation because of differing abilities to communicate relationship and socially-oriented information, such as affect. We next state our hypotheses that explain how the constructs described above are related to each other.

## 2.1. Concession and agreement

Negotiation entails back-and-forth communication involving a great deal of give and take with the objective of reaching agreement [31,53]. Agreement is reached when negotiators accept the same terms. For example, in a negotiation over the price of an item, an agreement is reached when both negotiators' offers are the same. However, negotiators typically make initial offers that are not equal, and tend toward equality via a series of concessions as they negotiate. Concessions occur when one negotiator makes an offer that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the interest of his/her opponent, while reducing his/her own bene<sup>fi</sup>t (for example, when a seller lowers his/her initial selling price). As negotiators make concessions, they send a signal to their opponents that they are willing to move toward agreement [69]. Along with this signal, both negotiators' actions are guided by two prominent social norms. One of these norms is that of reciprocity, which dictates the repayment of others for favors [28,39]. The other involves the avoidance of inequity, which includes being fair regarding the distribution of resources [1,93]. As a result of these norms, negotiators tend to act in such a way that an instance of concession will lead to other instances of concession. Over time, as concession increases, negotiators' offers are likely to converge to the same value, and agreement is reached. This leads to the following hypotheses.

H . Concession increases the likelihood of agreement.

## 2.2. Communicated affect and concession

Affect is a fundamental aspect of social interaction [2,91], and includes emotions (brief states directed at speci<sup>fi</sup>c stimuli), moods (states of longer duration than emotional experiences that are not directed at anything speci<sup>fi</sup>c), and traits (stable individual differences in the tendency to experience emotions and moods) [2,91]. Positive affect involves pleasant feelings such as excitement and happiness [9], and evokes pro-social and cooperative orientations in which individuals are perceived as emphasizing others' outcomes as well as their own [2]. These perceptions increase beliefs that individuals are acting out of good intentions [35,36], thereby promoting concession.

The emotion and mood aspects of an individual's positive affect can be enhanced via positive affect communicated to him/her by others [10,38]; such communication can involve solidarity, tension release, and acceptance [6]. This affect can be further enhanced through emotional mimicry (social contagion), which is automatic and often irrepressible (as when we mimic the laughter of another) [85,86,90]. In this way, positive affect communicated by one party generates positive affect in the other, who will then return the communication of positive affect, thereby increasing the level of positive affect in the <sup>fi</sup>rst. Therefore, among negotiating individuals, the communication of positive affect is likely to increase each individual's positive affect. This leads to the following hypothesis.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ The communication of positive affect leads to greater concession.

## 2.3. The influence of communication media on concession

Coordinated negotiations occur when individuals reciprocate each other's concessions; uncoordinated negotiations lack such reciprocation and often result in negotiations that take an inordinate amount of time, create much psychological strain, and reduce concession such that agreement is not reached [69]. As we suggested before, negotiators are more motivated to engage in coordinated negotiations when they care about the equity of outcomes [57,69] and are positively disposed toward reciprocity [69]. These conditions traditionally hold because people tend to be averse to inequity [1,93] and there is a powerful norm of reciprocation that is pervasive in all human societies, and which results in our feeling obligated to future repayment of favors, such as concessions [28,39].

However, motivations for equity and reciprocity come in part from social rewards and costs [96], wherein an individual behaves in an equitable and reciprocal manner in order to gain social acceptance and avoid social shame [28,96]. Anonymity can diminish these motivations because it reduces individuals' concerns about social evaluation [66,73], and IM increases anonymity because of the reduction in feedback due to its lack of audibility, simultaneity, and sequentiality [37]. For example, compared to telephone communication, IM's lack of simultaneity and audibility reduces feedback regarding an individual's behavior that can be provided by tone of voice and verbal expressions, such as “uh huh” [95]. This makes grounding less effective as people become less aware that they are interacting with another person and leads to “a greater sense of anonymity” ([95], p. 633).

The effect on grounding can also decrease social presence [41], thereby diminishing social control and stimulating antinormative behavior [27]. “Without nonverbal and paralinguistic reminders of the social context, people's attention turns away from others and so does their concern with being positively evaluated or with liking the other” ([45] p. 91). Individuals are thus less likely to act in socially desirable ways; for example they may be less sensitive to equity or reciprocity during negotiation. Thus, increased anonymity and reduced social presence associated with IM can reduce individuals' desires for equity of outcomes and reciprocity. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H . Compared to telephone, negotiations using IM will have reduced concession.

## 2.4. The influence of media on the communication of affect

Communication media typologies – distinguishing among face-toface, telephone, IM, etc. – are based on common sets of characteristics. As contrasted with face-to-face communication, other media are typically described as having characteristics that are relatively impoverished, in that they are less able to facilitate the transmission of non-verbal cues or paralinguistic cues or are less able to provide immediate feedback [8,85]. Such a typology that focused on grounding was introduced earlier, and suggested that, when compared to telephone communication, IM is relatively ineffective because it lacks audibility, simultaneity, and sequentiality. A case was made that media lacking these communication characteristics can severely restrict the communication of affect. Of special interest here is that these impacts of impoverished media on communicated affect have been found during negotiation tasks [85]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Individuals using IM rather than telephone will have a reduction in the amount of positive communicated affect.

## 2.5. The influence of media on the efficacy of communicated affect

Media that are ineffective in the support of grounding reduce negotiators' rapport with, and trust of, each other [59,62]. This can occur because such media are less able to convey the clues of a person's trustworthiness, such as through the pitch and cadence of his/her voice [29]. Without such clues, individuals perceive a greater potential of being exploited by their opponents [34,94]. When negotiators are strangers, this perception is heightened because of the sinister attribution error, where individuals are more likely to attribute others' actions to malevolent motives if the others are not part of the individual's ingroup [85].

As a result, when compared to telephone, IM can increase the likelihood that negotiators will misinterpret behavior as being exploitive. For example, an individual may perceive concessions by another as part of a manipulative strategy rather than as a sign of cooperation [23]. This potential for misinterpretation is exacerbated by the depersonalization of negotiations that occurs over IM. As described earlier, the psychological distance between negotiators is increased due to the limited communication of social and relational information resulting from using IM. This increased distance reduces individuals' abilities to empathize with each other [18], and results in expectations that negotiation opponents will be untrustworthy and engage in unethical behaviors [44,89]. These expectations increase the likelihood that negotiation behavior (such as communicated positive affect) will be interpreted negatively [44,89]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H . When strangers are negotiating, the use of IM rather than telephone for communication will reduce the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that positively communicated affect has on concession.

## 3. Method

One hundred and forty-eight students (40% female) in a large university participated in an experiment designed to test our hypotheses. These students were asked to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll roles as negotiators in a price negotiation for a lottery ticket. Each student was randomly assigned to a dyad and the role of either seller or buyer, but the identity of each dyad member was unknown to the other. We thus had 74 dyads, each negotiating with telephone and with IM. The dyads were divided such that about one-third negotiated with telephone <sup>fi</sup>rst and the rest negotiated with IM <sup>fi</sup>rst.<sup>7</sup> We next describe the experimental task and procedure, followed by operationalizations of the constructs.

## 3.1. Task

The negotiation object was a computer-displayed lottery ticket and the negotiation was about its value in units of points. Each student in a dyad was given \$10 for participating in the experiment and was told that at the end of it, he/she had a chance to receive an extra \$10, depending on the number of points earned as a result of the outcome of the negotiation and the lottery draw for the ticket's value. Each dyad negotiated the price of the ticket in terms of points. The ticket was displayed with two potential values: 12 and 34 points.

There were two chance events that determined which of these values was the <sup>fi</sup>nal worth of the ticket. (1) For each of the two potential values, there was a 50% chance for one of them to be the actual value of the ticket. (2) Along side the ticket, there was a prize wheel (see Fig. 2) that displayed the chance of realizing the actual value in terms of differently colored portions: one taking up 74% of the wheel representing the likelihood of winning if the actual value was 12, and one taking up 98% of the wheel representing the likelihood of winning if the actual value was 34. This results in the ticket holder having a 37% chance of winning 12 points $( 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 7 4 )$ , a 49% chance of winning 34 points (0.5⁎0.98), and a 14% chance of winning nothing $( 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 2 6 + 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 0 2 )$ . At the start of negotiation the seller was the owner of the ticket. If the seller decided to keep the ticket and won the lottery (the prize wheel landed on the win area), he/she received points equal to the actual ticket value; if the seller lost, he/she got zero points. In either case, the buyer received zero points. If the seller sold the ticket, he/she received points equal to the selling price, and the buyer received points equal to his/her lottery winnings (determined as described above for the seller) less the amount he/she used to purchase the ticket.

Fig. 2. Example prize wheel(s). (Since colors cannot be reproduced, the colored prize wheel displayed to dyads is illustrated above as two different prize wheels.)

Two different kinds of lottery tickets were used, in which the number of points was the same on each ticket, but the prize wheels differed. Half of the dyads received the lottery ticket described above with an expected value of 21.1 points. The other half received a lottery ticket with the same points (12 and 34) but with chances of winning reduced to result in a lower expected value of 2.9 points.<sup>8</sup>

Sellers and buyers were situated in separate locations. Each seller and buyer used a personal computer to see the lottery tickets and prize wheels. Personal computers were connected such that each simultaneously displayed the same information to the buyer and seller in the same dyad. Communication between buyer and seller was restricted to the available media, and they were not allowed to communicate with each other prior to the start of the experiment. Buyers and sellers in the IM treatment conversed with each other using their PCs for text-based instant messaging. Buyers and sellers in the telephone treatment also had PCs that display lottery information, but they could only communicate via telephone.

Earlier, we described negotiation as a special case of group decisionmaking, where negotiating parties perceive each other's goals to be incompatible with their own, though they must cooperate to reach satisfactory outcomes. This is true with our task. The negotiator within each dyad who has the greater number of points after the lottery is over can win \$10. The number of points for each individual in a dyad is determined as follows. The seller initially has a lottery ticket. If he/ she keeps the ticket, and wins the lottery, he/she can win the \$10; in this case, the buyer has no chance to win the \$10. If the buyer purchases the ticket, the seller has the purchase price and the buyer has the results of the lottery less the amount he/she paid for the ticket. For example, if the buyer gave 9 to the seller for the ticket, the seller has 9 points. If the buyer wins 12 points, he/she is left with 3 points, which is less than the seller, and the seller can win \$10. If the buyer wins the 34 points, he/she is left with 23 points, which is greater than that of the seller, and the buyer can win \$10. Thus, goal incompatibility exists because the seller wants to extract as much from the buyer as possible while the buyer is penalized for each point he/she gives the seller. However, due to different risk preferences, different perceptions of expected values, different feelings regarding luck, etc., there are usually a number of prices at which the buyer and seller both believe exchange of the ticket is preferable to no exchange. Thus, they must cooperate to some degree in order to make the exchange occur.

## 3.2. Procedure

As subjects arrived at the experimental site, they were randomly assigned a role (buyer or seller) and escorted to one of two locations, depending on that role. There they received instructions about the experimental task via PowerPoint, and completed three practice rounds of negotiation with an instructor. During these exercises, subjects were encouraged to ask questions if they were uncertain about the game procedures and objectives. In the practice rounds, they were told that the experiment began when a lottery ticket, a prize wheel, a timer, and instructions appeared on their computer screen. The buyers (sellers) were also told that sellers (buyers) were in a remote location and would see the same information at the same time. Further, they were told that anyone could initiate negotiation by contacting their dyad opponent via the assigned medium, either PC or phone. Finally, they were told that they had three and a half minutes with which to complete their negotiations.<sup>9</sup> Before the time elapsed, the seller had to indicate via the PC what the parties agreed on in terms of the decision to sell or not to sell the ticket. If the seller did not make such an indication and the time elapsed, both the seller and buyer would receive no points.

## 3.3. Operationalization of communication media

A single variable was employed in which IM communication was represented with a value of 1 and telephone communication was represented by a value of 0.

## 3.4. Operationalization of communicated affect

Positive communicated affect displays friendliness and supportiveness [42,59,85]. Such communication may involve the disclosure of personal information as well as politeness rituals that endorse what the other is saying (e.g., head nodding or saying “uh-huh”) [59,85].

During negotiation, all communication for each dyad was recorded. Audio <sup>fi</sup>les for telephone and written logs for IM of the messages were then coded using Bales [6] interaction process analysis categorization scheme. In accord with this scheme, each message was coded as having positive communicated affect if it:

• Showed solidarity, raised other's status, gave help and/or rewards (Bales category 1);

• Showed tension release, joked, laughed, and/or showed satisfaction (Bales category 2);

• Agreed, showed passive acceptance, understanding, concurrence, and/or compliance (Bales category 3).

Because over 2500 messages were exchanged over both communication media, nine coders were trained and employed to code these messages. The messages from each medium were randomly divided into three sets such that there were six sets of data in total. Each set of data was made up of messages from about 25 dyads. Three coders were assigned to each set of data. Those who were assigned the audio recordings listened to each message and assigned it to Bales' categories. Those who were assigned the written logs for IM read each message and assigned it to Bales' categories too. All coders were blind to the research hypotheses and worked independently of each other on coding the messages. The average inter-coder reliability was 0.91 for positive affect communication.

The relative amount of communicated positive affect (which will be hereafter referred to as positive affect) was determined in the following manner for each message. Each coder assigned a value of 1 to the message for each Bales category that they believed reasonably described the message, and a 0 for all other categories. For example, if a coder believed a message to show solidarity and understanding, he/ she would code a 1 for category 1 and a 1 for category 3. The coders evaluations for this message were then averaged. For example, if the three coders all agreed on category 1 but only one coder perceived an instance of category 3, category 1 would have a value of 1, while category 3 would have a value of 0.33. These category values were then added together to form one number for positive affect (in this case equal to 1.33).

## 3.5. Operationalizations of concession

## 3.5.1. Initial concessions

Concession is the change of an offer in support of another party's interests that results in a reduction of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to one's self [69]. Though not typically examined, <sup>fi</sup>rst offers can include concessions [49,54]. For example, a seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer may be relatively low because he/she wants to increase the likelihood that the buyer will reciprocate with a concession of his/her own, which would be re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a relatively high counter offer [49].

It would be nice to determine an individual's initial concession by contrasting his/her <sup>fi</sup>rst offer with the degree to which he/she valued the lottery ticket. For example, we could show each seller the lottery wheel (see Fig. 2) prior to negotiations, and ask him/her what would be the least amount for which he/she would sell the ticket. Similarly, we could ask each buyer what would be the most amount for which he/she would buy the ticket. These a priori values revealed by the buyers and sellers are called reservation prices. We might then contrast their <sup>fi</sup>rst offers with their reservation prices in order to determine their degrees of concession. For example, if a seller's reservation price is 20 and his/her <sup>fi</sup>rst offer is 25, the difference between the two values (5) would re<sup>fl</sup>ect a greater concession than a <sup>fi</sup>rst offer of 30, which would have a difference of 10. That is, the closer a seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer is to his/her reservation price (i.e., the less the seller is willing to take), the more he/she could be said to be making a concession. Unfortunately researchers have found that once negotiations begin, typical buyer and seller behaviors suggest that reservation prices change [72]. For example, a seller who <sup>fi</sup>nds he/she dislikes the buyer may increase the reservation price, while a seller who <sup>fi</sup>nds he/ she likes or empathizes with the buyer may lower the reservation price [72].

Therefore, without an unobtrusive way to directly determine the values individuals place on their lottery tickets, we employed the following two measures to help tease out such values. The <sup>fi</sup>rst assumes that most buyers have similar negotiation strategies (e.g., either cooperative or competitive) and most sellers have similar negotiation strategies (e.g., either cooperative or competitive). We then compare an individual seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer to the average of all sellers' <sup>fi</sup>rst offers in order to determine the individual's level of concession. Similarly, we compare an individual buyer's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer to the average of all buyers' <sup>fi</sup>rst offers in order to determine the individual's level of concession. We call this concession measure “relative to average”, and say more about it later.

In order to reduce the impact of the above negotiation strategy assumption, we pair this measure with one that is based on each individual's perception of the expected value of his/her lottery ticket. Since perceived expected value can play a key role in how an individual values his/her ticket [11], we contrast buyer and seller <sup>fi</sup>rst offers with his/her own expected value of his/her ticket in order to determine concession. We refer to this concession measure as “relative to perceived expected value”. Though this allows for individuality in the measurement of concession, it still is lacking because individual characteristics, such as risk preferences, are not included in the determination of ticket value. While we acknowledge that using one or the other of these two measures would be problematic, we believe that using them together in our analyses is a reasonable approach to estimate <sup>fi</sup>rst offer concession. These two measures are described in detail next.

## 3.5.2. Initial concession relative to average

The relative to average measure was calculated for the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover (the <sup>fi</sup>rst to make an offer) as the difference between an individual's offer and the average offers made by all <sup>fi</sup>rst movers. This measure of concession varied with the negotiator role and the type of lottery ticket that was the object of negotiation. This is because one would expect a seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer to be higher than a buyer's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer. In addition, as described earlier, each dyad negotiated over one of two lottery tickets with different expected values, and these different values should in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rst offers. Table 1a supports these expectations. As illustrated, when the lottery ticket had a high expected value, the mean for buyers' <sup>fi</sup>rst offers was 19.9, while that for sellers was 27.2. In contrast, when the lottery ticket had a low expected value, buyers <sup>fi</sup>rst offers had a mean of 10.8, and sellers <sup>fi</sup>rst offers had a mean of 21.1.

Thus, one of these four different averages was employed when determining concession for a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover, depending on whether he/ she was a buyer or seller and depending on whether the lottery ticket had a high or low expected value. When measuring a seller's concession, the seller's offer was subtracted from the appropriate average; when measuring a buyer's concession, the appropriate average was subtracted from the buyer's offer. Thus, more (less) concession had larger (smaller) values. For example, if the lottery ticket had a high expected value and the seller was the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover offering the ticket for 30, this represents a concession of −2.8 ( = 27.2 − 30); a seller offer of 26 would represent a seller concession of 1.2 (=27.2−26). The 1.2 represents a greater concession by the seller.

The second mover in a dyad was the individual who made an offer in response to that of the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover. Second mover concession was calculated in a manner similar to that for the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover. If the second mover was the seller, his/her offer was subtracted from the average seller-as-<sup>fi</sup>rst-mover offer. As described above, we also take into account the type of ticket for which the offer is made. If the second mover was the buyer, the average for buyer-as <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover offer was subtracted from his/her offer. For example, if the lottery ticket had a high expected value and the buyer was the second mover offering to purchase the ticket for 22, this represents a concession of 2.1 (=22−19.9). The averages of seller and buyer <sup>fi</sup>rst offers were used since these offers were made in the absence of other offers, and would therefore better re<sup>fl</sup>ect any concession.

These concession measures are reasonable to the degree that the buyers and sellers acted relatively consistently. For example, if sellers were typically competitive, on average their <sup>fi</sup>rst offers would be relatively high, and lower <sup>fi</sup>rst offers could reasonably be described as concession. In contrast, if sellers were typically cooperative, on average their <sup>fi</sup>rst offers would be relatively low, and higher <sup>fi</sup>rst offers could reasonably be described as a lack of concession. Although these separate measures of concession are reasonable, they do not fully account for buyers' and sellers' initial concessions. What is missing is some measure that accounts for individual seller and buyer idiosyncrasies. We therefore employed the second concession measure, described next.

## 3.5.3. Initial concession relative to perceived expected value

Before beginning negotiations, each participant was shown the lottery ticket in a manner similar to that in Fig. 2. They were then asked the following two questions: “Based on the prize wheel, how do you rate your chance of winning the 12 points?” and “Based on the prize wheel, how do you rate your chance of winning the 34 points?” They were asked to respond to each question using a 1 to 7 scale, with a higher rating re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a greater chance. We use the <sup>fi</sup>nal result as a measure of perceived expected value for the ticket. ## 3.5.4. Total concession

In addition to concessions that might be included in <sup>fi</sup>rst offers, other concessions are typically made in subsequent offers. Some of these concessions might be unilateral, some might be reciprocated, while others might be matching [69]. Unilateral concessions are those offered by an individual that are not in direct response to a concession by the dyad partner [12,54]. If concessions are not unilateral, then they are either reciprocated or matching [69]. Reciprocated concessions are those that pay back (to some degree) unilateral concessions, and matching concessions are those that do not pay back unilateral concessions but that make the offers equal at the end of negotiations and therefore directly result in agreement.

In Table 1b example, the seller's movement from 30 to 27 represents a unilateral concession of 3; when the buyer counters with a movement from 15 to 20, this represents a reciprocation of the seller's unilateral concession of 3 and, in addition, a unilateral concession by the buyer of 2. When the seller counters with an offer of 25, this represents a reciprocation of the buyer's unilateral concession of 2. The buyer's <sup>fi</sup>nal agreement at 25 represents a matching concession of 5 because it is a concession that ends the negotiation process.

We measured these types of concessions and added them to those that are based on <sup>fi</sup>rst offers in order to get two overall measures of concession. The <sup>fi</sup>rst measure used the average value for the initial concession and the second measure used the perceived expected value for initial concession.

## 3.6. Operationalization of agreement

In the context of this study, an agreement occurred when negotiators' offers were equal, resulting in the lottery ticket being exchanged from seller to buyer. When this occurred, an agreement indicator had a value of 1. Otherwise, it had a value of 0.

## 3.7. Operationalization of control constructs

Some dyads started with IM negotiation, which was followed with Telephone negotiation, and for others the media ordering was reversed. A dummy control construct was included in the models described below with paths to communicated positive affect, concession, and agreement to account for any media ordering effects.

Earlier we noted that the lottery ticket had two expected values, depending on the prize wheel with which it was associated. One of these values, 21.1, is between the two potential values of the ticket, 12 and 34. In situations where the price of the ticket is judged with respect to this expected value, one would expect negotiators to <sup>fi</sup>nd a wide range of values over which concession can be made and both negotiators are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the outcome. In contrast, the other expected value is 2.9, which is much lower than the least potential value (12) of the ticket. In situations where the price of the ticket is judged with respect to this lower expected value, one would expect negotiators to be hard pressed to <sup>fi</sup>nd a wide range of values over which they can both make concessions and be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the outcome. In order to control for the differences in potential for concession that might be induced by these different expected values (which is due to the chances on the prize wheels), we included a dummy chance control construct (not shown, but evaluated in research our model) with paths to concession and agreement.

## 4.2. Multilevel model analyses

Hypotheses 2 and 5 require a multilevel data modeling approach, which examines the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of affect and media on concession within dyads over time as well as across dyads. In this case, time is represented by an offer by the buyer or seller within each dyad. Thus, for example, the dyad illustrated in Table 1b would have 6 longitudinal points in time. For each offer, dyad concession is determined by accumulating all prior concessions up to and including the speci<sup>fi</sup>c offer. For example, the concession (relative to average) associated with offer number 3 for the Table 1b dyad would equal 5.7 (=−1.5+4.23+3), while the concession (relative to expected value) would equal −12 (=−8.7−6.3+3). The amount of positive affect is determined similarly, but with a one message lag. For example, the positive affect that is associated with offer number 3 in the Table would consist of the sum of all positive affect from all messages up to but not including the message that contained the third offer. This allows the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of all positive affect in messages that are antecedent to each offer.

Using this approach, time is not consistent within or across dyads. For example, two different dyads may make 6 offers but the time between each offer may (and probably did) differ among offers within each dyad as well as among offers with the same number (e.g., the second offer for each dyad) across dyads. In addition, the number of offers will typically differ across dyads; we found some having as few as two and some having more than 20. A modeling approach that can handle such data is described by Singer and Willett [79], and can be estimated using most of the common statistical packages. In accord with Singer and Willett, we employed the Mixed procedure using full maximum likelihood estimation that is provided in SPSS version 15. In order to make the results compatible with PLS results, data for all variables were standardized before being used in the procedure.

## 5. Discussion

It is well established that positive feelings lead to more cooperation and agreement [59]. However, understanding the way in which communication media in<sup>fl</sup>uence these feelings during negotiations is becoming increasingly important, and has only recently stimulated the interest of researchers [85]. As detailed in the introduction, our work extends negotiation research by (1) looking beyond the give-and-take exchanges of offers upon which most scholars have focused [50,55,92], and including an examination of the unstructured communication process, (2) providing insight into how communication media in<sup>fl</sup>uence the negotiation process as well as outcomes, and (3) speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examining the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of affect during computer-mediated negotiations.

We found a number of interesting results and questions that follow from them. First, we found that IM reduces concession. We explain this outcome in terms of the anonymity and reduced social presence that this medium provides to negotiators. We argue that as a result of such anonymity, negotiators are less concerned about equity and reciprocity. As such, they are less willing to engage in concession. This result leads to an interesting question: How can strangers use IM and avoid the risk of reduced concession? Answers might involve the negotiators initially exchanging information that could reduce the level of their anonymity. In this regard, Morris et al. [61] have suggested that negotiators engage in personalized conversations over the phone prior to negotiating via email.

Second, we found that negotiators, who use IM, rather than telephone, have a reduced communication of positive affect. We explain this outcome in terms of IM's lack of audibility, simultaneity, and sequentiality. The question then arises: Can these limitations of IM be overcome? Recent advances in IM software indicate that two of these limitations might be overcome. For example, Trillian is multiprotocol IM software that provides audio communication, which can eliminate the lack of audibility of traditional IM. It also records the time stamp for messages and so it can also reduce the lack of sequentiality associated with traditional IM.

Third, we found that media differences moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of affective communication on concession. To understand the full implications of this result, it helps to examine each medium separately. We found that, while positive communicated affect increased concession with telephone, there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with IM. This runs counter to traditional wisdom, which suggests that, though IM may reduce the level of positive communicated affect, the relationship between positive communicated affect and concession will still hold. We hypothesized that this lack of in<sup>fl</sup>uence was the result of individuals' fears of being exploited by their opponents because IM increases depersonalization and provides fewer cues on which to evaluate their opponents' trustworthiness. The fact that negotiators were strangers further exacerbated these feelings. As a result, individuals may have attributed positive communicated affect as part of a manipulation strategy.

This result is important because it suggests that efforts to increase the conveyance of positive affect without increasing individuals abilities to determine opponent trustworthiness may be wasted. While the suggestion that negotiators be trained to communicate positive affect may work to increase concession for telephone negotiations, this is less appropriate with IM. However, the Trillian software described above, which adds audio to IM text, may be more effective, since the transfer of audio cues may increase individuals' perceptions that they can determine their opponents' trustworthiness. Another alternative is to have negotiators engage in positive affective communication over the phone prior to, or concurrent with, negotiation via IM. This suggestion is in line with that recommended by Morris et al. [61].

## 5.1. Limitations and future research opportunities

External validity, and thus generalizability of our study is limited to non-face-to-face negotiations between individuals that are strangers to each other. However, this limitation is not as serious as it might seem since many negotiators do not know each other, at least during initial agreements. For example, there is an increasing proliferation of online dispute resolution services which permit direct negotiation between strangers who make purchases and sales online [43,88,97].

Our results suggest that, compared to negotiating via IM, negotiating via telephone better facilitates agreement when negotiators are strangers. It would be interesting to examine to what degree this relationship holds as parties become more familiar with each other. Our expectation is that the superiority of telephone over IM will decrease because over time individuals are able to overcome the cue limitations of various media [8,13,94].

Our study was also based on a relatively brief encounter between negotiators concerned with a single issue (price). This is not as unusual as it might appear because time limits are common in negotiation as negotiators desire an end to the uncertainty and emotional demands of the process and their constituents often press for quick resolution [14,19]. Furthermore, although many negotiations are concerned with multiple issues, they tend to progress on an issueby-issue basis [61], with a single issue (such as price) often dominating while other substantive issues are considered tangential [3,48]. Nevertheless, it is possible that if negotiators had more time for negotiation and were concerned with multiple issues, more complex communication and concession would occur. For example, more time allows for rumination by each individual, which can make problems seem larger [52], increase angry moods [75], and increase the psychological investment each opponent has with his/her argument [37]. Future studies that permit IM negotiations for longer periods and that involve multiple issues will provide an opportunity to examine the generalizability of our results.

Finally our study is focused on what Lim and Benbasat [51] refer to as an economic model of negotiation. As in most studies on this type of negotiation, our model is involves a bilateral monopoly [69]. That is one in which there is a single seller and a single buyer who must either agree on speci<sup>fi</sup>c terms of exchange between themselves or fail to exchange the object of their negotiation. But there are other models of this type of negotiation with more than two parties. Since more than two persons can communicate via IM and telephone, it would be interesting to investigate whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend to more than two parties. We suspect that social contagion will still promote the spread of affect [85,86,90], and that the type of communication media will remain an important in<sup>fl</sup>uence. However, different media might have a much greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the formation of coalitions (that can only exist in negotiations with more than two parties) as suggested by media richness theory [17].

## 5.2. Implications for practitioners

Many organizations are more likely to use telephone and computer mediated communication, such as IM, to conduct negotiations rather than face-to-face communication [59]. This study describes some challenges that they are likely to encounter and suggests how these challenges might be overcome. First, though IM can lower the cost of negotiation and increase its speed [43], this type of communication decreases concession. To overcome this problem, efforts must be directed at reducing the anonymity and depersonalization enabled by IM. Such efforts include setting aside time for negotiators to focus on getting to know each other. Second, since the communication of positive affect over IM tends not to increase individuals' positive affective states, it may be helpful if individuals are encouraged to communicate positive affect prior to, or concurrent with, negotiations via a richer medium, such as telephone. Third, organizations might increase their use of multiprotocol IM applications (such as Trillian) that are equipped with features, such as audio communication, that bring the characteristics of IM closer to that of telephone.

In summary, we proposed and empirically demonstrated that communication media employed in negotiations among strangers can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the negotiation process. IM was found to reduce concession as well as the preponderance of positive communicated affect that occurred naturally during negotiations. IM also negated the potential in<sup>fl</sup>uence of this positive communicated affect on concession, when negotiators are strangers to each other. Since concession is important for agreement, our work provides a better understanding of why researchers consistently <sup>fi</sup>nd fewer agreements being reached with computer mediated communication, such as IM, when compared to telephone or face-to-face negotiations. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend current research by providing a micro-level examination of naturally occurring communicated affect and its relationships with media, concession, and agreement.
