## ABSTRACT

The relationship between system usage and individual impact has received considerable attention in prior studies. However, the results for the relationship have been mixed. This study hypothesizes that the variation in the effect of system usage on individual impact may be voluntariness of use, number of organizations, number of respondents, type of respondents, and information system type, while controlling for geographic region and industry sector using metaregression methods to examine the findings from 74 studies published between 1992 and 2018. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

Keywords: System usage, Individual impact, Meta-regression

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The relationship between system usage and individual impact has been an enduring concern for information systems (IS) research and practice over time. Incorporated in models of IS success (DeLone and McLean 1992; 2003), the relationship underlines the importance of and individual impact—for individuals and organizations.

Prior research has given considerable attention to the relationship between system usage and individual impact. Somewhat inconsistent with theoretical expositions and practical expectations, the empirical results for the relationship have been mixed, including contradictory and inconsistent effects. While a positive relationship between system usage and individual impact is generally expected, prior studies have also found negative (e.g., Saeed et al. 2010) and no (e.g., Yoon and Guimaraes 1995) relationships between the constructs. Moreover, Chuang et al. (2012) found a positive effect whereas Wu and Wang (2006) reported no effect for system use on individual impact in examining similar systems (i.e., knowledge management system); Yoon and Guimaraes (1995) found no relationship while Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) found a positive relationship for system usage on individual impact in studies set in the same geographic region (i.e., North America); and Tong et al. (2015) found no effect while Sun et al. (2009) found a positive effect for system usage on individual impact when using the same type of respondents (i.e., professionals) in the survey.

Collectively, these results indicate the need for a closer examination of the relationship between system usage and individual impact. This study hypothesizes that the variation seen in the effect of system usage on individual impact across prior studies may be attributed to the

##

variation in the research contexts employed. In other words, as systems are designed to gain benefits, the differences in results may be driven by the differences in the empirical research designs of studies. Prior meta-analysis studies in the IS domain have offered clues that contextual factors may influence the variation in results (e.g., Dennis and Wixom 2001; Sharma and Yetton 2003; Wu and Lederer 2008). However, quantitative meta-analysis studies largely provide aggregations of effect sizes (e.g., correlation) across studies for a single bivariate relationship—e.g., system usage and user satisfaction (Bokhari 2005), user involvement and system success (Hwang and Thorn 1999)—or multiple bivariate relationships (e.g., He and King 2008; Ma and Liu 2004; Petter and McLean 2009; Sabherwal et al. 2006) without attending to the contextual factors.

In the context of system usage and individual impact, the results may vary because of differences in various attributes across studies. Geographic region may be a factor because organizations in more developed regions of the world may rely on IS to a greater extent relative to developing regions. Voluntariness could be a factor because individuals may experience varying levels of control over their decisions to use IS including mandatory use and voluntary use. Individuals may continue to use mandated systems whether or not they gain significant benefits, whereas they may not use voluntary systems as much but may experience considerable impact when they do so. The type of IS may be a factor in that individuals using complex feature-heavy systems may expend considerable time on the system to gain benefits relative to simpler feature-light systems that may not demand as much time. Therefore, this study tackles the research question: What characteristics of the research context influence variation in the effect of system usage on individual impact?

Meta-regression, a method in the family of meta-analysis techniques that enables the examination of patterns across multiple studies, is used to gain unique insights that cannot be provided by a single study (Stanley and Jarrell 1989; Stanley 2001; Kohli and Devaraj 2003; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj 2015). This study analyzes empirical results on the relationship between system usage and individual impact and data on contextual variables coded from 74 prior studies published between 1992 and 2018. This study provides preliminary evidence for the influence of research contexts on the effect of system usage on individual impact. Specifically, the effect of system usage on individual impact is influenced by the number of organizations, number of respondents, type of respondents, information system type, and geographic region but not by the voluntariness of use and industry sector.

## 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

The model of IS success proposes relationships among several constructs—i.e., system user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact—that may be used to examine IS success (DeLone and McLean 1992). This model was based on a narrative analysis of 100 studies published between 1981 and 1988 in seven publication outlets. The measures used to represent the six constructs were analyzed and aggregated and the model of IS success was proposed with interrelationships between the six constructs. Later revisions of the IS success model (DeLone and McLean 2003) combined individual impact and organizational impact into a single construct named net benefits, but also proposed service quality as an additional construct.

Two constructs—i.e., system usage and individual impact—found in the model of IS success provide the basis for this research. The models of IS success (DeLone and McLean 1992; 2003) propose that system usage directly influences individual impact. Despite the proposed relationship between system usage and individual impact (or net benefits) in the IS success models, prior studies have taken different approaches in examining the relationships in the IS success models. For instance, system quality and information quality—typically considered as antecedents of system usage in models of IS success—have been modeled as antecedents of net benefits while excluding system usage (e.g., Wixom and Watson 2001; Hwang and Xu 2008). Furthermore, task-technology fit (e.g., Lee et al. 2007), user satisfaction (e.g., McGill and Klobas 2005), and perceived usefulness (e.g., Rai et al. 2002) have been examined as direct antecedents of net benefits while excluding system usage.

The exclusion of system usage in prior research models may be attributed to various reasons. Common system usage measures such as duration of use and frequency of use may not provide an accurate assessment of success (e.g., Wixom and Watson 2001). System usage may seem irrelevant depending on the context of use—e.g., individuals may be required to use the system or may not have a choice in using the system (i.e., mandatory use), individuals may not be required to use the system but they may not have an alternate choice (i.e., quasi-voluntary), and individuals may have complete flexibility in using the system (i.e., voluntary use) (e.g., Rai et al. 2002). While system usage may seem relevant for voluntary use contexts, it may not seem as relevant for mandatory and quasi-voluntary use contexts. However, system usage is viewed as essential to individual impact in this study because individuals can realize benefits only when the system is actually used.

## 2.3. Research on System Usage and Individual Impact

The relationship between system usage and individual impact has received considerable attention in prior empirical literature. Models of IS success (DeLone and McLean 1992) propose a positive relationship between system usage and individual impact, i.e., users gain greater individual impact when they use the IS to a greater extent—which has been supported in prior empirical research (e.g., Schiffman et al. 1992; Yuthas and Young 1998; Deng et al. 2004; Venkatesh et al. 2017). However, findings have not always shown a positive relationship—for instance, studies found no relationship (e.g., Yoon and Guimaraes 1995; Iivari 2005; Tong et al. 2015) between system usage and individual impact. The unexpected nonpositive effects of system usage on individual impact may be in part due to the extent of discretion individuals possess in using the system (e.g., Iivari (2005) examined a system that was mandatory in nature), and the type of IS or the type of respondents (e.g., Yoon and Guimaraes (1995) dealt with executive IS meant for a specific class of users: executives).

The variation in the effect of system usage on individual impact across studies may thus be attributable to the differences in the research contexts. Studies examined various types of IS such as decision support systems (e.g., Szajna 1993), executive information systems (EIS) (e.g., Leidner and Elam 1993), enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems (e.g., Nwankpa 2015), knowledge management systems (Ko and Dennis 2011), and office productivity systems (e.g., Benlian 2015). Research has been situated in both voluntary-use (e.g., Sun et al. 2014) and mandatory-use contexts (e.g., Saeed et al. 2010). Both students (e.g., Rai et al. 2002) and professionals (e.g., Urbach et al. 2010) participated in research studies. The number of respondents ranged from low (< 100) (e.g., Leidner and Elam 1993) to high (> 1000) (e.g., Zhang 2017) across the studies. Research participants came from a single organization (e.g., Rai et al. 2002) or multiple organizations (e.g., Igbaria and Zviran 1996) within a single study and belonged to the manufacturing (e.g., Hou 2012) or service (e.g., Zhang 2017) sectors. Studies were set in different geographic regions including the Americas (e.g., Rai and Hornyak 2013), Europe (e.g., Gelderman 1998), Asia (e.g., Hsu et al. 2015), Australia (e.g., Ferguson 2007), Africa (e.g., Cohen and Kangethe 2015), and the Middle East (e.g., Rouibah et al. 2009).

Moreover, prior studies employed different designs such as lab or field experiments (e.g., Szajna 1993; Yuthas and Young 1998) or surveys (Leidner and Elam 1993; Nwankpa 2015) to gather data. Data analyses were conducted using different methods such as regression (e.g., Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Dasgupta et al. 2002), structural equation modeling with PLS, AMOS, or LISREL (e.g., Norzaidi et al. 2007; Saeed et al. 2010; Venkatesh et al. 2011), correlation (e.g., Benlian 2014; Rai and Hornyak 2013), and ANOVA family of techniques (e.g., Leidner and Elam 1993; Tan et al. 2012). Studies have been conducted over a period of time, during which time the nature, types, and capabilities of IS have undergone significant changes potentially engendering varying levels and types of impact for individuals.

The research context characteristics are based on prior meta-analysis studies that examined moderators or contextual factors. For instance, type of tool (system), type of group, and group size exerted different effects in the context of group support systems (Dennis and Wixom 2001); task interdependence altered the relationship between management support and implementation success (Sharma and Yetton 2003); data sources and research methods influenced the relationship between information technology investments and payoff (Kohli and Devaraj 2003); environment-based voluntariness impacted the relationships underlying the technology acceptance model (Wu and Lederer 2008); and the study’s methodological attributes and information technology progress (represented as time periods) altered the effect of information technology investments and business value of information technology (Sabherwal and Jeyaraj 2015). Such characteristics typically represent attributes of: the organizations or individuals, the information system, and the research methodology (e.g., research method referred to regression or non-regression method in Kohli and Devaraj 2003), and are relevant for the research problem (e.g., type of group represented virtual teams or other in the context of group support systems in Dennis and Wixom 2001).

## 3.1. Voluntariness of Use

Prior literature distinguishes between voluntary and mandatory use (e.g., Venkatesh and Davis 2000) based on the extent to which individuals can exercise control over the decision to use IS. Users may have control over the IS they can choose, the features of the IS they can use, and the extent to which they can use the IS.

Voluntary-use contexts allow individuals to determine the types of IS they want to use and exercise discretion over the features and capabilities of the system they use even when use of the system itself may be mandated (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003; Jasperson et al. 2005). In such contexts, systems are typically “pulled” by individuals based on their own search efforts and assessment of needs. As a result, such IS are more consistent with the needs of individuals and likely to provide them performance benefits. Mandatory-use contexts, by contrast, require individuals to use IS preselected by decision makers or use specific features and capabilities of the IS in compliance with the managerial or supervisory directives (e.g., Venkatesh and Davis 2000; Jasperson et al. 2005). Such IS are generally instituted by senior managers or superviso and “pushed” to individuals for use. As a result, mandated IS may encounter hurdles in initial acceptance and subsequent usage by individuals because they may not have provided any feedback into its development and implementation, their needs may not be completely satisfied by the IS, and they may require relearning due to upgrades and rollouts. It is also possible that individuals engage in limited use of the IS to meet institutional or managerial directives rather than to maximize benefits. Thus, system usage in voluntary-use contexts is likely to provide greater performance benefits to individuals. Therefore,

H1: The effect for the relationship between system usage and individual impact is higher for voluntary use than mandatory use.

## 3.2. Number of Organizations

Studies have examined system usage based on empirical data from a single organization (e.g., Rai et al. 2002) or multiple organizations (e.g., Igbaria and Zviran 1996). An organization possesses unique characteristics such as culture, identity, credibility of the IS function, and technological orientation, but there is greater heterogeneity in these aspects across organizations. The implementation of even similar IS within one organization may be very different from other organizations; however, organizations may implement varying types of IS, which could further amplify the differences between organizations. Moreover, the purposes for and the acceptance or resistance to the IS may differ between organizations, and hence, the IS may not be used similarly or to the same extent across organizations. Consequently, the effect of system usage on individual impact is likely to be subdued due to homogeneity within one organization, whereas it is enhanced because of heterogeneity across multiple organizations. Therefore,

H2: The effect for the relationship between system usage and individual impact is lower for a single organization than multiple organizations.

## 3.3. Number of Respondents

The number of respondents, referred to as the sample size, typically varies across the studies. Studies generally strive to meet or exceed a recommended minimum sample size to facilitate data analysis. Lower sample sizes are known to decrease statistical power (e.g., Baroudi and Orlikowski 1989) such that researchers strive to increase the sample size to the extent possible—which implies that the effect of system usage on individual impact is likely to be higher when the sample size is larger. Furthermore, the practical reason for such an expectation is compelling as well. The usage and impact experienced by a single individual may be lower than the aggregate usage and impact experienced by multiple individuals. Therefore,

H3: The effect for the relationship between system usage and individual impact is positively related to sample size.

## 3.4. Type of Respondents

Two types of respondents—students and professionals—have been commonly used in prior studies of system usage and individual impact (e.g., Rai et al. 2002; Urbach et al. 2010). Students used as respondents generally belong to undergraduate or graduate programs in academic institutions, and are typically tasked with: using a system as part of their course requirements to obtain relevant information, completing course projects or assignments using a system over a predetermined time period, or engaging in specific tasks for a research project that solicited their participation (e.g., Sun et al. 2014). Professionals are typically employees of small-and-medium or large enterprises, and use the system on the job for their everyday tasks, or perform specific tasks for a research project (e.g., Hsu et al. 2015). Professionals are likely to be aware of and have access to a broader range of IS than students. They would have greater opportunities to assess the capabilities and appropriateness of different IS for their purposes, and choose specific IS to accomplish specific tasks that can improve their performance on the job. Therefore, professionals are likely to have a more accurate rating of the extent to which system usage may offer performance impacts.

H4: The effect for the relationship between system usage and individual impact is higher for students than for professionals.

## 3.5. Information System Type

Different types of IS have been examined in the context of system usage. Prior research has striven to distinguish and classify the various types of IS. These include Type I (i.e., IS core process), Type II (i.e., IS products and services in the administrative core), and Type III (i.e., IS products and services with core business technology) innovations (Swanson 1994); information technology base, system development, and services (Lyttinen and Rose 2003); business process applications, communications and collaboration systems, computers, office applications, system software, and internet applications (Jasperson et al. 2005); and function, network, and enterprise information technologies (McAfee 2006).

Function IS generally refers to systems that enable standalone tasks or independent activities for specific roles or activities within departments. Examples of such systems include sales order entry systems, payroll processing systems, and production planning systems. Function IS can be differentiated from other types of systems such as network IS and enterprise IS—i.e., network IS enable interaction and collaboration between users, whereas enterprise IS support activities that span multiple departments or the entire organization.

Relative to network IS and enterprise IS (which involve or impact multiple users, tasks, and processes), function IS are more restricted in scope and impact. Individuals may not have to use function IS beyond their specific purposes and may not have to use them as frequently as they may use other types of IS. Function IS may not provide the opportunities to collaborate or interact with other individuals compared to network and enterprise IS. A combination of these dynamics suggests that the effect of system usage on individual impact will be lower for function IS relative to other types of IS.

H5: The effect for the relationship between system usage and individual impact is lower for function IS than other types.

## 3.6. Control Variables

In recognition of other potential effects on the relationship between system usage and individual impact, two variables are considered. First, geographic region could be a factor because of the considerable variation across regions in infrastructure, culture, and work. Second, the industry sector could be a factor because of the significant differences in both internal and external business processes with manufacturing and service sectors.

## 4. DATA AND ANALYSIS

## 4.1. Data

Data were gathered from journal articles published between 1992 (i.e., the year of publication of the original IS success model) and 2018 (i.e., the most recent year for which the articles were available at the time of data collection). The main sources for identifying studies were the online databases including Business Source Complete, Electronic Journal Center (includes ScienceDirect), IEEE Explore, and ACM Digital Library. Primary keywords for the search included “system usage,” “use,” and “utilization” on the metadata fields such as abstract, author-supplied keywords, and title. Refinements included additional keywords such as “success,” “impacts,” and “benefits” where possible using “search within results” option and restrictors such as “scholarly peer-reviewed journals” (in Business Source Complete), “research articles” (in ScienceDirect), “journals” (in IEEE Explore), or “periodicals” (in ACM Digital Library). Bibliographies of the articles included for analysis and studies that reviewed or aggregated prior literature were also reviewed to identify potential articles (e.g., DeLone and McLean 2003; Petter et al. 2008; 2013). The search yielded 410 articles.

Studies were discarded if they were published in journals that may be considered out of scope for the IS discipline (e.g., Journal of Hazardous Materials). At this stage, 165 articles were excluded and 245 articles remained for possible inclusion in the analysis sample. Studies were excluded from consideration if they: a) only offered theoretical descriptions, narrative reviews, or qualitative research (e.g., Fisher and Howell 2004; Mallmann et al. 2018); b) dealt with nonindividual units of analysis such as an organization or a group/team (e.g., Lee and Lim 2003; Mishra et al. 2007; Stone et al. 2007); c) reported results of a meta-analysis (e.g., Bokhari 2005; Wu and Lederer 2009); and d) did not examine the relationship between system usage and individual impact—i.e., study may have examined system usage or individual impact but not both—(e.g., Bock et al. 2008; Wixom and Watson 2001; Wu and Kuo 2008). Overall, 74 studies were identified for the analysis<sup>1</sup>.

## 4.2. Coding

Each study was coded as a unique observation except when a study reported multiple samples—three studies (Benlian 2015; Jain and Kanungo 2005; Sun et al. 2014) contributed multiple observations. A coding sheet was used to consistently code the findings reported in studies. The main statistic coded was the result of the relationship between system usage and individual impact (such as individual performance, net benefits, or user satisfaction) reported in the study: 1 (positive), 0 (no effect), and -1 (negative). Other relevant data coded for the study included: the focal IS, voluntariness of use, number of organizations, sample size (number of respondents), type of respondents, geographic region, and industry sector.

The variables were coded as is when the original study reported the relevant data or usable descriptions. Coding rules were developed and applied when necessary. The result for the effect of system usage on individual impact was coded using the regression results, when a study reported both Pearson correlations and regression coefficients.

Voluntariness of use was coded based on the description provided in the respective papers: for instance, Ferguson (1997) reported “captive use,” Rai et al. (2002) mentioned “quasi voluntary,” Urbach et al. (2010) specified “non-mandatory,” Dwivedi et al. (2013) described “self-service,” and Guimaraes et al. (1996) stated “voluntary.” Voluntariness was coded “voluntary” when the study: a) did not describe the context of use (e.g., Torkzadeh et al. 2011) partly due to the type of IS such as email and spreadsheet examined in the study; and b) did not provide clear or sufficient description about the context (e.g., Lee et al. 2005) because voluntaryuse contexts are the most common settings found in empirical studies on system usage and individual impact. Voluntariness was coded as “mandatory” for experiments (e.g., Szajna 1993; Yuthas and Young 1996) because the research participants typically did not have a choice but to use the IS provided.

The number of organizations from which research participants were recruited for the studies was used to code whether the study dealt with a single organization or more than one organization. The number of respondents was coded using the sample size, which was logtransformed for data analysis to handle skewness in the sample sizes between studies. The type of respondents was coded to differentiate between “students” and others such as employees, professionals, and citizens.

Information system type was based on the focal IS specified in the study, and coded to differentiate between function IS and other types such as enterprise IS (e.g., ERP systems), network IS (e.g., social media), or IS infrastructure (e.g., computers, workstations). Decisions to code as function IS were based on the relevant descriptions in the studies—e.g., Torkzadeh and Doll (1999) explained that their study represented 139 applications such as accounts payable, budgeting, financial planning, production planning, and purchasing, while Yoon and Guimaraes (1995) described that the focal organization in their study (IBM Corporation) had implemented over 460 expert system applications in areas, such as process control, integrated testing and analysis, and multimedia systems.

The geographic region was coded based on the authors’ address or details of grants if unspecified in the original study (e.g., Zhang 2017). Industry sector was coded as both Barki et al. 2007).

Overall, 77 observations were coded from the studies (because three studies contributed multiple observations due to multiple samples or systems examined). ## 4.3. Analysis

Meta-analysis allows the aggregation of quantitative results from prior studies and the examination of similarities and differences across studies to reconcile the apparently inconsistent findings (Hunt 1997). These methods also help generate new insights about the relationships of interest across multiple studies, which cannot be obtained from a single prior study (Sabherwal and Jeyaraj 2015). This study employed a specific method known as meta-regression (e.g., Kohli and Devaraj 2003; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj 2015) to analyze the data. The meta-regression method may be used to examine a DV (based on an aggregation across multiple studies) using multiple independent variables (representing various study-level characteristics) to develop insights across studies that may not be possible within a single study (Stanley and Jarrell 1989). Unlike the quantitative meta-analysis method (e.g., aggregating effect sizes) that may exclude prior studies that did not report relevant statistics (e.g., correlation), the meta-regression method used in this study does not exclude quantitative studies because the results (and not the effect sizes) are coded. Meta-regression methods are also appropriate for this study because of the simple research model involving direct relationships between the independent and DVs unlike metaanalytic structural equation modeling (e.g., Sabherwal et al. 2006) that handles complex research models involving direct and mediating effects on the DV.

The DV was computed as a continuous measure, which represented the proportion of findings on the relationship between system usage and individual impact that were significant relative to the number of findings examined in a study. For the main analysis, linear regression methods in SPSS 24 were used in two steps: the first step contained the control variables only (i.e., geographic region and industry sector) while the second step also included the variables for the hypothesized relationships.

Additional analyses were conducted to validate the findings. First, a logistic regression analysis was conducted using a categorical (binary) DV indicating whether or not the relationship between system usage and individual impact was positive. Second, linear regression analyses were conducted with the continuous DV but with alternate measures for the two control variables. For geographic region, an alternate variable representing regions other than North sector, an alternate variable representing both the manufacturing and service sectors was used instead of the variable for the service sector only. Finally, linear regression analyses were conducted with the continuous DV but with alternate measures for two of the five hypothesized variables. Specifically, alternate variables were used to represent voluntariness of use and information system type, the two constructs for which other variables could be envisioned. For voluntariness of use, an alternate variable representing voluntary use was used instead of the one for mandatory use. For information system type, an alternate variable representing network IS was used instead of function IS.

## 5.2. Discussion

This study was aimed at understanding the characteristics of the research contexts in studies that may influence the variation of the effect of system usage on individual impact across studies. While controlling for geographic region and industry sector, the research model hypothesized that voluntariness of use, number of organizations, number of respondents, type of respondents, and information system type may influence the variation in the effect of system usage on individual impact.

Table 7 provides a summary of the findings of the research. On the basis of the geographic region variable, individuals in the North American region experienced greater levels of system usage and individual impact relative to individuals in other regions. This could be due to various reasons such as: the quality, usefulness, and usability of IS being generally higher; the general dependence and acceptance of IS by individuals being higher; and the infrastructure, training, and support for IS being higher—all of which may drive IS usage in the North American region to a greater extent than in other regions resulting in increased individual impact. There were no significant differences in system usage and individual impact between individuals in the service sector relative to the manufacturing sector. This finding may be due to both organizations and individuals depending on IS as well as the availability, and ubiquity of IS for routine operations and everyday activities regardless of the industry sector.

Voluntariness of use was nonsignificant (H1), which indicates that there are no significant differences in the effect of system usage on individual impact between voluntary-use and mandatory-use contexts. This is somewhat surprising because voluntary-use contexts, in which individuals have the choice to opt for IS appropriate for their needs or use features of the system that are more relevant and appropriate for their specific tasks, could be expected to offer greater performance benefits than mandatory-use contexts, in which individuals are constrained to use IS mandated for use or restricted to specific sets of features that may or may not completely address their needs. Two potential explanations are possible for this finding—first, the IS mandated for use have proven effective for individuals in their respective work environments, and second, the IS chosen by individuals have not provided as much value as may have been expected.

The number of organizations (i.e., single organization vs. multiple organizations) had a negative influence on the effect of system usage and individual impact (H2). As hypothesized, this result implies that the effect of system usage on individual impact is lower when a single organization is considered but higher when multiple organizations are examined. As any organization may have its idiosyncratic cultural, social, and technological arrangements, it may be that a research study involving a single organization may not always be able to uncover a stronger effect of system usage on individual impact.

The number of respondents (log measure of sample size) positively influenced the effect of system usage on individual impact (H3). Consistent with the hypothesis, the effect of system usage on individual impact increased with an increase in the number of respondents in the study. It is not uncommon that the experiences of individuals with IS are different even for the same system—individuals differ in their beliefs and attitudes (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003; Sabherwal et al. 2006), which can lead to different behaviors relating to system usage, which in turn affects individual impact. Individuals have different levels of system usage, and all else being equal, the greater the number of individuals who report their usage, the greater the individual impact.

The type of respondents (i.e., students vs. professionals) had a significant influence on the effect of system usage on individual impact (H4). As hypothesized, the effect of system usage on individual impact was greater for students compared with professionals. As can be expected, students and professionals differ in their abilities and expectations, it is worthwhile to note that the IS used by students were more satisfactory and useful to them than those that were used by professionals, which raises questions about the quality and usefulness of IS in practice. These results also imply that professionals may report lower usage of IS than students, which could be due to greater expertise that require less time with the IS, habitual use such that they can complete the tasks faster, or minimal use to satisfy mandates.

Information system type (i.e., function IS vs. other IS) had a significant influence on the effect of system usage on individual impact (H5). Consistent with the hypothesis, the effect of system usage on individual impact was lower for function IS relative to other types of IS. Validation tests showed that the effect of system usage on individual impact: a) was higher for network IS when compared to other types of IS, and b) was not significantly different between enterprise IS and other types of IS. The combination of these findings suggests that IS designed for standalone usage or restricted to specific roles or internal activities of specific departments may be used to a lesser extent by individuals, and hence, may not yield as much impact as other types of IS, but IS that support interaction and collaboration between individuals may be used to a greater extent and offer greater impacts than other types of IS.

## 5.3. Limitations

The findings of the study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, the analysis was based on a meta-regression of findings reported in prior literature without access to the original data. This study assumes the robustness of the empirical studies included for data analysis. Second, the data for this study were coded based on the description and data provided in prior studies. Where the descriptions were insufficient or unknown, certain assumptions were made in coding the studies (as reported earlier). It is possible that the data thus coded may not be completely consistent with the research contexts of the original studies. Third, the coding process generally yielded binary variables (except for specific ones such as sample size), which is not ideal for all situations. Fourth, despite the additional tests to verify internal and external validity of the findings, it is possible that inclusion of additional studies or recoding the variables using other considerations may alter the findings of the study. ## 5.4. Implications for Research

Several implications for research may be drawn from the study. First, a more accurate estimate of the effect of system usage on individual impact may be possible when studies include multiple organizations in the research. Studies situated in the context of a single organization showed a lower effect of system usage on individual impact relative to those that were situated in multiple organizations. While case studies of a single organization are considered to be valuable in IS research and can provide rich explanations of system usage and individual impact, they may be less amenable to generalizability because of the idiosyncrasies within a single organization including the technology culture, user involvement, and facilitating conditions.

Second, the nonsignificance of voluntariness of use on the relationship between system usage and individual impact is intriguing. The finding seems to suggest that the voluntary-use contexts may not provide any advantages over mandatory-use contexts (though individuals can choose more appropriate systems for their needs) and/or that the mandatory-use contexts provide similar performance advantages as voluntary-use contexts (through vastly improved systems). Given that system usage may be symbolic and satisficing in mandatory-use contexts and may be based on productivity and need in voluntary-use contexts, the nonsignificance of voluntariness of use may benefit from future research. Furthermore, even mandatory-use systems may not be truly mandatory in the sense that individuals may have to necessarily use certain features of the system (e.g., routine reports, accounts receivables, and accounts payables) but have discretion over other features of the system (e.g., ad hoc reports and collaborative tasks). Future research may attend to the nuances in voluntariness and the extent to which such variations are likely to influence system usage leading to individual impact.

Third, the type of respondents may be a significant consideration for research. The effect of system usage on individual impact was significantly higher for students than for professionals in this study. This raises the question of whether students overestimate while professionals underestimate the effect of system usage. Compared to professionals, students may lack the necessary skills, experience, and domain knowledge to provide an accurate evaluation of the IS, which could result in overestimating the effect of system usage on individual impact. Another potential question relates to whether students and professionals possess significantly different expectations regarding the IS. While students may have specific needs and goals to be met by the IS, professionals may strive for best-in-class solutions for their problems. The finding also calls into question the extent to which students may be used as proxies—a widely prevalent trend in IS research—to evaluate the extent to which IS enables the work activities of professionals.

Fourth, the relationship between system usage and individual impact was altered by IS type. Based on the classification of function IS, enterprise IS, and network IS, results showed that network IS generated greater system usage and individual impact. However, function IS and enterprise IS—the two types of systems traditionally associated with organizations and their operations—showed lower or no effect of system usage on individual impact relative to other types. As the lines between the different types of IS become blurred in modern times—for instance, enterprise IS include certain capabilities of network IS such as communication if not collaboration—future research may examine the extent to which system usage may be influenced with changes in system capabilities.

Finally, the significant effect of geographic region (although not hypothesized)—which shows the effect of system usage on individual impact to be higher in the North American region—raises questions as to why there are differences by geographic region. While it may be reasonable to expect considerable differences in culture, norms, and regulations to differ between geographic regions, it is undeniable that IS are prevalent in most regions of the world and that organizations and individuals rely on IS for operations and activities. It may be useful to examine if the differences in the relationship between system usage and individual impact are due to the nature of IS, ways in which IS are used by individuals across regions, or other related drivers.

## 5.5. Implications for Practice

This study has implications for practice as well. First, professionals reported a lower effect of system usage on individual impact relative to students. It may be helpful to determine if professionals engage in lower levels of system usage (or they are more experienced or efficient such that lower usage is to be expected), deal with low-quality or low-utility IS (which does not engender greater usage), possess the skills and expertise to complete the system-based tasks quickly, or hold high expectations about IS that are not satisfied. Answers to such questions may motivate IS designers, developers, and implementers to attach greater emphasis on increasing the quality, usefulness, and relevance of IS for professionals.

Second, the effect of system usage on individual impact was lower for function IS relative to other types (and higher for network IS relative to other types although not different between enterprise IS and other IS types). In other words, network IS seem to be used to a greater extent and yield greater impacts than other types of IS while function IS seem to have relatively lower usage and impact. Organizations may find it useful to build systems with capabilities for interaction and collaboration because individuals can communicate with other individuals, gain new information and knowledge from other sources, and collaborate with other individuals on tasks through network IS—enterprise social media is an example of such a system currently available within the organization. It may be beneficial for organizations to evaluate the extent to which enterprise IS, which are typically expensive, are actually needed for their operations because they were seemingly similar to other systems in terms of system usage and individual impact. Third, the effect of system usage and individual impact increased as the number of respondents increased. Organizations may find it beneficial to design and implement IS that may be appropriate for a larger population of individuals. As the number of individuals using the IS increases, the resulting network externalities and social capital impact the extent to which the IS may be used, thus influencing individual impact. This dynamic can also be noted based on the positive influence exerted by network IS (i.e., IS that may be appropriate for a large population) on the relationship between system usage and individual impact. Fourth, voluntariness did not seem to play a significant role in the relationship between system usage and individual impact. This raises questions about the advantages of mandating use of systems for individuals within organizations. It seems likely that individuals may use systems on their own volition if the systems provide value or convenience (even if they are not mandated specifically by organizations). Furthermore, individuals may experiment with systems such that they may begin using capabilities of the system that are not mandated. In this regard, it may be useful to have motivated individuals champion or evangelize the use of systems.

##

Finally, the significant difference in the effect of system usage on individual impact between North America and the other regions implies that there may be opportunities for learning across regions. Results show that the North American region experienced significant advantages in system usage and individual impact, which implies that the North American region has identified ways to maximize the benefits gained from IS implementations possibly through specialized expertise, advanced infrastructure, and technical support, and superior championship of newer systems. Organizations and individuals in other regions may apply the proven practices about IS implementation, adoption, and usage from the North American region to maximize usage and impacts from using IS.

## 6. CONCLUSION

This study examined the characteristics of the research contexts, which influenced the effect of system usage on individual impact. An analysis of prior findings was conducted using meta-regression methods. Results showed that the number of organizations, number of respondents, type of respondents, and information system type (along with geographic region) influenced the effect of system usage on individual impact. The findings offer initial evidence of the characteristics of research contexts that might be taken into consideration in designing research studies involving system usage and individual impact.

## Biography

Anand Jeyaraj is a professor of Information Systems in the Raj Soin College of Business at Wright State University. His research interests include the diffusion and adoption of information systems, success and payoff of information systems, and methodologies. His research has been published in journals including MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, and Journal of Information Technology.
