# Financial Returns to Firms’ Communication Actions on Firm-Initiated Social Media: Evidence from Facebook Business Pages

Sunghun Chung,<sup>a</sup> Animesh Animesh,<sup>b</sup> Kunsoo Han,<sup>b</sup> Alain Pinsonneault<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. The primary goal of this study is to investigate the financial returns to firms communication actions on a firm-initiated social media platform by focusing on Facebook Business pages. To this end, we conceptualize and quantify two types of firms’ communication actions on social media: posts and responses to customer messages. Furthermore, we classify a firm’s responses to customer messages based on the valence of customer messages—positive versus negative—and examine the effects of volume as well as timeliness of the two types of a firm’s responses to customer messages on firm performance. Using a sample of 63 South Korean firms across industries over a three-year period (5,566 firm-week observations), we find that the volume and timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative customer messages, which are associated with an increase in customer satisfaction, have a significant positive impact on the firm’s market performance measured by abnormal returns and Tobin’s q. Interestingly, the results suggest that a firm’s posts and its responses to positive customer messages are not significantly associated with firm performance. Furthermore, we find that a firm’s posts and its responses to negative customer messages exhibit complementarities in contributing to firm performance. Our results are robust to various alternative specifications, econometric concerns, and Facebook’s policy changes, such as EdgeRank and Promoted Post. Our findings underscore the business value of firms’ actions on social media and provide unique and important implications for theory and practice regarding the appropriate ways to use social media for building and managing customer relationships.

## 1. Introduction

Social media can provide business value by enabling the formation of online customer communities that can support branding, sales, customer service, and product development (Culnan et al. 2010). In order to tap into this potential, many organizations are actively utilizing social media (for example, Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn) to communicate and interact with their current and potential customers (Ling 2013).

For example, many firms have official Facebook “pages,” where they post messages for their customers (for example, product/service promotions) and respond to messages left by their customers (“fans” of a certain page). As of February 2018, there were approximately 65 million business pages on Facebook, and 1 million are being added every month.<sup>2</sup> Many industry reports suggest that social media may positively impact businesses’ bottom line and brand image (Wessel 2011, Hardy 2012). More importantly, social media are emerging as a key source of information for investors when they make investment decisions. A recent market report reveals that almost 80% of institutional investors use social media as part of their regular work flow, and the majority of these investors say that information obtained through social media has directly influenced an investment recommendation ordecision (Connell 2015). Another report suggests that over five million investors in North America are using social media to research financial decisions; in particular, they are using Facebook business pages to gauge public sentiment (Borzykowski 2016).

Particularly, a firm’s Facebook business page helps the firm strengthen firm-customer and customerfirm communications as well as monitor and mediate customer-to-customer communications, which are fundament of word of mouth—the most persuasive form of promotion (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010). Handling customer messages on Facebook business pages enables firms to capture customer feedback, enhance market research, and display the interaction of a firm with other customers. For instance, Starbucks, widely regarded as a leader in the effective use of customer-focused social media, utilizes its Facebook business page to draw praises, handle complaints, support requests, and illicit product suggestions. By focusing attention on its business page where content can be managed, Starbucks interacts with its over 15 million fans and provides an easy way for customers to opt in to broadcast messages (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010).

Even though communicating with customers through social media seems valuable, firms have been questioning the return on investment (ROI) of such actions and often hesitate to integrate social media initiatives in their marketing strategy (Hoffman and Fodor 2010, Kumar and Mirchandani 2012). In addition, many practitioners remain skeptical about the returns to investments in social media because of the low reach rate of firms’ social media presence and the difficulty in measuring returns (Mueller 2011).

Recently, a stream of literature has begun to examine the important topic of the value of social media (Yang et al. 2012, 2014; Goh et al. 2013; Rishika et al. 2013). Although this stream of literature suggests that use of social media is positively associated with consumer engagement and firm performance (Luo et al. 2013, Miller and Tucker 2013, Yu et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2018), most of the extant research has focused on the impact of consumer-generated content on third party- or consumer-owned social media platforms, such as blogs and consumer ratings (Chen et al. 2011, Luo and Zhang 2013, Luo et al. 2013). We complement and extend this stream of research by examining the impact of firm-generated content on a firm-owned social media platform (that is, Facebook business pages). Specifically, we study the effects of a firm’s posts on its business page (which are broadcasted to the followers of that business page<sup>3</sup>) as well as the firm’s responses to the customer messages posted on its business page (which reaches only those customers who are engaged in that conversation thread) on firm performance.

Although a few studies have examined firm’s communication actions in social media, they have mainly examined the impact of firm’s actions on customers behavior, such as consumer engagement (Miller and Tucker 2013, Lee et al. 2018) or purchase behavior (Goh et al. 2013). In this study, we extend prior work in this stream of literature by examining the impact of firms’ social media actions on their stock market performance, which captures both tangible and intangible outcomes of their actions and reflects future value of such actions. In so doing, we focus on how the communication between a firm and its existing as well as potential customers on firm-owned social media influences investors’ decisions, which will be manifested in the firm’s stock market performance.

Despite the importance of understanding how a firm should optimize its strategies for communicating with customers on a given social media platform, there is a paucity of research that provides guidance to a firm about what types of communication actions on social media that firms should focus on to maximize the returns and how different communication actions contribute to firm performance. Given that firm-owned social media platforms afford greater control but require substantial investments (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010), we examine the impact of two distinct types of communication actions used by firms on social media, namely a firm’s posts and a firm’s responses to customer messages, as well as the interplay between these two types of actions on firm performance. These two communication actions that a firm uses on social media not only have distinct objectives but also, require different levels of efforts. A firm’s posts on its business page are used to primarily push marketing messages to its fan/subscriber base. To the extent that these fans “like” and share these broad casted messages within their social network, the exposure of the messages can increase exponentially.

A firm’s responses to customer messages are crafted by a firm to respond to messages posted by customers on the firm’s Facebook page. In contrast to a firm’s posts, its responses to customer messages are limited in terms of exposure, because such posts are pushed to only those fans that are involved in a specific conversation. However, a firm’s responses to customer messages allow the firm to effectively develop and manage relationships with its subscribers who may be current or potential customers. Posting responses to customer messages can help create a stronger and loyal fan base by addressing the needs, comments, complaints, and feedback of its stakeholders. Given that the objectives and the required efforts of a firm’s responses to customer messages can vary substantially depending on the valence of customer messages (for example, complaints versus positive feedback), we disaggregate a firm’s responses into responses to negative customer messages and responses to positive customer messages, and we examine the impact of these responses in terms of volume (the amount of responses) and timeliness (the speed of responses) on the firm’s market performance. Furthermore, we investigate whether the two types of communication actions—a firm’s posts and responses to customer messages—are substitutes or complements.

To summarize, in order to provide guidance to firms on how to formulate their communication strategies on firm-owned social media platforms, this study focuses on examining the value of multiple types of firms’ actions (volume of a firm’s posts and volume/ timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative and positive customer messages) on a firm-owned social media platform and the interplay between a firm’s posts and its responses to customer messages. Specifically, we develop a research model that extends and complements recent studies on the value of social media (Goh et al. 2013, Luo et al. 2013, Rishika et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2018). We address the following research questions. (1) Do a firm’s posts influence its market performance? (2) Do a firm’s responses to customer messages influence its market performance? Are there differences between responses to negative customer messages and responses to positive customer messages? Do volume and timeliness of responses matter? (3) Are a firm’s posts and responses to customer messages substitutes or complements?

We empirically examine these research questions in the context of Facebook business pages. Since Facebook pages began rolling out for businesses in May 2009, marketers have insisted that Facebook pages have the strongest marketing effectiveness across the multiple social media platforms and can generate real customers (HUBSPOT 2014). Based on detailed data collected from 63 South Korean firms’ Facebook pages and their financial data over three years (2010–2012) coupled with content and sentiment analyses, we find that the volume and timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative customer messages are positively associated with firm performance, whereas a firm’s posts and its responses to positive customer messages are not significantly associated with firm performance. This implies that firms’ social media strategy focusing on the interactions and engagement with customers pays off more than the strategy that focuses on broadcasting its marketing and promotional content. More importantly, we find that intensively and promptly responding to negative customer messages is particularly important in influencing investors’ perception of the firm’s future cash flows. We control for a comprehensive set of factors, including a firm’s activities outside of social media; conventional news media; other social media, such as blogs and Tweets; and a firm’s financial variables. Our results are robust to various alternative specifications, econometric concerns, and Facebook’s policies, namely EdgeRank and Promoted Post, that were implemented during our sample period.

Our findings highlight the importance of social media strategies geared toward managing relationships with customers—especially responding to customers’ negative messages. This is in line with prior studies that emphasize the importance of relationship marketing vis-a-vis mass marketing (Deighton and \` Kornfeld 2009) and a firm’s role in interacting with complaining customers (Gu and Ye 2014, Gans et al. 2017). Furthermore, we find complementarities between a firm’s posts and responses to negative customer messages, suggesting that, although a firm’s posts alone are not effective in creating value for the firm, they can contribute to firm performance when combined with intensive and timely responses to com plaining customers.

This study makes several contributions to research and practice. First, we are among the first to rigorously examine and provide evidence of the financial returns to firms’ social media actions on a firm-initiated social media platform. Second, our study makes a contribution by conceptualizing and quantifying a firm’s posts and responses to customer messages based on the detailed content and sentiment analysis. In particular, we make a novel contribution to the literature by separating a firm’s responses to customer messages based on the valence of customer messages (that is, negative or positive) and examining the effects of volume as well as timeliness of the two types of firms responses on firm performance. Third, we broaden the scope of enquiry in the literature on social media strategy (for example, Aral et al. 2013) by theorizing and empirically validating the interplay between a firm’s posts and responses to customer messages. Fourth, our findings can help managers gauge financial returns to firms’ social media actions and realize the importance of investing in firm-owned social media to communicate and interact with customers. Our study also provides valuable insights into how firms should formulate their social media strategies to increase the returns to their investments in social media

## 2. Prior Literature

Research on the value of social media has primarily focused on the impacts of consumer-generated content on consumer behavior (Goh et al. 2013, Rishika et al. 2013) and firm performance (Chen et al. 2011, Tirunillai and Tellis 2012, Luo and Zhang 2013, Luo et al. 2013). Although a few studies have examined the value of firms’ actions (that is, firm-generated content) on social media, they have focused solely on the impact on consumer behavior (Goh et al. 2013, Miller and Tucker 2013, Chen et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2018)

Next, we briefly review the prior research identified above and highlight our unique contributions. Prior studies on the impact of social media can be categorized into two streams. The first stream has examined the impact of consumers’ actions in social media on firm performance (cell (1a) of Figure 1) and consumer behavior (cells (2a) and (2b) of Figure 1). Notably, Luo et al. (2013) found that consumers actions in social media (that is, posting content about a firm on blogs as well as posting reviews about the firm and its products on the website of CNET.com) can influence the firm’s market value. Similarly, Luo and Zhang (2013) examine the impact of consumer buzz (that is, online reviews, blog postings, etc.) on consumers’ website visits and firm’s market value. Based on individual-level data on consumers’ social media participation (Facebook page) and transactions, Rishika et al. (2013) find that customer participation in a firm’s social media leads to an increase in the frequency of customer visits. Yu et al. (2013) investigate the relative effects of social media and conventional media on firm performance and find that social media has a stronger impact on firm performance than conventional media. Although these studies provide valuable implications by highlighting the importance of social media in a firm’s strategy, they do not shed light on the financial returns to firms’ specific social media actions.

The second stream of research, which is more closely related to our study, has examined the impact of firms’ actions in social media on consumer behavior (cells (3a) and (3b) of Figure 1). In general, active firm promotion involving word of mouth has been found to be effective in managing firms’ social presence (Godes and Mayzlin 2009). A few studies have examined the impact of firm’s social media actions on consumer behavior (for example, engagement and purchase). For example, based on cross-sectional data of 5,035 hospital Facebook pages, Miller and Tucker (2013) find that firms (for example, hospitals) that actively manage their Facebook pages through regular posts are likely to induce more postings from internal employees compared with external customers. Although their study evaluates the benefits of firms social media actions, it only examines the benefits in terms of the engagement of employees and customers and does not distinguish different types of actions that a firm can take on social media. More recently, Lee et al. (2018) analyze the impact of firm-generated content on consumer engagement. Specifically, they compare the impact of informative (that is, deals and promotions) content versus brand personality-related content on consumer engagement captured by the number of likes and comments by consumers. The study by Lee et al. (2018) takes a step forward in understanding how different actions of firms on social media can lead to differential outcomes. However, unlike our study, their study focuses solely on the firm’s broadcast communication and its impact on consumer engagement.

In addition, although Goh et al. Furthermore, we examine the presence of complementarity between the two types of firms’ communication actions. Moreover, whereas Goh et al. (2013) focus on a consumerlevel outcome (purchase expenditure), we use a firm’s market performance as the outcome variable, which captures the overall tangible as well as intangible impacts of firm’s actions. Finally, unlike the work of Goh et al. (2013), which is based on analysis of a single firm, our sample includes more than 60 publicly traded firms across industries.

Taken together, although the studies in the second stream provide important implications regarding the impact of firm’s social media actions on customer behavior, none of them have provided evidence that relates a firm’s social media actions to its stock market performance by scrutinizing the impacts of different dimensions of firms’ social media actions. Furthermore, the results from prior studies (Goh et al. 2013, Miller and Tucker 2013) suggesting that firms’ social media actions are often not as effective as consumergenerated social media provide additional motivation to scrutinize the impact of firms’ social media 5 actions more thoroughly.

Given that one of the primary mandates of publicly owned firms is to enhance shareholder wealth (Rappaport 1986), researchers have highlighted the importance of investigating factors affecting market performance (Dewan and Ren 2007). Therefore, we focus on the impact of firms’ posts as well as their responses to customer messages (in terms of both volume and timeliness) on market performance, which not only represent how investors value firms but also, reflect the net present value of firms’ expected future cash flows (Rappaport 1986).

## 3. Hypotheses Development

Firms have long spent large amounts of time and money to improve their relationship with customers. Before the advent of social media, firms interacted with customers via traditional media—either in mass communications through printed or broadcast advertising or individually through phone calls, face-to-face encounters, emails, or postal mail. These communication strategies were broadly termed mass marketing and one-to-one marketing, respectively. In mass marketing, firms promote the same products and services to customers and try to ensure that their marketing messages reach as many customers as possible. In contrast, one-to-one marketing (also called relationship marketing) focuses on identifying and satisfying the unique needs of each individual customer by providing personalized messages and customized products and services (Peppers et al. 1999).

Emergence of social media has enriched traditional firm-customer interaction by enhancing firms’ ability to directly engage customers, thereby strengthening the communications between a firm and its customers. To help understand firm and customer communication via social media, Gallaugher and Ransbotham (2010) propose a framework with three types of interactions: the Megaphone, the Magnet, and the Monitor. The Megaphone is a firm-initiated social media communication to share firms’ messages with the world, whereas the Magnet is customer-initiated social media communication for communicating customers’ feedback and complaints to firms. Lastly, the Monitor is customer-to-customer social media communication that can be monitored by firms (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010).

Although this framework represents comprehensive communication paths involving a focal firm and its customers on social media in general, interactions on a firm-owned social media, which is the focus of our study, mostly fall into the categories of Megaphone and Magnet. Therefore, we categorize firms communication actions on a firm-owned social media site into two types: (1) a firm’s posts that are used by firms to disseminate information about its products/ services, promotions, and other messages primarily to customers and (2) a firm’s responses to customer messages that focus on engaging directly with customers by responding to customers’ posts or comments.

We argue that a firm’s efforts to communicate with its current/potential customers through social media can affect its market value, because the information disseminated through social media can provide potential and current investors with signals about the marketing strategy, customer service quality, and growth potential of the firm. Next, we develop hypotheses on the impacts of these two types of social media actions on firm performance as well as the relationship between them.

## 3.1. Firms’ Posts and Firm Performance

Prior research has studied the relationship between a firm’s marketing activities and firm performance (Joshi and Hanssens 2010). For example, Srinivasan and Hanssens (2009) suggest that a firm’s marketing and advertising efforts can directly affect stock returns over and above the indirect effect of marketing communication on stock returns through increased sales. Given that firms are broadcasting content related to their marketing mix on social media, it can be argued that potential investors are, in addition to tracking the intensity of conventional marketing efforts, likely to also monitor the intensity of communication actions of a firm in social media. Higher intensity of a firm’s posts (that is, broadcast communication) would mean more opportunities for the firm’s current and potential customers to see and act on the information disseminated by the firm. This strategy, similar to traditional mass communication through such channels as television, can create an exposure effect that enhances customers’ awareness of a focal firm through the propinquity effect (Slater 2004). By actively managing their social media sites through regular posts, firms can induce more user-generated content (Miller and Tucker 2013), identify important issues, and foster innovation (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010).

A firm’s posts on a social network platform can be highly visible and contagious because of the presence of two modes of information propagation: subscription and social contagion. In the former, the information posted by a firm is immediately delivered to the current subscribers (fans) of the firm’s page through its news feed. Then, nonfans who are friends of current fans can also observe activities and interactions on the page involving their friends through social contagion (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010, Aral and Walker 2011). Although a firm’s posts are initially propagated through the subscription mode, this propagation of information can be eclipsed by the exponential increase in the reach and exposure of the posts owing to social contagion, which leads to the viral propagation of messages to those who otherwise would not receive the messages (Zhang et al. 2015).

More importantly, compared with traditional media, which has been a key source of information for the investment, a firm’s posts on a firm-owned social media site provide up-to-date information, including firms offerings, promotions, and initiatives, with easy access (Connell 2015). In today’s information-oriented society where the immediacy of information is key, a firm’s posts on social media provide a quicker and more efficient way of gathering data compared with traditional media for investors; indeed, a majority of personal and/or institutional investors are making investment decisions based on information that they have obtained from social media (Borzykowski 2016 Wallens 2016). Although there is growing importance of social media in investment and wealth management, relatively little attention has been paid to the economic impact of a firm’s broadcast communica tion actions on its market performance. To the extent that a firm’s posts increase the reach and exposure of the firm’s marketing messages and provide up-todate information on the firm’s business, the investors would take into account the intensity of a firm’s posting activity when assessing the market value of the firm. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 1. A firm’s posts on social media are positively associated with the firm’s market performance.

## 3.2. Firms’ Responses to Customer Messages and Firm Performance

Although firms provide information to investors through financial statements and other disclosures (for example, annual reports), numerous studies have suggested the existence of asymmetric information between firms and investors (Healy and Palepu 2001) To overcome this asymmetry, investors seek additional information from multiple sources, such as new product announcements (Chen et al. 2011), and user-generated content (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012). In this regard, communication between a firm and its consumers on social media provides one such source of information to investors, because they can infer the firm’s level of involvement with its customer base and the firm’s commitment to improving relationships with customers from the firm’s social media actions.

Firms’ responses to customer messages can be distinguished from firms’ posts in terms of the nature of firm’s participation in social media (Godes et al. 2005). Although in general, a firm posts messages to convey information to its subscribers, the firm may also choose to interact with its customers by responding to customers’ posts or comments with an objective to build valuable long-term relationships with its customers. Compared with the traditional marketing communication channels that firms used to primarily broadcast its messages, the social media platform allows firms and customers to easily engage in oneto-one communication (Barger et al. 2016). Therefore a firm, which earlier was only able to have occasional one-to-one communication (that is, customer contacting the firm through phone, email, etc.,), can expand its interactions with its customers using social media (Barger et al. 2016).

Although a firm’s responses to customer messages may be inferior to a firm’s broadcasted posts in terms of the reach and exposure of information, the former has an edge in terms of developing stronger relationships with the customers. According to the communicationbased model of relationship marketing (Duncan and Moriarty 1998), interactive communication affects brand relationships and drives brand value (Duncan and Moriarty 1997). Because firms seek to build relationships with customers to promote brand loyalty and positive word of mouth (WOM), they must engage in interactive communication with its customers and strive to create repeated positive interactions. However, a firm’s responses to customer messages carry a relatively high cost, because they require the firm to customize messages (rather than broadcasting the same messages to all subscribers). In spite of the high cost of engaging with customers through a firm’s responses, researchers suggest that being responsive (as well as being perceived as responsive) is critical to firm’s survival and building strong social capital on a social media platform (York 2012).

In addition, it has been suggested that a high level of interaction between a firm and customers can reduce customer complaints and price elasticity, signal repeat buying, and increase market share and longterm growth of the firm (Anderson et al. 1994). To the extent that high level of interaction between a firm and customers on a social media site can provide a signal to the investors about the firm’s intention and capability to manage relationships with its customers, firm’s responses to customer messages would be positively associated with firm performance. Specifically, we examine the volume and timeliness of such responses. Moreover, because the effect of a firm’s responses to customer messages may depend on the valence of customer messages,<sup>6</sup> we distinguish firm’s responses to negative customer messages (for example, customer complaints) from firm’s responses to positive customer messages (for example, customers positive testimonials or feedback).

3.2.1. Volume. Given that interactions between a firm and consumers allow the firm to listen and respond to its customers, which can impact the brand relationships and increase the customer lifetime value, observing and monitoring such interactions can provide valuable information to investors about the customer orientation of the firm and serve as a signal of its future market value. Therefore, a firm’s social media actions aimed at developing relationships with customers can act as a signal of firm’s sound customer relationship management and influence firm’s market value. However, because customers’ posts/comments are a precursor of a firm’s responses, the signal value of a firm’s responses to investors would depend on whether the original customer messages are negative or positive.

Although customer messages with negative valence convey negative WOM and are likely to have a negative effect on firm’s market performance, a firm can respond to such negative messages to address and mitigate the issues that lead to the negative post from consumer. Gu and Ye (2014) suggest that a firm’s response is effective in favorably influencing customers’ attitude toward the focal firm. They analyzed online word-of-mouth and management responses on a travel agency website and find that online management responses to customers’ reviews increase future satisfaction of the complaining customers who receive the responses.

Complaint handling has been argued to be an important strategic tool for firms (Gilly and Hansen 1985), because effectively handling complaints increases the likelihood of customer satisfaction and reduces the negative WOM (Blodgett 1993). Although negative customer messages are indicative of issues with product failure and/or lapses in the service quality being raised by unsatisfied consumers, the firms that respond quickly and positively to complaints about their products or services can convert those dissatisfied consumers into loyal customers (Clark et al. 1992). Strauss and Hill (2001) find that firm’s responses to consumers’ email complaints lead to customer satisfaction as well as likelihood of repeat purchase. Therefore, we argue that firm’s response to negative customer posts/comments will mitigate the adverse impact of customer’s negative experience on customer satisfaction and loyalty In fact, firms’ appropriately handling customers negative posts on a social media platform can have greater impact on firm performance compared with email and other conventional channels, because unlike these channels, a social media platform makes such complaint resolution efforts of the firm visible to the public. Thus, complaint resolution through social media may not only affect the attitude of the concerned consumers toward the firm but also, influence the credibility and trustworthiness of the firm among all of the customers and investors who observe such efforts.

To the extent that investors consider the firm’s response to negative customer messages to be an indicator of firm’s complaint management capability, which is an essential component of customer relationship management (Strauss and Hill 2001), the amount of such responses to negative customer messages is likely to be associated with firm’s market value. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2a. The volume of a firm’s responses to negative customer messages is positively associated with firm performance.

Unlike customer messages with negative valence, customer messages on a social media platform with positive valence convey positive WOM and are likely to have a positive effect on firm’s market value. In response to a positive customer message, a firm generally posts an appreciative message. Such efforts tend to increase social presence (Godes and Mayzlin 2009) and enhance the depth of information sharing (Miranda and Saunders 2003). Responding to the comments/posts by customers who are satisfied and sharing positive endorsement of the focal product/ firm can lead to the feeling of being recognized and appreciated by the firm, and it is likely to further strengthen a customer’s commitment toward the firm and the brand equity.

Though researchers argue that the management of unsolicited compliments or positive feedback provides opportunities to initiate and establish long-term relationships as well as reinforce the favorable disposition of the consumers, little research has examined the value of firm’s response to positive feedback (Erickson and Eckrich 2001). Although unlike complaining customers, the complimenting customers do not require corrective action, they are likely to expect at least an acknowledgement (Erickson and Eckrich 2001). In fact, although customers may have higher expectations for receiving responses to complaints compared with positive messages, they are more likely to evaluate a firm’s response to positive messages more favorably than firm’s response to complaints (Smart and Martin 1992). This implies that the effectiveness of firm’s responses to positive messages in reinforcing the favorable disposition of satisfied customers may even be stronger than the effectiveness of its responses to negative messages in placating the dissatisfied customers (Smart and Martin 1992).

To summarize, it has been argued that responding to positive customer messages gives the firm an opportunity to actively engage with customers, thereby enhancing customer loyalty (Peppers et al. 1999, Brondmo 2002). To the extent that the investors consider the volume of (appreciative) responses of a firm to positive customer messages as an indicator of customer loyalty and brand equity in the future, this metric will be positively associated with the market value of the firm. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2b. The volume of a firm’s responses to positive customer messages is positively associated with firm performance.

3.2.2. Timeliness. Although customers expect the firm to engage with them by responding to their messages, it is not just the existence of the response but also, the timeliness/speed of the firm’s response that can affect their attitude toward the firm (Strauss and Hill 2001). Providing fast responses is a key to successful resolution of customer complaints (Hart et al. 1990, Johnston 2001). Also, speed of response to customer complaints has been found to increase customer satisfaction with complaint handling (TARP 1981) and customer loyalty (Conlon and Murray 1996) as well as lower the amount of negative WOM (Martin 1985). Furthermore, Strauss and Hill (2001) found that quicker response to consumer’s email complaints leads to greater customer satisfaction. Though many studies have examined the impact of firm’s response to customer complaint, there is a lack of empirical work on the value of timeliness in responding to customer complaints, especially in the context of firm-initiated social media platform. Given that the social media platform used by firms is open to the public where messages, including complaints, can spread virally to a large population very quickly, the timeliness of firm’s response to negative messages is of paramount importance to protect its brand reputation (Pfeffer et al. 2014).

In a recent study, Istanbulluoglu (2017) examined the impact of timeliness of a firm’s response to customer complaints on social media and found that quick response leads to higher satisfaction. To the extent that investors consider the timeliness of a firm’s response to customer messages, especially those with negative valence, as an indicator of firm’s customer orientation and believe that timeliness of firms’ responses to negative customer messages would lead to higher satisfaction and improve firm’s future cash flows, they will take such metrics into account while making investment decisions. Furthermore, the timeliness of firm’s response to a negative message also reduces the likelihood that the message would become viral (that is, reach a large number of potentia customers rapidly through online sharing), thereby mitigating the adverse consequences. Given that even short delays in responding to complaints on social media can have a significant adverse impact on firms financials as well as intangible assets, such as brand equity (Pfeffer et al. 2014), investors are likely to consider the timeliness of firm’s response to negative customer messages in assessing the future value of a firm. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2c. The timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative customer messages is positively associated with firm performance.

Earlier, we suggested that a firm’s responses to positive customer messages on the social media platform are likely to increase customer satisfaction and brand loyalty. Although it is possible that the consequences of responding slowly to positive customer messages are not severe, because such satisfied customers require no redress and may not even expect a response, it has been argued that quick response to positive customer messages is likely to strengthen customer relations and thereby, offset any (minor) negative encounter that the consumer might have in the future (Martin and Smart 1988). However, prior research has not examined the value of quickly responding to the positive feedback from consumers.

Speed of response to customer positive feedback and testimonials increases social presence, results in the higher level of trust and intention to engage in the focal firm (Han et al. 2016), and could be a valuable opportunity to convert new buyers into long-term and loyal customer (Marban 2014). Martin and Smart (1988), focusing on consumer package goods manufacturers, found that, though firms differ in terms of their timeliness to respond to customers’ messages, they consider the timeliness in responding to a positive customer message as important as the timeliness in responding to a negative customer message. This suggests that quick responses to positive customer messages are also likely to cultivate customer relationships and improve customer retention. To the extent that the investors consider the prompt responses of a firm to positive customer messages as an indicator of future customer loyalty and brand equity, this metric will be positively associated with the market value of the firm. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2d. The timeliness of a firm’s responses to positive customer messages is positively associated with firm performance.

Another interesting yet unexplored question is whether the value of a firm’s responses to customers positive messages differs from the value of firm’s responses to customers’ negative messages. Although it is an open empirical question, we suggest that a firm’s responses to negative customer messages have a stronger impact on firm performance than actions to respond to positive customer messages. In general, negative information may seem more credible than positive information (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), and prior research has shown that negative reviews have a greater influence than positive reviews in the online word-of-mouth literature (Yin et al. 2016). More importantly, the effect of a firm’s response to complaining customers has been found to be stronger than that of a firm’s response to satisfied customers in terms of increasing customer loyalty (Gu and Ye 2014). In addition, previous studies suggest that responsiveness to customer complaints is more important to increase customer satisfaction and loyalty than responses to other types of customer messages (Conlon and Murray 1996). This suggests that a firm’s responses to negative customer messages would be more effective than its responses to positive customer messages in terms of contribution to both customer satisfaction and firm performance. To the extent that investors take into account the relative importance of a firm’s responses to negative vis-a-vis positive customer\` messages in increasing customer satisfaction and loyalty when making financial decisions, the former would have a greater impact on the firm’s market performance than the latter. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2e. The association between a firm’s responses (in terms of volume and timeliness) to negative customer messages and firm performance is stronger than the association between a firm’s responses (in terms of volume and timeliness) to positive customer messages and firm performance

## 3.3. Complementarity Between Two Types of Socia Media Communication Actions

Thus far, we have argued that both a firm’s posts and responses to customer messages will be positively associated with the firm’s market value. However, it is possible that these two types of communication actions exhibit synergies such that the impact of firm’s posts is strengthened in the presence of firm’s responses to customer messages. Duncan and Moriarty (1998) suggest that broadcast (similar to firm’s posts) and one-to-one communication (similar to firm’s response to customers’ messages) can be used strategically to reinforce each other and that interactivity can be maximized by combining broadcast and oneto-one communication. Whether the two dimensions of social media actions are complements or not has important implications on firms’ strategy in social media. Given the fact that firms have limited resources, if the two dimensions are independent, firms would gain more benefits by focusing on the dimension that has a stronger association with firm performance while minimizing actions in the less valuable dimension. In contrast, if they are complements, firms need to increase efforts in both dimensions, which would require additional resources or balancing the two types of actions given the limited resources.

Although a firm’s responses to customer messages may reach a smaller proportion of customers than broadcast messages (that is, a firm’s posts) as explained above, such responses can engender greater trust and loyalty of those customers (Algesheimer et al. 2005), thereby creating a loyal segment of customers who may act as influencers for broadcast messages. Specifically, by actively responding to customer messages, a firm not only can increase the satisfaction and loyalty of the involved customers but also, can increase the possibility that these existing and potential customers become strong proponents of the firm and its product/services and engage in the firm’s social media site through their postings, comments, and likes, thereby increasing the exposure and reach of firm’s broadcast messages via social contagion. Therefore, a firm’s responses to customer messages in terms of volume and response time can send a signal to its current and potential investors that the firm’s broadcasted posts will be more effective and valuable. This implies that increasing the intensity and speed of a firm’s responses to customer messages can strengthen the positive association between a firm’s posts and the firm’s market value. Therefore, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 3a. The association between a firm’s posts and firm performance is positively moderated by the firm’s responses (in terms of volume and timeliness) to negative customer messages.

Hypothesis 3b. The association between a firm’s posts and firm performance is positively moderated by the firm’s responses (in terms of volume and timeliness) to positive customer messages.

## 4. Data and Methods

## 4.1. Data Collection and Sample

We acquired the Facebook business page data set from a South Korean market research firm. The firm had chosen South Korea’s top 100 firms in terms of the total market value, sales, and net income as sample firms. The data set was collected by developing a custom-designed Apache Hadoop Hive-based crawler on a distributed computing platform. These customized agents, running in parallel on 30 Linux x86 servers, queried the Facebook servers to acquire the specific posts and comment information on each firm’s Facebook page. As a result, all available data through the Facebook graph application program interface (API) for each firm were collected. However, of 100 firms, data could not be collected for 37 firms because of technical issues beyond our control, and as a result, the actual sample contained 63 firms.<sup>7</sup> It is important to note that we were able to obtain the timestamp of the posts/comments and the anonymized unique identification numbers for each user as well as each page. Therefore, our data set contains detailed information on all activities (for example, posts, comments, and likes) from these 63 firms’ official Facebook pages from January 2010 to December 2012.

Similar to Da et al. (2011), we aggregated the data at the weekly level for each firm and constructed a panel data set containing each firm’s weekly social media activities and customers’ weekly responses (from Saturday to Friday). We do not use daily data, because the variation of firms’ social media activities at the daily level was relatively small (the average number of postings was 3.21 per day). Because some firms operate several Facebook pages for different purposes (for example, brand page, corporate social responsibility (CSR) page, promotion page, and so on), we manually tracked every official Facebook page for each sample firm and then, aggregated all activities from the multiple Facebook pages for each week of our sample period.

We combined the Facebook data set with a quarterly firm-level financial data set that we obtained from Korea Investors Service, Inc. Because the data set includes large firms that are also listed on the Korea Stock Exchange or Korean Securities Dealers Automated Quotations, we were able to get the financial data for all of the sample firms. The focus on large firms is also pertinent, because they are likely to make higher levels of investments in social media (because of a relatively larger marketing budget), and larger firms tend to have more active interactions with customers. To match those financial variables with our weekly social media metrics, we followed the datacoding scheme used in prior studies (Luo 2009). Specifically, we assigned the same values of earnings forecasts, cash flows, and other financial data to all 12 weeks contained in a given quarter. We checked the robustness of our results by using VAR-bootstrapping specifications with 5,000 simulated databases (Luo et al. 2013), which confirmed that our results are not sensitive to temporal aggregation. Our final sample consists of 5,566 firm-week observations from 63 firms over the period from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2012, spanning 156 weeks. Our sample firms operate in diverse industries (see Table 1) and have, on average, 10,455 fans who like their Facebook pages.

On average, a firm posts about 12 times per week; the average valence is 0.7, which is highly positive. Approximately 58% of a firm’s posts contain enriched information, and the average length of posts is approximately 200 characters.

4.2.2. Firms’ Responses to Customer Messages. To capture a firm’s responses to customer messages, we use the total number of a firm’s comments on customers’ posts/comments in week t. In order to examine the differential effects of a firm’s responses to customer messages with different valence, we conducted sentiment analysis of customers’ posts and comments (see Online Appendix B) and constructed three variables that capture the volume of a firm’s responses (RES) to negative, positive, and neutral customer messages (CM), which were calculated as total numbers of a firm’s responses to customers negative, positive, neutral posts, and comments, respectively.<sup>8</sup> On average, a firm responds to its customers’ posts/comments about 14 times per week, and 38% (29%) of a firm’s responses are to negative (positive) customer messages. Similarly, the three variables capturing the timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative, positive, and neutral CM were measured by the average time lags (hours) between customers posts and a firm’s comment within a focal thread of comments in week t. Note that we use the label “delay” rather than “timeliness” for the variables to avoid confusion. The shorter the delay, the more timely a firm’s responses to customer messages are. On average, a firm’s response time to negative customer messages is much shorter (1.8 hours) than a firm’s response time to positive customer messages (3.0 hours). Note that the variables capturing volume and timeliness of a firm’s response to neutral customer messages are included as control variables. In addition, we include the valence of a firm’s responses (RES Valence) and the average comment length (RES\_Length) to control for the sentiment and amount of information in firms’ comments.

4.2.3. Firm Performance. As our main dependent variable, we use abnormal returns that capture a firm’s market performance beyond the firm’s expected stock market returns. To estimate abnormal returns, we used the extended Fama–French model (Fama and French 1993, 1996). The Fama–French model has been recognized as a good proxy for stock market performance that considers market risk and firm size as well as multiple value factors (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012, Luo et al. 2013). Specifically, we used the following extended Fama–French model:

$$
\begin{array}{r} R _ {i t} - R _ {f t} = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1 i} (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}) + \beta_ {2 i} S M B _ {t} + \beta_ {3 i} H M L _ {t} \\ + \beta_ {4 i} M O M _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

where $R _ { i t }$ is the returns for firm i in week t, $R _ { f t }$ is the risk-free rate, $R _ { m t }$ is the average market returns, SMB is the small minus big capitalization factor, HML is the high minus low book-to-market equity factor, and MOM<sub>t</sub> is the momentum factor in the given time period. Stock price data were obtained from Korea Investors Service, Inc., and data for the Fama–French factors and momentum (R<sub>ft</sub>, R<sub>mt</sub>, SMB<sub>t</sub>, HML<sub>t</sub>, and MOM ) are obtained from FnGuide, Inc. We estimated the above equation for a rolling window of 250 trading days before the target day. The Abnormal Returns were then calculated as the difference between the actua returns and the expected returns.

Second, Volume of Customer Messages, measured by the number of posts and comments made by customers in week t, is included to control for the impact of customer-generated content. Controlling for customer messages is important, because firms’ social media actions have been shown to influence customer engagement (Lee et al. 2018), which in turn, can affect firm performance. We also control for volume of positive customer messages (CM\_Positive) and volume of negative customer messages (CM\_Negative). In addition, we included customers’ positive expression measured by the number of customers’ likes on a firm’s posts in week t (CM\_Positive\_Expression) and the average length of customer messages in week t (CM\_Length).

Third, we control for Web Traffic to a firm’s websites (other than the firm’s Facebook pages), because web traffic acts as a valuable signal to the investor community about the market share and growth of a firm by complementing the accounting data (Luo and Zhang 2013). To capture Web Traffic, we manually tracked every official website of each sample firm, because most firms operate several websites for different purposes. We then collected the web traffic data from Nielsen Korean Click and aggregated all web traffic of the multiple websites for each week of our sample period. Among web traffic measures, we report the results based on reach rate, because reach rate is the most comparable measure across firms (Luo et al. 2013).<sup>9</sup> Next, we control for Search Intensity, which captures the intensity of the internet searches related to a firm (see Da et al. 2011). To measure Search Intensity, we obtained internet search data from Google Trends and calculated the mean of the “firm keywords”<sup>10</sup> search frequencies (range from 0 to 100) at google.com in week t.

Fourth, we control for a firm’s activities outside of social media. Three research assistants independently identified a firm’s activities outside of social media and classified these activities into four weekly dummy variables after reading major newspaper and magazine articles from the LexisNexis database (interrater reliability = 0.94): new product/service introduction, mergers and acquisitions, launching a new advertising campaign, and competitive news/buzz within industry. In addition, we also control for conventional news media and other social media (that is, Blogs and Tweets) (Yu et al. 2013). Based on firm keywords, for the focal firm, four research assistants independently searched and carefully read every conventional news article from Google News, all blogs from Google Blogs, and Tweets from Twitter. Then, they identified and counted overall positive, neutral, and negative sentiment conventional news articles, blogs, and Tweets for the focal firm in week $t . ^ { 1 1 }$

Fifth, we control for a number of financial variables that can affect firm performance. To control for growth opportunities available to a firm, we include R&D Intensity computed as the total R&D expenditures in a given quarter divided by total sales. In addition, we control for Total Assets (to control for firm size), $A d -$ vertising Intensity (the ratio of advertising expenditures to total sales), Operating Margin (ratio of net income to total sales), Leverage (the ratio of long-term debt to total sales), and Liquidity (the ratio of current liability to current assets). At the industry level, we control for Industry Concentration by including the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) (Han and Mithas

2013). Finally, week dummies were included in our model to control for week-specific common shocks. Table 3 provides correlation among key variables.

## 5.2. Additional Analyses and Robustness Checks

5.2.1. Different Types of Firm’s Posts. Given that our main result about the impact of a firm’s posts represents the average effect across various types of firm’s posts, it is possible that some types of messages are valuable, whereas others are not. In order to explore this possibility, we examine differential effects of different categories of a firm’s posts on firm performance. Based on a detailed content analysis (see Online Appendix B), we found that only the content relating to a firm’s core business activities in a firm’s posts (that is, new product announcement, discount or sales promotion, and sponsored events) has a significant positive association with firm performance, suggesting that posting messages unrelated to a firm’s business is not an effective strategy (see Table 5). These results underscore the importance of firm’s social media strategies focusing on communicating business-related messages and not overloading customers with irrelevant messages (see Online Appendix G for more details).

We test the robustness of our results by using alternative specifications and considering various econometric concerns and Facebook’s policies, namely EdgeRank and Promoted Post. Furthermore, in order to address concerns about endogeneity and reverse causality, we report the results of running a panel vector autoregression (VAR) model and conducting Granger causality tests.

5.2.2. Alternative Speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. We reestimated our model with several alternative specifications. First, we estimate a fixed effects model (FE) and a random effects model via maximum likelihood (RE-ML), and we report the results in Table 6. Second, we estimated a population-averaged (PA) model that allows for an exchangeable correlation structure of a generalized linear model (Model 3 of Table 6). The coefficient estimates from these specifications are qualitatively similar to our main results in Table 4, indicating the robustness of our results. In addition, we reestimated our model with Tobin’s q as an alternative measure of firm performance. Tobin’s q is a market-based measure of a firm’s tangible and intangible value, and it is forward looking, risk adjusted, and less susceptible to change in accounting practices. Thus, it has been widely used in prior literature (Bardhan et al. 2013). We calculated Tobin’s q for our sample firms on a quarterly basis during our sample period. We chose a quarterly interval, because it is the smallest interval available from firms’ financial statements. On a quarterly scale, we calculated the quarterly average of all of our independent variables and included firmspecific financial control variables from the firms quarterly financial statements, resulting in 632 firmquarter observations over 12 quarters in total. Overall, the results corroborate our main findings based on weekly abnormal returns (Model 4 of Table 6). Furthermore, to allow for the possibility of delayed exposure of firms’ social media actions to investors, we reestimated our model using one-week lagged values of our independent variables—a firm’s posts and responses to positive/negative customer messages As shown in Table 7, the results are qualitatively similar to the results based on contemporaneous values.

5.2.5. Panel Vector Autoregression with Exogenous Variables. To address potential biases, such as endogeneity, autocorrelations, and reversed causality, we estimate a panel vector autoregression model with exogenous variables (VARX). VARX has been used by previous studies (Luo et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2015) and allows us to examine the dynamic interactions between a firm’s social media actions and firm performance. The details of model specification, unit root test, lag order selection, and Granger causality tests are provided in Online Appendix E. The estimation results of our panel VARX model in Table 9 indicate that a firm’s social media actions have a significant effect on firm’s abnormal returns after controlling for lagged endogenous variables and exogenous variables. ## 6. Discussion

Our study examined the relationship between a firm’s social media communication actions and firm performance while also considering the complementarity between the two types of communication actions—a firm’s posts and its responses to customer messages. Our findings suggest that a firm’s social media actions yield significant financial returns, and the magnitude of these financial returns depends on the types of communication actions: that is, a firm’s (broadcasted) posts versus its responses to customer messages. Furthermore, we find that the value of a firm’s responses to customer messages depends on the valence of customer messages (negative versus positive) that the firm responds to. Specifically, our findings indicate that a firm’s responsive communication action that focuses on responding to complaining customers is the most critical to capturing value from socia media. Our study highlights the importance of responding to customer messages (in terms of both volume and timeliness), negative messages in particular, because doing so not only contributes to firm performance directly but also, amplifies the effect of firm’s posts on firm performance.

As the primary mechanism underlying our hypotheses, we argued that investors’ perceptions about the impact of firms’ social media communication on customer satisfaction and consequent future cash flows to the firm affect their investment decisions, which in turn, lead to abnormal stock returns. To the extent that the investors utilize the information about a firm’s intensive and timely responses on social media to infer the customer satisfaction, loyalty, and brand equity in the future, their investment decisions based on such information would be reflected in the firm’s abnormal stock market returns. In order to empirically validate the mechanism, we conducted a couple of additional analyses (see Online Appendix F for details). First, we estimated the effects of firms responses to customers’ messages (as well as firm’s own broadcast posts) in week t on the number of customers’ positive testimonials,<sup>14</sup> the overall valence of customers’ posts and comments, and the number of likes by customers in week t + 1. We find that the volume and timeliness of a firm’s responses to negative customer messages significantly increase customers likes and the number of customers’ positive testimonials as well as the overall valence of customer messages. Second, we estimated the impact of firms’ posts and its responses to customers’ messages on customer satisfaction. Using the National Customer Satisfaction Index as a measure of customer satisfaction, we found that the volume and timeliness of firm’s responses to negative customer messages are positively associated with customer satisfaction, and in turn, customer satisfaction is positively associated with abnormal return. Taken together, these additional analyses provide support for the mechanism that we proposed and suggest that investors are rational in using a firm’s communication actions on social media as a predictor of customer attitude and satisfaction toward the firm, especially among complaining customers, when making their investment decisions about the firm.

We find that the impact of a firm’s responses to positive customer messages on firm performance is weaker than that of responses to negative customer messages. Although consistent with our Hypotheses 2e, this finding is somewhat unexpected. We provide possible explanations for why a firm’s response to positive customer messages does not affect financial returns. First, positive customer messages may be less salient compared with negative messages. In the online word-of-mouth literature, positive reviews have been found to have less influence than negative reviews (Yin et al. 2016). Also, positive information may seem less credible than negative reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). As a result, investors may not pay much attention to a firm’s responses to positive customer messages. In contrast, customers’ complaints can be more salient than their praises, and therefore, a firm’s efforts for resolving complaints can more appealing to the investors. Our empirical result in Table 4, showing that the volume of negative customer messages (that is, CM\_Negative) has a stronger impact on the financial returns than the volume of positive customer messages (that is, CM\_Positive), is consistent with this explanation. Second, a firm’s response to a positive customer message is typically appreciative in nature and does not contain substantive information, and as such, it might often be considered unnecessary (Smart and Martin 1992). Therefore, a firm’s responses to positive customer messages may have little informational value to investors. On the contrary, responses to complaining customers can help resolve issues without attracting attention (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010) and can indicate the firm’s complaint management capability, which is an essential component of customer relationship management (Strauss and Hill 2001).

## 7. Contributions

Our study makes several important contributions to the literature. First, our study is among the first to rigorously examine the financial returns to firms’ communication actions on a firm-initiated social media platform. Although recent studies have examined the outcomes of firm’s participation in social media with consumer-level outcomes (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012, Goh et al. 2013, Miller and Tucker 2013, Rishika et al. 2013), we extend previous studies by showing that firms’ actions on a firm-owned social media significantly contribute to their stock market performance, demonstrating that firm-owned social media is indeed an important source of information for investors. Our results provide an explanation as to why many firms are investing heavily in social media management (Wessel 2011) and interacting with their customers on firm-initiated social media sites. Given the challenges in the valuation of firms’ intangible assets, a firm’s social media actions, viewed as endeavors to deliver informative messages about the firm and communicate with its customers more efficiently and effectively, may function as a signal to investors that a firm has valuable assets (Marks 2014)

Second, based on detailed content and sentiment analyses, our study makes a contribution by examining the differential effects of different aspects of firms’ social media communication—especially with regard to a firm’s responses to customer messages. Specifically, we make a novel contribution to the literature by not only separating a firm’s responses to customer messages based on the valence of customer messages (negative versus positive) but also, examining the effects of volume as well as timeliness of a firm’s responses to customer messages on firm performance. Matching a firm’s comments with customers posts and comments depending on their sentiment (negative versus positive) to quantify a firm’s responses constitutes an important empirical contribution. Although considerable research has been conducted on social media, prior research has not considered the market value of firm’s distinct communication actions on social media. Our findings highlight the importance of social media communication actions geared toward managing relationships with customers, which include not only responding to customers’ negative messages (Gu and Ye 2014) but also, ensuring that the responses are prompt. Furthermore, whereas not part of the main analyses, we developed a sophisticated categorization scheme for a firm’s posts (that is, broadcast communication) based on content analysis and find that only a few types of content relating to a firm’s core business activities in the broadcast communication pay off.

Third, we broaden the scope of enquiry in the literature on social media strategy (Aral et al. 2013) by theorizing and empirically validating the interplay between the two communication strategies on social media in contributing to firms’ market value. Although a few studies have examined the effects of different content characteristics of firms’ social media messages on consumer behavior (Goh et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2018), no prior studies have investigated the value implications of different types of firms’ communication strategies in social media and the possible interaction between them. Specifically, we find that a firm’s responsive communication strategy (that is, responses) provides higher business value than broadcast communication strategy (that is, posts) and that the two strategies are complements in creating value for the firm. Importantly, our results suggest that such complementarity is particularly salient for a firm’s responses to customer complaints, which further underscores the importance of managing the relationship with dissatisfied customers. Together, our study provides a rich understanding of the performance impacts of firms’ communication actions on a firmmanaged social media platform.

The findings of this study provide practical implications as well. First, our findings provide a rationale for firms to invest heavily in their social media while countering those who question the value of social media (Mueller 2011). We found that a 1% increase in volume (timeliness) of firm’s responses to customers’ negative messages is associated with a 0.04% (0.03%) increase in abnormal return. Our conceptual framework and empirical methodology can help firms more accurately gauge the ROI of their social media investments and tailor their efforts accordingly. Second, in addition to providing evidence about the positive effect of investments in social media on firm performance, our study provides valuable insights to firms about how to formulate their social media strategies that will affect the returns to their investments in social media. Most importantly, our results suggest that promptly responding to customer complaints is a key to increasing customer satisfaction and firm performance. Furthermore, consistent with the findings of recent reports that indiscriminate social media messages can cause digital fatigue in consumers (Fouts 2013), our results suggest that only those broadcast messages directly related to firms’ core business activities contribute to firm performance. Therefore, social media managers should focus their efforts on communicating business-related messages and not overloading customers with irrelevant messages. Third, although firms may be tempted to overallocate their resources to broadcast communication because of its visibility and potential reach (Gallaugher and Ransbotham 2010), our finding about the complementarity between the two types of communication actions suggests that complementary investments in one-to-one communication (especially in responding to complaining customers) are critical to gaining benefits from social media initiatives. Taken together, our study provides guidance for firms on how to optimize their social media strategies to extract greater value from social media.

Our study has some limitations that offer directions for future research. We focused on large firms that are listed in public stock markets and have already adopted social media. Our results may not generalize to small firms, which may not invest in social media as actively. Although a recent report suggests that smal companies also need to invest in social media (Hardy 2012), future research should replicate our analyses for small firms. However, compared with prior studies that have examined the business value of social media, our results should be more reliable, because we use a large data set from many Facebook pages of publicly traded firms across industries, whereas most of the prior studies have relied on the data from a single Facebook page (Goh et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2018). Another limitation in terms of generalization may be owing to the fact that our data set comprises South Korean firms. Given that South Korean firms have actively used Facebook pages as their dominant firmowned social media platform, we believe that the use of data from South Korean firms is warranted. In fact, an increasing number of recent studies published in Information Systems Research and other premier journals have used data collected from South Korea (Ghose et al. 2013) as well as China (Fang et al. 2015) India (Aggarwal et al. 2015), and other Asian coun tries (Goh et al. 2013). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the results may not generalize to other geographic regions because of cultural or other differences across countries. Future research should replicate this study in different contexts, including different geographies. In addition, our research design does not allow us to consider the difference in firm value before and after adopting social media. To alleviate some of these issues, one fruitful direction for future search is the use of field experiments and difference-in-differences techniques (Rishika et al. 2013). Although there are no differences in terms of distribution, advertising expenditure, sales, and market value between the firms in our sample and the firms that were excluded because of technical issues, not fully retaining our samples remains a limitation of our study. Furthermore, we acknowledge that there are more nuanced factors that can influence the value implications of firms’ communication actions on social media. For example, the types of complaints (for example, product specific versus general) and firms’ strategies for responding to complaints (for example, responding to all complaints versus responding to selected complaints) can influence the financial returns to firms’ communication efforts. Another factor that can matter is the nature and extent of resolutions to the complaints in firms’ responses. It will be interesting to examine these factors to generate more nuanced implications for firms, although it will require very detailed content analysis on a few (or one) firms. Finally, similar to other empirical studies using secondary data, we cannot claim causality despite our best efforts to address reverse causality and endogeneity. Additional studies are needed to demonstrate causality between firms’ social media actions and firm value. We hope that our research inspires future studies exploring the business value of social media.

Social media is a reality for contemporary firms, and it has become an important communication channel between firms and their customers. Although potentially powerful, using social media, such as Facebook, as a communication medium is complex and multifaceted. As a result, its financial returns and impact on customers are still not clear, and the evidence provided by prior research is scant. As such, researchers’ ability to provide concise and actionable guidelines that can help managers effectively and efficiently use social media to communicate with customer is relatively limited. This paper extends research on this topic and answers several questions related to social media use as a communication tool. The paper also raises a series of new questions that we hope will trigger additional research on this important phenomenon.
