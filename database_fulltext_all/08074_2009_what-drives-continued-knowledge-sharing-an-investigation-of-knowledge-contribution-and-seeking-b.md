# What drives continued knowledge sharing? An investigation of knowledge-contribution and -seeking beliefs

Wei He <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Kwok-Kee Wei <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, China

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 20 November 2008

Keywords: KMS continuance Belief Behavior Knowledge contribution Knowledge seeking

## a b s t r a c t

Knowledge management (KM) research has yielded extensive explanations regarding the individual's moti vation to share knowledge, each with different sets of factors. Yet the study of continued knowledge sharing is rare. There has been little research investigating this issue from contributing and seeking perspectives—the two distinct, but closely interrelated, facets of continued knowledge sharing. We propose that knowledge management system (KMS) users' beliefs are contextually differentiated, and thus a distinction between knowledgecontribution and knowledge-seeking behaviors and an adequate emphasis on their variance in terms of user belief is needed. By incorporating the knowledge-contribution and knowledge-seeking perspectives in a single study, we model and examine the differences among driving factors in two behavioral contexts, provide the conceptual comparisons and preliminary discussions, and thus advance our understanding of continued knowl edge sharing via the KMS.

## 1. Introduction

Managing knowledge has long been a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant topic for contemporary organizations. Effective leveraging of organizational knowledge resources can ensure that the right knowledge is available to the right people at the right time, and, as a result, improves the quality of decision making [31]. Therefore, information systems (IS) researchers have increasingly explored information technology (IT)-related solutions to knowledge management (KM), speci<sup>fi</sup>cally knowledge management systems (KMS) [3].

One of the fundamental advantages offered by KMS is the relevant, accurate, and timely knowledge it provides in different circumstances that can enable a company and its individuals the ability for effective problem solving and decision making [27,57,63]. Decision makers can <sup>fi</sup>nd and exploit novel and diverse information and knowledge embedded in the organization by searching in a corporate KMS, and thus enhance the organization's ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and increase its competitiveness [57]. In addition, the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of successful experiences, advice, insights, and ideas in the KMS by the knowledge workers can be valuable when reused and leveraged in other applicative decisions in the organization [63]. Despite the ubiquity of the KMS, knowledge codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, storage, dissemination, and reuse via the KMS remains a challenge [57]. In particular, infrequent use of the IT may result in a considerable waste of an organization's resources and efforts in its development and implementation processes [32].

Once IT is implemented, it can only meet the organization's expectations when the technologies are continually used by the employees [60]. IS scholars use the term continuance to refer to continued use behavior of a particular IS [5], relative to its <sup>fi</sup>rst adoption. Both IS adoption and continuance research streams constitute a complete picture of IS usage behavior. Although IS continuance research has attracted more attention in recent years [5,32,35], there has been surprisingly little of this research in the KM area, though managing organizational knowledge and promotion of knowledge sharing have long been believed to be a vital source of competitive advantage for contemporary organizations [42]. Thus, understanding the factors that drive and sustain KMS continued usage is still a central concern for both researchers and practitioners.

Although KMS is by nature an IT-based system, its continuance issues are distinctly different from those of other IS—successful KMS continuance requires that system users be willing to codify and share their knowledge in the KMS, while also seeking out and reusing the codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge [37,38,63]. The usage of KMS by knowledge seekers may produce positive feedback, increase contributor recognition, and eventually lead to more contributions [22]. However, there are potential negative outcomes, such as information overload and opportunity costs of contributors, which may threaten the KMS sustainability [22,26]. Thus, an organization's KMS must feature a dynamic balance of contribution and seeking behaviors. Previous studies have indicated that users may have distinct needs and motivations that lead to different behaviors in the KMS, i.e., the behaviors associated with knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking [37,38,63]. In the existing literature, however, the two perspectives are usually examined independently by researchers (with a few exceptions, cf. [22,63]).

As a result, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to view, through a coherent theoretical lens, the varied sets of KMS user' signi<sup>fi</sup>cant beliefs and distinctions in the two behavioral processes.

The main objective of this study is to bridge this gap and progress toward a comprehensive understanding of KMS continuance. Drawing on the theory of cognitive integration [4] and the IS continuance model [5,6], this study adopts a belief-based view in order to understand how continued knowledge sharing is associated with a variety of users' beliefs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose that KMS continuance be studied by applying the IS continuance models. However, it is necessary to list the continuance models of two distinct types of behavior and examine their motivations separately. Furthermore, recognizing that user beliefs are at the heart of IT usage behavior [17] and, most importantly, user beliefs could be context-dependent and multifaceted [19], this study focuses on the further decomposition of the user's cognitive beliefs: What drives employees to contribute knowledge in a KMS and what drives them to continually seek knowledge? We propose that a KMS user responds to several attributes (such as enjoyment in helping, reciprocity, image, etc. in a contributing context; and perceived usefulness, ease of use, organizational reward, etc. in a seeking context) and con<sup>fi</sup>gures these responses into a single unit (belief) that combines these attributes [55]. The differentiated belief sets speci<sup>fi</sup>c to each behavioral context are derived from the existing literature and are tested with empirical data from the industry. This study is thus one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to provide a conceptual comparison of knowledge workers' beliefs in two behavioral contexts, discuss its applications, and thus advance our understanding of continued knowledge sharing via the KMS.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. IS continuance research

Prior IS research has implicitly examined the continuance concept by suggesting that IS adopters reevaluate their earlier acceptance decision during a <sup>fi</sup>nal “con<sup>fi</sup>rmation” stage and decide whether to continue or discontinue using an innovation [54]. Several scholars have explicitly elaborated the importance of IS continuance and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of understanding IS continuance behavior [5,10,35].

Bhattacherjee [5] adapts the expectation-con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT) to theorize about and validate one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst IS continuance models. He <sup>fi</sup>nds that the intention toward IS continuance is strongly predicted by the user's satisfaction and perceived usefulness is a secondary predictor. In this early model, the conceptualization of satisfaction is derived from marketing literature. However, “satisfaction is a transient, experience-speci<sup>fi</sup>c affect, while attitude is a relatively more enduring affect transcending all prior experiences” [5] (p. 354). For example, a user could have a positive attitude toward the IT usage but still feel dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed if his experience is below expectation. Therefore, users' attitude is chosen to act as a more reliable determinant of continuance in the post-adoption stage [6].

Hong et al. [32] advance Bhattacherjee's model [5] by integrating perceived ease of use into the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [18] to make it a richer post-adoption model. The extended model is further tested with data from mobile Internet users. Results show that the newly extended model and TAM are comparable in terms of variance explained for intention to continue IT usage $( R ^ { 2 } = 6 7 \%$ and 63%, respectively). This study also implies that TAM—the most parsimonious and generic model in predicting IT usage intention—can be applied to study both <sup>fi</sup>rst-adoption and continued-usage behaviors.

On the basis of reviewing eight user-acceptance models, Venkatesh et al. [60] formulate a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model, called the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). UTAUT implicitly deals with IS continuance by positing experience as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderator in most of the relationships in the model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, UTAUT posits two main determinants of usage behavior (usage intention and facilitating conditions), and three direct determinants of intention (performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence). Its results indicate that effort tends to be an insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in the continued-usage stage, while the effect of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on intention, and the effect of facilitating conditions on continuance behavior become much more important.

Despite the noteworthy contributions of these in<sup>fl</sup>uential studies on IS continuance, the predominant role of intention is questioned by the argument that IS usage would transcend conscious behavior and become part of normal routine activity [10]. Scholars examine the moderating effect of habit on the relationship between IS continuance intention and actual continued behavior. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the moderating effect of habit increases over time, while the impact of a user's IS continuance intention on IS continued behavior weakens over time.

Jasperson et al. [35] address post-adoption behavior as the interplay between organizational intervention and individual cognition. Although this study does not develop the measures, nor does it empirically validate its model, it does shed light on the need to seek a more complete understanding of a user's belief rather than perceived usefulness as a single factor in predicting the user's behavior. Prior studies of IT usage predominantly focus on perceived usefulness because it is parsimonious and easy to understand [6,32]. However, a key issue here is to understand what kind of utilities “perceived usefulness” represents to the users in different system contexts. Unless we know more about what perceived usefulness really means to users in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS environment, we cannot claim to enhance the utility of IT and further improve IT usage (for a detailed dissertation on rethinking the construct of perceived usefulness, see [19]). For instance, in KMS, does perceived usefulness always mean improved work performance? If the answer is NO for knowledge-contribution circumstances, what are the factors we should adopt to predict the continued usage intention of knowledge contributors? For knowledge-seeking circumstances, is it possible there are other aspects that are at least as important to the users as utility in <sup>fi</sup>nishing their tasks? How do we understand the varied impacts of the factors listed in different studies? In order to answer these questions, we propose that KMS users' beliefs are contextually differentiated, and thus we must create a distinction between knowledge-contribution and knowledgeseeking behaviors, as well as place an adequate emphasis on their comparison in terms of user belief.

## 2.2. The structure of belief: the theory of cognitive integration

Belief is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the subjective probability of a relation between the object of the belief and some other object, value, concept, or attribute [23]. Research on IT usage is primarily grounded in models from social psychology, such as the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [2] and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [1]. These intentionbased models regard behavioral intention as a weighted function of multiple attributes, where the user's attitude is equated with one parsimonious belief: perceived usefulness [56].

However, we argue that this line of research, with its simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed concept of user belief, is inadequate to address what motivates individual knowledge workers to engage in continued knowledge sharing in the KMS. Several de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of monolithic belief can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. First, knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking and reuse are two distinct types of behaviors. Knowledge contribution refers to the “codi<sup>fi</sup>cation and storage of existing knowledge into knowledge repositories or databases such that it can be accessed and reused by other individuals within the <sup>fi</sup>rm,” while knowledge seeking is usually taken to mean “one individual using knowledge generated by a different individual or group within the same <sup>fi</sup>rm in order to be more effective and productive in their work” [63] (p. 143). The IS literature on KM issues has shown that different needs and motivations are associated with these two types of behaviors in KMS [37,38,63]. Thus, the user's belief sets idiosyncratic to one's behavioral environment should be developed independently.

Second, although they have unique motivational features, contribution and seeking are a pair of closely interrelated and inseparable behaviors—for the presumed bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of KMS to occur, both must happen. On one hand, the act of seeking and reusing knowledge may predict the frequency of knowledge contribution under certain circumstances [22,63]. On the other hand, the presence of critical mass (a small number of individuals contributing knowledge continually) can insure a KMS with plenty of useful knowledge available for searching and reuse by potential knowledge seekers [48]. A tight interplay of the KMS and its principal part—the users—may exist, and may lead to the continuance spiraling upward. The unique characteristics of KMS imply different cognitive belief structures and consequences for the continued knowledge-contributing and -seeking behaviors.

A growing literature attempts to address the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors in successful knowledge sharing [13,37,38,48,62]. While these studies have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved our understanding of IT-mediated knowledge sharing, most researchers address only one <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge rather than both contribution and seeking processes. Their results have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinants of knowledge contribution or seeking, but they have also demonstrated differing patterns across behavioral environments. The lack of a coherent theoretical interpretation limits our understanding of KMS continuance. Our study begins to <sup>fi</sup>ll in this gap.

The theory of cognitive integration provides a framework re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the idea that “a user's attitude can be determined by his or her evaluation of a set of relevant system attributes weighted by his or her perceptions of the importance of these attributes” [55, p. 89]. According to Sethi and King [55] and Anderson [4], the foundation and mechanism for cognitive integration is in the form of two concepts: valuation and integration. Valuation refers to the process of determining the evaluative scale values and the weights assigned to each item of information that contributes to the attitudinal judgment. Integration refers to the process of combining the information units into an overall attitudinal judgment in terms of the sum of all weights. In the current study, our goal is to propose and examine user belief models, which are conjunctive decision-making models developed in response to the different behavioral environments: contributing or seeking. We simultaneously propose contribution belief and seeking belief, integrated from two sets of belief structures for respective contexts. In addition, we test the KMS continuance for knowledge contribution and for knowledge seeking based on synthesized IS continuance models from prior literature.

To base our research on the existing literature, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the salient beliefs popularly considered to play a role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing knowledge sharing among individuals in recent KM studies. In order to optimize the research focus, we use “knowledge management system,” “knowledge repository,” and “knowledge sharing” as key words to search for relevant literature in referred journals such as Organization Science, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and others. After screening out those studies focusing on non-individual factors (e.g., institutional factors), we summarized the studies emphasizing individual beliefs in the usage of KMS, listed in Table 1. Their research contexts are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in order to facilitate our next step of categorizing in terms of contribution or seeking behaviors. We synthesize these factors to theorize that seven constructs form a user's contribution belief and six constructs shape a user's seeking belief.

## 2.3. Factors influencing knowledge contribution

Image is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the perception of an increase in positive reputation due to contributing knowledge [37]. Prior research <sup>fi</sup>nds that building reputation is a strong motivator for active participation in electronic networks of practice [21]. Recent work con<sup>fi</sup>rms that people contribute knowledge when they perceive their professional reputations will be enhanced [62].

Enjoyment in helping others is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the perception of pleasure obtained from helping others through knowledge contribution [37]. Prior research shows that people who contribute their knowledge gain satisfaction stemming from their intrinsic enjoyment in helping others [8,61]. In turn, enjoyment in helping others can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the knowledge contributor's IS usage [37].

Reciprocity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the bene<sup>fi</sup>t expectancy of a future request for knowledge being met as a result of the current contribution [37]. There is evidence that people who share knowledge in online communities believe in reciprocity [61]. In addition, reciprocity is thought to exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence on information sharing by means of a “return-inkind” attitude [40].

## 2.4. Factors influencing knowledge seeking

Perceived usefulness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which an individual believes that knowledge seeking in the KMS would enhance his or her work performance [17,18]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition is consistent with that of performance expectancy in UTAUT [60]. Perceived usefulness is a salient belief in IS use that has a predominant and persistent effect on usage intention across time and various IS contexts [5,59]. Also, prior KM research shows a strong, positive relationship between perceived usefulness and knowledge-seeking behavior [7,20,38].

Knowledge growth is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the knowledge seeker's perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t of enhancing his or her own learning and experience [61]. In other words, it is the perception by users that participation in seeking knowledge online results in learning and personal access to new knowledge or innovations [7,27,61].

2.5. Factors influencing both knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking

Organizational reward is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the importance of economic incentives provided for knowledge contributors and knowledge seekers [37,38]. Rewards are considered important components of the KM process [51]. In order to encourage knowledge-sharing behaviors, organizations may provide various forms of reward such as salary raises, bonuses, job security, and opportunities for promotion [8,37]. Hall [30] presents the effectiveness of organizational reward in shaping employees' attitude. However, some researchers have found evidence indicating that organizational reward may in fact exert a negative effect on an individual's attitude toward knowledge sharing [8].

Management influence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which an employee perceives that the management believes he or she should contribute or seek knowledge via KMS [60]. There exists abundant research on the importance of management support or leadership in KM initiatives [39]. The more the employees believe that information sharing is “correct and socially expected workplace behavior,” the more they would be willing to share [15].

Effort is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of energy associated with the knowledge-contribution or -seeking behavior, in terms of time and exertion required [37], and this factor is categorized into contribution effort and seeking effort. Expected effort is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of intention and behavior [60]. Therefore, we use the effort construct to represent and cover multifaceted obstacles in knowledge-contribution and -seeking behaviors, such as lack of appropriate tools [30], dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of searching and <sup>fi</sup>nding [64], insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciency of personalization [47], and time and effort constraints [27,37].

Social relationship is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual's perception of other KMS users (supervisors, subordinates, and peers) with whom the person has social interactions [16,57]. Information sharing is believed to be distinguishable from other simple exchange behavior where individuals act merely from rational self-interest [34]. Researchers believe that knowledge sharing is a kind of social interaction among people. The existing literature provides suf<sup>fi</sup>cient evidence that social relationship can facilitate collective action, while the lack of relationship between the contribution side and the seeking side is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a major barrier to knowledge transfer [46]. From prior literature, we identify three aspects of social relationship that are particularly conducive to knowledge sharing: trust [13,20,27,43,45], norms [8,13,37, 45,51], and tie strength [13,41,52].

## 2.6. Factors theorized not to be dimensions of belief

Self-efficacy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the judgment of one's ability to use a technology to accomplish a particular job or task [14]. Prior research identi<sup>fi</sup>es that self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy's effect on intention can be fully mediated by perceived ease of use [58,60]. Consistent with prior research, we will not consider self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy accounts to be a key dimension of belief with the presence of effort expectancy.

Subjective norm is a person's perception that most people who are important to him or her think he or she should or should not perform the behavior in question [1,23,56]. However, Taylor and Todd suggest the decomposition of this normative belief into referent groups (i.e., peers and superiors) since their expectation may differ. In this study, we have adopted the constructs of management in<sup>fl</sup>uence and social relationship to represent the effects of top management and the employee's colleagues, which are more speci<sup>fi</sup>c than a subjective norm.

Perceived behavioral control re<sup>fl</sup>ects perceptions of internal and external constraints on behavior [56]. The role of this construct, which encompasses self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, resource-facilitating conditions, and technology-facilitating conditions has been tapped by effort expectancy and facilitating conditions in the UTAUT [60]. We therefore follow UTAUT's argument to drop this embodied concept.

We argue that in order to further understand KMS continuance behavior, two sets of decomposed cognitive beliefs should be considered. Distinct dimensions of user's belief are examined from two perspectives of behavior: knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking.

## 3. Research model

IS continuance research suggests that the user's intention toward continued system usage is predictable by measuring user's belief and attitude [5,6], which provides a platform for our study of KMS continuance. We therefore take the advantage of this platform and integrate the factors veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in other continuance studies to formulate a comprehensive understanding of KMS continuance. We suggest that a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed KMS continuance model can be coherently applied to both knowledge-contribution and knowledge-seeking contexts in order to explain the potential determinants of continued knowledge-sharing behaviors, although the structure of user's beliefs varies across two different contexts.

Prior research has extensively elaborated behavioral intention as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct determinant of IS continuance [5,60]. However, in most of the studies on knowledge sharing, this link between intention and actual usage appears to be missing. The prior literature either stops at the prediction of intention (e.g. [8]) or relates a group of factors directly to the actual behavior (e.g., [37,38,62,63]). We argue that including the intention–usage link can capture and gauge a user's action process in a more delicate manner. Since KMS continuance is basically one type of IS usage, we expect that users' continuance will be positively associated with their intention to contribute or to seek knowledge. Thus, we have developed the following hypotheses:

H1a. Users' contribution intention has significant impact on KMS continuance for knowledge contribution.

H1b. Users' seeking intention has significant impact on KMS continuance for knowledge seeking.

Further, organizational support is related to readiness for change. Venkatesh et al. [60] provide empirical evidence that the facilitating conditions have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on IS usage in the continuance stage. Prior research on KM also suggests that organizational facilitating conditions, such as training and having suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources available for the employees for assistance can stimulate the knowledge transfer and sharing. For instance, Watson and Hewett [63] argue that employees are more likely to participate in the desired outcome (seeking and reusing knowledge in the KMS) if they receive better training on how to use the system. These arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

H2a. Facilitating conditions have significant impact on KMS continuance for knowledge contribution.

H2b. Facilitating conditions have significant impact on KMS continuance for knowledge seeking.

Recent research has noted that habit can moderate the relationship between continuance intention and continuance behavior [10]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the more usage is performed out of habit, the less intentional behavior is involved. Until now, there has been no evidence regarding the role of habit in the usage of IT-mediated knowledge sharing. We anticipate that:

H3a. The influence of contribution intention on KMS continuance for knowledge contribution is moderated by user habit; the positive effect of contribution intention on continuance is weaker when a high level of user habit is present.

H3b. The influence of seeking intention on KMS continuance for knowledge seeking is moderated by user habit; the positive effect of seeking intention on continuance is weaker when a high level of user habit is present.

The key construct we focus on is the user's belief. Consistent with our theoretical platform, we expect that employees will have a strong intention to contribute or seek knowledge in the KMS if they have formed a positive belief about the proposed behavior. Similarly, if the user has formed a positive attitudinal evaluation regarding contribution or seeking behavior in the KMS, he or she will have a stronger intention to use the system for that behavior. Hence, we propose that the user's belief and attitude both have direct effects on intention.

H4a. User contribution belief has significant impact on contribution intention.

H4b. User seeking belief has significant impact on seeking intention.

H5a. User contribution attitude has significant impact on contribution intention.

H5b. User seeking attitude has significant impact on seeking intention.

According to IS continuance research [6] and the theory of cognitive integration [4], people derive an overall attitudinal disposition from an array of beliefs they hold about an object. For example, if a user believes that contributing in the KMS can gain him/herself intrinsic pleasure, he or she is more likely to hold a positive attitude toward using the KMS to contribute knowledge. In addition, user satisfaction has been theorized and validated as an important predictor of attitude toward IS continuance. It is easy to understand that a user who is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the contents and functions of the KMS is likely to establish a positive attitude toward knowledge seeking via the KMS. Hence, we hypothesize the user's attitude toward KMS continuance as being determined jointly by the user's belief and satisfaction.

H6a. User contribution belief is positively associated with attitude.

H6b. User seeking belief is positively associated with attitude.

H7a. User satisfaction has significant impact on the contribution attitude toward KMS continuance.

H7b. User satisfaction has significant impact on the seeking attitude toward KMS continuance.

Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation (the extent to which a user's expectation is con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed) is an important construct in the continuance literature. Based on the two-stage model of cognition change [6], con<sup>fi</sup>rmation (or discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation) acts as an emergent construct in<sup>fl</sup>uencing a user's belief and satisfaction in the continuance stage. In other words, con-<sup>fi</sup>rmation is positively related to satisfaction with IS usage because it implies realization of the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IS usage [5]. The more the users feel their expectations around electronically contributing knowledge (such as enhanced image, achieved organizational rewards, better social relationships with other users, and others) are con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, the more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed they are. However, it is also suggested that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation tends to elevate users' original belief, and discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation reduces such cognitive perceptions [5,6]. For example, if the KMS user cannot perceive the utility of reusing the knowledge in a KMS, he or she is likely to modify the prede<sup>fi</sup>ned belief structure in order to bring it into accord. Hence, we propose that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation is positively associated with user belief.

## 4. Method

Testing of the proposed hypotheses was predicated on a Webbased survey in an international IT company. The company implemented its knowledge repository beginning in January of 2004. A knowledge repository is one of the most common forms of KMS. It is a structured collection of documents storing various organizational knowledge, such as product knowledge, customer knowledge, and best practices of the organization and of the industry [7,38]. At the targeted company, 20,000 employees worldwide can log in to the system to share their knowledge, collaborate, and communicate with each other. Both onsite and offsite trainings on how to use the KMS have been offered to all the employees along with the system implementation. Employees in branches were allocated extra resources for hardware and network con<sup>fi</sup>guration. Also, <sup>fi</sup>ve members of the KMS project team were available to support any colleagues having dif-<sup>fi</sup>culties or to respond to requests and questions on KMS usage. Top management presented a high degree of commitment and support (e.g., the CEO himself used the KMS and advocated the KM championship in formal meetings periodically). In order to encourage the creation and reuse of knowledge via the platform of KMS, the company performed assessment trials based on individual performance in the system, for example, “top 10 most valuable articles,” “the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential expert,” or “knowledge-seeking star of the month,” and offered corresponding rewards. However, the leveraging of knowledge resources through search and reuse of existing knowledge in the KMS was not found to be satisfactory. Hence, the management solicited measures that would enhance the system usage. System users with more than six months of usage experience were chosen as our research subjects. Such a criterion was used so the samples would be eligible for our study of continued behavior. Although the adoption decision to implement the KMS was made by management, individual employees usage of this system was entirely voluntary.

## 4.1. Data collection

Data was collected through questionnaires. We chose three functional units in the company—manufacturing, research and development, and marketing—in which to conduct the survey because these had implemented the system earliest, as part of the pilot, and the system was used in these units in a wide scope but with varying levels of popularity. In total, 500 eligible system users were invited to answer a set of (both contribution and seeking) questionnaires online. Users chose to answer either the contribution or seeking questionnaire, or both, according to their own usage experiences. A detailed de<sup>fi</sup>nition and a practical example of knowledge contribution were given on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the contribution questionnaire to help the respondents discern the speci<sup>fi</sup>c behavior and elicit its corresponding beliefs. A corresponding cue was offered on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the seeking questionnaire. Since the questions measuring common beliefs (social relationship, organizational reward, and management in<sup>fl</sup>uence) are separated in two disjunctive questionnaires, the responses are considered to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the diverse behaviors. Participation in this survey was voluntary, with an offered incentive of a small amount of cash in the form of a coupon. In sum, 161 participants answered the questionnaire regarding knowledge-contribution behavior, and 201 answered regarding knowledge-seeking behavior. Table 2 shows the respondents' characteristics according to system use type and the functional unit to which they belonged. One month later, their usage behavior data was collected separately by asking the respondents to report their time spent in the KMS for knowledge contribution and seeking, respectively.

## 4.2. Measures

We used operational measures that have been validated by prior research. Consistent with the recommendations of Fishbein and Ajzen [23], all questions in the survey were adjusted to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of KMS continuance rather than general IS continuance. The scales were also shortened in order to make possible the inclusion of all constructs of interest in the questionnaire.

Users' beliefs are conceptualized in terms of varied dimensions, thus they are modeled with a formative higher-order model. A formative model is appropriate because the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order subconstructs (seven in contribution model and six in seeking model) are examining different facets of user belief and are posited to de<sup>fi</sup>ne or form the contribution and seeking beliefs [49]. Moreover, the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order subconstructs do not necessarily covary with one another. For example, any change in perceived enjoyment in helping by a knowledge contributor would not necessarily imply a corresponding change in the other <sup>fi</sup>rst-order subconstructs, such as image as an expert or perceived management influence. Since the model contains two secondorder variables, i.e., contribution belief and seeking belief, we created the superordinate second-order constructs using factor scores for the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order construct [12]. User's con<sup>fi</sup>rmation is modeled to be a formative construct. Formative constructs are a composite of multiple measures, and each measure captures differing aspects of the construct [49]. For example, using seeking perspective, con<sup>fi</sup>rmation in seeking model is operationalized using six measures: user's con<sup>fi</sup>rmation on social relationship, perceived usefulness, knowledge growth, organizational reward, management in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and seeking effort, respectively.

All of the other constructs were modeled using re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators. Following previous work in KM literature [28], we asked the respondents to think of knowledge as a mix of expertise, experience, process, conceptual information, and insights that provide a framework for decision making or problem solving. One example of knowledge sharing is that people doing similar projects at different sites can access archives and progress reports on past projects and also codify their own observations, procedures, interpretations, and judgment (such as tips for solving certain problems in research development or bidding proposals) and publish the personalized knowledge in the knowledge repository. For all constructs except tie strength, satisfaction, and attitude, we used a 5-point Likert scale to measure them (from strongly disagree to strongly agree; the items involving closeness and frequency were adjusted accordingly). The items measuring satisfaction and attitude are anchored by different end points to avoid commonalities in the scale anchors and formats [50]. Tie strength was measured by frequency and closeness. As other IS researchers (e.g., [60]) before us have done, the actual continuance behavior was measured via a single indicator—the usage time spent in the system. Two authors coded the results from 5-point scales ranging from rarely used (1) to extensively used (5). The actual usage data was collected one month after the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey on beliefs, attitudes, and intentions was distributed and collected. This temporal design is consistent with the causality chain in psychology literature, which considers motivations and behavior as sequential and not simultaneous events [44]. Additionally, by proximally separating the measures of motivation and behavior, we diminished the potential concern regarding common method biases [50].

Several control variables were included in the analysis to rule out alternative explanations and build on prior research. Since motivation theories have suggested that prior knowledge and skills may affect individual behavior [9], we included education and years in the organization (job tenure), two widely used proxies for employees' level of knowledge, as well as job skills [53] as control variables. Moreover, past experience in using KMS was included as a control variable since the extent to which an individual has used a system could in<sup>fl</sup>uence his or her future usage behavior [10].

An initial version of the instrument was re<sup>fi</sup>ned through two rounds of pilot tests using employees in the same company who were later excluded from the main survey. We further interviewed some respondents to improve the questionnaire with the purpose of avoiding vague concepts and keeping questions simple, concise, and relevant to the target respondents. The <sup>fi</sup>nal items used in the survey are shown in Appendix A.

## 4.3. Data analysis

Partial least squares (PLS) is a multivariate analytic technique that enables path analytic modeling with latent variables [11]. Unlike standard linear regression, PLS does not require multivariate normality when estimating parameters. Moreover, PLS is appropriate for testing the causal model because it is suitable for assessing theories at an early stage of development [24], as is the case with this study. Finally, the PLS method is more suitable for use with smaller samples than are other structural equation–modeling techniques (e.g., LISREL, AMOS, and EQS) [12]. PLS Graph Version 3.00 was used to test the proposed models in two steps: a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis was <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted to assess the unidimensionality of the items; following that, the structural relationship was examined.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Discussion of the empirical results

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide strong support for the proposed KMS continuance model. In agreement with other scholars [10], we <sup>fi</sup>nd that habit exhibits a strong moderating effect on the relationship between intention and behavior in both contexts. Thus, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to test the role of user habit in knowledge-sharing settings.

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result regarding facilitating conditions recon<sup>fi</sup>rms the conclusions found in prior studies of knowledge sharing [7,38], that facilitating conditions matter in knowledge-seeking behavior. Notably, our analysis reveals that organizational resources and facilitating conditions are also very important, and probably more important, for continued knowledge-contribution behaviors. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the explanatory power of the facilitating conditions is slightly weakened in the seeking perspective. One plausible reason is that in the need-driven behaviors such as knowledge seeking, intention is still the main causal mechanism used to explain why people continue or discontinue using an IS.

Social relationship is found to represent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant aspect of the KMS user's belief in both contexts. It has been widely realized that social relationship can affect an individual's attitude toward knowledge sharing (e.g., [8,13,57]. Our study not only echoes the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior studies but also highlights the importance of social relationship in knowledge-seeking scenarios, thus advancing the understanding of how social relationship, one of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant aspects of user belief, affects KMS users' attitude and intention, and eventually determines the KMS continuance behavior.

Contrary to the commonly held notion, a seeker's knowledge growth shows to be a nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in forming the seeking belief for KMS usage, a <sup>fi</sup>nding that is both surprising and interesting. The research subjects included in a prior study by Bock et al. [7], where a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between knowledge growth and EKR usage was found, were MBA students in a large university. Student goals are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition aligned with knowledge growth. Our results imply that corporate employees' intrinsic motivation for seeking knowledge for the sake of growing knowledge may not be as salient as that of students in the educational setting. Indeed, in a study of 182 sales teams in a management consulting company, Haas and Hansen [29] found that sharing codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge in the form of electronic documents can save time during the task, but does not necessarily improve the individual's competence. Likewise, Kane and Alavi [36] argued that using the electronic knowledge repositories seldom contributes to the seeker's knowledge growth or long-term learning. Based on our results, we maintain that in the context of a corporate environment, the ultimate goal for employees to use a KMS is not to learn new things, but rather to perform their jobs more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively. The current study thus extends the prior research by further investigating the knowledge growth–KMS usage behavior linkage in a broader task context.

An interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study is that management in<sup>fl</sup>uence may exert different effects on KMS users in contribution and seeking behaviors. As indicated in the literature review, the common argument is that management can greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' attitude and intention so as to promote knowledge-sharing behavior. However, according to our results, the management in<sup>fl</sup>uence takes effect only in the knowledge-contribution context. When a user aims at knowledge seeking, his or her cognitive belief structures simply include performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social relationship, which is in accord with the results of previous work [60].

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support prior research that <sup>fi</sup>nds the reciprocity bene<sup>fi</sup>t of knowledge contribution is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognition in knowledge sharing [62]. This indicates that reciprocal expectation may not be an important concern at the continuance stage. Although extrinsic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as reciprocity have proven effective in stimulating people's knowledge-contribution behavior in the early stage of IS adoption, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence may weaken over time. When the user steps into the post-adoption stage, the intrinsic motivations, such as enjoyment in helping and social relationships, begin to stabilize and become the dominant beliefs.

As is evident from the literature, the effect of organizational reward on knowledge contribution and seeking is still subject to controversy. Previous work has found reward to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinant of knowledge contribution (see [37]). However, other work has found that reward has a negative effect on knowledge-sharing attitudes (see [8]). Our results suggest that organizational reward is irrelevant to an individual's KMS continuance behavior. It should be noted that all relationships tested in this study are embedded in a voluntary setting. Therefore, it is necessary to recognize the potentially contingent impact of reward.

The last dimension of user's belief discussed in our study is the perceived effort associated with knowledge contribution and seeking behaviors. Recent literature suggests that ease of use, i.e., effort construct, tends to be insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in predicting IS usage, due to the growing number of IT-savvy users nowadays, especially in the continued-usage stage [60]. Nevertheless, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that effort might remain to make sense in continued usage of KMS when we model perceived effort as one dimension of user's belief. In particular, effort presents as a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dimension in knowledge contribution than in knowledge seeking contexts, indicating that effort expectancy cannot be ignored in altruistic behaviors—knowledge seeking tends to be like that.

Variance explained in the continuance behavior is not very high (24% in contribution and 23% in seeking). One reason could be that we did not include other potential determinants (e.g., prior usage) and control variables (e.g., individual characteristics) in this study. Previous work has found that prior behavior signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacts future behavior [10]. The 46% variance in user's intention in this study can be explained. Compared with the explanatory power of 70% in some of the prior research [6,60], this value was below our expectation. However, the discrepant results might be partially attributed to different theoretical objectives and corresponding research approaches. As Taylor and Todd [56] comment regarding why TRA and TPB cannot explain usage intentions as well as TAM, “the belief measures used for TRA and TPB were based on a salient belief elicitation measure which develops a scale idiosyncratic to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c setting… under such conditions, measures of beliefs may be less than ideal” (p. 151). This statement implies that for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c research context, such as KMS continuance in this study, focusing on the understanding of a set of cognitive beliefs will inevitably result in unsatisfactory measures.

## 6.2. Limitations and future study

Prior to discussing the implications of this work, it is necessary to recognize some inherent limitations of this study. First, the data was collected within one company. This was done to rule out unnecessary variance generated by various forms of KMS in different companies. However, one danger of this approach is that the external validity of the results cannot be ensured. Future studies can replicate this study using responses from different organizational settings and with different KMS to generalize the results. Second, a large portion of the respondents (55%) are common for both behavior models. Although certain precautious measures were taken, there still is a danger that they cannot distinguish their contribution and seeking perceptions when answering two questionnaires. More rigorous design in future study can mitigate this problem inherent in KMS continuance research and increase the reliability of the research. Third, due to the scale of the survey and the process of eliciting unquali<sup>fi</sup>ed participants, the size of the sample for <sup>fi</sup>nal analysis is limited. Although the sample size is acceptable for PLS analysis, a larger and more heterogeneous sample would bring more statistical power and allow more rigorous model testing. Four, as discussed earlier, this study is only able to explain less than a quarter of the variance in KMS continuance. Future research is needed to extend our theoretical models to account for any unexplained behavioral variance, e.g., prior usage, organizational culture, and so forth. Finally, whereas a complex construct such as user's belief in KMS context deserves a more thorough measurement and analysis [49], some attention should be to the trade-off between the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using multidimensional construct to fully capture a domain topic and the price of increased complexity of analysis.

## 6.3. Implications for theory

The previous research on IS continuance has suggested that individuals continue to use IS based on a set of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors, but these potential mechanisms of continuance in the KMS context have never been explicitly explored. Furthermore, most existing KMS research was either conducted in a public domain [48] or in public organizations [7,37,38], whereas corporate KMS in the private sector have not been extensively studied (with a few notable exceptions, e.g., [27]).

This study may advance our knowledge in four ways. First, we describe a belief-based view to understand how people are driven to continued knowledge sharing in the KMS. Adopting the IS continuance model as a theoretical platform, we develop a comprehensive belief view and examine whether the factors prevalent in the KMS adoption stage are still signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the continuance stage. The varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of these factors in belief formation is therefore explored in a legible way, which enriches the understanding of what drives people to contribute and what drives them to continually seek knowledge.

Second, our research is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts at a comparative investigation of user belief for knowledge-contribution and -seeking behaviors within a single study—which is still a theoretical gap in the existing literature. In particular, based on cognitive integration theory and the KM literature, we theorize the complicated and distinct sets of determining beliefs and examine their particular impacts on continued contribution and seeking behaviors in a KMS. With the presence of two behaviors examined in a coherent theoretical framework, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the current study have challenged some basic assumptions in the KM literature, such as the real effects of knowledge growth and management in<sup>fl</sup>uence in stimulating knowledge-sharing activities via the KMS. We believe it will be interesting and insightful to further examine the statistical comparison between the knowledge-contribution and -seeking perspectives in future research.

Third, we contribute to KMS research by revealing that the in-<sup>fl</sup>uences of motivations on KMS usage behavior for knowledge sharing may be different in private organizations. We offer preliminary evidence and a theoretical explanation of why the impact of knowledge growth on seeking behavior in KMS in private organizations is distinct from those found in public settings such as universities, government agencies, and public Internet areas.

Four, by reviewing the IS continuance literature extensively and applying the models from this stream to the context of KMS, this study has supported and further re<sup>fi</sup>ned the IS continuance research. As Hong et al. [32] comment, the IS continuance model is a relatively new one that “requires more empirical validations across different technology contexts to determine its generalizability” (p. 1822). In this study, we summarized the salient factors evidenced in existing IS continuance research to propose a more comprehensive KMS continuance model. Empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the industry are supportive of our proposed models and therefore offer generalizability to the KMS that are prevalent in commercial organizations. The current study has also veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the strong moderating effect of habit in KMS continuance using the industrial data, thus emphasizing the important role of user habit in future IS continuance research.

## 6.4. Implications for practice

For practitioners, understanding the contingent nature of motivational mechanisms on two aspects of knowledge sharing is critical to stimulate a full use of KMS and capitalize on the versatile intellectual resources within organizations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study offer insights into the complexity of employees' beliefs on KMS continuance.

First, organizations must ensure that they have provided suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient facilitating conditions to the KMS users to avoid unintentional abandonment of the system if they intend to encourage more usage behaviors. This factor has not been emphasized in prior scholarly work on knowledge sharing, though it has been fully documented in the knowledge-seeking context. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects of organizational facilitating conditions on both contribution and seeking continuance suggest that organizations should never neglect resource availability and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient trainings when they hope to stimulate more knowledge contributions.

In addition, we believe that acknowledging the importance of social relationships in KMS continuance is critical. In today's global economy, organizations are increasingly relying on technologies to promote knowledge sharing and collaboration among their geographically scattered employees. Relationship appears to be a strong indicator of continued KMS usage for not only knowledge contribution but also knowledge seeking. Therefore, successful KMS continuance demands managers' effort to establish within the organization a trustworthy social relationship based on a set of shared values, norms, and expectations, which thus evolves into stronger electronic communication and interaction.

To encourage knowledge-seeking behaviors via the KMS, managers should <sup>fi</sup>rst challenge the general belief that “searching in the KMS can lead to personal knowledge growth” and recognize that using the KMS to search for knowledge is perceived as a work-related task. Firms should consider increasing contextual descriptions of “why” and “so what” of a codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge that may help increase the employees' interest in gaining knowledge by using KMS.

Finally, some commonly taken measures (e.g., the management in<sup>fl</sup>uence) work well to promote knowledge contribution, but these may not be relevant to knowledge seeking, and vice versa. Caution must be exercised when management attempts to use rewards (such as salary raises, bonuses, job security, or promotion) to encourage people to use the KMS in the long run, since the effect of rewarding on user behavior is complex.

## 7. Conclusion

To answer the question, “what drives continued knowledge sharing?”, this study develops and validates the models of KMS continuance from two perspectives: knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking. In the KM context, this study synthesizes the model of continuance in which the user's continuance intention and organizational facilitating conditions collectively predict the continued knowledge-sharing behavior via the KMS, while the continuance intention is determined by the user's belief and attitude. Notably, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study give strong evidence for how users' cognitive beliefs may differ in terms of the role they play in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the users' behavioral intention in different contexts. By deconstructing beliefs, this study makes the relationships between those fragmented factors found in previous KM research clear and context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c. In particular, our results show that knowledge workers go to the KMS to contribute knowledge because of social relationships; enjoyment in helping others; management support; and the consideration of cost associated with contributing behavior; and not for reasons of image, reciprocity, and organizational reward. In the seeking context, knowledge workers present continued usage behavior based on the utility of the system, social relationship, and seeking effort involved, but surprisingly are not motivated by the seeker's knowledge growth, organizational rewards, or management support.
