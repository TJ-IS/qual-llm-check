# A comprehensive analysis of U.S. counties’ e-Government portals: development status and functionalities

## Abstract

This research presents a comprehensive analysis of U.S. counties’ adoption of e-Government and the functions of the websites. By using content analysis methodology, the services and functions of U.S. county e-Government portals are scrutinized. The investigation instrument is based on political and technological theories, an e-Government stage model, and the review of literature. The research finds that U.S. counties’ adoption of e-Government is highly associated with certain socioeconomic factors; in addition, the functionalities of U.S. county e-Government portals are significantly related to six socioeconomic factors according to the multiple regression analysis. The research provides insights for government officials and practitioners to understand and improve e-Government practice. It also sheds light on e-Government research by bringing in a valuable research instrument and comprehensive data about e-Government adoption. The implications for future research are discussed.

Keywords: e-Government; content analysis; local government; stage model; socioeconomic

## Introduction

Governments at different levels around the world are increasingly using the Internet technology to improve and leverage their services delivered to citizens. For many years, the United States government has strived to improve their service quality and reduce operational costs by adopting advanced information technologies (Peristeras & Tarabanis, 2000; Ho, 2002; Moon & Norris, 2005; Norris & Moon, 2005; Huang, 2006). When the Internet is widely accepted and used, e-Government has been adopted as a highly regarded approach to support public governance. e-Government refers to the transformation of traditional public sector services and processes into an electronic format with greater accessibility and interactivity to citizens (Huang & Bwoma, 2003). It uses the Internet to bring citizens, businesses, institutions, and governments together. The benefits of e-Government are tremendous, including reducing government spending and increasing interest earning, improving delivery of government services, and promoting e-democracy etc. (Huang & Bwoma, 2003). According to Watson & Mundy (2001), for instance, e-Government can significantly reduce government transaction costs. In their study of property tax payments, Web-enabled payment reduced processing costs from more than 5 U.S. dollars to around 22 cents per transaction. The potential savings of e-democracy could be as much as \$110 billion and Euro 144 billion a year; some \$3 trillion exchanges happen between citizens and U.S. governments each year (Watson & Mundy, 2001). Other research reports that e-Government can transform bureaucracy form and allow public sectors focus on user satisfaction and control and flexibility in service delivery (Ho, 2002) and can support businesses (Gengatharen & Standing, 2005) etc.

Most current e-Government research has focused on nation- and state-level e-Government practices (e.g., La Porte et al., 2001; Criado & Ramilo, 2003; West, 2004; Ciborra, 2005; Holliday & Yep, 2005), seldom was conducted toward investigating local governments’ efforts (Moon & Norris, 2005). Less was systematic empirical analysis of online government services (Musso et al., 2000; Moon & Norris, 2005). Because of the lack of comprehensive data about e-Government practice, Moon & Norris (2005) contended ‘few rigorous empirical studies on e-Government have been conducted’ (p. 56). Besides the shortage of empirical studies in the field, the research methodologies adopted by previous studies also restricted their contribution. Most existing studies examined the phenomenon from merely internal perspective, that is, government officers and employees’ attitudes toward e-Government (e.g., Koh & Prybutok, 2003), few studied the e-Government practice from external angle, that is, citizen’s perspective. While studies from internal perspective can bring insights to the literature, objectivity may not be achieved if the investigation is focused on inside factors alone. There is often a gap between what government officials initiate and expect and what are really delivered to the citizens. From this point, government officials also need an external and objective feedback on their e-Government efforts and effects.

This study conducts a comprehensive content analysis of U.S. counties’ e-Government portals. It takes a ‘citizen’s eye’ perspective to evaluate the status of e-Government practice at the local level in the U.S. The content and functions of U.S. counties’ e-Government websites are evaluated based on a research instrument. There are totally 1744 e-Government portals in 3099 U.S. counties coded and analyzed. The paper is organized as follows. First, relevant literature is reviewed and research questions are introduced. Second, theoretic foundation is discussed; an explorative research framework for e-Government adoption and diffusion is established and research propositions are presented. After that, research methodology is explained. Data analysis and results are presented in the fourth section. Lastly, contributions and limitations of this research, as well as implications for researchers are provided.

## Literature review

Because of its abundant national income, technological advancement, citizen’s universal acceptance, as well as the efforts and dedications of federal government agencies, the United States has aligned itself along the top tier of e-Government adoption countries (La Porte et al., 2001). Although the U.S. is the leading country in e-Government adoption, the practice is not balanced across its administrative levels. That is, while federal and state governments have been able to develop the stateof-the-art e-Government websites (West, 2001, 2004), local governments often lack necessary technological expertise and budgets to deploy this advanced technology (Musso et al., 2000; Norris & Moon, 2005). For example, in a case study of e-Government implementation by a Texas city – Corpus Christi, Jorgensen & Cable (2002) found that the local government was struggling in the process of creating an effective e-Government. It lacked relevant e-business experience, faced big challenges of control and coordination among city departments, found it difficult to maintain fiscal and political support, and was inept in selecting the most appropriate and efficient technology (Jorgensen & Cable, 2002).

While some studies provided descriptive details of e-Government practice (Koh & Prybutok, 2003; West, 2004), other research has noticed the importance of socioeconomic factors on e-Government adoption and functions, and started the investigation in this aspect (e.g., La Porte et al., 2001; Ho, 2002; Moon & Norris, 2005; Huang, 2006). For instance, Ho (2002) hypothesized that population, ethnicity (minority population), and income were influencing factors on cities’ progressiveness in Web development; but he found that population, per capita income, and the ratio of elderly population insignificantly related to cities’ website adoption. Moon & Norris (2005) found the association between a culture of innovation (i.e., managerial innovation orientation) and the adoption of e-Government at the local level. In countries’ e-Government research, La Porte et al. (2001) found that a nation’s wealth only explained about 30% of the variation in the number of websites across national ministries.

In the development of e-Government, the government has to undergo various stages of sophistication and technology before it can realize the benefits of e-Government. Citizens’ acceptance of e-Government appears to be a staged process. Many studies, thus, proposed stage models to delineate e-Government development (Howard, 2001; Lau, 2001; United Nations, 2003). For example, Howard (2001) and Lau (2001) proposed an e-Government model that had four major stages: information publishing, two-way communication, transaction, and integration. In a survey conducted by the United Nations on its members about e-Government development, a five-stage model was used (United Nations, 2003). These stages include emerging, enhanced, interactive, transactional, and seamless (United Nations, 2003). Although these e-Government stage models are conceptually sound, they lacked empirical evidence support. In addition, there was not a standard definition of e-Government stages and the distributions of e-Governments on these stages were not justified.

There are over 3000 counties in the United States. Current literature provides little guidance on local governments’ adoption of e-Government. Because the majority of U.S. citizens are related to and serviced by local governments and a great number of administrative functions are delivered at this level, there is an imperative need to investigate and understand the status and progress of local e-Government practices. The following questions appear significant and valuable for researchers to examine. For example, how many local U.S. governments have adopted e-Government practices? What are the differences between those that have adopted e-Government and those have not? What functions, applications, and characteristics do local e-Government websites have? What adoption levels have local e-Governments reached? To what extent are the adoption levels related to independent factors? Answering these questions will not only bring insights to the research of local e-Government practices but also help understand the phenomena at other levels. In addition, what is learned from U.S. governments can shed light on e-Government research in other countries.

By critically reviewing the e-Government literature and the reality of U.S. counties’ e-Government development, following three research questions (RQ) become critical in understanding the adoption of the status of e-Government by U.S. local governments:

RQ1: What is the status of e-Government adoption by U.S. counties and how are the socioeconomic factors related to the adoption status?

RQ2: What functions are provided by U.S. county e-Government websites and how are the socioeconomic factors related to these functions?

RQ3: What growth pattern does U.S. county e-Government practice follow?

The answers given to these three research questions will provide great insights to the e-Government adoption literature.

## Research development

Different theories have been sought to help identify factors that contribute to the adoption and diffusion of e-Government, including innovation diffusion theory (Rogers, 1995), technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989), theory of growth and development (Hacche, 1979), and regional development theory (Dawkins, 2003) etc. Among these, innovation diffusion theory (Rogers, 1995) has been mostly adopted to investigate governments’ adoption of e-Government innovation from a society’s perspective (e.g., Watson & Mundy, 2001; Moon & Norris, 2005). According to the theory, an organization’s adoption of innovation can be split into two phases: adoption and diffusion (Kimberly, 1981; Premkumar et al., 1994). During adoption, an organization tests an innovation’s feasibility and users’ reaction toward the innovation. In diffusion, an innovation is widely embraced and the organization often customizes and leverages the innovation to maximize its functionalities, based on the experience from initial adoption. While studies focusing on the e-Government adoption are interested in the characteristics of a government or society that make it receptive to innovation, studies focusing on the diffusion attempt to understand why and how an innovation spreads in the government and what factors lead to widespread acceptance. After an organization has formally adopted an innovation, the use of the innovation has to spread within the organization for the innovation to provide its full benefits (Premkumar et al., 1994). Some innovations, because of their fad value or other organizational or environmental pressures, may be adopted in organizations, but because of other constraints, such as lack of technological expertise and financial support, their use may not spread within the organization.

Therefore, an e-Government website may not be effective and successful before it reaches full diffusion phase, that is, being able to provide adequate e-Government functions to its citizens. From the literature review, we believe that e-Government adoption among U.S. local governments is at different stages and such difference can be observed and analyzed. In addition, the e-Government adoption and diffusion is subject to the influence of certain factors, specifically, socioeconomic factors. To explore the e-Government adoption phenomena, an explorative framework (Figure 1) is posited to depict the relationship between socioeconomic factors and e-Government adoption and diffusion. This study explores the determinants of e-Government practice as indicated by solid lines in Figure 1, as well as the e-Government diffusion pattern as indicated by dashed lines. Three propositions based on this research framework are discussed below.

## e-Government adoption and determining factors

There have been studies that empirically examined the e-Government phenomena at the national and international levels (La Porte et al., 2001; Reddick, 2004; West, 2004; Ciborra, 2005; Holliday & Yep, 2005; Siau & Long, 2006). West (2004) investigated federal and state e-Government websites for their information, services, privacy and security, disability access, foreign language support, and democratic outreach in his annual research of U.S. e-Government websites. He found that e-Government capabilities were improved in general in the U.S. In his study in 2005, 77% of state and federal government websites had fully executable services – up from 56% in 2004 (Anonymous, 2005), while only 25% of the websites examined offered services that were fully executable online in 2001 (West, 2001). Federal government websites did a better job of offering information and services to citizens than did state government websites, while overall big improvements could be seen at both levels over years. However, though West’s studies provided a good description of e-Government development status at federal and state levels, they did not explore determining factors that drove such development.

La Porte et al. (2001) evaluated nearly 2000 government websites of over 120 countries for their organizational transparency, interactivity, and openness using WAES evaluation system. They found that a nation’s wealth explains around 30% of the variation in the number of websites across national ministries. Other factors, such as integration with world economy and computers and Internet hosts weakly supported the e-Government openness. Based on economic growth (Hacche, 1979) and regional development (Dawkins, 2003) theories, Siau & Long (2006) proved that income level, development status, and region were three key factors that differentiate e-Government development in countries. Reddick (2004) examined the development of public-sector e-commerce at the state level with a focus on financial management. He found that information technology (IT) management capacity and social services IT capacity were significant in explaining e-commerce development at the state level; however, the traditional factor used to explain technology diffusion, being state wealth, was not found to be statistically significant.

Researchers have started noticing the void of research on local e-Government practices and several studies witnessed the efforts in this direction (Musso et al., 2000; Ho, 2002; Koh & Prybutok, 2003; Moon & Norris, 2005; Norris & Moon, 2005). In their study of municipal e-Government practice, Moon & Norris (2005) found that managerial innovativeness orientation and city size were the most compelling determinants of municipal e-Government adoption. Ho (2002) conducted a content analysis of the websites of 55 largest (most populous) cities in the U.S. and surveyed their webmasters, finding that larger cities tended to be more innovative in e-Government practice. In addition, he found the higher percentage of white population, the better functions were provided by city e-Government websites. He concluded that because cities were in a learning curve of e-Government adoption, larger cities that had more experience and resources had advantages in designing better websites. Further, he speculated that cities with larger minority populations and a lower per capita income were less likely to adopt e-Government because there may be insufficient citizen’s demand for web-based services.

Because of dramatic social, political, economic, and demographic differences among countries, states, and municipalities, it is hard to draw a consistent conclusion on what factors influence e-Government practice. While most studies have associated e-Government adoption with certain societal and environmental factors, few studies have systematically investigated the effects of socioeconomic factors on e-Government adoption. Owing to the fact that many government initiatives and reinvention are under the pressure from citizens (Ho, 2002), socioeconomic background of citizens represents a good indicator of e-Government adoption. Two propositions are drawn thereafter (as indicted by line 1 and 2 in Figure 1):

Proposition 1: The adoption of e-Government is significantly related to socioeconomic factors.

## Proposition 2: The diffusion of e-Government is significantly related to socioeconomic factors.

Governments adopt and continually improve their e-Government websites in order to satisfy citizens’ all kinds of needs. Citizens with diverse social and economic background possess different needs and pressure for e-Government services. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the socioeconomic factors are significantly associated with e-Government adoption and diffusion.

## Development pattern

e-Government development is still at early stage. Some studies explored the e-Government adoption status by local governments, mostly municipal governments. Koh & Prybutok (2003) used a three-ring model to measure e-Government functions that were categorized into informational, transactional, and operational areas. In their study, a survey was sent to employees at a municipal government asking questions about 29 different e-Government functions. They found that city’s use of the Internet remained in an early stage of e-Government evolution. Musso et al. (2000) examined the extent to which innovative applications of Web technology advanced local governance reform. From the content analysis of 270 Californian municipal websites, they found that that the majority of municipal websites were poorly designed, with few innovative applications of technology and no clear mission (Musso et al., 2000).

Norris & Moon (2005) conducted two nationwide surveys to investigate local government adoption of e-Government, website sophistication, the perceived impacts of e-Government, and barriers to the adoption and sophistication of e-Government. They concluded that that e-Government adoption by the local governments was progressing rapidly (if measured solely by deployment of websites); however, the movement toward integrated and transactional e-Government was progressing much more slowly.

However, as suggested by innovation diffusion theory, the adoption of an innovation is a complicated process. Simply categorizing the adoption by local governments as ‘early stage’ or ‘poor level’ is vague, confusing, and does not provide a prescription to the problem. During the e-Government development process, a government has to undergo various stages of technology adoption and sophistication. According to Huang & Bwoma (2003), e-Government development follows a stage-model. The major stages include (1) information – the government posts information on the websites for the constituents to review, (2) communication – citizens can communicate with the government and make simple requests, (3) transaction – websites can accommodate processing of executable transaction, and (4) integration – government services are integrated together. Watson & Mundy (2001) introduced the concept of e-democracy that entailed two elements: e-Government and e-politics. While e-Government refers to services of providing citizens basic information about the government, e-politics refers to the use of the Internet technology to improve the effectiveness of political decision-making by making ‘citizens aware of the how and why of political decision-making and facilitating their participation in this process’ (p. 28). The ultimate objective of e-Government is to develop e-democracy that allows both effectiveness and efficiency of governments to serve citizens. Therefore, the following proposition is given that hypothesizes the path of e-Government diffusion from low to high stage (as indicated by line 3 in Figure 1).

## Proposition 3: The e-Government diffusion follows an information, communication, transaction, and democracy stage model.

The higher diffusion stage, the more complicated functions need to be provided, which demands more technological expertise and financial sources. e-Government website design is not only an art, but also a science that may have significant influence on a citizen’s life. A well-designed e-Government website with useful functions and easy navigation will attract a citizen to use the services delivered online; conversely, a poorly designed website will drive citizens away (Cappel & Huang, forthcoming). Most times, the effectiveness of an e-Government’s website supersedes other factors such as interface design because the functionality of an e-Government website has a lot more to do with a citizen. Clearly identifying development stages and the associated functions can help a government allocate sources in correct developmental directions.

## Research methodology

This research investigates the status of U.S. counties’ e-Government practice using content analysis research methodology. Content analysis is a research technique for the objective, systematic, and quantitative description of the manifest content of communicator (Kassarjian, 1977; Kolbe & Burnett, 1991; Krippendorff, 2004). It has been intensively applied in research of consumer behavior, public communications, and media analysis. Recently, it has been frequently used to investigate the Internet usage such as website content and structure (Bauer & Scharl, 2000; Cappel & Huang, forthcoming). For, government services and functions are delivered via the Internet media, content analysis presents an effective methodology of evaluating e-Government practice. It provides a good way to objectively and systematically examine the current status of local e-Government adoption and diffusion from a citizen’s perspective.

## Research instrument

By integrating the established four-stage e-Government practice model (Huang & Bwoma, 2003) with Watson & Mundy framework (2001), a research instrument is set up in which the measures of e-Government functions are organized into four categories: information, communication, transaction, and democracy. The original model (Huang & Bwoma, 2003) included integration as the highest level e-Government should reach. However, it is difficult to measure the integration of e-Government functions across departments and governments using content analysis methodology; therefore, it is replaced by democracy. For e-democracy has been advocated as the final destination of e-Government in its evolution path (Hague & Loader, 1999; Watson & Mundy, 2001).

To maintain a high reliability and validity of the research instrument, e-Government measuring items were adapted from empirical studies in the area (see Appendix A). An interview of local government officers and researchers in the area was conducted to ensure the validity of all the measures. Some new items were added to the instrument, while several others were removed for being not relevant to a county government’s functions. The items that have an ‘X’ in Appendix A were adapted from relevant empirical studies whereas those not were developed from the interview of government officers and political science scholars.

The instrument contains four categories of e-Government functions. Information category comprises the information-distributing functions. There are 12 items in this category that measure if county mission, county budget, county news and events, and so on are presented on the websites. Communication category covers the two-way communicating functions provided by e-Government websites. At this level, not only governments can distribute information to citizens, but citizens can also upload forms, file complaints, and send comments to the governments online. There are 16 items in this category. Transaction category presents a high stage that e-Government websites desire to reach. At this level, e-Government websites can support executable transactions. For example, citizens can pay the government online for purchasing hunting permits or renewing vital licenses. Democracy is the highest stage in the framework. At this stage, government functions are integrated horizontally and vertically, and most importantly, citizens can participate in government decision making and even vote online (Watson & Mundy, 2001). There are three items in this category. In addition, counties’ socioeconomic factors such as population, ethnic, and income factors were collected from U.S. Census Bureau’s State & County QuickFacts website to support analysis. Totally, there are 15 socioeconomic factors in the list (Appendix B). The unit of analysis is county e-Government portal.

An e-Government portal is as an official entry website that contains information about and links to the services provided by all county departments and agencies. A portal is created from the idea of ‘one-stop service center’ (Ho, 2002). An e-Government portal is an umbrella website where services of different administrative levels, departments, agencies are organized together. Counties are relatively stable administrative units in which missions, tasks, and scopes of services are well defined. Therefore, an investigation of U.S. counties’ portals can help understand the status of local governments’ adoption of e-Government (NACO, 2005). Fortyeight U.S. states have county governments. Alaska and Louisiana call their county-type government boroughs and parishes, respectively. Connecticut, Vermont, and Rhode Island are divided into geographic regions called counties, but they do not have functioning governments at this level, as defined by the Census Bureau (NACO, 2005). Instead, townships and municipalities are major functioning governments for these states. Boroughs and parishes in Alaska and Louisiana were treated as county governments in this study. All U.S. counties’ e-Government websites, when available, were coded and analyzed.

## Data collection

The list of U.S. counties was obtained from us-counties.- com – a premier website about U.S. local governments, and was verified against the county list on the website of National Association of Counties (naco.org). Totally, there were 3099 counties found in 50 U.S. states and verified by the time of data collection. Most counties’ e-Government websites, if they existed, could be obtained from the above two websites. Where there was no website listed for a county, the researchers searched Google and another search engine (Yahoo or MSN) using the county name in an effort to find its website. All counties’ websites were double checked to make sure they were official county portals by following pre-established evaluation criteria: (1) if a website has a tag reads like ‘XXX County’s Official E-government Site’ or some texts alike on the home page, (2) copyright information belonging to the county, (3) a county logo, and (4) administrative functions of three or more county departments. If a website satisfied three or more criteria, it was determined as an official county e-Government portal. Using these criteria, websites of a single department, often commerce or tourism department, were filtered out.

Three trained, independent coders examined and coded U.S. counties’ e-Government portals from March to August 2005. As suggested by Kolbe & Burnett (1991), the author did not participate in the data collection to ensure objectivity and avoid any potential bias. The three coders were trained to understand the definition and purpose of e-Government, departments and functions of U.S. counties, functions of e-Government websites, and the purpose of this research. They were instructed to code an e-Government portal based on the instrument. After that, they were required to collect data for the same 20 pre-selected county e-Government portals. Their results were compared and contrasted in meetings where all three coders and the researcher attended to find out the difference in interpreting and coding these websites. An agreement on all data items was reached among the coders after two meetings and the coders’ rechecks of the websites. The data of these 20 e-Government websites were included in the final statistical analysis but not used in the calculation of the interjudge reliability as discussed below.

A pre-test was conducted toward 137 county e-Government websites (around 8% of 1744 e-Government portals) from six randomly selected U.S. states: Arizona, Idaho, Maryland, Montana, Oklahoma, and Washington. As an additional check of consistency across coders, the independent ratings of three coders were compared for the 137 websites. Interjudge reliability was evaluated using coefficient of agreement, that is, the total number of agreements divided by the total number of coding decisions (Kolbe & Burnett, 1991; Krippendorff, 2004). The results of this procedure revealed that there was an acceptable interjudge reliability of 94.7%.

## Data analysis and results

The content analysis looks at whether an official e-Government portal provides functions listed in the measuring instrument. The measure is coded as one if the function is found, otherwise coded as zero. Frequencies and percentages are calculated for these measures and an overall score is calculated for four e-Government function categories. County social and economic data are correlated to the overall scores to determine whether there are any significant differences in county websites based on these factors. The overall score is designed to be an overall assessment of the service and functionality of a county’s e-Government portal.

Of 3099 U.S counties, 1744 have e-Government portals, which accounts for 56.3% of the population. About half of county e-Government portals choose .US as domain names (49.8%). Next, 23.0% select .ORG domains, 15.9% .COM, 6.1% .NET, 4.9% .GOV, and 0.3% select other domain names. The top five states in which counties have highest e-Government presence rates are, respectively, Delaware (100%), North Carolina (96.0%), California (94.8%), Nevada (94.1%), and Wisconsin (91.7%). The bottom five states in which their counties have lowest e-Government presence rates are South Dakota (10.6%), West Virginia (12.7%), Oklahoma (13.0%), Mississippi (14.6%), and Kentucky (19.2%). There is no county e-Government website found for Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Vermont because townships or municipalities, instead of counties, are major administrative units in these three states. The detailed information of U.S. counties’ e-Government adoption is listed in Table 1.

## e-Government adoption

In order to find out what factors distinguish counties that have adopted e-Government portals from those that have not, independent t-tests are conducted to compare the means of two groups over socioeconomic factors. The results of the independent t-tests show that U.S. counties that have adopted e-Government portals differ significantly from those that have not (see Table 2). The counties that have e-Government portals have bigger population than those that do not (134,678 vs 36,706 in 2003; 129,861 vs 35,892 in 2000) and faster population growth from 2000 to 2003 (2.34 vs 0.23%). Ethnic factors can also help explain the difference. The counties that have higher percentage of foreign-born persons in their populations (4.39 vs 2.17%) adopt e-Government portals significantly more; and languages other than English are spoken more at home in these counties (9.88 vs 6.77%). Citizens’ education levels make a difference too. The adopters have more high school graduates (79.59 vs 74.29%) and more people with bachelors or higher degrees (18.62 vs 13.38) than non-adopters.

Housing factors are also significant in distinguishing two groups of counties. Median value of housing units is much higher in the counties that have adopted e-Government than those have not (\$96,379.47 vs \$67,369.59, nearly \$30,000.00 difference). However, it is interesting to note that home ownership rate is significantly lower in adopter counties (73.35 vs 75.23%) than non-adopters. e-Government adopters and non-adopters are also different in income and business factors. Median household income and per capita income are higher in adopter counties than non-adopters (\$38,237.36 vs \$31,307.11 and \$18,679.66 vs \$15,805.55, respectively). Adopter counties have significantly more private nonfarm employees (53,953 vs 12,421) and retail sales per capita (\$8236.62 vs \$6320.62). Although adopter counties have got more federal funds and grants than nonadopters (\$779,747.17 vs \$597,930.07), this factor is insignificant in distinguishing counties on their e-Government adoption. Also, persons-per-square-mile is not a significant factor for explaining the phenomenon though adopter counties have much higher population density (820 persons/mile vs 186 persons/mile).

## e-Government diffusion

To understand more about the e-Government diffusion status and the functions provided on the websites, a multiple regression analysis is conducted to find out what factors are correlated with the functions of the e-Government portals. It focused on examining the e-Government diffusion status, that is, the number of functions delivered by U.S. county e-Government portals, and the relationship between these functions and socioeconomic factors. An ordinary least squares (OLS) multiple regression with stepwise method is used to check the relationship between TOTAL e-Government functions – the summary of information, communication, transaction, and democracy functions – and socioeconomic factors. Insignificant factors (at 0.01 level) are withdrawn from the model step by step until all residual factors are significant for the regression model and the whole model is significant.

The regression model is significant at 0.001 level (R<sup>2</sup> ¼ 0.189) and six independent factors are found to be significantly related with the overall functionalities of e-Government websites (Table 3). The model can successfully explain about 19% of U.S. county e-Government portal functions. The percent of population change (Po0.001), median value of housing units (Po0.001), median household income (Po0.001), and federal funds and grants (Po0.001) are positively related to the overall e-Government functions.

It means, when both population and population change are significantly related to e-Government adoption, only population change helps explain the e-Government diffusion. The higher median value of housing units and median household income, the more functions a government will provide to its websites. Although federal funds and grants are not significantly related to e-Government adoption (see Table 2), they are significant in explaining e-Government diffusion. The more federal funds and grants a county can get, the better functions it will provide to its citizens via online forms. Very interestingly, the factor of languages other than English spoken at home is negatively related with local e-Government functions (t value ¼ 3.183, Po0.01), while it is positively related to e-Government adoption. It means that the higher rate of other languages spoken at home, the higher rate of immigrants and minorities is in the population, and the less functions can be expected from the e-Government websites. Also, home ownership rate (t ¼ 4.471, P ¼ 0.001) is negatively related with county e-Government functions. The higher home ownership rate, the less functions are provided. Therefore, Proposition 2 is supported that e-Government diffusion is significantly related to certain socioeconomic factors.

At transaction level, counties show slow movement and provide very limited number of executable functionalities (Table 6). There are 32.1% county e-Governments that can collect local taxes. Other than this, executable functions are quite limited: only 14.5% endorse transactions of vital records and 10.0% support online payments for utilities. Local governments have great potential to improve their services at this level.

It seems that counties strive to provide more democratic content and services via their e-Government portals (Table 7). Relevant information is provided online, including election and voting policies information (45.0%), election forms (45.2%), and election results (32.2%). It is arguable that the items listed in this category are mainly information distributing or communication functions. However, this group of service, due to its nature, can be separated from information and communication services because e-democracy is the ultimate goal of e-Government development (Hague & Loader, 1999; Watson & Mundy, 2001). This category presents a good benchmark for future e-Government studies that investigate e-democracy functions, for example, e-voting. In a further examination, it is found that some county e-Government portals (16 portals, 0.9% of the 1744 websites) have a vote link. Because the coders are not local residents and cannot register as a voter, the real voting function cannot be tested.

Lastly, to unveil the growth pattern of local e-Government portals, the percentage of functions provided by county e-Government portals at each stage is calculated, that is, the total number of functions in one particular stage divided by the total number of possible functions in that stage. For example, there are totally 10,870 information-distributing functions coded and the number of possible information-distributing functions in 1744 websites is 20,928 (12 information-distributing functions  1744 county portals). Therefore, the percentage of information-distributing functions provided by county e-Government portals is 51.9% (10,870/20,928). The results are presented in Table 8 and Figure 2, respectively. Averagely, U.S. county e-Government portals could provide half (51.9%) of the information-distributing functions (12 items), 29.6% of two-way communication functions (16 items), 9.5% of transactional functions (five items), and 40.8% of democratic functions (three items).

The uncovered trend (Figure 2) shows that most U.S. counties’ e-Government adoption is primarily at the information-distributing level and few portals have reached transaction stage. Proposition 3 is supported that e-Government adoption and diffusion follows an information, communication, transaction, and democracy stage model. The higher the stage, the more complicated the website can be, and the less e-Government portals can reach.

## Research synthesis

The findings of previous e-Government research and this study are synthesized in Table 9. While Ho (2002) found that population and the ratio of elderly population were insignificant in explaining city government’s functions, other studies found that population a factor significant in explaining e-Government progress (Musso et al., 2000; Moon & Norris, 2005; Norris & Moon, 2005). Moon & Norris (2005) found larger municipal governments were more likely to pursue e-Government initiatives because they might have more stakeholders to satisfy and have greater administrative resources. Musso et al. (2000) found that municipalities that adopted websites were substantially larger in population, and had significantly more governmental expenditures and revenues. Norris & Moon (2005) also found that the adoption of websites was strongly related to local government size (population). This study takes a further step by investigating the effect of population growth on e-Government development; interestingly, it finds that population growth cannot only explain the adoption but also the diffusion of e-Government. A county with higher population growth has to provide more functions to its website to meet the more diverse needs often associated with a fast growing population.

Ethnicity has been found to be a significant factor in e-Government development (Musso et al., 2000; Ho, 2002). Ho (2002) found that a higher ratio of minority population was significantly related to administrativeoriented website and functions. Musso et al. (2000) found that adopting cities had proportionately more white residents than nonadopters. This study investigates the effects of two ethnicity issues: foreign-born persons and languages other than English spoken at home. It seems that languages other than English spoken at home works better than foreign-born persons in explaining e-Government behavior because it not only relates to e-Government adoption but also diffusion. It is important to note that languages other than English spoken at home negatively relates to e-Government diffusion. It indirectly proves Ho’s (2002) hypothesis that cities ‘with larger minority populations and a lower per capita income were less likely to adopt progressive web design because there might be insignificant citizens demand for web-based services’ (p. 439).

While no previous research was found to study the effect of housing factors on e-Government adoption, this study raises the importance of this category of factors. Two factors, home ownership rate and median value of housing units both relate to e-Government adoption and diffusion. Interestingly, home ownership rate is negatively related to both e-Government adoption and diffusion. Education and income are proven two important categories of e-Government factors. Musso et al. (2000) found that residents of e-Government adopter cities had higher median incomes and with a college education. However, Ho (2002) found that per capita income was insignificant in explaining city government’s functions. In this study, high school graduate (percentage), Bachelor’s degree or higher (percentage), median household income, and per capita income are all found significantly related to e-Government adoption. In addition, median household income is significant in e-Government diffusion.

Business and geographic factors were less studied by previous e-Government research. In this study, four business and geographic factors are investigated, two are found significant in explaining e-Government adoption (private nonfarm employment and retail sales per capita) and one significant in e-Government diffusion (Federal funds and grants). Some other factors that were significantly related to e-Government adoption in previous studies are not investigated in this research. For example, Moon & Norris (2005) found that more innovation-oriented cities were more likely to advance e-Government and the form of government was not significant. Musso et al. (2000) found that political factors such as ratio of registering to vote, percentage of democrats were significantly related with e-Government adoption. Norris & Moon (2005) found that adoption was related to the type and form of government and metropolitan status.

## Conclusion and implications

In this study, the e-Government adoption and diffusion by U.S. counties is investigated. An exploratory framework is posited to delineate the relationship of socioeconomic factors and e-Government adoption and the staged pattern of e-Government development. Three propositions are hypothesized and proven in this study.

There are 56.3% U.S. counties that have adopted e-Government portals and 43.7% have not. Content analysis results show that U.S. counties’ adoption of e-Government is significantly correlated with a county’s population, ethnicity, education, housing, income, and business factors. The adopter counties have bigger population, faster population growth, and more diverse ethnic structures. Their citizens have better education, higher income, higher employed population, but lower home ownership rate. The study also proves that e-Government diffusion is different from e-Government adoption in terms of functional sophistication. After e-Government is adopted, a government continually needs to put more efforts to sophisticate its design and add more executable functions. Only so can a government develop effective and successful websites. e-Government diffusion is significantly related to population growth, language other than English spoken at home, home ownership rate, median value of housing units, median household income, and federal funds and grants. By comparing counties’ e-Government practice with the established e-Government stage model, this study unveils a local e-Government growth pattern. While a small percentage of e-Government websites can deliver advanced transaction and democratic functions, the majority is still at preliminary stage. There is great potential for local governments to improve their e-Government practice, especially in two-way communication and executable transaction aspects.

Limited research has studied local e-Government adoption and diffusion. This research contributes insights to the literature by empirically investigating factors affecting e-Government adoption and diffusion. It does not only provide a description of current e-Government adoption status but also prescribes a set of socioeconomic variables that impact e-Government adoption and diffusion. This research offers an e-Government instrument for future research that examines e-Government services and functions; especially for those studies that use longitudinal methodologies, this research provides a benchmark of local e-Government functions. Furthermore, this study quantifies the growth pattern of e-Government adoption and aligns e-Government development with a stage model. It is helpful for practitioners to check their e-Government practice against this model, thus making right decisions for future development.

This study, like any other, has its limitations. First, it only examines the effects of social and economic factors on e-Government adoption and diffusion. According to innovation diffusion theory (Rogers, 1995), technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989), and studies in the field (e.g., Musso et al., 2000; Moon & Norris, 2005), the adoption of e-Government is subject to the influence of many other factors as well. For example, Moon & Norris (2005) found that managerial innovativeness orientation was a significant determinant of municipal e-Government adoption, while Musso et al. (2000) found that the form of governments could make a difference. Therefore, while the multiple regression model is significant in this study, it can help explain only 19% of the variation. Future research is recommended to investigate a broader set of variables. A holistic research framework that integrates internal variables such as governance format, managerial orientation, technological capacity, and management support with external variables such as socioeconomic factors will contribute more insights to the literature.

Second, the content analysis research methodology casts some limitations. Content analysis as a rigorous methodology provides an objective, systematic, and quantitative evaluation of e-Government practice in this study. However, because it investigates the phenomena from external perspective, it cannot help find the causal relationships internal to the subject. That is, it cannot answer questions how the adoption decision is made and why certain functions are provided and others not.
