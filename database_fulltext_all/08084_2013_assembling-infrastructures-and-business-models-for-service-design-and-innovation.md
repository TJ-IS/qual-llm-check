# Assembling infrastructures and business models for service design and innovation

George Kuk\* & Marijn Janssen<sup>†</sup>

\*Nottingham University Business School, Jubilee Campus, Wollaton Road, Nottingham NG8 1BB, UK, email: g.kuk@nottingham.ac.uk, and <sup>†</sup>Faculty of Technology, Policy and Management, Delft University of Technology, Jaffalaan 5, 2628 BX Delft, The Netherlands, email: m.f.w.h.a.janssen@tudelft.nl

Abstract. Most of the service innovation in e-government initiatives has been initially focused on making a digital version of an equivalent physical service, followed by creating new services through either adopting new business models or overhauling existing infrastructures. The former approach uses variants of e-commerce business models to drive service innovation whereas the latter entails changes to the backend for driving system efficiency and integration, which later serve for reorganising and improving service provision. Each approach presents a different way of assembling services, infrastructures, people and business models. The ramifications of each assemblage to service innovation are less understood. This paper seeks to examine the intended and unintended consequences of these two contrasting approaches inductively and deductively. Our findings show that a frontend approach enables quick improvements and enacts a variety of structures, with each structure inscribing a set of new path-dependent routines in the infrastructures. In the backend approach it took longer to introduce new services. Yet the services were of a higher quality and required less modification in the long run. Quantitative analyses confirmed these findings. We attributed the alignment advantage of backend approach to better interdepartmental collaboration organised around a technology platform for system and service integration, concentrating resources on a coherent rather than a diverse set of business models. This approach realigns practices and routines and technology internally, whereas the explorative, diverse use of business models in the frontend approach for service innovation is less sustainable.

Keywords: e-government, business models, service innovation, service design, service development, enterprise architecture, duality of technology, practice based approach

## INTRODUCTION

The ways to proceed from an online presence and basic transaction services to innovative service delivery have become a major practical challenge for public managers (Layne & Lee, 2001; Andersen & Henriksen, 2006). At the heart of this is to understand how to attain service innovation through the ways infrastructures, people, services and work processes are being set up. Two specific practices have been considered in the literature of public service innovation (Richardson et al., 1990; Safizadeh et al., 2003; Millard et al., 2004; Bovaird, 2005; Schmidt & Buxmann, 2011). The first practice involves creating online services to meet the frontend business logics and requirements, with an initial focus on digitising physical services, followed by either incremental or radical changes to the underlying infrastructures and/or business models. The second practice seeks to reassemble infrastructures and back offices to meet the operational and technical requirements for service delivery. The first practice focuses upon the frontend in the pursuit of service innovation in products and services offerings (e.g. Safizadeh et al., 2003; Markides & Charitou, 2004) and assembling technology to realise business strategy (e.g. Chan & Reich, 2007). Whereas the second practice concerns acquiring information technology (IT) assets, building complementarities (e.g. Kohli, 2007) and improving efficiency at the backend (Safizadeh et al., 2003). The former practice explores new services and business opportunities prior to assessing the existing IT capabilities. The latter exploits the available IT capabilities (including resources and complementarities) to enable business process change in support of developing frontend services.

Both practices represent different approaches to service innovation. Each presents a unique sequence of how governments go about to assemble their infrastructure and develop their business models to innovate their services. Each approach intends to enable change but can have unintended consequences (see Boudreau & Robey, 2005), including constraining change at a later stage, and/or requiring further workarounds or improvisations (e.g. Wagner & Newell, 2006; Sia & Soh, 2007). The backend approach reassembles the existing infrastructures for organising and improving system efficiency. The frontend approach strives for introducing new services, and readily procures and implements new technologies to meet citizenry demands and competition.

To explore the varying scopes of enabling and constraining elements of these two contrasting practices, we use a practice-based view to explore the relations of frontend and backend approach to the design and implementation of infrastructures, services and business models by two municipalities. Rather than studying practice in isolation, this approach examines how practices and/or practicing manifest in situ and emerge in the processes of organising under different social and material conditions (Nicolini, 2011).

For clarity of presentation, we review the literature prior to the discussion of the research setting and our findings although we examined it throughout our research. Following the discussion, we provide more detail on the methods, and then present the findings in two parts: the first presents qualitative findings from the data, and the second presents a deductive statistical analysis to confirm and extend the inductive findings, focusing specifically on the approaches that sustain service innovation. In the first part we compare and contrast two cases to examine the interrelationship between infrastructures and business models. Each case initially adopted a different approach. The first case developed services first and then procured and assembled new technologies. The second case focused on reassembling existing infrastructures, with their subsequent use as a platform for organising and developing new services. The two cases aim to provide a grounded understanding of how municipalities design and develop their infrastructures in relation to their business models and/or infrastructural requirements, and how this relationship unfolds over time. In the second part we further explore the inductive insights by formulating three hypotheses, and test their predictive validi ties on a sample of public agencies.

## DUALITY OF TECHNOLOGY

In studying the relationship between technology and organising, Orlikowski (2000) takes the stance that practices temporally emerge as a socio-material assemble of technology and people, that is, it is through a collective use of technology in situ within institutional contexts, that a variety of structures will be enacted, and forms the change engine of institutionalisation (Leonardi & Barley, 2010). The emergent structures can play a part in the assembling dissembling and reassembling of existing practices and systems. These structures can both enable and constraint development and change. This underscores the central tenet of the duality of technology, in that the design and development of technology is heavily influenced by the actions (including decisions) of human agents, and that technology enacts structures, which can either enable or constraint change in a recursive manner (Orlikowski, 1992; 2000).

Volkoff et al. (2007) use a longitudinal case to show how roles and routines that were inscribed in technology in the past can circumscribe the scope of actors’ activities at the present. Leonardi (2011) shows that the ways material and human agency interlock in a particular sequence can result in different forms of change. When the existing technology is perceived to support realising a new set of goals, human agents will alter their routines; whereas if the technology is perceived to constrain their ability to attain their goals, human agents will seek to change the technology.

The effect of changing existing practices to align with the technology capabilities reduce internal varieties whereas the effect of introducing new technologies can induce more varieties as new technologies may be incompatible with existing technologies and established routines. The technology alignment approach, in which existing practices are aligned with technology, has the effect of redefining the existing social orders at the workplace. It underscores the dynamic interplay and a constant readjustment between technologies and social orders (Leonardi & Barley 2010). This notion of constant change dovetails from the earlier script model of technology, in that technology restrains activities in a straitjacket manner. The alignment model makes provision that practices are inherently social and interconnected, and hence provisional and contestable, and mediated by a whole range of social and material conditions (Nicolini, 2011). Against this context, we examine the relationship between technology and organising in relation to the frontend and the backend approach towards service innovation.

We argue that the starting point is secondary to the recognition that each approach presents a different set of challenges; and amidst a unique set of learning opportunities to improve the capability of problem solving (Nickerson & Zenger, 2004), each approach involves the creation and subsequently inscription of new practices into social and technical systems that can enable and constrain future change (Feldman, 2004). By tracing the underlying processes and sequences involved in each approach, we can better understand the path dependency implications of the specific sets of factors and conditions that will enable and constrain present and future change. To develop our account, we combined inductive with deductive methods including interviews with key informants and testing of empirical data based on a Web-based analysis of e-government portals.

This research provides two distinct theoretical contributions. First, the case study allows us to define the elements of the backend and frontend approach, specifically the enabling and constraining elements for change and service innovation. Differentiation between backend and frontend types of business models is relevant for development. Second, the case study illustrates adherence to either frontend or backend logics can both result in successful service innovation but in a different time frame. In practice, this suggests that public agencies have to be mindful of the near- and long-term significance of the frontend and backend logics in relation to service innovation.

## BUSINESS MODELS

Service innovation requires new business models in which the business logic is aligned with the services provided. In many countries, governments seek to improve their services for citizens and businesses, displaying a higher level of responsiveness in a dynamic and continuously changing environment (Chen, 2002). In the late 90s, the Dutch national government set up a website monitor (http://www.monitor.overheid.nl) to stimulate competition among governmental agencies in the development of websites and the dissemination of best practices. The monitor was used to periodically evaluate agencies based on the services available on their websites and deploys criteria including website layout, content, services and communication as a proxy of the quality of service delivery. This kind of benchmarking exercise can often induce a rush mentality among governmental agencies to jump upon the e-government bandwagon (Kuk, 2003). Mosse & Whitley (2009) warn against uncritically copying benchmark criteria. Rightly or wrongly, the benchmarking can distract governmental agencies from the underlying business logics, which is often used interchangeably with the term business model.

The concept of business model is about shaping the relation between an organisational strategy and systems (Hedman & Kalling, 2003; Al-Debei & Avison, 2010). In e-business and e-commerce, a Web-based business model is a method of doing business online through which a company generates revenue (Rappa, 2002). In the public sector, instead of generating revenue the focus is on delivering public values (Jørgensen & Bozeman, 2007) and e-government business models aim at using the internet to add public value to the constituents (Janssen et al., 2008). Al-debei & Avison (2010) argue that the business model concept is stil fuzzy and vague. They find little consensus regarding its compositional facets and propose four dimensions: value proposition, architecture, network and finance. In their view the infrastructure changes is part of the value architecture. They argue that the dimensions are interrelated. Although their work shows which elements constitute a business model it does not show the process of assembling such a model. Based on the e-commerce models of Weill & Vitale (2001), Janssen et al. (2008) identify seven e-government business models that are applicable to the e-government context, comprising content provider, service provider, full-service provider, value-net integrator, infrastructure provider, market, collaboration, and community business models. Yet which of these model ties in better with service innovation from the perspectives of a frontend or backend approach is not clear. Our case studies show that some models are more related to the frontend and others to the backend innovation which is already indicated in Table 1.

The logic behind value creation differs between the backend and the frontend. The backend is expected to take advantage of standardisation, consolidation and automation to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of operations, whereas the frontend comprises the logic for adding value by directly interacting with citizens and/or businesses (Richardson, 1994; Safizadeh et al., 2003). The backend comprises all business processes that do not directly involve customer interactions, such as the processing of tax returns (after having received the information in the frontend) and related processes. The frontend and backend should be kept separated, as they have very different objectives and characteristics that might be conflicting to some extent. The main obiective of the frontend is to create responsive and demand-driver service delivery, whereas the main objective of the backend is to achieve efficiency by reducing the operational costs.

For public managers, their decision revolves around either developing the frontend business logics or starting with the backend logic to redress integration challenges across often dispar ate and disjointed organisations. The two cases that follow pursued the assembling of a new organisation of the services in a different sequential order. The first one started with the frontend service innovation, whereas the second started with the infrastructures. Both cases had attained relative successes in electronic service delivery compared with most of the municipalities in the Netherlands. To maintain confidentiality, the first case which is a mediumsized municipality is called Gamma and the second case, a relatively large-sized municipality is given the pseudonym Theta.

## Part 1: inductive approach

Inductive, exploratory research was chosen due to the scarcity of empirical work related to the relationship between frontend and backend development and service innovation, and the need to investigate the nature and background of the business models. Case research is useful in our situation as we dealt with a broad and complex phenomenon, and the existing body o knowledge is insufficient to permit posing causal questions (Yin, 1989). Part of this research can be described as a longitudinal qualitative case study analysis, as the learning experience is investigated with reference to a specific event, in a bounded context (Yin, 1989; Creswell, 1994).

We investigated two different municipalities. The first is a medium-sized municipality, emphasising service innovation in the frontend. The second is a large municipality known for its focus on creating its own infrastructure. In terms of data collection, we studied all available documentations and interviewed 12 people at three time points (2004, 2008, 2011); all twelve were interviewed in 2004, 9 out of 12 in 2008 and 8 of them in 2011. Our informants comprised project managers, system architects, IT staff, administrative staff and line managers. The documentation comprised a number of press releases, internal documents such as memos, emails, reports and presentations. Data triangulation was used by comparing and contrasting the interview findings with observation results and document reviews as it was necessary to validate and verify the findings of the primary data with secondary information (Mingers, 2001).

With regard to the league table of quality of Web services (http://www.monitor.overheid.nl), Gamma was ranked in the top 5 for 2 consecutive years (2004 and 2005) but then dropped ou of the top 25. On the other hand, Theta barely made to the top 40 in 2004 but rose rapidly and appeared in the top 5 since 2007.

## Case 1: Gamma (frontend)

Gamma’s frontend approach was primarily customer-centric, striving to create new services and products on a centrally sourced infrastructure. With over 3000 employees distributed across multiple departments, Gamma was known for its innovative services. It was the first municipality to offer an online garbage collection service, in that citizens used an interactive, visual map to select a time frame for collecting their garbage. Yet the underlying infrastructure was perceived to be fragmented, ‘with the systems architects tucked away in the ICT departments’, and operated as a silo, independent from service operations and business planning. New services were created reactively to meet external requirements from citizens and politicians, and often without considering the ramifications of these new services on the existing infrastruc tures, and/or other services. Citizens and external opinions were more influential than internal stakeholders. Being mentioned in the news was more important by politicians than the actual usage of the services. Gamma used two front- and five backend departments in assembling services, people and infrastructures. Although the back offices procured technology including software from one single provider, the information and communications technologies (ICT) staff at the frontend stayed independent of the backend. This was deliberate, aiming to foreground services as a means to an end in outperforming other municipalities in service development. This arrangement seemed to work initially as Gamma was ranked in the top 5 for 2 consecutive years.

In addition to managing the frontend services, the ICT teams were also responsible for getting information out of the legacy systems and using it in the frontend. Gamma pursued a point-to-point approach in integrating new systems into its existing infrastructure. Based on requests made by politicians and with the aim to achieve quick response to external social and business requirements, often ad hoc connections between frontend and backend applications were made without due consideration to maintenance issues. This approach allowed a new service to develop within 3 to 5 working days, and for each service a new project was started without reusing existing parts including software source codes. Developers used their own set of routines, methods and standards to carry out their work. These agile software development projects involved users, programmers and administrative staff to collaborate in creating the user-oriented service within a short time frame. Yet no standards emerged neither automatic integration was accomplished. Each project took its own course independently of the knowledge and experience of other projects. For situations in which multiple services were required, the users were left with the onerous task to make sense of what services were needed and in what order in terms of applications. For example, if users wanted to assess the impact of road works on parking they resorted to view the road works and parking information separately, and then needed to combine them manually.

The frontend driven technological change increased the scopes of services and products. There were two flagship websites, one dealt with transactional services (i.e. a direct-to-customer business model), and the other one with information provisioning (i.e. a content provider business model). The latter made use of up-to-date information provided by the public and the private parties. Searchable information included schedules of theatre plays, road works and other local events. The variety of information made the direct link between the websites and the municipality non-obvious. Because of the accuracy and timeliness of the information, it attracted new visitors and retained regular visitors to form a virtual community (i.e. virtual community business model). This opened another opportunity to harness this community by involving them in local policymaking. Various social Web tools were then added to increase involvement and participation (i.e. a collaborative type of business model). These four distinc tive business models were consequential of Gamma’s frontend approach which was largely reactive to the ad hoc nature of external demands and expectations. Some of the business models such as the virtual communities and direct-to-customer concerned external partnerships and less on internal integration of services across departments. The new services, content and provision had led to a concentration of visitors, and in turn increased participation and collaborative activities among citizens, public workers and the policymakers.

When it came to scaling, Gamma often resolved the technical limits by resorting to patching the backend through software solutions provided by outside vendors. They relied completely on the software vendors for the design and maintenance of the infrastructures. If the software vendors had a new release, this was quickly adopted and installed. New services were accomplished by directly integrating the frontend to the systems in the backend departments. Gamma often explored new business models by exploiting the centrally sourced infrastructures, and resolved any technical gaps through patching. The city council and administration put the citizens first and thereafter the architecture. The solutions were created to satisfy the criteria that ‘it should be customer enjoyable and convenient’ despite the fact that the ‘internal might not be efficient; this does not matter as long as the customer is not noticing it’. In recen interviews it was revealed that this statement was superseded due to the rising maintenance costs and cut in budgets. Consequently, the frontend approach had resulted in accomplishing quick wins in the 2004 and 2005 league tables. This approach worked well with a small number of services, but scalability was an issue. Yet over time the control and maintenance costs increased, as information and services needed to be updated or changed. Updating and changing was time-intensive, in that there was hardly any documentation and each service followed other patterns and logic than others. Whereas the initial focus was on the development of new services, the interviewees estimated that approximately 70% of the capacity was used for control and maintenance purposes. The situation became worse following the budget cuts in 2010.

Although the innovation of single services was relatively easy, the implementation of the integrated environmental permit proved to be cumbersome. The environmental permit was founded by a new law which sought to replace and combine 18 permit applications into one. The single permit application allowed citizens, and in particular businesses, to submit one application to a single agency. In this way, an applicant could specify various activities in one permit application. For example, activities such as breaking-off part of a monumental building, removal of asbestos, use of a public parking space for storing a dumpster and rebuilding could all be included in one single application resulting in a single decision rather than multiple ones. Prior to this, multiple applications had to be made and some permits might be granted whereas others might not. Yet the single permit application was technically challenging, as it required integrating and streamlining the existing permit applications across disjointed systems and infrastructures. In 2007 this resulted in the procurement of a platform for integrating the frontend and backend and for ensuring that business processes could be defined without programming. Initially this was a heavyweight platform in which the multiple functionalities of the backend information systems would be integrated. After experiencing difficulties with the complexity coming from the large number of functionalities that should be included, a lightweight platform with less functionality was chosen (which gave the advantage that there was no or less duplication of functionalities with the backend), but it did not include functionalities for implementing advanced services (such as the link to the garbage collection schedule). The introduction of these platforms proved to be difficult:

We already had many connections made, all these needed to be transferred to the new platform. A nightmare as this affected the webpages, the backend . . . we were undoing all our good work.

## Case 2: Theta (backend)

Theta was one of the largest municipalities and had many problems in making their services available, as services were operated and provided by several large departments which remained autonomous in their procurement of software systems and processes. Theta initially considered taking on a similar approach as Gamma. But with the complexity of having many stakeholders, the need to develop interfaces, integration platforms and business processes, the task of developing multiple services simultaneously was perceived to be extremely challenging.

We were changing a system for the purpose of one service, whilst the next day somebody else came to tell us that we should change the system for the purpose of developing another service . . . we were confused, and started complaining. At one time they started to develop architecture first.

The challenge was compounded by multiple procedures and processes sourcing from multiple software vendors, with each adhering to a different set of standards. Against this, there was a shared perception of the advantages of integrating and streaming multiple workflows and systems, e.g. having a single login for all systems rather than creating multiple and separate accounts.

Although several business models were manifested (including service provider, value-net integrator, infrastructure, internal markets), the way in which the process was implemented was markedly different from Gamma. The goal of performing well in the website monitoring league table remained but Theta pursued a different timeline and different priorities. The initial focus was about achieving a technical solution dealing with the siloed and fragmented archi tecture before tackling the frontend services.

Although the politicians wanted to push their agendas with e-government, this was slowed down by the complexity of Theta’s landscape. The urgency to proceed was high and IT managers were pushed to ensure that all conditions were met to introduce frontend services. Prior to 2003/2004, the freedom in procurement had created a myriad of poorly integrated systems with duplicated systems and functionalities across different agencies. There was very limited insight into the overall architecture; and decisions were taken at local levels without having a single architecture in place to connect the various systems. Recognising the discon nect problems, Theta pursued a technical integration strategy based on an enterprise service bus (ESB). This architecture was all based on a modular service-oriented architecture (SOA). The ESB contained asynchronous and synchronous message transformation, validation and routing. Building blocks for security, identification and workflow and document management were defined. To utilise the modular infrastructure, adapters were developed to enable message exchange among applications. This infrastructure platform enabled the creation of new electronic services through reusing functionalities and applications, and a secure infrastructure for sharing of information across agencies (the infrastructure provider type of business model). The reuse and sharing exercise facilitated trust building and identification of opportunities for further inter-agency collaboration.

Theta had many large departments which hardly interacted with each other, had their own ICT support functions, and made decisions independently of each other. Their management tended to adopt management techniques from large enterprises. This was boosted as some of the managers that were hired were formerly employed by large companies. There was a large distance between departments and the hierarchical lines of management. They embraced enterprise architecture as an instrument to ensure compliance to standards and that decisionmaking among the departments consistently followed the outlined vision, in the mitigation of managing diverse systems. They decided to consolidate their ICT infrastructure and to run it as a cost centre, aiming to facilitate interdepartmental transactions and to reduce the head counts of ICT staff.

In addition to the enterprise architecture, a governance policy was deployed to ensure that all new initiatives were centrally mandated and compliant with the standards. Much emphasis was spent on guiding and informing practices, ensuring that standards were followed. Nevertheless, there were arguments about why certain business processes were not supported by the infrastructures. Instead of compromising, Theta stood firm on the ground that all working processes had to be compatible with the standards to ensure seamless service delivery.

In 2004/2005, Theta went further to integrate backend systems across departments. The integration drew upon not only the technical skills but also on the knowledge and understanding and insights that staff acquired through working with colleagues across agencies in the initial phase of system integration. A lot of effort went into standardising data formats, opening legacy systems and deploying Web service technologies. System were integrated and information exchange was facilitated (i.e. value-net integrator business model). This resulted in the relatively quick and easy implementation of the environmental permit (in comparison with Gamma). Departments already had formal procedures and routines in place to interact with other departments, for example requests coming from other departments were first checked on completeness and appropriateness. Having these kind of procedures and controls in place helped to automate the cross-departmental workflow (i.e. full-service provider business model). Also external professional analysts were deployed to determine the mission critical information and processes from a business standpoint to align the processes with the underlying platform and technology. This resulted in a robust and scalable platform. This proved to be a critical success factor, as other municipalities failed to scale their platforms.

Theta was driven by creating the backend first. A major reason for doing this was the complexity, as pursuing a frontend strategy was difficult.

There was no overview, we simply did not know how to realise frontend models. The carefu development of the infrastructure platform should fit the organisation. This will determine the acceptance and our ability to create the desired services.

The infrastructure as a platform was widely construed to be the way forward by both technical and business managers, in that it helped to define the interrelationships among the components at the backend, and used the basic/critical services such as authentication as a way to link up disjointed departments with siloed based systems/processes. The infrastructure of Theta preceded the frontend business logics.

First we developed a vision of the needed infrastructure, whereas many other departments had difficulties in using a systematic and structured development approach and by putting the architecture first, the architecture enables much more opportunities; and we are only halfway of realizing this.

## Findings and discussion

The two cases have two different starting positions with Gamma striving for service innovation and Theta for infrastructure by integrating systems. Over a period of 7 years, seeking to bring new services to the public, Gamma compromised the underlying infrastructures. In contrast, though initially lagging behind in service innovation, with system and platform integration, Theta was able to catch up, and to improve and develop services both internally and externally. Table 2 summarises the findings of the case studies. Apart from the frontend. Gamma had little backend development activities. Under pressure from politicians, Gamma competed for a better ranking on several website monitors by investing significantly in frontend services. Though being able to achieve the acclaim in service innovation at the beginning, the unsystematic use of technology created more integration problems in the longer run and was unable to sustain further service innovation.

With a larger budget, Theta was confronted which an unmanageable complexity and frag mented backend to pursue the frontend approach. Their policymakers, managers with backgrounds in business and technology architectures resorted to the use of an enterprise architecture plan to develop their infrastructures. Although support was not unanimous initially, this approach was gradually accepted. A critical factor of the backend approach was requiring each department to adhere to the standardisation policy towards adopting a single technolog platform to drive system integration and tighten practices.

Compromising would result in customization, exceptions resulting in high costs and might even result in the abandon of the infrastructure, as everybody could give a reason for not wanting to use the platform.

The technology platform provided Theta a means of organising for change. Each department was required to realign their business processes to the infrastructural standards and stayed mindful of the implications of making any future changes, in that it was essential for changes to complement each other both in terms of technology development and service provision.

The frontend approach of Gamma focuses more towards exploring new business models. Frontend experimentation with new business model is important for exploring new services and business models innovation. Experimentation is necessary to identify services as preferred by citizens. Yet developing services before integrating systems and tightening practices can induce more variety, and result in heterogeneous systems and increased complexity of maintaining and sustaining service provisions. Seemingly, the frontend and backend approaches are not complementary to each other. Theta used a shared infrastructure to build complementarities for service innovation. The use of infrastructure is more prescriptive by concentrating resources on a shared infrastructure and on a smaller and more coherent set o backend-related business models. This approach was pursued by ruling out any new developments which deviated from the existing infrastructural technologies. In contrast, Gamma used business models to drive service innovation, which is explicit in the pursuit of novel business ideas and opportunities.

Gamma and Theta tackled the development of their services differently. The backend infrastructure of Theta relied heavily on modular software applications and interoperability standards, including data standards to facilitate information exchanges. The integration was accomplished by developing an ESB based on standardised SOA protocols. Whereas the reliance of Gamma upon patching the frontend with the backend and the reliance on using outside vendors for backend innovations made further inclusion of additional partners and services difficult. By focusing on the frontend, Gamma was able to create new services quickly. Each of the programmers used their own experience and knowledge to create new services by ‘scripting their way out’, neglecting the architectural standards and logic used in previously developed services. The developers framed the service development using technologies they were most familiar with, instead of pursuing standardised technology and reusing building blocks as was the case with Theta. The frontend approach was quick as there was no need fo defining standards, agreeing on development routines and building blocks nor any consider ation of issues related to the backward and forward compatibility of software development and applications. In the longer run, this resulted in control and maintenance problems, as all the ad hoc connections between frontend and backend and heterogonous systems needed to be maintained and updated. Gamma focused more on the economics of scope of its business. Whereas Theta focused initially more on the scale of its operations and created a flexible and adaptive infrastructure that could be used for various purposes, in this way providing a basic infrastructure for frontend innovation. Only after having realised its backend, the attention was shifted to the frontend.

Prior to 2003/2004, there were inherent control and maintenance problems with Theta, because of its size as one of the largest municipality and legacy integration problems. On the other hand, Gamma did not have these problems. Hence the initial strategy for Theta was one of focusing upon standardisation and control and reducing maintenance costs, but Gamma on agile and incremental development. By continuously adding new services the complexity increased and more and more budget was spent on control and maintenance. In this respect, Gamma eventually ended up in a situation that was similar to Theta’s initial position, and following Theta’s strategy in procuring a platform for integrating the frontend with the backend.

With different sets of business concerns and technical issues, the frontend and the backend routes were unique in creating different instances and opportunities for problem solving and learning for those involved. For Gamma, the instances were more customer-focused. But for Theta, they were focused on dealing with complexity, which serendipitously required working with colleagues across agencies. The former might result in more transient solutions that possibly reflected a fad whereas the latter resulted in more integration and collaborative work across agencies over time.

## Part 2: deductive approach

From our qualitative data, it would appear that the frontend and the backend approach resulted in different sets of service at a different speed and time frame. Specifically, the frontend leads to more services that are innovative in the short term with the backend lagging behind, and vice versa in the long term. To test this prediction, we modelled the results on a sample of public agencies over time, and used website quality measures as a proxy of the quality of service innovation. Based on our inductive findings, we formulate several hypotheses to test the relational links between business models (associated with either the frontend or the backend) and service innovation.

The case of Gamma focused upon improving simple transaction services with citizens and private businesses at the frontend. The service innovation involved creating new services that were responsive to local needs, and generating content that was timely and relevant to the local contexts, which further enhanced external communication. Several business models emerged comprising content provider, direct-to-customer, virtual communities and collaboration. Yet new services were often stand-alone, and disconnected with the existing services. The focus was on improving the content, layout and communication with citizens and businesses. In the Theta case, the priority was markedly different. It focused upon developing a standardised technological platform, aiming to realise seamless and standard services across multiple agencies and systems. The initial effort was aimed at solving the connectivity problems inherent to having multiple legacy systems and less on service innovation. Two specific backend innovations emerged, comprising infrastructure service provider, followed by the business model of a full-service provider. Because the backend approach directly tackles more internal rather than external aspects of services, emphasising system integration, enabling multichannel services and taking steps to integrate existing services across departments, it is likely that this concerted effort will significantly improve services available on the websites but is not a factor contributing to content, layout and communication as these attributes are more externally oriented.

Following from this, we predict that the frontend approach will be associated with more noticeable improvement of service innovation in terms of the layout, content, (non-integrated) service and communication resulting in single and isolated services. Whereas the impact o backend approach on website quality was less noticeable and mostly visible in the improved quality of integrated and multichannel services (as in the case of authenticated ID check).

H1a: Business models associated with the frontend approach will improve the overal website quality in terms of layout, content, service and communication.

H1b: Business models associated with the backend will improve (integrated multichannel) service and less on the website quality (layout, content and communication).

Our qualitative data analysis underscored the importance of the question which approach will result in a more coherent set of business models, with the value sets delivered by each model complementing each other. Resources are limited and business models complement each other. The backend approach of Theta concentrated initial resources in integrating systems, and later on allowed them to build and orchestrate the development of a set of coherent business models (value-net integrators, full-service providers and infrastructure service providers) through increased interdepartmental collaboration. Whereas the frontend approach is largely reactive to the ad hoc changes and the demands of external stakeholders. This can result in increased use of business models, with the possibility that some of them may incur costs due to using technical workaround (as in using software to patch up the systems) and maintenance. Also the ad hoc nature of the frontend approach runs the risk of adopting an incoherent set of business models, and draining resources. Such an ad hoc approach can inadvertently compromise the system integrity, and induce more integration problems in the long run. In the Theta case, the platform provided a standardised way to streamline work processes, and to increase interoperability across systems and departments. This results in a coherent and a smaller set of backend innovations with each complementing the other. The complementarities among the backend innovation are more intuitive and effective in creating a value stack (Gao & Iyer, 2006), essentially harnessing the strength of the platform, and providing a reference model to trim and select new processes and services. This allows public agencies to concentrate on projects that build on service integration (as in the case of permit) rather than spreading their resources in the pursuit of business models as advocated by the frontend approach, and attempting to attain excellence in all of the four categories (layout, content, service and communication) in the website monitors. Hence, we argue that agencies that seek to indiscriminately copying features of the top-ranked sites without discerning the underlying fit between business models and infrastructure are likely to deliver poor services.

H2: Concentrating on combining a set of coherent business models is likely to improve service innovation.

Yet both too little and too much concentration will affect the website quality. Too much concentration is the case when websites rely heavily on a single business model, without harnessing the complementarities of others models (Markides & Charitou, 2004). Whereas too little concentration indicates that multiple incoherent business models may over-serve the users technologically, and the rich information content may complicate search and confuse the users (Christensen & Overdorf, 2000). Pursuing frontend and backend approaches simultaneously will be difficult as such an approach takes into consideration different sets of criteria (and less coherence among business models). Developing a frontend business model simul taneously with a backend model will likely not result in synergy effect as the backend standards and infrastructures are not in place to facilitate frontend development. So either a backend or frontend approach will be taken or one will end up in the middle. As suggested in the Gamma case, an increased number of business models as a response to customers’ needs resulted in more technical patch-ups, which in turn hampered recruitment of new business partners and affected the introduction of new services over time. We argue that if public agencies follow either the frontend or the backend logic, it is logically to deploy two or more models that serve to complement each other, enhancing value capturing. This suggests that moderate concentration offers a middle ground to enhance performance, and yet too little or too much concentration is bad. Hence we formulate the following hypothesis.

H3: Concentration exhibits a curvilinear relationship with the design and quality of services, first rising and then declining (an inverted U-shape), reflecting that too little and too much concentration on business models innovation exerts a negative effect on the quality of services.

## Research methods

We conducted a survey of public and private websites in the regions of Gamma and Theta, which resulted in a final sample of 68 websites.

## Measures

Service innovation. We derived our performance data of the quality of service delivery by using the website quality measures from the Dutch government monitor (http://www. monitor.overheid.nl). This monitor comprised four evaluation criteria. The first three criteria were used to measure website quality comprising: layout (relating to uniform format, clear menu structures, current position, etc.); content (relating to the use of standard information like addresses, opening hours, news, developments, etc.); and communication (relating to old and new policy information, activities, minutes of council meetings, permit request, etc.). One criterion was related to service quality (relating to number of products available at what level, information, printable forms, online transactions, the integration of services, innovative services like recommender services, etc.). Each criterion was an aggregate score in percentages based on rankings carried out by a panel of judges, including customers, organisation staff (internal) and website experts. The ranking was primarily related to the frontend features of the websites that were directly visible and accessible to the citizens. We used an average composite of the first three criteria to test Hypothesis 1a and the service quality measure to tes Hypotheses 1b.

Frontend and backend business models. We used the associated business models with the frontend and backend approach (as shown in Table 1). Two researchers independently surveyed the websites in relation to the presence and absence of the seven generic Web-based business models. Hence the scores ranged from 1 (the presence of one business model) to 7 (the presence of all seven Web-based business models). For several websites, additional information was required to understand the type of business models used. In addition to the iterative classification, we therefore also conducted interviews with the persons in charge of the management and/or development of the websites. In total, 11 interviews were held with representatives of seven different websites. The interviews lasted between 60 and 90 minutes and were aimed at clarifying the type of business models used in the websites and ascertaining the relationship between infrastructures and business models. The interviews results were used to describe a detailed example of each business model and its relation to either frontend or backend. The results concurred with our inductive analysis that the frontend and the backend logics, respectively, manifested into a distinctive set of business models. In the current analysis, the frontend business model was a mean composite measure of the four business models as found in the Gamma case comprising content provider, service provider, collaboration and community business models, whereas the backend business model was a mean composite of service provider, full-service provider and value-net integrator.

Concentration. To test the level of concentration of Hypothesis 2, the Gini coefficient was used to provide a measure of the numbers of unique business models manifested on each website. The Gini coefficient has a value ranging from 0 to 1: with ‘too little’ concentration (i.e. adoption of all business models), the Gini coefficient approaches 0; and with ‘too much’ concentration (i.e. adoption of one specific business model), the Gini coefficient approaches 1.

## Statistical analysis

Hypothesis 3 relating to the inverted U relationship between the use of diverse business models and outcome measures is tested using ordinary least squares (OLS) hierarchical multiple regression. In Step 1, variables including frontend and backend business models, and concentration were first entered into the regression equation. In Step 2, a squared term of concentration was entered. The inclusion of the squared term is a widely accepted approach to test for the curvilinear association by means of an incremental F-test between an additive (variables entered in Step 1) and a curvilinear model (Tabachnik & Fidell, 1989; Jaccard et al., 1990). Before squaring, the raw data of concentration was centred using mean subtraction to reduce the potential problem of multicollinearity (Aiken & West, 1991). Assuming that the distribution of the predictor variable is normally distributed, the mean-centring is used to produce orthogonal predictor variables by separating the purely linear component from the purely quadratic component such that, after recoding, the correlation between participation inequality and its squared term is zero. Finally, two further manipulation checks were conducted. The first check was to identify any potential skewness and kurtosis with the data. The results indicated no significant departures from the normality assumption. Thus, no transformation was required. As Hypothesis 3 required testing of a curvilinear relationship between concentration and performance, any significant relationship might be attributed to a few outliers in the measure of concentration of business model. In the second manipulation check, the standard box-plot method was used to identify any potential outliers that were outside the reasonable lower boundary and the reasonable upper boundary (see Hinkle et al., 1994, p. 68). The results indicated that all data fell within the boundaries and there were no unusual data that warranted special consideration.

## DISCUSSION

The present findings concur with the theory of duality of technology, in that technology not only enables but also constrains change. In the case of Gamma, technology was used to meet the requirements of a specific business model, and more importantly to expedite service innovation at the expense of increased complexity and maintenance cost of the underlying infrastructures. The experimentation resulted in the development of innovative customer-centric services like the garbage collection service. The use of a patching-up approach at the software level inscribes a set of new path-dependent routines in the infrastructures. For example, the two flagship websites were managed relatively independently. Services provisioning and information provisioning was handled in different ways. Each frontend of the website was tied to the backend information and transactions independently of each other. In the case of Theta, the initial focus on the backend system integration enabled more integration and collaboration across different departments but at the same time constrained what the municipality could do in pushing service innovation.

Although taking the frontend and the backend approaches are the results of human actions, each offers different opportunities for change. Orlikowski (1992) cautions that the more users participate in the design, the less interpretative flexibility a technology will have; so the more rigid the technology becomes. This is particularly true with the case of Gamma. The frontend logic in response to customer demands coupled with a patching-up approach to technologica limits may introduce inconsistency in the design of the architectures, and constrain further change. Whereas the backend logic has less interference from politicians, and is less reactive to the constant flux of change to satisfy customer requirements. It enables future progress, such as integrating service, moving towards the multichannel service delivery and so forth, and yet it provides no quick win to politicians.

The findings of the first hypothesis show that although initially a frontend approach scores better, on the long run the backend approach outperforms the frontend approach. Yet for the backend logic, as it takes longer to realise its potentials. As such public managers might be reluctant to adopt the backend approach. This is likely to become increasingly the case as public managers are subject not only to meeting benchmarks and deadlines but also to the pressures from elected politicians to yield quick results. Furthermore, a backend approach might be prone to risk, as failure of development will delay the creation of visible results and can hamper further development. This finding can be explained with reference to the Gamma case. The frontend patching-up approach in dealing with scaling issues of the technology limits, and the over-reliance upon external vendors and central provision had proven insuffi cient in a longer run. The frontend logic provides quick successes. Due to the technological approaches it took, their backend became inherently complex and different to connect to new ventures and partners over time. This reinforces the duality of technology, underlying that the frontend logic might enable business agility in terms of shortening the lead time of providing new services and products, but over time this might lead to technological rigidity which becomes a constraint to enact any future change. Hence, public managers have to be mindful of the enabling and constraining elements of service innovation on the basis of taking a frontend approach.

The backend and frontend approach present a different set of challenges. Addressing them simultaneously requires a realistic timeline. Scarce resources might block pursuing both strategies at the same time; however, one approach can provide attention to the other to avoid constraining potential future opportunities. Another way of dealing with this might be to alternate between frontend and backend approaches and in this way exploit synergies among them. Further research is necessary in which approaches are more fruitful than others.

The curvilinear relationships suggest that concentration of business models benefit content (one of the three measures of website quality) and service to a very large extent. Our finding shows that when there is extreme concentration on a few business models, the Web delivery in terms of content and service starts to decline. This can be illustrated by taking examples in the survey. One of the surveyed cases focuses only on the content provider business model, but basic content related to policymaking was not included, and policymak ing issues were not addressed. Thus neglecting content related to other type of business models, i.e. collaboration. Another website included almost all business models; however, it become difficult to navigate, find the right content and services and hardly any attention was given to retain visitors and in this way create a sustainable community. It seems that efforts were focused on running too many business models resulting in lower electronic service quality.

The difficulties in having both a backend and frontend approach are underlined by the confirmation of the second hypothesis that concentration on a limited number of business models improves electronic service delivery. Agencies indiscriminately copying the features of the top-ranked sites without discerning the underlying business logics will fall ahead. That is, in keeping up appearances and staying competitive in the benchmarking exercise, public agencies may lose sight of the fact that doing the right things for the wrong reasons is worse than not doing the right thing at all. This approach might reinforce the duality of technology as adding more complexity by introducing too many business models contributes to a high complexity and path dependencies constraining further development. By purposefully selecting and developing the right business models and considering the implications of the frontend and backend on each other further constraints might be circumvented.

The creation of value-creating activities is one of the main themes in business models research (Mahadevan, 2000). A value-creating activity realises a business model by translating the frontend changes to the backend implications. An e-government business model is aimed at adding value to the constituents. And yet the value-creation logic architecture is subject to the enabling and constraining elements attributed to the duality of technology. In that, the reciprocal relationships between business models and infrastructures require both to be assembled in tandem in achieving the economics of complementarities and in creating long-lasting impacts and values. By taking a holistic and long-term view on the frontend and backend architecture for realising the appropriate business models, the constraining elements might be addressed and ultimately avoided.

This research has several limitations. First, the ranking of the government monitor was primarily related to the frontend features of the websites that were directly visible and accessible to the citizens. There are not indicators used for measuring the backend. The latter provides a challenge that is hardly addressed by researchers. A suggestion for further research is to develop backend measures and to include them in e-government monitors. One of the backend measures might be related to information quality. In Gamma’s architecture similar information was stored in databases at several locations and there are often inconsistencies among the information. Theta has a backend focus and its architecture is based on the principle of information stewardship in which data is stored in one location and reused by others. A second limitation is that we used the quality of website (layout, content and communication) and service measures to reflect electronic service delivery. This showed on the long run that a backend strategy outperformed a frontend strategy. Electronic service delivery might be influenced by a broader range of aspects, including increased ease of finding information, customer-centric design and so on. These aspects were not included and need attention in future research. Finally, we did not investigate factors influencing the choice between taking a backend or frontend strategy. Variables like the level of fragmentation, size, available resources and expertise, political ambition and software vendors might influence the selection of a backend or frontend strategies as suggested by our case studies. Statistica research into these factors can shed light on the factors influencing the strategy.

## CONCLUSIONS

Using a practice-based approach two service innovation routes were examined, the frontend approach explored new opportunities and the backend approach aimed at exploiting existing IT capabilities and improving them. Our study acknowledges the relationship and connection to past practices. The frontend approach in response to external pressure is dominated by the website benchmark, experimentation with services and managers enacting new technologies as part of the sense-making process of external changes and/or demands. Experimentation with new frontend services and business models is necessary for exploring innovations. The backend approach focuses upon integrating systems and creating a shared infrastructure across departments. This approach leads to changes in existing practices and routines followed by service innovation. Both approaches enable and constrain development and change.

The research presented in this paper shows that it is important to distinguish between frontend and backend business models and how they influence service innovation and to ensure coherency among the types of models. Furthermore, it suggests that a frontend approach initially results into more services made online and to a higher website quality, but is outperformed in the long run by organisations focusing on their backend as these enabling the creation of integrated and multichannel services. The frontend logic might enable business agility in terms of shortening the lead time of providing new services and products, but over time these results in a lack of alignment and in technological rigidity which becomes a constraint to enact any future change, whereas the backend creates a technology infrastructure that enables new opportunity in the long run. The backend approach is less disruptive because it realigns practices and routines and technology internally first, whereas technology enactment accumulatively can be disruptive especially when the business models are developed in isolation without following some common standards and routines. As such, public sector managers should pay attention to the available IT capabilities for service innovation as this can constrain further improvements. The backend and frontend approaches present a different set of chal lenges and simultaneously addressing them requires a realistic timeline and avoids situations when one constrains the other. The cases show that adherence to either frontend or backend logics can both result in successful service innovation but in a different time frame.

The research shows that concentration on a limited number of business models improves the time frame of service innovation and also that having too many or too few business models result in lower quality of service quality. The concentration of business models exhibits a curvilinear relationship with the design and quality, first rising and then declining as an inverted U-shape. Having too many business models might reinforce the duality of technology as more business models add to the complexity. Not surprisingly this implies that the best strategy is to determine those business models that are appropriate for the public organisation given its objectives, customers, resources and capabilities and again shows the duality of technology. Often a limited number of business models are needed; having all types of business models is not likely to fit an organisation’s mission. By purposefully selecting and developing the right business models and considering the implications of the frontend and backend on each othe further constraints and focusing on creating enabling elements. Combining backend and frontend strategies might be viewed as a way of overcoming the duality of technology by avoiding the creation of constraints. This might be difficult due to a lack of resources and capabilities for addressing both and might result in the accomplishment of the constraining elements of both approaches. Research in the complementariness of frontend and backend strategies is needed.
