# Innovation in IT firms: An Investigation of Intramural and Extramural R&D Activities and Their Impact

## Abstract

This research examines how intramural activities that are developed internally within the firm and extramural activities that are undertaken in collaboration with an external partner lead to product and process innovation. We use hierarchical regression analysis on data collected for European and Chinese firms and determine that intramural activities have a profound positive impact on process innovation whereas their impact on product innovation is positive only for large firms. We discover that process innovation positively impacts product innovation but not vice versa. The results are important for managers who guide R&D activities of a firm to product and process innovation.

Keywords: Determinants of innovation; Extramural activities; Intramural activities; Process innovation; Product innovation; R&D.

## 1. Introduction

Bradford Goldsense, President of Goldsense Inc. in an article stated that companies don’t understand the intertwining between product innovation and process innovation. “Corporations have often appended and/or pulled activities out of their product-development process (PDP) rather than build innovation into their process” (Goldsense, 2013). Innovation has intrigued researchers and practitioners alike for decades, more so for technological innovation, because of its quicker impact and faster outcomes. Innovation is essential tool for achieving excellence in today’s competitive environment. The implications of innovation for firms can be understood from the fact that more and more CIO roles are changing titles to Chief Innovation Officers (Baya, Gruman & Muthaisel, 2011).

The need to understand the core tenets of innovation and the factors which lead to higher innovation outcome have become much more prominent in recent years. This has also been highlighted by one of the leading global consulting firms AT Kearny® which in its 2013 report shows that although the need and importance of innovation is rising, the investment in innovation is decreasing (Eul, Hagen & Miller, 2013). A better and deeper understanding of activities leading to innovation in firms will make it feasible and efficient for new age CIOs to have higher innovation at lower costs.

The case of innovation in IT firms in this respect is even more curious. Though IT has been widely established as one of the primary drivers of innovation in firms (Davenport, 2013; Bharadwaj, 2000), innovation within IT firms has not been widely studied. IT has long been considered to be the driver or enabler of all organizational innovation and hence the adoption and diffusion of such innovation in both manufacturing and service firms have been studied from multiple perspectives over the years. However how product or process innovation emerges within IT firms is a question that is not adequately answered in the extant literature.

Kleis et al (2012) have found that IT innovations, though very infrequently patented, have a very deep impact on firms’ ability to continually innovate. The human capital view of IT firms has found some interesting results in the domain of innovation in IT firms. Banker et al. (2008) have found that higher employee education leads to higher internal innovation in R&D firms. Banker et al.’s (2011) elaborate research on innovation concludes that diversification is a more important source of innovation rather than internal R&D. These research endeavors lay the foundation for a more in-depth analysis of activities leading to innovation in IT firms, which the research described in this paper attempts to address.

The literature on innovation discusses that R&D activities that are practiced by the firm are closely linked to innovation outcome both quantitatively and qualitatively and differ for both product and process innovation (Adner & Levinthal, 2001). There are two types of innovation activities. First, those activities that are completely internal to the firm, e.g., setting up of internal R&D team or developing new products for market using that team. Such activities are called intramural activities (Lundvall, 2009; Sharif et al., 2012, Sterlacchini, 1989). An example of intramural innovation is the development of the SABRE airline reservation system by American Airlines in 1953 (Hopper, 1990). The second is extramural activities where a firm outsources the activities related to innovation. This can be done to consultants, academic/researchers or other firms. In this arrangement the firm pays a predefined amount of money to an external body to develop solutions for its problems (Sterlacchini, 1989; Lundvall, 2009). A notable example of extramural innovation is that of the mouse by Xerox PARC. This innovative product was developed and marketed by Apple Inc® for Xerox (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002). Researchers around the world have investigated the impact of both intramural R&D and extramural R&D on outcomes of innovation (Sterlacchini, 1989; Murovec & Prodan, 2009; Wallsten, 2000). Researchers around the world have investigated intramural R&D and extramural R&D and their impact on innovation outcomes (Sterlacchini, 1989; Murovec & Prodan, 2009; Wallsten, 2000). The debate is still continuing and the jury is still out (Barbosa et al., 2014; Hashi & Stojcic, 2013). We take the debate further by extending it to IT firms and attempt to uncover which activities are important for innovation outcomes in IT firms.

Our key research question is whether intramural and/or extramural innovation has a positive impact on product and/or process innovation. We also attempt to find whether there is any relationship between product and process innovation in firms. We explore these questions for firms located in two diverse geographies (i.e., Europe and China) to understand whether the relationship between R&D activities and innovation outcomes are generalizable or geography specific. Our research extends the earlier work of researchers attempting to understand the role of internal and external R&D activities in IT firms and provides guidance for managers that oversee innovation related activities in IT firms.

Analyzing the data on firm’s innovative activities, we find that antecedents of product and process innovation differ to a great extent. Different R&D activities lead to different results in terms of innovative products and processes. We find that process innovation is intimately related to a firm and driven by in-house R&D that is based on knowledge of the firm and its existing processes as well as in-house R&D that is influenced by ideas generated by members who are external to the firm. In case of product innovation, in-house R&D that is influenced by external members does not significantly impact the innovation outcome whereas in-house R&D without external support impacts product innovation for all types of European firms as well as large sized Chinese firms only. We also discover that process innovation in firms lead to product innovation, but the reverse is not true. The key results mostly hold for both European and Chinese firms.

This paper is arranged in sections. In section 2 we provide an overview of product and process innovation and discuss their interrelationships in IT firms. In section 3 we discuss the firm level activities that lead to innovation and formulate the research hypotheses. Section 4 provides a description of the data related to innovation in Chinese and European firms that is used in this study. In the next section, we explain the choice of the dependent and independent variables for this research. Thereafter, section 6 explains in detail the research findings. We check the robustness of the research results through several case-based subsample analyses in section 7. Section 8 explains in detail the managerial and academic implications of this research. Finally, section 9 concludes this paper.

## 2. Product and Process Innovation

The term invention and innovation are different and treated as such in this research. We use invention exclusively for ‘new creation’ and innovation for the complete process consisting of the three overlapping stages of invention, implementation and diffusion (Dosi, 1988). For the last several decades, innovation researchers have attempted to identify the activities and characteristics that make one firm highly innovative vis-à-vis others. Extant research on innovation has identified its various types, namely, product innovation (Ettlie & Bridges, 1982), process innovation (Gallouj & Weinstein, 1997), incremental innovation (Damanpour, 1991), and radical innovation (Duchesneau et al., 1979), among others. The decision of the firm to indulge in a particular strand of innovation depends on the returns and the expectations of the firm from such an activity. We focus on the product versus the process innovation view in our current work. It is worth mentioning that both product and process innovation can be a result of any innovation approach (i.e., radical, incremental etc.).

## 2.1. Innovation in China and Europe

Europe has traditionally been a hotbed of technological innovation since the days of the industrial revolution. Innovative activities of European firms have fuelled the technological progress of the world (Jacob, 1997). A number of products and services firms in the IT sector have been based out of Europe (Storey & Tether, 1998). They include AVG, F-Secure, Nokia, Opera Software etc. In recent times, European nations have made a concerted effort to document and monitor innovation. The Community Innovation Surveys (CIS) are a step in that direction (Waarts & Van Everdingen, 2005). These surveys and the academic research that make use of the data from these surveys have unveiled the stark differences in the way the different sectors and institutional systems impact innovation in Europe (Cooke et al., 1997).

In the last few decades major technological innovation has been seen in several Asian countries including China. Product innovation is commonly taking place in many Chinese firms (Li & Atuahene-Gima, 2001). With the offshoring of production and software development Asian locations have become the new centers of innovation related activities. A large number of multi-national firms have their product (hardware or software) development centers in China. These include global giants like Samsung, Apple etc. as well as pure Chinese players like Lenovo, Chinasoft etc. In the face of growing competition the technological innovation capabilities of Chinese firms have been rising rapidly as well (Crescenzi et al., 2012; Yam et al., 2004).

While the innovation culture and innovation related activities of European firms have been studied over the years, the same is almost absent in the extant literature on Chinese firms. The need to understand how the firms in China innovate vis-à-vis their European counterparts is a timely question. While the free trade borders and the easy movement of human and goods across borders have helped in the development of European innovation systems over the years, this has been taking place in a limited manner over the last 20 years for China (Lundvall, 2007). China is developing itself as a global destination for low cost and high-tech innovation and China’s close international tie-ups with firms in different continents is a major influence in this direction (Schanz et al., 2011). Even in the IT sector, China’s reputation has grown manifold as a low cost and high innovation destination with the rise of firms like Lenovo, Huawei etc.

European IT firms are moving more and more towards software development with products like gaming, anti-virus etc. being developed in large numbers. On the contrary, Chinese firms are consolidating their lead in the hardware segment as well as expanding in the IT services segment (Popkin, 2006). The current research attempts to fulfill the gap in understanding how Chinese IT firms innovate and how this innovation compares with that of European IT firms.

## 2.2. Product and Process Innovation in IT firms

Although academic research seems to indicate that product and process innovation paradigms are complementary in some way (Mantovani, 2006; Mohnen & Roller, 2005), innovation outcomes at many firms do not seem to agree with that. An evidence of this is in the way firms approach these two innovation dimensions. Process innovations generally are an attempt to improve the output by improving the underlying production or other processes. These can include ERP implementation, social media strategy etc. (Jha & Bose, 2014) Product innovation is considered to be hard innovation by firms where R&D efforts are employed to a greater extent.

In the specific case of IT firms, not a lot of research has been done to understand the outcomes of innovation. Chen (2004) in the study on Taiwanese IT firms R&D in China has found that the R&D activities in firms in most developing economies have close linkages with their global counterparts. The presence of network effect in IT firms’ innovation efforts is strongly visible. Hertog (2000) has found that knowledge intensive businesses like IT have long been viewed as enablers of innovation and the efforts in such firms are different turn than those in traditional firms due to the knowledge intensive nature of these firms. The strong demarcations between product and process innovation that is visible in traditional firms may not be so distinguishable in IT firms because the boundaries between hard ‘product’ innovation and knowledge driven ‘process’ innovation often blur.

Autio (1997) describes new technology based firms that are based in the Silicon Valley and find that the characteristics of these firms are different from those of traditional firms. The work that is based on the systemic view of technological innovation establishes the presence of strong systems and iterative processes in innovation efforts at such firms. These findings lead us to believe that the process to product innovation linkage may be much more pronounced in IT firms.

## 2.3. Drivers of Product and Process Innovation

Till Rosenberg’s (1982) seminal research on social processes of innovation, economic analysis has treated innovation as a black box. Later more complex assessments have emerged that have highlighted the importance of social and institutional factors that stimulate innovation (Sorensen & Levold, 1992). King et al. (1994) have posited that in any economic scenario and any geography, the decisions taken by the management of the firm deeply impact the innovation outcomes. These organizational factors include the managerial decisions related to cooperation and R&D activities that a firm takes up. Tsuji (2014) in his analysis of ASEAN economies’ innovation has attempted to demystify the various cooperation decisions that are instrumental in driving innovation.

These studies have established the importance of organizational and firm level factors that influence innovation in a firm. The impact of clearly delineated activities targeting innovation in firms has been widely studied as well (Hashi & Stojcic, 2013; Pires, Sarkar & Carvalho, 2008; Sharif, Baark & Lau, 2012). Different R&D activities lead to different outcomes in terms of products, processes, marketing etc. Multiple surveys like CIS and the World Bank enterprise survey have attempted to capture these R&D activities. The different types of R&D activities have been broadly classified into two types. These are ‘intramural’ (internal to firm)

activities and ‘extramural’ (external to firm) activities (Hashi & Stojcic, 2013; Sharif, Baark & Lau, 2012). Next we discuss the impact of both types of activities on product and process innovation in IT firms and formulate related hypotheses.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

Prior research has found contradictory results about the impact of R&D activities on firms’ innovation output. While some studies identify internal R&D spending of a firm to be an important contributor to firms’ innovation output, there are significant contrary studies as well. De Marchi (2012) in his analysis of R&D related cooperation for environmental innovation has observed that even in the presence of intense innovation, there is scant expenditure on intramural R&D. The same observation has also found support in other research which state that in an increasingly globalized world greater collaboration in research gives rise to better product outcomes (Arora et al. 2013; Siller et al. 2014).

Figure 1 depicts the research model that we propose in this article. We intend to investigate the impact of intramural and extramural R&D activities on innovation outcomes. We consider ‘pure’ and ‘partial’ intramural R&D activities as the two types of internal R&D activities that are pursued by the firm. The external R&D activities that are adopted by the firm include either contracted R&D or licensing of machinery and technology. As shown in Figure 1, innovation outcome can be of two types i.e., product and process innovation. The direction of arrows in the figure indicates the hypothesized impacts on the various types of R&D activities pursued by the firm on innovation outcomes. The research model also studies the interrelationship between the two types of innovation outcomes.

## 3.1. Intramural R&D Activities

Past research suggests that innovation is deeply connected to the internal R&D investments that are made by the firm. A panel data analysis of Dutch firms has indicated that internal

#

R&D investment is a major contributor to firm’s innovation efforts (Lokshin et al., 2008). Extant research has also established that another important role of internal R&D is that it extracts benefits out of external R&D. Researchers agree that external R&D is an important contributor to firm’s overall innovation outcome only in the presence of high internal R&D spending (Lokshin et al. 2008). After analyzing the R&D efforts of Canadian firms, Czarnitzki et al. (2011) have suggested that internal R&D investments stimulated by tax credits given by the government has produced higher innovation output for these firms. External funding and research in the form of government R&D or extramural R&D can complement internal R&D but not substitute it (David, Hall & Toole, 2000). Primarily research tends to favor the inference that internal R&D spending by the firm significantly contributes to its new product and services development. This leads to the first hypothesis.

H1: Intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new product or service development.

This hypothesis can be broken into two sub-hypotheses based on two types of intramural activities. The two types of intramural R&D activities are pure intramural activities and partial intramural activities. This distinction is based on the participation of the focal firm alone or other agencies at various stages of development of innovation (Davenport, 2013; Eluinn, 2000). Pure intramural activities can be described as those activities where the entire process including idea generation and R&D takes place internally in the firm. An example of such an activity is the development of the virtual reality device Oculus® VR which was designed and developed internally by Oculus<sup>1</sup> (Jerald et al., 2014). The second type of intramural activities are those where the idea is generated externally by academicians, consultants etc. but these ideas are developed, adapted and implemented within the firm. Many consulting firms take part in such activities to allow higher innovation productivity within their client firms. An example of this is the innovation of a breakthrough concept for preventing infections associated with surgery by 3M after getting input from a theatricalmakeup specialist who was knowledgeable about preventing facial skin infections and who acted as a consultant for 3M (Poetz et al., 2014). The second type of R&D activities has been referred to as partial intramural activities. These are different from the extramural innovation activities or outsourced innovation activities.

H1a: Pure intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new product or service development.

H1b: Partial intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new product or service development.

While most firms put a continuous effort to introduce new products and services leading to product innovation, process innovation is not so religiously pursued (Acs & Audretsch, 1988; Pires, Sarkar & Carvalho, 2008; Ar & Baki, 2011). Process innovation is considered by some researchers as being more difficult to implement and hence adopted only by large and aged firms (Acs & Audretsch, 1988). A contrary view is presented by Pires et al. (2008) who show that younger firms are more likely to introduce process innovations due to nimbleness associated with them (Pires et al., 2008). This is because process innovation builds up only by the practical implementation of new concepts by firms themselves. Process innovation demands a deep internal understanding of the firm and its systems. It is difficult to indulge in process innovation for outside agencies without formally understanding the intricacies of the firm (Ar & Baki, 2011; Davenport, 2013). Based on this understanding of literature, we formulate the second hypothesis.

H2: Intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new process development in a firm.

This hypothesis can be broken down into two sub-hypotheses for ease of analysis as shown below:

H2a: Pure intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new process development in a firm.

H2b: Partial intramural R&D activities have a significant and positive impact on new process development in a firm.

## 3.2. Extramural R&D Activities

One important type of R&D activities practiced by a firm is the extramural activities that the firm engages in. The extramural activities are those that are not conducted internally in the firm (Sharif, Baark & Lau, 2012). The OECD Oslo manual identifies two types of extramural activities. These are extramural activities outsourced to an external partner or extramural activities where readymade solutions in terms of patented machines or IPR are purchased for use in the firm’s product/process development (Banker et al., 2011). Drawing from the earlier discussion on intramural R&D activities, we understand that available literature point to the fact that it is more beneficial for firms to conduct R&D activities internally (David, Hall & Toole, 2000). Extending the same logic to the two types of extramural R&D activities we hypothesize that R&D activities where firms license the underlying technology but adapt it in-house is expected to be more impactful for innovation outcomes than purely outsourced ventures. This leads to the third hypothesis.

H3: Licensing of machinery and technological equipment has a higher positive impact on product and process innovation as compared to outsourced extramural R&D activities.

## 3.3. Relationship Between Process Innovation and Product Innovation

Innovation in itself is not a one-time activity but an effort that needs to thrive over time throughout the firm to nurture innovative thinking and bring out something new. It requires careful planning and execution over a long time horizon (Sood & Tellis, 2005). It is an iterative process with a starting point but no definite end and all activities feed into one another to create an ecosystem leading to the introduction of new products and services or processes (Lundvall, 2009). The two major types of innovation outcomes i.e., new products and new processes seemingly follow different paths (Davenport, 2013) but a number of researchers have suggested that those paths intersect at points more than one (Lundvall, 2009). Process innovation, aimed at improving ‘how’ things are done in a firm, has implications for ‘what’ is done in a firm (Utterback & Abernathy, 1975). Process innovation has been found to be closely related to the efforts and innovation drives of top management in some firms and such firms have shown a greater propensity for product innovation (Li et al.,

2013). Davenport (2013) provides various case studies of firms that indulge in revamping of business processes and business models and are able to achieve higher overall innovation. Process innovation that is more tightly related to the business soul of the firm enhances a firm’s ability to innovate and also opens up more avenues for improvement (Becker & Egger, 2013). The innovation culture of a firm impacts the ability of a firm to produce a larger number of innovative products in the market (Martin-de Castro et al., 2013). The extant literature suggests intricate links between the process innovation and product innovation. We state the hypothesis as follows.

H4a: Process innovation has a significant and positive impact on the development of new products and services.

H4b: Product innovation has a significant and positive impact on new process development.

## 5. Choice of Variables

Our analysis is focused on identifying the determinants of innovation. For doing so, we focus on the activities and outcome questions answered by the firms. The detailed questions are provided in Appendix A2.

## 5.1. Dependent Variables

Two dependent variables are used in this study. These are product innovation outcome and process innovation outcome. The two dependent variables are not used simultaneously and are used in separate regression analysis.

a. Product Innovation Outcome: Product and service innovation outcome clubbed together as product innovation outcome refers to the new product or services that are introduced by the firm during the period of analysis. The innovation may be in terms of either entirely new product or services that are introduced by the firm or incremental addition to an existing product or service that can be characterized as an innovative addition to the existing products or services.

b. Process Innovation Outcome: Process innovation outcomes are the new processes or ways of doing business that are introduced by the firm. The process innovation defines the way things are being done at the firms. It is the innovation of ‘How’. It includes new managerial processes, administrative processes or quality control processes that are introduced by the firm.

## 5.2. Independent Variables

The independent variables that are used in the study have been selected from the host of variables provided by the enterprise surveys data based as per the needs of the developed hypotheses. These independent variables are:

a. Intramural R&D: This represents the effort made internally by the firm to create innovative products/services or processes. This can either be entirely indigenous research to create a new offering, or it can be an internal adaptation of a product or service or process existing within the firm or adapted from a competitor or some other firm. This has been captured in the dataset through a binary variable that is set to one if a firm has confirmed the presence of internal R&D activities for the preceding year.

b. Internal R&D from External Idea: This is a specific R&D activity where the firm introduces new products/services or processes based on the knowledge or insight given by external partners which may be hired partners like consultants or neutral external public sources like university/academicians etc. In these cases the generation of the idea is external and the firm implements the innovative idea. This has been captured from the data by means of a binary variable which is set to one if a firm has confirmed the presence of internal R&D activities for the preceding year that is aimed at internalizing ideas from external partners like consultants, universities etc.

c. External R&D: This is the purest form of extramural activity practiced where firms do not have the resources or skills to internally pursue R&D projects. The R&D projects are outsourced to an external partner and the innovation is directed by the partner although the partner may have a lot of input from the firm itself. This has been captured in the dataset using a binary variable that is set to one if a firm has confirmed outsourcing or contracted R&D activities for the preceding year. Under this arrangement the firm does not take part in any product/process development apart from explaining its requirements to the contracting firm.

d. Licensing of Technology/IP etc.: This is a type of extramural activity where the firm does not completely outsource the R&D activities. In this type of arrangement, firms procure or license the IPR of some other firm. This may also entail procuring critical machinery, technology etc. This has been captured in the dataset through a binary variable that is set to one if a firm has confirmed the licensing of technology like machinery or any patent etc. in the preceding year.

It is to be noted that a firm may not indulge in a single type of R&D activity. There may be cases where a firm has indulged in some combination of the above mentioned four types of R&D activities. To measure the impact of product and process innovation on process and product innovation outcomes respectively, we have used the last three years data of product and process outcomes as independent variables in the hierarchical regression model. This has been done to ascertain the impact of past product and process innovations on the current process and product innovation outcomes respectively.

## 5.3. Control Variables

The control variables, also referred to as context variables or environmental variables, have been used in this research to establish the independence of the results from the contexts. The control variables used in this study are as follows.

a. Firm Revenue: Firm revenue can deeply influence a firms’ innovation capability. Firms with large revenues have more resources and capabilities to indulge simultaneously in multiple activities of production as well as innovation. Firms that are smaller in terms of revenue have fewer resources to invest in multiple simultaneous activities (Tsai, 2001; Damanpour, 1991). We have measured firm revenue by calculating the natural log of the revenue generated by the firm.

b. R&D Spending: R&D spending of a firm is the second control variable that is used in this study. R&D spending deeply impacts the firms’ capacity to produce a large number of new products or processes (Parthasarathy & Hammond, 2002; Sampson, 2007). This is because innovation itself is a very resource intensive affair and higher R&D spending supports innovation related activities in general. We have used the natural log of total R&D expenses (on internal as well as external R&D) as a control variable.

c. Firm Age: Various studies have found that the age of the firm is an important explanatory variable in innovation (Almus & Nerlinger, 1999; De Jong & Vermeulen, 2006; Cefis & Marsili, 2006). A large body of research posits that new and younger firms are more innovative on account of the adaptability of new technology and leaner organizational structure (Baldwin & Lin, 2002; Heunks, 1998).

From these tables we observe that younger firms in China conduct more innovation oriented activities whereas applies to older firms in Europe. One possible explanation for this is that China is a relatively new economy with a large number of new firms that are innovating aggressively to stay competitive globally.

To determine the extent to which the independent variables can explain the variability of the dependent variables after considering the control variables, we resort to the hierarchical regression method. Hierarchical regression analysis is a method of choice for researchers who want to analyze the impact of an additional variable on the dependent outcome (Tsai, 2001). The method has been used very frequently in analyzing the impact of a new variable under study in innovation related research. Examples of the use of the hierarchical regression can be seen in both organizational studies (Tsai, 2001; Chen et al., 2014) as well as in research related to information systems (Hasan, 2006; Harrison et al., 1997). At each step of the analysis additional variables are introduced to trace the change in the values of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ . In step 1 of the hierarchical regression model the analysis is conducted using control variables only. The independent variables are introduced in step 2 of the analysis. In step 3 of the analysis we include process/product innovation as an explanatory variable as well. The analysis traces the change in $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ as the analysis moves from one step to the next.

## 6. Research Findings

Table 6 and Table 7 show the results of the hierarchical regression analysis. The analysis estimates the effects of various R&D activities practiced by the firm on product and process innovation for Chinese and European firms. From the results of the hierarchical regression, we find that $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ and adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values increase successively in each step of the analysis indicating increasing explanatory power provided by the dependent variables of the regression.

## Intramural Activities for Product Innovation

The results in Table 6 indicate that intramural activities are not a significant contributor of product innovation in Chinese firms. This is true for both pure intramural activities as well the partial intramural activities. On the contrary, extramural activities are a significant and positive contributor of product innovation for Chinese firms. Table 7 shows that for European firms both intramural as well as extramural R&D activities significantly and positively impact product and process innovation. We also observe that pure intramural R&D activities have a larger value of the regression coefficient than that for extramural R&D activities for both Chinese and European firms. Hypothesis H1a that focuses on the impact of pure intramural activities on product innovation is rejected for Chinese firms but not European firms. Hypothesis H1b that assesses the impact of partial intramural activities on product innovation is rejected for both Chinese and European firms. We also observe that for Chinese firms, the regression coefficient for partial intramural activities is higher than that of pure intramural activities for product innovation.

## Intramural Activities for Process Innovation

Table 6 and Table 7 show that process innovation is linked to a firm’s intramural R&D activities. Hypotheses H2a and H2b are accepted for both Chinese and European firms. The results for pure intramural activities are significant at the 1% level of significance whereas the results for partial intramural activities are significant at the 5% level of significance. The results also show that extramural activities are not a significant contributor of process innovation as the corresponding regression coefficients are not significant even at the 5% level of significance.

## Extramural R&D Activities

Analysis of the hypothesis that studies the impact of extramural R&D activities on product and process innovation generates the most interesting results. The two geographies that we study depart slightly from one another with respect to this hypothesis. Step 3 of the hierarchical regression is conducted to test hypothesis H4. Expenses targeted at procurement of external R&D for process innovation do not have as much impact as internal R&D activities. ## 7. Robustness Checks Through Analysis of Subsamples

We perform robustness checks on the results to discover any possible anomalies in the results. The subsample analysis is performed to ensure the generalizability of the results in all cases covered by the dataset (Hong & Zhu, 2006; Phillips, 1981). We conduct the check for robustness using the following steps:

1. Conduct subsample analysis to confirm the validity of results in different cases.

2. Perform a rigorous check on the results of hypothesis H1a and H3 that shows divergent results for Chinese and European firms.

For both product and process innovation in Chinese as well as European firms, three types of subsample analyses are performed. This subsample analysis is performed on the full model as shown in Table 6 and Table 7. The following cases are used.

Case 1: Test for Small Sized Firms: In case 1, we test the hypotheses for smaller sized firms to check whether the results that are reported in section 6 hold for smaller firms. For

#

this analysis, the subsample that we consider is made up of small sized firms with less than 20 employees. The sample of interest is made up of 107 Chinese firms and 56 European firms. The analysis produces results that are same as those for the full sample. The only exception is Hypothesis H3. In case 1, hypothesis H3 is fully rejected for European firms. Also, we observe that for Chinese firms the coefficients for extramural R&D activities is significantly larger than those that are obtained for the full sample and significantly smaller (approaching 0) than those that are obtained for intramural R&D activities for the full sample. This indicates a greater dependence on extramural R&D activities for smaller Chinese firms.

Case 2: Test for Large Sized Firms: Next we check the robustness of results and its applicability to large sized firms. Large firms are those firms that have more than 100 employees. This case analysis can be conducted for Chinese firms only with a sample size of 81 firms. Although with only 11 firms in this group, results of the analysis for European firms are not statistically valid. However, we still go ahead with this analysis to gauge how large European firms innovate. The results for case 2 should thus be used only for illustrative purpose deeper analysis with larger data set is required to validate the findings for large European firms. The analysis of case 2 shows surprising results for large Chinese firms. The results deviate from that of the full sample and hypothesis H1a is accepted for Chinese firms as well for this case. This makes the results similar to that obtained for European firms. This implies that large sized Chinese firms behave similarly to the European firms. The major point of departure is that H3 is not rejected for either product or process innovations. This implies that for large European firms licensing is significantly activity as compared outsourcing innovation for both product and process innovation.

## 8. Discussion of Results

The results that are obtained from the analysis of the Chinese and European firms’ data have important implications for academicians and practitioners. We have performed the analysis at two levels for both geographies. First the analysis is performed at an aggregate level to get broader and generic results that are then tested for validity on subsamples created from the full sample. Empirical analysis of data related to hypothesis H1a shows that Chinese firms behave differently from European firms. The rejection of intramural R&D activities as a

#

significant contributor for product innovation reinforces the fact that Chinese firms are very intricately networked with multiple partners and most of the new products are possibly a result of R&D activities at other firms. The finding is in line with earlier research which finds information sharing and cooperation to be very important for Chinese firms (Shin, Ishman & Sanders, 2007). The presence of many young Chinese firms may be one of the factors responsible for low intramural R&D and more external effort.

This has important lessons for managers of firms at Chinese and similar emerging economies which can adopt similar approach of external innovation building in their earlier years to grow fast and become competitive at a global level. Intramural R&D cannot be neglected and policies need to be put in place for the same as prior research has indicated that inherently innovative firms last much longer than non-innovative firms (Heunks, 1998; Klette & Kortun, 2002). The subsample analysis also provides deeper insight into this case by showing that firms that are larger in size and have large R&D spending tend to innovate internally. The process thus looks like one of firm evolution where firms initially innovate externally and gradually grow to have strong internal R&D units that become prime drivers for innovation. The results for European firms indicate a balanced impact of both intramural as well as extramural activities on the product innovation outcome. This shows the evolved innovation ecosystem at European firms, where both internal and external innovation is important although internal innovation reaps richer dividends.

The results for process innovation are consistent for both Chinese and European firms. These results are also a logical extension of past academic research in this domain. Various studies, as mentioned in section 3, have established that process innovation is very closely linked to understanding the intricacies of how firm functions. Our results support the conclusion that process innovation is highly impacted by internal R&D activities.

Use of acquired technology and IPR does not give rise to high returns and our study does not find it to be a significant contributor except in the case of process innovation for European firms. Also, the subsample analysis of European firms finds that for large firms these results are significant for both product and process innovation. The absence of this result for firms with large R&D spending establishes the fact that such results hold only for large firms with a huge emphasis on extramural R&D activities. This leads to a very interesting implication for managers of these firms. Appropriating technology/machinery/IPR etc. that is developed by other firms to a particular firm’s use is a difficult exercise. Firms should either contract out the complete R&D activity or do it internally to generate significant innovation outcomes. Appropriating IPR is a rather difficult exercise.

We also conclude that process innovation contributes to product innovation but not vice versa. Hypothesis H4a and H4b show very stable results in all subsample analysis. Many studies have indicated previously that constant improvement in how things are done at a firm is very important for doing better things and for introducing new products and services in a firm. The results from this study validate that. For managers it means that continuous process innovation has to be inbuilt in the organization as it leads to a higher product innovation outcome as well.

In terms of contribution to the theory of innovation, our findings add to the debate in academic literature on two major topics of innovation, as discussed in section 3. These are impact of R&D investment on innovation and impact of age and size of firm on process innovation. The results presented in this paper suffer from the usual drawbacks associated with the choice of methodology and the dataset. The inferences are drawn from data of Chinese and European firms and hence its generalizability to other geographies is questionable. The result presented in this paper can be significantly enhanced by conducting a more comprehensive analysis of firms in other geographies and this can be a natural extension of the current research. Also, the possibility of firms reporting a higher innovation quotient than the actual in the survey also exists although the effect of this is expected to be minimal due to the reasonably large sample size. Although we study the impact that different types of R&D activities in a firm have on the innovation outcome we do not attempt to open the black box that can explain why this happens. A future research agenda may be to conduct in-depth case studies on some of the firms to interpret and understand why intramural R&D activities lead to higher process innovation in a firm. An important determinant of innovation is the organizational culture that is prevalent in a firm. Although this is beyond the scope of the paper, an important extension of this research can be to study whether organizational culture moderates the impact of R&D activities on outcomes of innovation.

## 9. Conclusion

This paper attempts to inquire whether intramural and extramural R&D activities impact process and product innovation in firms and also whether the process and product innovation reinforce each other. In order to test related hypotheses it pursues the method of hierarchical regression analysis of secondary data on R&D activities of European and Chinese firms collected by the World Bank. An important contribution of the paper is that it improves the understanding of innovation processes in economies as different as China and Europe. This paper advances the theory of innovation by conducting econometric analysis on large survey data collected by the World Bank.

By identifying the impact of intramural or extramural R&D activities on product and process innovation in IT firms this paper extends the literature on determinants of innovation in IT firms. It highlights that intramural R&D activities are crucial for process innovation and not product innovation implying that external help is not needed when it comes to improving the processes within a firm. However, the situation for product innovation is somewhat different as intramural as well as extramural R&D activities play a role. The research provides interesting insights into the implications of process innovation on product innovation by showing that continuous process innovation has positive impacts on product innovation. Incrementally better processes provide an ecosystem for innovating new products and services in firms. These insights are likely to benefit the business managers who guide R&D activities at firms. At a general level the work that is presented in this paper significantly enhances the understanding of the impact of managerial decisions and firm R&D activities on innovation outcomes.

## Appendix

## A1.Unsuitability of patent data

A lot of innovation based researchers have used patent data as a proxy for measuring the output of innovation. For IT firms the futility of using patents as a measure of innovation is widely covered in works of various authors who have emphasized primary data collection and interview or questionnaire based analysis for collecting better data on innovation outputs (Watanabe, Tsuji & Griffy-Brown, 2001; Fleming & Sorenson, 2004; Laursen & Salter, 2004). Griliches (2007) also states that patents as a measure of R&D productivity seldom gives statistically coherent outcomes and are relevant only for traditional manufacturing industries. A fundamental issue that crops up with the use of patent data or similar objective data is that patent data symbolizes invention and invention may or may not be an innovation. Inventions characterized by patents may not cover all innovations happening in firms and inventions that are recorded as inventions may not be innovations themselves (Arora et al., 2013). This phenomenon can be best described as “all inventions need not be innovations and all innovations are not patented” (Arora et al., 2013). In view of such rising concerns, most innovation researchers have migrated to national survey based measures to take stock of R&D activities taking place in firms. Such surveys have been standardized in the late 1990s by the OECD Convention on innovation (Oslo Manual, OECD Publishing, 1997).

## A2.World Bank Enterprise Survey<sup>3</sup>

World Bank Enterprise Surveys collect a whole lot of information about a country's business environment and its evolution over time. The full firm level microdata is made available to researchers on demand. Innovation module is implemented as a follow-up to the original

#

questions of Enterprise Survey; it gathers information on four types of innovation: product, process, management and organization, and marketing. This module of questions is administered for research purposes to measure the nature and determinants of innovation in different countries. These surveys follow the globally accepted practices and methods. Innovation questionnaires are based on widely accepted OSLO manual principles. Various researchers globally use this data for their research purposes. Data from the Enterprise Surveys serves as the primary input, complemented with similar firm-level surveys and other relevant data sources.

The Enterprise Survey is conducted by private contractors hired by World Bank<sup>4</sup>. It does not rely on government agency due to the confidential nature of questions involving business and government. The survey is answered by business owners and top managers. The survey samples more than 1200 firms in large economies using stratified random sampling.

As specified above the survey constituted a whole host of questions. We have focused on the innovation module of the survey data to derive the insights for the paper. The data used was the latest available for the geographies studied. Panel analysis is not always possible in all the specified economies as different countries are not surveyed in same periodicity due to local factors.

In the survey to answer the questions regarding the innovative activities, the firms were asked to indicate whether they have indulged in any of the following R&D activities

a. Expenses for new product and services R&D

b. Expenses for process R&D

c. R&D with clients

d. R&D with suppliers

e. Implementation of idea of external source e.g., consultants, academics etc.

Options a and b are combined and treated as intramural R&D activities. Innovation outcomes are also categorized on the basis of responses of the firms to these questions of whether they have introduced the following or not: A. New technological product B. New services C. Additional features for existing products and services D. New managerial processes E. New administrative or quality control processes

Options A, B and C are combined together and treated as product/service innovation because extant literature treats product and service innovation in the same vein. Options D and E are combined together and treated as outcomes for process innovation.
