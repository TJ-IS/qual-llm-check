# Managing stakeholders around inter-organizational systems: A diagnostic approach

Albert Boonstra <sup>\*</sup>, Jan de Vries

University of Groningen, Faculty of Economics & Business, Landleven 7/ P.O. Box 800, 9700 AV Groningen, The Netherlands

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 3 October 2007 Received in revised form 18 April 2008 Accepted 20 April 2008 Available online 3 June 2008

Keywords: Inter-organizational system Stakeholders Interests Power Diagnosis

## a b s t r a c t

Inter-organizational systems (IOS) are generally used in a context of many interested parties. If these parties are not identified and if their power and interests related to the IOS are not explored and taken into consideration, implementation is likely to be disappointing and troublesome. This paper presents a diagnostic approach to the identification of stakeholders around IOS and the assessment of their power and interests with respect to these IOS. This approach is illustrated by means of an in-depth case study. This case study indicates that focusing on power and interest relationships may help make the critical success factors in the implementation and improvement of inter-organizational systems more explicit. The framework explicitly stimulates users to repeat their analysis of the players and their power and interests in order to acquire new ‘pictures’ of the playing field. Given the various positions, these modified pictures then have to be interpreted and used to make new plans of action.

## 1 Introduction

Especially since the rise of the Internet, there has been a clear tendency toward the use of inter-organizational systems (IOS). Many organizations as well as industry boards and government departments are involved in the development and implementation of such systems. The possibilities of IOS are clear: closer links between the value chains of organizations can lead to lower transaction costs and a quicker delivery of goods and services, Also intangible benefits of IOS are considered to be important, including flexibility (Golden and Powell, 2004) and improvement in services and closer partnerships (Bytheway and Dhillon, 1996). Although the potential benefits seem to be attractive, many of the parties involved in the development and use of IOS have experienced that these advantages are not always achieved easily. Although some systems have become success stories, others are not doing so well at all and can even be called failures. Clearly, the implementation of IOS systems is a complicated endeavor, both from a technical point of view and from many other perspectives, including strategic, organizational, political, and cultural viewpoints. Moreover, a large number of stakeholders from different organiza tions are involved in developing and using IOS (Howard et al., 2003, 2006). For this reason, the question of who participates in the analysis, development and implementation of IOS becomes even more difficult, since decisions are no longer only made internally (Pouloudi and Whitley, 1997; Cavaye, 1995; Kumar and Dissel, 1996; Webster, 1995; Ndubisi and Wah 2007; Markus and Steinfield, 2006). Knowledge of implementation barriers and approaches to analyze inter-organizational systems are of great importance to practitioners, such as project managers involved in developing and implement ing IOS or those who have to decide whether or not to participate in IOS proposals

Of course there are methods for stakeholder identification and categorization. These approaches are often from organization and management disciplines, e.g. Eden andAckermann (2004); Bryson (2004); Bryson et al. (2007). They usually focus on specific aspects of stakeholder management, such as the identification of stakeholders (Vos and Achterkamp, 2006), the assessment of their relative importance (Mitchell et al., 1997) or ways to involve these stakeholders (Bryson, 2004). This means that these methods are often diverse in focus and nature and not particularly specified to IOS development and implementation. Within the IS field, Pouloudi and Whitley (1997) suggest a dynamic method for stakeholder identification in IOS contexts. An important contribution made by Coakes and Elliman (1999, 2002) is a proposition how stakeholders can be identified and related to IS contexts. They also suggest an approach to establish different boundaries of the system.

This paper intends to build on this work by presenting a diagnostic framework, focused on assessing potential critical success factors from the power and interest perspectives of the various stakeholders around IOS projects and to illustrate this framework by means of an in-depth case study. The contribution of this paper is that it specifically concentrates on stakeholders within IOS projects and that it combines various theories and approaches into a practical and consistent method. This framework can then be used before the start of the project by those responsible to predict its potential success and the critical factors playing a role in its implementation and improvement, but also during the project to analyze changing circumstances.

## 2. A stakeholder perspective on inter-organizational systems

## 2.1. Literature review

Studies in the field of information systems indicate that the human, social and organizational contexts in which information systems operate, play a central role in understanding the acceptance of information systems (Coakes, 2003; Westbrook et al., 2007). Therefore, establishing the best fit between the technical system (e.g. the technical features of the information systems) and the social system (e.g. social practices and attitudes toward exchanging information) is widely recognized as being one of the main challenges that organizations are confronted with. Since mainstream research on information systems strongly focuses on the participation of participants such as developers, intended users and managers (Ward et al., 2005) this approach is frequently criticized from a socio-technical point of view as being too narrow. Klecun and Comford (2005) for instance, argue that all information system programs are social phenomena that are socially and historically conditioned. Consequently, evaluation and diagnosing tools of information systems should reflect social conditions and focus on uncovering competing interests and power networks (Klecun and Comford, 2005). Additionally, studies have been performed that highlight organizational determinants that influence the speed and level of information technology adoption (Bruque et al., 2004), the social shaping of information technology trajectories (Horton and Wood-Harper, 2006) and the so cio-technical environments of information sharing systems (Lee and Rao, 2007).

Especially in the case of an IOS, the range of the people and parties involved is much broader than the traditional participants like developers, intended users and managers. IOS crosses organizational borders, which means that the stakeholders are more loosely coupled. Identifying these stakeholders and exploring their perspectives in terms of their interests in the system and their power to ‘make or break’ the system are essential steps in establishing a diagnostic framework aimed at assessing the critical factors of an IOS (Coakes and Elliman, 1999, 2002; Pan and Flynn, 2003; Rodon et al., 2007).

Stakeholders can be identified in many different ways (Mitchell et al., 1997). There are different kinds of stakeholders, such as people as well as groups inside and outside an organization. In this research study we apply Freeman’s classical definition of stakeholders to IOS (Freeman, 1984). ‘A stakeholder is any individual, group, organization or institution who can affect or is affected by the inter-organizational system under study.’ There are various approaches to identify stakeholders, which can be regarded as complementary to each other.

The first approach is using frameworks (Mitchell et al., 1997), often in the form of checklists specified to the generic types of stakeholders, such as ‘customers, suppliers, competitors, users, managers, and developers’ (Cummings and Doh, 2000). Such checklists can be adapted to make them more suitable for particular industries, e.g. ‘doctors, patients, pharmacies, pharmaceutical companies’. They can then be used to determine which specific groups of stakeholders are important in relation to the IOS. For example, ‘neurologists, patients with neurological diseases, pharmacy A, Merck’. Often the result is still quite generic but it could serve as a useful starting point.

A second approach is posing questions concerning the IOS and its nature. Relevant questions that help identify relevant groups and individuals in this context are (Pouloudi and Whitley, 1997; Cavaye, 1995):

\- Who are the initiators of the system?

\- Who are the sponsors of the system?

\- Who have to adopt the system and make it work?

\- Who are the intended users?

\- Who will receive the output of the IOS?

\- Who are the intended developers and operators of the IOS?

\- Who will be impacted and affected by the system?

\- Who will win or lose by using the IOS?

Answers to these questions may reveal stakeholders not yet identified by the top-down approach or make it possible to refine certain categories of stakeholders into relevant subgroups.

A third approach that complements the other ones is the network approach. This approach is a very important one in the diagnosis of an IOS, because it focuses on the network of relations among organizations. Analyzing these dynamic networks of interactions and activities may help identify stakeholders and their roles (Walsham, 1993). Network theory also emphasizes the dynamic, flexible and contextual nature of (inter) organizational relations. This implies that stakeholders may influence each other, and therefore they cannot be viewed in isolation. Perceived interests and power of stakeholders are interactive and they may change over time (Pouloudi and Whitley, 1997).

When stakeholders are identified, the results of this analysis can be visualized in a stakeholder map (Newcombe, 2003), also called a stakeholder web (Coakes and Elliman, 1999, 2002). This is a diagram that shows and categorizes stakeholders and their mutual relations. Since stakeholders depend on a specific context that may change in time it is not possible to draft one single stakeholder map.

Stakeholders may often have different perceptions of an IOS and interpret it in different ways (Walsham, 1993). In many cases the perceived interests in the IOS differ among the stakeholders involved. The degree to which parties are interested in an IOS can vary from low to high (Coltman et al., 2001). In the case of a low interest level, the stakeholder may be inclined to believe that the IOS will lead to increasing operational costs as well as a decrease in efficiency due to incompatible internal and external technologies, and that it will offer insufficient support in the control of the primary process. On the other hand, a high degree of interest relates to the perception that an IOS contributes to the overall goals of the company. In this study, we define interest as ‘perceived interest’, which means that a party believes it will benefit from using an IOS and that the benefits will outweigh the costs. The perceived (dis)interest normally includes several elements and it is often based on strategic, organizational, financial, technological and behavioral aspects (Bendoly et al., 2004; Chen, 2003; Coltman et al., 2001; Davis, 1993; Golden and Powell, 2004; Günther and Grote, 2006; Parker et al., 1988; Porter, 2001). These aspects are not of equal relevance to all stakeholders: for instance, intended users have other kinds of interests than business managers (e.g. Lee and Myers, 2004).

Often the various stakeholders do not only have different perceptions of an IOS, but they also differ in terms of power and power relationships. A powerful party with a clear interest in an IOS may apply its power to force less powerful parties to also start using the IOS, independent of their perceived interest in it (Standifera and Wall, 2003). At the same time, if certain parties only have little interest in an IOS it might be rather difficult for parties with a great deal of interest but with a lack of power to implement the IOS successfully. In this paper, we will define power as the capacity to exert one’s will over others in order to realize certain intended benefits (Buchanan and Badham. 20o0) Since power is the capacity to exert one's will, it is possible to indicate the source of this capacity, or in the context of IOS: parties may possess different sources of power to urge others to use (or not to use) an IOS. In this context, it is important to emphasize that power is a relational construct, which means that a particular party may either have a large amount or a small amount of power in relation to another relevant party. Fincham (1992) suggests that we can view the concept of power from three perspectives: processual, institutional and organizational. The basis of processual power lies in the social interaction among interest groups. Institutional structures of inequality form the external components on the basis of which power is mandated within organizations. Organizational power is embedded in the internal structure of organizations (Wilson and Howcroft, 2005). Since our focus is on the impact of IOS on the relations among organizations, we will concentrate on processual and institutional perspectives on power. However, IOS implementation may also have a high influence on organizational processes and structures. This approach corresponds with the work of Hardy (1996) that includes four dimensions of power (resource, process, meaning and system) in a framework.

We can conclude that the current research indicates what stakeholders are and how they can be identified. Apart from their identification, the theory provides approaches to assess the variety of interests that stakeholders may have, for instance with regard to an inter-organizational system. Other researchers offer approaches that shed light on the sources of power that stakeholders may have to advance their interests. However, with regard to enterprise information systems in general and inter-organizational information systems in particular, there are no methods available that combine these theories into a coherent framework that can help implementers of inter-organizational information systems to assess the power and interest positions of the various stakeholders and relate these positions to each other. This research study contributes to the theory and practice by providing such a framework and by applying it to a case history.

## 2.2. A framework to diagnose IOS from a power and interest perspective

The perspectives as explained in the previous section can be combined in a diagnosing framework for assessing the barriers to the development and implementation of an IOS. By linking the dimensions of power and interest, the stakeholders involved in setting up and implementing the IOS can be categorized in a matrix. In this way, the different IOS relationships can be distinguished. IOS relationship A for instance (Fig. 1) shows all characteristics of a balanced IOS. In this situation both parties believe that they can benefit from applying the IOS: both possess an equal amount of power when it comes to its development and implementation, Relationship B on the other hand, can be categorized as an unbalanced JOS, which means that its stakeholders differ in their interest in or power related to the IOS. In this case, one party benefits significantly more from the IOS than the other one does (Rodon et al., 2007)

Fig. 2 summarizes the main stages of diagnosis as well as the key issues to be addressed when applying the framework. The diagnosis starts with a formal agreement on the (proposed) IOS, its aim, the characteristics of the IOS and the planning of the development and implementation process. This is an agreement between those who propose the IOS and those who are responsible for the development and implementation of the system. Then, the stakeholders involved are further identified (phase 1) in accordance with the guidelines as indicated above. The approaches as described in this section can be used to identify the stakeholders and to draft an initial stakeholder map (Coakes and Elliman, 1999, 2002; Newcombe, 2003). In phase 2 the power and interests of each stakeholder are assessed by applying the dimensions and issues addressed in the previous section. Finally, the third phase concentrates on finding the main barriers to the implementation of the IOS or, in case the IOS has already been developed, the operating-barriers. This phase obviously includes an action plan encompassing issues of power relations as well as the interests in and alternatives to changing the IOS relationships.

The diagnosing framework can be applied in different manners. First of all, it appears to be helpful in assessing the potential barriers when setting up an IOS. When exploring the potential benefits of an IOS as well as the barriers to its implementation, an overall assessment of the power and interests of the different parties involved is required. This assessment may show that the framework is beneficial to all stakeholders involved in the early stage of adopting the IOS. However, this framework is not only helpful during the implementation of an IOS. After its implementation, the diagnostic framework can fulfill a monitoring function. Given the dynamic character of IOS relationships and the changes to which they are subject, this monitoring function is also very valuable. Frequently re-assessing the different power and interest characteristics of the IOS parties involved can prevent the IOS situation from becoming dysfunctional. Identifying significant changes in the power and interests of the parties as well as in their perception of these two aspects will contribute to a more successful IOS-application in a dynamic situation. Finally, the framework might be useful as an ex-post evaluation tool. Both from a practical and a theoretical point of view, the evaluation of an IOS can contribute to our understanding of why it has not been successful; for example, why the parties involved failed in establishing an IOS relationship. In other words, the diagnostic tool depicted in Fig. 2 can play a significant role in both the early stage of adopting an IOS system and in its operating stage. Further, when parties are successful in implementing an IOS, the framework can also serve as an evaluation tool.

This diagnosing framework is primarily designed to be applied by those responsible for the successful development, implementation and use of inter-organizational systems, such as project leaders, sponsors, managers, consultants and evaluators. Further, it can also be used by groups, such as steering groups, to monitor the context of the project. If such groups are homogenous, which means that they have similar interests in the IOS, there is a risk of an over optimistic view regarding the perceived desirability and feasibility of the system. If only managers are included, a one-sided rational managerial view can be the result. This effect can be enforced by the negative effects of groupthink (Janis, 1982). We think that the best results can be expected when a small team applies the framework, and when people with various roles and backgrounds, preferably from the various organizations who are involved in the IOS, are consulted to inform the users of the framework. Users of the framework should be aware of the fact that all people who are involved in its use, including themselves, have partial and sometimes temporary views, based on their own interests and power. This means that users of the map should be aware of misinterpretations and one-sided views (Eden and Ackermann, 2004).

## 3. Research methodology

The empirical part of our research is based on an in-depth study of an IOS project that took place during the period 1997– 2005. The case study deals with the introduction as well as the operating phase of a dedicated and complex IOS in the retail sector. Wholesale Corp.<sup>1</sup> is a purchasing organization representing independent retail formulas in the Netherlands. By means of joint purchasing, it is able to achieve substantial financial benefits for its members, e.g. supermarkets and retailers. Wholesale Corp. covers about 20% of the entire food market in the Netherlands and represents 23 affiliated members, including Multi-store Corp. Multi-store Corp. is a medium-sized chain of supermarkets consisting of two distribution centers and 26 stores. The company has a turnover of €200 million and employs about 1500 employees.

The initial research objective of the project was to study the potential benefits of and the barriers to the implementation of an IOS linking Wholesale Corp. to Multi-store Corp. During the early stage of adoption, however, it appeared that political processes played an important role. It was therefore generally expected that the success or failure of the IOS would be closely linked to the interests and power of the JOS parties involved in these processes. For this reason the research obiective of the project was gradually shifted from a feasibility study to a monitoring study, focused on exploring how power and interest relationships interact with each other and how the operation and modifications of the IOS are influenced by these two dimensions. However, this was clearly a ‘how’ question about a contemporary set of events over which the researcher has no control In addition, it had some open and explorative characteristics Consequently, a case-study approach seemed to be the most appropriate choice (Yin, 2003) and we therefore conducted a longitudinal case study in order to compare the different strategies used by the companies to implement and adopt an IOS.

Initial access was negotiated through an IS manager (staff department of Multi-store Corp.) in 1997. In the following year a research assistant visited the company on six occasions, during which time interviews were conducted. On the basis of exploratory interviews a list was prepared containing relevant stakeholders (and typical representatives of each group) who could be approached for participating in the research. The first phase of data collection was an open inquiry into the recent past. A second round of interviews took place between 1998 and 2002 to inquire about the progress of the project. Finally, some interviews were held in 2005 to assess the status of the project and to evaluate its recent developments. Table 1 presents the distribution of the interviews over the various categories of stakeholders during the period 1997–2005. Clearly, many of the interviewees presented in Table 1 were directly involved in the process of setting up and implementing the IOS between Wholesale Corp. and Multi-store Corp. Only the sales manager of Wholesale Corp. and the data manager, purchase manager and financial manager of Multi-store Corp. played a background role and participated only indirectly in the project. During the course of the project also some (potential) users of the IOS system were interviewed. Four retail stores were visited and in total five persons were interviewed twice. The main objective of these interviews was to provide the research team with background information on the perception and attitude of the (potential) users regarding the system being developed. In this way, the researchers verified whether the attitudes and opinions of the project members were in line with those of the potential end-users of Multi-store. From these interviews it was concluded that the (potential) end-users had no direct influence on the decision-making process, taking place on a central level in Multi-store Corp. Moreover, employees working in the four retail shops were not at all consulted by the project members and had no means to influence the decision-making processes. In addition to the interviews with the consultant (cf. Table 1), three other employees of the consultancy bureau were interviewed twice. These interviews were also conducted to provide the researchers with back ground material, which enabled them to verify the information provided.

The duration of these interviews varied from 1 to 2 h during which notes were made that were later transcribed for analysis. By applying a non-directive interview technique the interviewees were able to express and develop their own ideas. All the interviews were conducted under conditions of explicit confidentiality. Furthermore, access was gained to personal and project archives, formal reports and handbooks, minutes of meetings, memos, letters, etc. The data obtained covered the period 1995–2005. Additionally, the research assistant attended some important project meetings in 1998 as well as during the period 2001–2002.

It is important to note here that during the first part of the research, the role of the researchers was solely restricted to making observations to provide the research team with analytical – theory-based – data. The research team, in other words, did not directly interfere in the social and political processes related to the IOS and there was no direct involvement of the researchers in the project. Hence, during 1997–1998 the case study merely concentrated on assessing the feasibility of a tentative framework by describing the power and interests of the stakeholders involved. Based on the information gathered, a first prediction was made whether setting up an IOS would be viable. Clearly. this provisional assessment was not communicated to the project team; it was only conducted in connection with the research objectives of the research team.

During the second phase of the research (1998–2001), the scope of the study slowly shifted toward refining the tentative framework by including the dynamic character of the power and interest relationships between the stakeholders and by addressing their influence on the adoption of the IOS. During this phase, the stakeholders involved in the project influenced the framework only in an indirect way. By gathering more detailed information about the characteristics of the stakeholders, rather abstract constructs like ‘power’ and ‘interest’ could be operationalized into more specific and concrete items as compared to the tentative model composed earlier. Additionally, the interaction among the stakeholders was further operationalized by means of techniques and methods based on a soft systems approach (e.g. Checkland and Scholes, 1999; Flood and Jackson, 1991). Cognitive mapping and rich pictures, for example, were used for disclosing potential problems with respect to the IOS and for structuring cause-effect events. Further, stakeholder maps were constructed to unravel the dynamic interactions among the stakeholders. To avoid any distortion in terms of influencing events, the researchers gathered data only in an indirect way. Moreover, the framework was not presented to the project members until 2001 and no direct discussions took place between the researchers and the project members about the rich pictures and the cause-effect events that were assumed. In doing so, the role of the researchers was restricted to collecting information from multiple sources, while the open interviews mainly aimed at corroborating the written sources and observations made during the project meetings.

Contrary to the first two stages, the final part of the research (2001–2005) included a more active role of the stakeholders in the research. Due to changing circumstances the IOS project did not seem to be very successful, which was reflected by a lacking performance and rather problematic JOS relationships. Therefore, the framework developed during the previous episodes was applied as an evaluation tool aimed at exploring the barriers to the implementation of the IOS. Although not in detail, the framework was presented to the members of the project group in 2001. During successive meetings (potential) barriers were discussed, including the power and interest relations among the main stakeholders involved in the project. During this process the role of the researchers was restricted to structuring the ongoing discussions within the company and providing the project group with analytical material regarding the issues being discussed. In other words, the framework was mainly used by the project group as a tool to assess the rather troublesome implementation of the IOS. The assessment followed the framework by explicitly addressing the three phases depicted in Fig. 1. Clearly, the main objective of this assessment was to overcome the barriers faced by the project group regarding the ongoing IOS adoption process. The assessment process influenced the framework only on a detailed level. For instance, during the project meetings it became clear that the ‘barrier’ concept needed to be operationalized in more detail. On an overall level however, the framework was not affected by the stakeholders and no direct influence of the research context on the framework was observed

Ultimately, the IOS proved to be unsuccessful, which led to the decision to terminate the project. For this reason, the final part of the research focused on analyzing the dominant barriers to its implementation and the reasons why in this particular case the IOS project had been unsuccessful. In doing so, the framework served as a guideline for analyzing case study evidence in order to ‘explain’ the cause of events from a stakeholder perspective.

From a process perspective, the development and implementation of an IOS can be depicted as a set of recursive and overlapping ‘episodes’ that move via initial awareness and the selection of particular ideas, to developing and implementing the IOS. In the next section, these episodes are taken as a starting point in order to show how the dimensions of power and interest may have played a role in the process of shaping the IOS and how the diagnostic framework has been applied in practice.

## 4. Wholesale–Multi-store case

## 4.1. Episode 1: backgrounds of the project and agenda formation

At the end of the 1990s, after a long period of discussions and informal meetings, Wholesale Corp. decided to initiate a feasibility study on the design and implementation of an IOS. It was generally acknowledged by Wholesale Corp. that a substantial competitive advantage could be achieved by linking the purchase process of Wholesale Corp. to its main affiliated members, including Multi-store Corp. Moreover, it was generally believed that the implementation of an IOS would lead to significant cost reductions for Multi-store Corp. because of the complexity of the logistical processes. Although many products were bought by Multi-store Corp. via Wholesale Corp., there was a direct and intense relationship between Multi-store Corp. and the suppliers of both food and non-food products. Almost all products were directly shipped from the suppliers to the stores and distribution centers. As a consequence, Multi-store Corp. directly ordered the products to be delivered from the suppliers, and via Wholesale Corp. only financial information was exchanged. Fig. 3 summarizes the main good flows as well as the information flows among the stakeholders involved in the supply chain.

Starting from the notion that substantial competitive advantages could be gained by designing and implementing an IOS five driving forces triggered the feasibility study conducted in 1997:

\- the exchange of data between Wholesale Corp. and its affiliated members is characterized by high volumes;

\- the food market is under high pressure to reduce costs;

\- many supermarkets and retailers experience decreasing profit margins;

\- supermarkets are confronted with a high competitive market forcing Wholesale Corp. to be more efficient;

\- the aspect of ‘time’ is crucial in the ordering-process of food and retail products.

The feasibility study mainly focused on the technical aspects of the JOS and included issues such as JOS-software the protocols to be used, the necessary technological changes to be made and the costs associated with implementing an overall IOS standard to be applied by Wholesale Corp. and its members. Clearly, during the early stage of adopting the IOS many stakeholders were involved in the project. A project group of senior managers of Multi-store Corp. and Wholesale Corp. representing different business areas (sales, logistics, finance, IT) and some other affiliated members, worked closely together with external consultants. The focus of the project group was on the feasibility of the development and implementation of the IOS as well as on its potential benefits and the main barriers that had to be overcome. At the same time, the interests of the stakeholders involved in the project were evaluated. The group actively gathered information about the financial and technological benefits of the IOS and an assessment was made of the strategic risks to each of the stakeholders. Especially representatives of Multi-store Corp. feared that they would become too dependent on Wholesaler Corp. So during the several project meetings they explicitly addressed this issue. Obviously, the discussions contained some political elements, such as power and dependence, and therefore they were chaired by an external consultant specialized in group processes. At the beginning of 1998, it was concluded that both Wholesaler Corp. and Multi-store Corp. would benefit from the IOS and an agreement was made upon the functionality requirements of the system. The project group also defined an action plan to be carried out by the stakeholders to overcome potential barriers.

## 4.2. Episode 2: development and implementation of the IOS

At the end of 1998, Wholesale Corp. started with the development and implementation of an electronic procurement system that linked the ordering systems of the supermarkets and retailers to a central database. A substantial reduction in transaction costs, a further optimization of the internal processes and increasing service delivery levels were expected to be the main benefits of this IOS. However, its implementation within both Multi-store Corp. and Wholesale Corp. was confronted with numerous problems. Although some of these problems seemed to be of a technical nature, Wholesale Corp. also faced a number of organizational difficulties in its communication with Multi-store Corp. Project meetings were often canceled, information was not provided in time and pilot proiects were often severely delaved. By the end of 20o0, only a few functions of the IOS had been implemented, which were only partly used by Multi-store Corp. At that time both Multi-store Corp. and Wholesale Corp. concluded that the costs of implementing the IOS had largely outreached the benefits. It was therefore decided to reassess the functions of the IOS and to re-design the administrative procedures between Multi-store Corp. and Wholesale Corp. At the same time, however, the implementation and usage of a sophisticated IOS by Wholesale Corp. and some large supermarket chains appeared to be a great success. By means of this IOS these leading three supermarkets were all successfully linked to Wholesale Corp

At the beginning of 2001, the IOS became the subject of a great deal of discussion within Multi-store Corp. and it was concluded that technical barriers as well as a miss-fit of the organizational procedures had prevented both IOS parties to implement the IOS successfully. At this point in time, Multi-store Corp. decided to downsize its project efforts, which re sulted in less frequent project meetings and a cut in the project budgets. At the same time, both parties initiated a study aimed at evaluating the ongoing IOS adoption process. The study followed the framework outlined in the previous sections and concentrated on making an in-depth analysis of the power and interests of the stakeholders involved in the project.

## 4.3. Episode 3: identifying power and interests

Starting from the model depicted in Fig. 1, the analysis shows that initially both parties possessed important sources of power. At the start of the project, the interdependence between Wholesale Corp. and Multi-store Corp. was strongly related to the company goals of Wholesale Corp. When buying products from suppliers, Wholesale Corp. was expected to represent the interests of its affiliated members, including those of Multi-store Corp. As a consequence, Wholesale Corp. strongly favored the development and implementation of an IOS, which was reinforced and stimulated by the informal strategic alliance between Wholesale Corp. and some large supermarket chains. The ability to develop and maintain a complex and sophisticated IOS as well as Wholesale Corp.’s control over an influential IOS alliance with some large chains of supermarkets can be considered as the main sources of power of Wholesale Corp. Multi-store Corp. seemed to have little power to influ ence the IOS at the start of the project, since it was one of the smaller affiliated members and did not have a substantial influence on the policies and decision-making processes of Wholesale Corp., which were dominated by the above-mentioned alliance. However, an in-depth analysis of the procurement situation of Multi-store Corp. showed that in 2001 the company had considered to renounce its procurement cooperation with Wholesale Corp. because of some very attractive alternatives Moreover, Multi-store Corp. had informally decided that remaining independent of Wholesale Corp. would be of vital importance to its competitiveness in the long-term.

Regarding the (potential) interests in the IOS, at the start of the project both Wholesale Corp. and Multi-store Corp. were convinced of its benefits. It was expected that the IOS would be helpful in optimizing their internal processes and that it would significantly contribute to a reduction in both companies’ transaction costs. It was therefore concluded that the initial IOS situation for both parties could be categorized as one with high power and interest levels (see Fig. 4). An analysis performed in 2001 showed, however, that during the course of the project Multi-store Corp. was confronted with an increase in costs related to the IOS. At the same time, the technical specifications of the IOS changed in favor of the large supermarkets and the dominant coalition. As a result, Multi-store Corp. was not only confronted with expensive hardware investments but also with a very complicated organizational adoption process. The administrative procedures, for instance, were assumed to be related to scanning data from the stores. To Multi-store Corp., this process was highly complicated because of the relative independent character of the stores and a lack of coordination among their ordering processes. In 2001, Multi-store Corp. was convinced that the benefits of the IOS did not outweigh the costs. Consequently, the IOS situation of Multi-store Corp. as initially assessed, changed from a high power, high interest situation to a high power, low interest situation (see Fig. 4)

## 4.4. Episode 4: overcoming barriers

It is interesting to observe that the IOS relationship between Wholesale Corp. and Multi-store Corp. can only be partly isolated from Wholesale Corp.’s IOS relationship with the other members. Clearly, due to the fact that the IOS played a dominant role in the alliance between Wholesale Corp. and some large supermarket chains the situation changed from a balanced to an unbalanced IOS. During the project, Multi-store Corp. realized that this dominant coalition in fact dictated the technical standards and protocols as well as the organizational context of the IOS. In 2001 a steering committee consisting of line managers and representatives of the board of directors of Multi-store Corp. concluded that

\- designing and establishing an IOS ought to be linked to a long-term relationship with the current suppliers;

\- Multi-store Corp. had to focus on pursuing a differentiating leadership strategy and for this reason the potential benefits of the IOS needed to be re-assessed;

\- the supermarkets forming part of the dominant coalition were a serious threat to Multi-store Corp.

At this point in time, Multi-store Corp. was confronted with two options regarding the shaping of the IOS. Option one was to slowly shift to a ‘shared advantage but different power’ situation. In this case, Multi-store Corp. would be forced to comply with the IOS requirements as defined by the dominant coalition of the procurement partnership, which would probably result in a loss of power. However, Multi-store Corp. opted for becoming more independent of Wholesale Corp. and it therefore decided to accept this disadvantage, be it on a very small scale. The complexity and dynamic nature of the trade off between a reduction in power in favor of an advanced IOS that definitely offered some advantages became even more manifest during the period 2001–2003. After a period of isolation in which the IOS was used on a very small and limited scale, Multi-store Corp. started a strategic redefinition process in 2004. After a four year period (2000–2003) of reviewing the IOS situation there were some signs that Multi-store Corp. was now changing its course toward a long-term IOS relationship with Whole sale Corp.

## 5. Discussion

IOS are often presented as beneficial for all user organizations. The general assumption is that such systems may help to strengthen the business relations among participants. Stronger partnerships are normally associated with benefits such as clearer patterns of supply and demand, just-in-time, lower transaction costs, and closer vertical integration (Cavaye, 1995). However, closer partnerships can also have disadvantages. Organizations might feel overshadowed by their more powerful business partners and they may have to give up part of their independence. They may become dependent on these partners, a dependency that may have existed before the IOS partnership, but that will become stronger, more explicit and more tangible when the organization becomes electronically linked with these more powerful businesses. This explains the low degree of success in the case history described where Multi-store Corp was not willing to become dependent on Wholesale Corp. The diagnostic framework presented in Section 2 can improve our understanding of the less obvious dimensions of the design and implementation of IOS. Moreover, it can be used to gain insight into the dynamic interactions among stakeholders during the development of an IOS. On the basis of this insight IOS projects can be managed more effectively, enabling organizations to plan and evaluate their projects in relation to other stakeholders. In this section, some important issues related to the application of the framework are discussed.

The framework as presented in this paper can be a helpful tool in identifying the most relevant stakeholders involved in an IOS proposal. The guiding questions as well as the other approaches mentioned in the backgrounds section are effective methods to identify this group. Identifying stakeholders is an important activity in relation to a broader goal, which is th management of stakeholders. Promoters of a certain JOS initiative have a clear interest in building an effective coalition of parties that may not have the same interests, but that may find each other through pursuing congruent goals that can be (partially) achieved by the successful implementation of an IOS.

The diagnostic framework acknowledges that IOS partners may have a variety of reasons to support or to resist an IOS initiative. These reasons can be divided in strategic, operational, financial, technological and behavioral motives. However, this categorization is merely meant as a tool to identify all sorts of reasons that may influence the stakeholders’ interpretations. These interpretations determine the attitude toward the IOS. In practice, the reasons are intertwined. In the case history, Wholesale Corp. faced numerous technological barriers when trying to introduce the IOS. However, these technological barriers appeared to be the result of miscommunication and the fact that parties were not prepared to exchange crucial information or change their organizational processes in favor of the IOS to be introduced. The technological problems were actually often used as an alibi and as a means to exert power and influence on the other IOS party. Clearly, the challenge for business analysts is to talk to the interest parties involved, to interpret their opinions and to uncover their real interests.

Stakeholders as well as their interests and power are subject to change over time. This means that due to factors such as changing environments, the progressive nature of the system, and the shifting interpretations and perceptions of the parties involved, IOS development is not static but inherently dynamic. Sometimes these changes are the result of a well-defined and explicit decision-making process. In the case history, Multi-store deliberately moved its position from a high interest, high power situation toward a low interest and high power situation. However, the dynamic character of IOS design and implementation is more often the result of implicit processes. Changing market circumstances, technological evolutions, as well as actions from other parties may influence the interpretations and activities of the different stakeholders. This means that identifving stakeholders and assessing their power and interests should be a repeating process and that current stakeholder maps should be reviewed with respect to changes over time. IOS design and implementation as a dynamic process can be divided into rather straightforward phases like initiation, development, implementation and use. In practice however, new assessments should be made whenever major changes occur. For instance when a new player enters the domain or when major events, external as well as internal, influence the IOS development and implementation process.

The case study illustrates clearly the intertwined nature of the stakeholders’ actions during the design and implementation of the IOS. Stakeholders are not isolated entities with fixed interests and interpretations, but they negotiate and influence each other, which may lead to new perspectives on and new interpretations of the system. Another related issue is that IOS projects are usually not single projects; they form part of a large number of trajectories initiated to support a larger initiative. These trajectories will depend on each other, and have effects throughout the organizations. In managing individual IOS projects, managers have to be aware of their inter-relatedness with other projects. This understanding is important because it stands in contrast with the often linear and rational approaches of system development within the field of IS development. Sources of power and interests are also interrelated and not always easy to categorize. Power and interests may reinforce each other when firms are really determined to achieve a certain result.

The framework as suggested in this paper explicitly stimulates users to repeat their analysis of the players and their power and interests in order to acquire new ‘pictures’ of the playing field. Given the various positions, these modified pictures have to be interpreted and used to make new plans of action. Such an action plan may include information provision, organizing meetings, ‘backstage activities’ and implicit or explicit negotiations with one or more parties. Negotiations of this kind may also lead to adapting the system’s specifications, which in turn may lead to changes in the playing field.

## 6. Conclusions and further research

This paper examined the influence of stakeholders on the design and implementation of IOS. To address this problem, we have presented a framework that can be helpful in diagnosing the barriers to implementing an IOS. In doing so, we concentrated on the power and interests of the stakeholders involved in JOS projects. As can be concluded by the literature study the outcome of projects in the area of IOS is often heavily influenced by the power and interests of different stakeholders However, the literature study also reveals that practical tools and diagnostic frameworks are apparently scarce, while little empirical evidence seems to exist regarding the application of these tools. In this paper, we tried to fill this void by exploring the process of designing and implementing an IOS by means of a longitudinal case study. This case study encompassed a period of more than seven years during which a diagnostic framework was developed and applied. This framework is based on the notion that addressing power and interest relationships may help make the critical success factors in the implementation and improvement of an IOS more explicit. In doing so, this study builds further on the work of Coakes and Elliman (1999, 2002) who present a method that can be used for defining system boundaries and stakeholder identification.

Several conclusions can be drawn from our longitudinal case study. Most importantly, this case study clearly indicates that decisions made during the course of an IOS project are only partly based on a rational, well-defined, straightforward approach. The shaping of an IOS encompasses many different dimensions, such as the organizational setting of the IOS, an informational infrastructure, and technical elements. Clearly, all these areas can be subjected to negotiation behavior among the stakeholders involved in the design and implementation of the IOS. This negotiation behavior however, is often of a dynamic nature. As can be concluded from the case study, the power and interests of the stakeholders involved in an IOS project can change due to altering interpretations and perceptions as well as explicit decision-making processes. In line with this conclusion, our case study also underpins the necessity to reassess the position of the stakeholders involved in an IOS project on a frequent basis. For instance, in a time span of five years the position of Multi store Corp., altered from a high interest, high power position to a low interest, high power situation and back again. Since inter-organizational information systems increasingly affect the interests of the many parties involved, it is essential for management to focus on these parties by identifying them and assessing their interests and their capabilities to influence the design, implementation and use of the system proposed. Especially when enterprise systems are applied to support links outside the organization, it becomes more and more crucial to management to focus periodically on internal as well as external users (Günther and Grote, 2006; Lu et al., 2006).

The method outlined in the previous section can be used before the start of a project and during its course, but also afterwards, to evaluate the project and to explain its degree of success as well as particular problems. This may improve the organization’s learning capabilities when starting new projects. Sufficient insight may also help executives of interested organizations to use their influence to move the project into the desired direction. Using the model to diagnose inter-organizational system initiatives and trying to divide the benefits among the influential parties prior, during and after implementation may prevent failure and disappointment. The method may help managers to be sensitive to their environment and to shift from a one-dimensional, technological and linear approach of system development to a multiple perspective assessment.

The method also acknowledges and addresses the time dimension of stakeholder management. During the design and implementation process stakeholders may come and go, while their interests and power positions may change. The method should therefore be used on a continuous basis to assess and monitor the stakeholders’ positions and interests.

The model may also help practitioners discuss IOS-initiatives in non-technical terms: only if there is a sustainable basis in strategic, economical and social terms, an IOS appears to be viable. From this point of view, the model can help practitioners be vigilant and shift from a narrow, one-dimensional focus to a multiple perspective assessment. Approaching factors such as limitations and constraints from different perspectives may help organizations affect these restrictions and stimulate more favorable conditions for the successful deployment of an IOS.

Clearly. further research is required to understand in more detail what specific interests parties may have in JOS-use and how these interests should be assessed and measured. Research into IOS in different industries may uncover different kinds of interests related to such systems. We assume that the interests of e.g. manufacturing, health care and government agencies in boundary spanning systems may differ to a large extent. A more profound understanding of the stakeholders’ interests may result in better systems that are also more focused. Further, more detailed research into sources of power in relation to IOS may also improve the insight into the success and failure of these systems. Such insights may help organizations develop and use their power more effectively.
