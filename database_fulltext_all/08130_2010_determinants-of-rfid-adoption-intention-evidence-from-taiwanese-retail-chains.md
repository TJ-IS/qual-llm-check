## A B S T R A C T

Our study identified important factors affecting industrial RFID adoption intention using statistical analysis. Because of its inter-organizational nature, the study extended the theory of diffusion of innovation and developed an analytical model correlating relative advantage, complexity, organizational readiness, and supply chain integration with adoption intention. Retail chains in Taiwan were chosen for an empirical survey; 134 valid data sets were obtained and structural equation modeling was used to analyze our model. The results indicated that the four constructs were all significant in affecting RFID adoption, and supply chain integration produced an effect similar to that of conventional innovation. An indirect effect of it on organizational readiness was also found to be significant.

## 1. Introduction

RFIDs have become increasingly important in industry, the research community, and the media. They improve activities in the supply chain, to allowing greater control and efficiency. A wide range of benefits is claimed, including tighter control and management of the supply chain and of inventory management, creating cost savings, improvements in customer service, reductions in shrinkage, and clearer targeting of customers and tracking of their purchasing behavior [26]. Adoption of RFID technology is hindered by many technical and business challenges, including standardization, patent, cost/finance, infrastructure, and barcode issues [4]. Behavior intention is viewed as an important indicator of whether potential customers will adopt a new technology. However, researchers have yet to use a model of behavioral intention to analyze industrial behavior in adopting RFID technology. Statistical evidence is also lacking.

The theory of diffusion of innovation (DOI) offers a robust perspective for analyzing organizational adoption [10] of technology. In DOI, characteristics that affect innovation may include relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, trialability, and observability. Among these, relative advantage and complexity are more important in predicting user intention to use a technology. Also, research has found that environmental and organizational characteristics may affect technology innovation and bear on adoption decisions. In today’s business environment, this decision is strongly associated with the behavior of its upstream or downstream business partners. An increasing number of companies have found that developing long-term coordination and cooperation can improve supply chain efficiency [12]. Thus, IT is increasingly seen as inter-organizational and integrated throughout the supply chain. This implies that a study of supply chain and firm-specific factors is needed in assessing technology adoption [22]; however, research using DOI theory has seldom been considered.

The purpose of our study was to develop an extended DOI and use it to investigate the effects of innovation, organization, and supply chain integration on RFID adoption intention. Retail chains in Taiwan were chosen for our empirical study.

Channel power has shifted from manufacturers to retailers over time [23], and retail chains have become more important because of its collective power of suppliers over procurement, distribution, and marketing practices. In Taiwan, retail chains rely heavily on IT for business innovation and are able to spend large sums on IT. With both the government and local RFID manufacturers promoting technology, retail chains in Taiwan have become a prime candidate for RFID implementation. However, the study of retailer IT adoption has been limited, since much of it is proprietary or difficult to obtain. Our study therefore searched for statistical evidence from the retail chain industry on the downstream side of the supply chain that has been little explored, particularly in developing countries [5].

## 2. Analytical model

Diffusion has been defined as the process by which an innovation is communicated over time among the members of a social system. An innovation is an idea, practice, or object that is perceived as new by an individual or group. DOI models the innovation decision process, the determining factors of adoption intention, and different categories of adopters [7]. It helps in predicting the likelihood and adoption intention of an innovation.

Based on a broad range of research literature, we identified four dependent variables affecting user adoption intention of RFID; they included relative advantage, complexity, supply chain integration, and organizational readiness. To identify the measurement items, we held a focus-group meeting, consisting of six professionals: two RFID developers, two retailer practitioners, and two academics from related fields. This group bridged the full domain of IS design, manufacturing, and usage. The experts were asked to write down the important variables from their own perspective, based on the references that had been provided. After discussion, the group members were asked to reach a consensus: 18 measurement items associated with four constructs resulted. They are shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.1. Innovation characteristic

Previous studies have suggested that relative advantage (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than previous idea) and complexity (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use) are the best predictors when evaluating the benefits and challenges of technology innovation. The effects of relative advantages and complexity on behavioral intention to use were examined in hypotheses $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ Relative advantage has a positive effect on adoption intention.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ Complexity has a negative effect on adoption intention.

Based on previous RFID studies, the expected advantages of RFID adoption may be classified into: (1) increasing cost efficiency (through improved asset visibility reducing stock loss and saving human assets) (termed $R _ { 1 } )$ [3]; (2) improving inventory replenishment (through better tracking of products) (termed $R _ { 2 } ) ;$ (3) consolidating marketing strategy (by formulating the a better marketing strategy and increasing customer service) (termed $R _ { 3 } )$ [21,11]; and (4) increasing product security (by increasing the accuracy of the information on the movement of physical goods) (termed R ) [19].

Complexity challenges may include: (1) a lack of customized solutions for RFID system and their incompatibility with existing IT systems (termed $C _ { 1 } )$ [18]; (2) little or no harmonization of interfaces due to the lack of unified standards for RFID (termed C ) [25]; (3) large investment and maintenance costs of establishing RFID and related IS (termed $C _ { 3 } ) ;$ (4) poor tracking reliability due to poor reception of RFID radio waves absorbed or distorted by moisture or metals (termed $C _ { 4 } ) ;$ and (5) reduced business security because company supply chain flows can be tracked by monitoring the RFID equipment (termed C ) [17].

## 2.2. Supply chain integration

Environmental characteristics play a role by affecting technological innovation since they are interdependent. One aspect is the effect of the integration of supply chain members from outside organizations. Cooperation and coordination between supply members has received considerable attention from researchers [6,20]. RFID may be regarded as inter-organizational IS (IOS). A key emphasis of IOS is to improve supply chain operation through electronic integration [9]. Firms are increasingly using such systems to enhance supply chain visibility and improve coordination between members. RFID utility increases only if supply chain members use it. Hence, the greater the collaboration, the greater the synergy created by the new technology. As with environmental characteristics [16], supply chain integration may affect the adoption of technology innovation. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ Supply chain integration has a positive effect on adoption intention.

Depending on the degree to which supply chain members are integrated, four items were identified: (1) consistency of the IT platform (termed $S _ { 1 } )$ , supply chain members should possess compatible IT to communicate; (2) information sharing through an online data access system to communicate financial and sales information (termed $S _ { 2 } )$ [15]; (3) collaborative planning (termed $S _ { 3 } ) ;$ and (4) integration of operations (termed S ).

## 2.3. Organizational readiness

Organizational characteristics have been found to be important in IT adoption. Models of the adoption of RFID must account for the impact of RFID systems in a variety of industrial situations. The readiness of retail chains may affect the system’s installation. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Organizational readiness has a positive effect on adoption intention

The readiness of a retail chain can be described by using five key elements: (1) top management support (termed $O _ { 1 } ) ;$ (2) chain scale related to the slack in resources for adoption (termed $O _ { 2 } ) ;$ (3) chain IT expertise and ability to operate RFID (termed $O _ { 3 } ) ;$ (4) chain culture, the attitude in the company toward the new technology (termed $O _ { 4 } )$ [13]; and (5) championing the firm’s image, the firm’s ambition to enhance its image status (termed $O _ { 5 } )$

Finally, retailers’ suppliers and chain stores may deploy strategies or develop capabilities to support integrating arrangements of the retail chain [1]. They must adapt to these changes, modifying behavior to meet both the internal and external demands of the retail chains. Therefore, organizational readiness may affect supply chain integration. The greater the intention to integrate the supply chain, the more likely the retail chain will be ready to adopt the IOS. However, this indirect effect has yet to be empirically examined. Accordingly, hypothesis H examines the effect of supply chain integration on organizational readiness.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ Supply chain integration has a positive effect on organizational readiness.

## 3. Data collection

## 3.1. RFID development in Taiwan

Over the past two decades, the IT industry has become a mainstream of Taiwan’s economic development. Retail chains are highly influential through their collective power over procurement, distribution, and marketing practices. As a result, they were considered prime candidates for RFID adoption in Taiwan.

## 3.2. Survey profile

In 2006, Taiwan had 1187 retail chains, of which 517 were involved in physical flow operations for merchandise retailers; these chains involve both 90 general stores (such as supermarkets or convenience stores selling general merchandise) and 427 specialty stores (such as grocers, apparel shops, pharmacies, boutiques, bookstores, etc.). RFIDs at specialty store chains were easier to use. Therefore, we targeted specialty store chains for data collection using a systematic sampling method.

An 18 statement questionnaire was developed to collect the sample’s perception of the importance of our four constructs. Items used in the questionnaire to measure them were adapted from prior research with wording changes to make them appropriate for an RFID adoption study. A pilot test was carried out by 10 managers from 3C and pharmacy retail chains to confirm that our factors were meaningful and to modify any statements when necessary. All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), as shown in Appendix A.

Our survey was conducted over a period of 14 months (details are shown in Table 1). To ensure the reliability of respondents, only retailers that had participated in RFID relevant activities were chosen. Data from 140 chains were collected from a population of 427, with 134 remaining (31.4%) after the removal of invalid questionnaires. Only 2% of the retail chains surveyed had conducted an RFID test plan, the rest had either been involved in RFID-related activities, such as participating in RFID conferences and workshops, or had been contacted by the RFID industrial alliance as potential users. Personal (face-to-face or telephone) interviews were conducted to ensure understanding of the questions asked, together with carefully selected respondents to ensure credibility of the results. 51% of respondents surveyed were division managers or above, with an average tenure in the retailing industry of 8.4 years.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Reliability and validity tests

Table 2 shows outputs of our reliability and validity tests. SPSS 12 was used for the analysis of construct item reliability. As indicated by the Cronbach a coefficients, all constructs exhibited high composite reliability. Measurement items that failed to contribute were eliminated, and manifests were stepwise modified to improve the fit indices. As a result, $C _ { 4 } , C _ { 5 }$ and $O _ { 2 }$ were eliminated, with 15 items retained for the four constructs of interest.

Content validity defines how representative and comprehensive the items were in representing the hypothesis. Our definitions of relative advantage, complexity, supply chain integration, and organizational readiness were based on the review of the innovation theory and research on RFID adoption. A focus-group style meeting, consisting of 6 professionals, was carried out to ensure face validity. The measurement items were scrutinized to measure and cover constructs domains.

For testing unidimensionality, a measurement model was constructed using the four latent factors. Each item was restricted to load on one specific latent variable only, and one latent variable per indicator was allowed (e.g., a perceived quality item may be related to the perceived quality factor but not to any other). While using maximum-likelihood method, a complete standardized solution through LISREL 8.51 showed all 15 items to load highly on their corresponding factors, provided strong empirical evidence of construct independence and validity of the construct. Also, factor loadings greater than 0.3 and t-values for the loadings ranging from 5.0 to 10.4, indicated adequate convergent validity. The overall fit statistics of the measurement model $( \chi ^ { \bar { 2 } } /$ $\mathrm { d f } = 1 0 4 . 3 2 / 8 4 = 1 . 2 4$ , satisfying the test condition of less than 3) were: Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) and Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI) 0.90 and 0.86 respectively; and comparative goodness-of-fit indexes composed of Normed Fit Index (NFI), Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Incremental Fit Index (IFI) were 0.80, 0.92, 0.94, and 0.94 respectively, indicating that the model fit the data. Furthermore, a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) of 0.043 lower than the acceptable value of 0.08, and a Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) of 0.067 further indicated good model fit.

## 4.2. Path analysis

Structural equation model (SEM) was used to estimate parameters of the structural model, and the complete standardized solutions were computed using the LISREL 8.51 maximumlikelihood method. Goodness-of-fit statistics indicated the overall structural model to be acceptable: $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 1 2 6 . 9 2 / 1 1 1 = 1 . 1 4$ RMSEA = 0.033, SRMR = 0.075, GFI = 0.90, AGFI = 0.86, NFI = 0.91, NNFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.99 and IFI = 0.99. All path coefficients were measured to be significant $( p \mathrm { - v a l u e } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The p-value of the estimates for hypothesis testing was determined through the use of one-tailed t-tests. Because of the directional hypotheses, the tvalue rule of 0.05, 0.01 and 0.001 significance level was used as the critical value at 1.96, 2.58, and 3.29. In most cases, the effect sizes, signs, and significance of the estimates were consistent with the results of the measurement model, as well as the intercorrelation among the constructs. ## 5. Conclusions

## 5.1. Findings and managerial implications

RFID is regarded as an inter-organization information system (IOS) that provides electronic infrastructure for sharing between organizations. However, the effect of the inter-organizational relationships was absent from the diffusion model that identifies the relationship between perceived innovation attributes and adoption intention. Our empirical study extended the DOI model and confirmed the supply chain effect on adopting an IOS. The four constructs identified were relative advantage, complexity, supply chain integration, and organizational readiness, all of which significantly affected the RFID adoption intention. Among these, supply chain integration and organizational readiness experienced a similar effect to that of conventional innovation characteristics. Further, we found a noteworthy indirect effect of supply chain integration on organizational readiness. Statistical results indicated that the inclusion of both direct and indirect effects of supply chain integration in models improved the model fit and enabled better understanding of user adoption.

In line with previous conceptualizations of DOI [2], both relative advantage and complexity were found to affect RFID adoption intention. Potential retail chain users have recognized these as important elements for technology innovation. These results corresponded to those found in previous research on using an IOS to improve internal/inter-organizational efficiency, increasing agility and responsiveness to environment changes and creating business value. However, the effect of complexity was relatively significant in our case, as opposed to [14]. This may be attributed to retailers’ conservativeness towards the use of RFID. In addition to two identified conventional innovation factors, supply chain integration and organizational readiness were found to affect RFID adoption intention. Compared with other retail sectors, retail chains have had more experience in supply chain integration as

RFID may be used to exert control over the chain’s upstream suppliers and downstream stores, as well as enabling information exchange and facilitating cooperation and collaboration [24]. Further, supply chain integration had a heavy effect on organizational readiness. When a retail chain intended to integrate or coordinate its supply chain members, it influenced other organizations through its early adoption of IT. Such institutional forces on outside organizations in the spreading innovations such as RFID are worth noting.

To explore the managerial implications, we analyzed factor loading manifests of four identified constructs, indicated in Table 3. The empirical evidence may provide guidance for RFID developers and retail chains in analyzing their marketing strategies and the use of emerging IT.

Relative advantage plays a role in enhancing the rate of innovation adoption. Among those four advantages identified, increasing cost efficiency and improving inventory replenishment were found to be most advantageous. In response to severe price competition, the retail chains in Taiwan are predominantly concerned with reducing distribution costs. Efficient costs and effective inventory replenishment were therefore considered to be the best way of using RFIDs. Adoption of RFID technology helped retailers to track and control stock in real-time. When integrated with backend IT systems, RFID can reduce lead time and, improve replenishment efficiency, thus reduce product misplacement, stock level, out-of-stock, and labor costs. Also, product security was another advantage; theft losses are of serious concern in Taiwan, with an estimated annual loss of over US\$3.7 billion. Retailers that employed RFID technology can use it to provide greater security.

Three factors of complexity were found to be significant. The challenges of customization and high costs were the most significant elements of complexity. Few key complexities in the RFID adoptions were found. First, the RFID systems must be customized for a specific working environment and application purpose. For instance, RFID hardware must withstand high temperatures and humidity in Taiwan, at the same time, operate in a variety of work environments and product materials. Therefore, the interference between the RFID backend system and existing IT systems must be adjusted for better data transmission. However, few companies in Taiwan can provide the required consulting services and customized solutions. Second, high investment/maintenance costs are other aspects of RFID complexity. Retail chains in Taiwan invest heavily in IT systems. However, costs related to the RFID operation such as tags, readers, and IT infrastructures, must be shared by the retailer and its suppliers. The willingness of supply chain members to pay for modification to their IT system can be a problem for retailers.

This is worsened by the absence of RFID standards. As in other regions, there are two major organizations working to develop international standards for RFID technology in Taiwan, EPC global and the International Standards Organization (ISO). However, these two interfaces have yet to be harmonized in the retailing industry. As a result, different standards require different tags and reader devices. This further increases the risk to retailers in choosing and investing in a technology that may be invalid in future. In contrast, the perceived effect of poor system reliability and security were minor. Further, because information is already regularly exchanged among chain stores and suppliers, business security was not a significant concern.

Supply chain integration and organizational readiness have a similar effect on user adoption. The effect of vertical integration among the supply chain members in optimizing the system is greater in the retail industry. Collaborative planning was a most significant purpose for retailers when considering RFID, followed by the use of a consistent IT platform and integration of operational support. This indicated that retailers sought deeper supply chain integration. Conversely, information sharing, already extensively practiced, was seen as less important.

Top management support was shown to be vital. Organizational culture may have a normative influence on the intra- and interorganizational behavior of the retail chains. A championing image indicates the ambition of retail chains to enhance their business image by being pilot adopters of innovative technology. The image of novelty produces a mimetic influence and is itself a business asset. These results may help justify the indirect effect of supply chain integration on organizational readiness for IOS adoption. However, IT expertise was not a serious concern of the retailers in our survey, as IT had already been used in retail chains and the staff already possessed a proactive attitude toward new technology. Lastly, even though organizational scale has been found to be important for innovation in previous studies [8], it was not found to be significant in our study.

## 5.2. Limitations

There were several limitations in our study. First, our results reflected only Taiwanese perspectives; different cultural contexts and environmental settings may generate different results. The response rate of 31% may also have affected the generalization of our findings and the depth of our statistical analysis. Second, RFID technology is still maturing and the industry is relative new. Third, retail chains may have included the RFID investment as expenses, such as the cost of tags, which may have been assumed to be the responsibility of the suppliers.

## Appendix A

1. Relative advantages:

1.1 RFID may increase cost efficiency, e.g. through improved asset visibility that reduces stock loss and saves human assets.

1.2 RFID may increase inventory replenishment, e.g. through better tracking of products.

1.3 RFID may consolidate competitive marketing strategy, e.g. by gaining the capability to formulate more competitive marketing strategy and increase customer service.

1.4 RFID may increase product security, e.g. through increasing the accuracy of the information on the movements of the physical goods that are visibly recorded.

2. Complexity:

2.1 RFID may encounter customization and compatibility challenge, e.g. due to a lack of customized solutions for RFID system or incompatibility with existing IT systems.

2.2 RFID may encounter little or no harmonization between standards, e.g. due to the lack of unified standards for RFID that may increase the complexity of application or operation.

2.3 RFID may require large costs, e.g. investment and maintenance costs associated with establishing RFID systems and related information systems.

2.4 RFID may encounter poor tracking reliability, e.g. due to poor reception of RFID radio waves absorbed or distorted by moisture or metals.

2.5 RFID may reduce business security, e.g. because company supply chain flows can be tracked by outsiders.

3. Supply chain integration:

3.1 We may use RFID to build a consistent IT platform in which supply chain members possess certain information technologies in order to communicate with the parent firm through supply chain members.

3.2 We may use RFID for information sharing through an online data access system to communicate financial and sales information through the supply chain members.

3.3 We may use RFID for collaboration planning through supply chain members.

3.4 We may use RFID for operations integration through supply chain members.

4. Organizational readiness:

4.1 Top management support is important for RFID operation.

4.2 Chain scale related to the slack in resources is important for implementing RFID.

4.3 IT expertise related to the ability to operate RFID is important.

4.4 Chain culture relevant to the attitude in the company toward a new technology is important for RFID implementation.

4.5 Championing image related to the ambition of the chain to enhance business Image status is important for RFID implementation.
