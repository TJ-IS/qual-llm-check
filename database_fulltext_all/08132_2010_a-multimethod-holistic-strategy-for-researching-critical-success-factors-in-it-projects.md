# A multi-method, holistic strategy for researching critical success factors in IT projects

Ulrich Remus\* & Martin Wiener<sup>†</sup>

\*AFIS Department, College of Business and Economics, University of Canterbury, Private Bag 4800, Christchurch 8020, New Zealand, email: Ulrich.Remus@canterbury.ac.nz, and <sup>†</sup>boardeleven Management Consultants, Munich, Germany, email: mail@martin-wiener.de

Abstract. In the field of information systems (IS) there is an observable trend towards the use of multi-method research. Using different research methods allows for the cross-validation of data obtained via multiple approaches, with the potential to increase the robustness of research results. Such a multi-method approach is applicable to a comprehensive research agenda on critical success factors, an agenda that needs to take into account not only the identification, but also the analysis and management of critical success factors. The goal of this article is to contribute new knowledge on how to carry out research on critical success factors in IS projects using a multi-method approach. For this purpose, two research projects are presented, each a variation of the research design customized to particular circumstances. First, there is an outline of the research approach taken for a critical success factor research project in the field of portal implementation, with discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of the project. Taking into consideration these experiences, the research approach of a similar critical success factor research project in the field of offshore software development is then described. Finally, recommendations for using the multi-method research approach in critical success factor research are presented.

Keywords: critical success factor, multi-method research, offshore software devel opment, enterprise portal, management of information systems (IS) projects

## INTRODUCTION

According to Krcmar et al. (2004), research on critical success factors (CSFs) is comprised of the identification and examination of influencing factors that explain the success of a company or a project. However, CSF research in practice is often limited to the identification of such factors (Kuang et al., 2001). Only a few researchers also examine the relevance of these factors in general (e.g. Esteves & Pastor, 2000) or across different implementation stages (e.g.

Somers & Nelson, 2001). Almost no research has been conducted using a more holistic approach that considers altogether the identification of CSFs, the analysis of CSFs in differen project contexts and the management of these CSFs.

When one looks at the research methods used in CSF research, it is apparent that researchers have implemented quantitative as well as qualitative research approaches, and have used a variety of methods to do so (Esteves, 2004), including action research (e.g. Jenkins et al., 1999), case study research (e.g. Sumner, 1999; Caldeira & Ward, 2002), (focus) group interviewing (e.g. McPherson & Nunes, 2006), literature reviews (e.g. Esteves & Pastor, 2004) and structured interviewing (e.g. Van Bullen, 1986). Each of these methods has its respective strengths and weaknesses. Group interviewing, for example, can be a comparatively efficient strategy, exploiting group synergy and therefore taking significantly less time than some other approaches, whereas the Delphi technique seems to be an ideal technique when little information about CSFs is available (Khandelwal & Ferguson, 1999).

In order to overcome the limitations of each method, and ‘as a pragmatic way of using the strengths of both approaches’, the mixed methodology has become more popular (Tashakkor & Teddlie, 2003). A survey of IS journals revealed that approximately 20% of the published papers in these journals have used a combination of methods (Mingers, 2003). Here, qualitative and quantitative research methods need not be seen as mutually exclusive (Van Maanen, 1979). Rather, the application of different methods for data collection provides a wider range of coverage, one that may result in a deeper understanding of the unit under study (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988). In addition, by using multiple research methods, the cross-validation of data obtained by the different methods (method triangulation) can increase the trustworthiness of the research results (Creswell, 2003). This is particularly important for a comprehensive CSF research agenda that takes into account the identification, the analysis and the management of CSFs.

Altogether, increasing attention to the relevance and the management of CSFs, together with the advantages of using a multi-method research approach, creates a need for guidelines regarding what steps and what methods are to be used within a multi-method CSF research agenda. Nevertheless, there is a shortage of examples of multi-method application (Mingers, 2001), a shortage especially apparent in the field of CSF research, although initial steps have been made by Esteves & Pastor (2004), who explored the use of multi-method research in CSF research that examined CSFs for Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)-implementation projects.

The goal of this article is to extend this work by further exploring the use of the multi-method research approach for investigating the CSFs in IS projects. In particular, we want to reflect critically on its application for similar research projects in the future and show how the multi-method approach can be customized in order to meet specific needs of different CSF research settings. Similar to Kaplan & Duchon (1988), the article has a methodological focus, as it describes the process of the research and omits all but sketches of the data and analysis necessary to understand how the research evolved.

We report on two research projects that investigated CSFs for IS projects. Both projects were carried out in newly emerging fields, were structured into several project phases and pursued similar research goals by considering all stages of CSF research. The first project investigated CSFs for portal implementation projects from the perspective of portal integrators, whereas the second project examined CSFs for offshore software development projects, primarily from the client’s point of view, but also taking into account perspectives from other stakeholders. The second project profited substantially from the experiences gained in carrying out the first project, thereby improving the trustworthiness of the research.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

According to a study by Esteves (2004), the CSF approach has been established and popularized over the last 30 years by a number of researchers, beginning with Rockart (1979). Today the approach is increasingly used by consultants and IS departments as a means of support to IS strategic planning (Esteves, 2004). Ramaprasad & Williams (1998) underlined this position by stating that ‘there is a great deal of attention devoted to the concept in the IS literature as many argue that the use of CSF can have a major impact on the design, development, and implementation of IS’. In particular, CSF studies are valuable for making sense of problems where many potential factors influence the outcome, and where the researcher hopes to make a set of practical recommendations based on the most influential factors (Lam, 2005). CSF research is also regarded as bridging the gap between rigour and relevance. By conducting performance analyses in which sophisticated quantitative methods are applied, researchers can demonstrate academic rigour. Similarly, the identification of CSFs would have relevant consequences for practice (for the current rigour vs. relevance debate, see Kieser & Nicolai, 2005).

However, there is ongoing and controversial discussion of the practical contribution of CSF research, in particular with regard to quantitative CSF research based on performance as a dependent variable (March & Sutton, 1997; Nicolai & Kieser, 2002). One key problem is the overselling of CSFs as ‘objective’ knowledge that forms a directly applicable problem-solving technology. However, the identified CSFs do not claim to be purely instrumental, causal o objective. Rather, they are very conceptual, in that CSF research becomes particularly relevant in unfolding a dialogue between research and practice that adds new perspectives and generates different courses of action.

As Monod & Boland (2007) critically state: ‘Relying on causality and objectivity, two concepts challenged by contemporary physics, we leave ourselves little hope of making Social and Human Science progress’. Instead, ‘we can only understand knowledge if we consider it as situated, embodied and linked to experience in the Life World, to culture, and to power’ (p. 139, see also Hassell, 2007). The latter focus on situating, embodying and linking knowledge is especially true for understanding CSFs, with their multiple dimensions, characteristics and perspectives. Essentially, success factors need to be analyzed according to situational and contextual factors. Consequently, management implications based on the analysis of CSFs can only be suggestions, rather than normative, objective guidelines. This interpretive viewpoint assumes that our knowledge of reality is gained only through social constructions such as language, consciousness, shared meanings, documents, tools and other artefacts (Klein & Myers, 1999). Considering this within a multi-method approach would add a broader perspective than the previously dominant positivist research on CSFs, thus mutually enhancing the research findings.

Overall then, an holistic, multi-method approach to CSF research emphasizes both objective and subjective points of view, recognizing that CSFs cannot be isolated from their research context nor necessarily applied directly to practice. In this sense we see this CSF research agenda as pluralistic, taking into account both the positivist and the interpretivist paradigms. As Mingers (2001) points out: ‘Critical pluralism can encompass multiparadigm research combinations’ (p. 248) and allows the consideration of more than one paradigm by adopting ‘a critica stance towards the necessity and validity of current social arrangements’ (p. 248). It hereby criticises the idea of paradigm incommensurability based on the objective–subjective duality (see also section ‘A multi-method design for CSF research’).

## CSF definition

Several definitions of CSFs exist in the literature, with the key term ‘success’ often vague and/or difficult to define. For example, Dibbern et al. (2004) refer to satisfaction, expectations and their realization, as well as to performance as ‘types of variances in outcome’ or success respectively. Several circumstances make it difficult to define and measure success. An investigation of ERP projects by Markus et al. (2000) revealed that success depends on the point of view from which it is measured. Often, members of the implementation team define success in terms of completing the project plan on time and budget, whereas adopters and users rather look at a smooth transition to stable operation with the new systems and achieving business improvements. Success is also dependent on when it is measured. There are examples of projects that were successful in the short run but became unsuccessful in the long term. Of course, different success metrics are used to measure success in different points of time. For an overview of metrics at different stages in ERP adoption see Markus et al. (2000).

The term ‘critical success factor’ initially appeared in management literature, where the focus was on industry-related CSFs (Daniel, 1961). Anthony et al. (1972) went a step further by emphasizing the need to tailor CSFs to both a company’s particular strategic objectives and to its particular managers. Combining these two perspectives, Rockart (1979) described a study of three organizations that confirmed that organizations in the same industry may exhibi different CSFs as a result of differences in geographic location and strategies (among other factors). Nevertheless, Rockart was also able to identify analogies between the CSF lists, suggesting that these are the all-encompassing industry-based factors. The remaining factors appear to have been generated by differences in environmental situation, temporal factors, geographic location, or strategic situation (Rockart, 1979, p. 87). For him, CSFs are ‘the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will insure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where things must go right for the business to flourish’ (p. 85). The emphasis is on a limited number of CSFs – the critica ones – on which ‘management is able to provide a constant focus until they are successfully achieved’ (Khandelwal & Ferguson, 1999).

In a similar fashion, Leidecker & Bruno (1984) defines CSFs as ‘those characteristics, conditions or variables that, when properly sustained, maintained, or managed, can have a significant impact on the success of a firm competing in particular industry’ (p. 24), while Pinto & Slevin (1987) regards CSFs as factors that could significantly improve project implementation chances. However, both these definitions avoid addressing Rockart’s concept, which stresses the importance of identifying an optimum match between environmental conditions and business characteristics for a particular company (Esteves, 2004). Here the CSF concept is seen as a tool to implement business strategies considering both the primary industry related and also the company-specific factors. It should be noted that current CSF research also considers the perception of CSFs by different stakeholders (Sedera et al., 2004), e.g. emphasizing that a group of managers in a particular role, but belonging to different organizations, may identify the same CSFs (Khandelwal & Ferguson, 1999).

Today, Rockart’s CSF approach is particularly relevant within the limits of project management and IS implementation. This is confirmed in a study conducted by Ramaprasad & Williams (1998), in which the results from 263 responses indicate the major areas in which the CSF approach is utilized: project management (63.49%), IS implementation (49.21%), and requirements gathering (47.62%).

## CSF research

Whereas in some areas of IS, research into CSFs has already achieved significant prominence, e.g. in ERP projects (Clemons, 1998; Holland & Light, 1999; Stefanou 1999; Sumner 1999; Scheer & Habermann 2000; Kuang et al., 2001; Somers & Nelson 2001; Nah Fui-Hoon et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2003; Esteves, 2004) and in IS projects in general (e.g. Reel, 1999; Ryan, 1999; Kendra & Taplin, 2004; Karlsen et al., 2005), there are areas in which CSF research can still be described as preliminary and still limited. This is particularly true for particular types of information system under study (e.g. enterprise portal vs. ERP system), different contexts (e.g. general CSFs vs. country-specific CSFs), different stages (general CSFs vs. stage-specific CSFs) or different research agendas (identification of CSFs vs. relevance of CSFs).

In general, CSF research projects can be categorized according to four research phases (Esteves, 2004):

1 State of the art: The purpose of this phase is to provide a substantiated basis for the examination of CSFs. Depending on the already existing literature, various research methods can be applied, such as an extensive literature survey or a qualitative approach, e.g. using grounded theory.

2 Identification: The purpose of this phase is to identify and define CSFs. Often a CSF model is created by classifying the identified CSFs into different dimensions. Surveys are commonly used for identifying CSFs.

3 Relevance: The purpose of this phase is to analyze CSF relevance in general and along the stages of an IS project. There are many methodologies that represent IS projects as a sequence of phases depending on the type of software developed and implemented (e.g. the systems development life cycle, as well as other methodologies that try to structure the IT implementation process (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). With regard to analyzing stage-specific CSFs, two different approaches can be found. The approach implemented by Pinto & Slevin (1987) is based upon the same set of CSFs at all times, examining the degree of importance of each CSF in different phases of the project. In contrast, other studies have chosen to define different sets of CSFs for each project phase. Although differently executed, both concepts generally tend to refer to the same set of CSFs. Researchers most frequently use case studies as well as surveys based on interviews. Most of the time, participants are asked to either create a list of the most relevant CSFs for each project phase or to examine the relevance of individua CSFs using a scale that indicates a low, normal or high relevance (Esteves, 2004).

4 Management: The purpose of this phase is to identify and systematically structure management activities that need to be considered within a successful IS project, as well as supportive methods for these activities. Key performance indicators can be developed in order to improve the management, control and monitoring of the previously defined CSFs. Often case studies are used (Esteves, 2004).

## A multi-method design for CSF research

Although the majority of researchers do either qualitative or quantitative research, an increasing number of researchers combine qualitative and quantitative methods. This type of research is referred to as mixed-/multi-method research. In line with Creswell (2003), Table 1 gives an overview of qualitative, quantitative and multi-method research approaches.

With respect to the use of more than one research method within a single research project, the terms ‘mixed-method research’ and ‘multi-method research’ are found in the literature. Even though the terms are often used interchangeably, Morse (2003) emphasizes the need to distinguish between these terms. Mixed-method design ‘is the incorporation of various qualitative and quantitative strategies within a single project that may have either a qualitative or quantitative theoretical drive. The “imported” strategies are supplemental to the major or core method and serve to enlighten or provide clues that are followed up within the core method’ (p. 190). Multi-method design also incorporates various quantitative and qualitative research methods. However, within a given project, each of the research methods is conducted rigorously and is complete. The underlying assumption, whether it is based upon a pragmatic (Tashakkori & Teddlie, 1998) or pluralistic perspective (Mingers, 2001), is that quantitative and qualitative methods are compatible, and the combination of both in a multi-method approach will mutually enhance the research findings. Here, the researcher’s decision to choose from a large variety of both qualitative and quantitative methods is solely based on the research question and the phase of the research project (Tashakkori & Teddlie, 1998). Even more, a pluralist approach focuses ‘attention on different aspects of the situation, and so multi-method research is necessary to deal effectively with the full richness of the real world’ (Mingers, 2001, p. 243). Altogether, this argues against the position that differing research paradigms (such as positivist and interpretivist) are incommensurable.

Other main advantages of a multi-method approach, resulting from adopting this strong pluralist perspective, are (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988; Tashakkori & Teddlie, 1998; Mingers, 2001): (1) triangulation: cross-validating data and results by combining different kinds and sources of data, methods, perspectives or investigators, thus increasing the robustness of the results; (2) creativity: discovering fresh or paradoxical factors that stimulate further work; and (3) expansion: widening the scope of the study to take in contextual aspects of the situation.

However there are also some limitations, namely: the cognitive feasibility of moving from one paradigm to another (Mingers, 2001), as well as the need to have a well-rounded researcher, skilled in both quantitative and qualitative methods and/or to collaborate with researchers having different research paradigms. As Kaplan & Duchon (1988) admit, such collaboration could be rife with frustration if ‘neither researcher initially realized the extent to which differing values, assumptions, and vocabularies would interfere with the research project’ (p. 581). In addition, there are practical problems such as how best to sell multi-method research to funding agencies and journals (Mingers, 2001).

The advantages of the multi-method design are especially relevant to the CSF research approach explained in this article’s Theoretical Background section, where the research comprises a number of different phases, with each phase suggesting different suitable methods. When looking at CSF research published in the literature, often purely qualitative or quantitative research methods are used. An overview of research methods utilized in CSF research (especially for the identification of CSFs) can be found in Esteves (2004), with a brief description of strengths and weaknesses of each method in Khandelwal & Ferguson (1999). Recently there have been some exceptions. One example is Esteves & Pastor (2004), who explored the use of multi-method research in the field of CSF research by studying CSFs fo ERP implementation projects. The portal concept promises to provide secure, customizable, personalized, integrated access to dynamic content from a variety of sources, in a variety of source formats, wherever it is needed (e.g. Detlor, 2000; Dias, 2001; Hazra, 2002; Smith, 2004). Thus, the portal concept can be applied across a broad range of industries and application areas, enabling the implementation of such differing portals as: knowledge portals, employee portals, ERP portals, collaborative portals, process portals and partner portals (Dias, 2001). In practice, there seems to be a lack of knowledge on how to guide the complex implementation process of an enterprise portal. In particular, a full understanding of the state-of-the-art in methods, work procedures, CSFs and best practices is missing; filling in these knowledge gaps may help to overcome existing weaknesses in portal projects (Hazra, 2002).

The main research questions are:

1 State-of-the-art: What is the state-of-the-art in portal implementation projects, e.g. with regard to the main players in the portal value chain and the tasks when implementing enterprise portals?

2 Identification: What are the CSFs for portal implementation projects?

3 Analysis: How important are the identified CSFs in general and across the stages of implementation?

4 Management: How can these CSFs be managed, particularly by the use of software tools?

In order to accomplish the research goals, we followed a multi-method approach with a strong focus on qualitative research methods (Table 3). Multi-method research can be carried out in manifold combinations with regard to the implementation sequence (‘→’ indicates a sequential application process) and the priority given to the qualitative and quantitative research methods (‘QUAL’ indicates a qualitative-driven research design, ‘QUAN’ indicates a quantitative-driven design) (Morse, 2003). Basically, we followed the four-phase CSF research framework presented at the beginning of this article.

## State-of-the-art analysis

We found only a limited number of academic papers in journals and conference proceedings dealing with enterprise portals (keyword search in scientific online databases between 1997–

2003). Although the topic is considerably covered by vendors and consultants in practitioner journals, up to now there seems to be a relative dearth of academic papers in this area (Danie & Ward, 2006).

Because of this limited information, we decided to enhance our findings by conducting explorative expert interviews. We chose portal integrators as our target group (in contrast to client companies implementing portals) because we were convinced that portal integrators in particular, with their experiences from multiple portal projects, have the necessary expertise to give in-depth answers to our explorative questions. Through web-based research, we identified 56 companies in the business of portal integration and consulting in Germany, 15 of which agreed to give interviews (response rate: 27%). All of the respondents had experiences in the management of, on average, five portal projects. The interview guidelines addressed the topics of portal strategy, portal projects, portal technology, portal engineering and portal management.

The majority of the interviews were carried out by phone and lasted, on average, 1 hour. We chose to take notes of the respondents’ comments rather than record the conversations because of some of the perceived drawbacks of recording, in particular, that a respondent may be less inclined to speak openly about sensitive topics (Urquhart, 2001). It is also in line with grounded theory’s view that the taping and transcribing of interviews is counterproductive (Glaser, 1998). Nevertheless, we did make sure that at least two researchers were able to take notes, to avoid losing important information during the interviews.

In order to develop the main concepts with regard to portal development, and to give the analytical process precision and rigour, we applied coding procedures from grounded theory. In addition, we used literature both before and during our study, in accordance with Strauss & Corbin’s (1990) assertion that literature can be used to stimulate theoretical sensitivity and questions, can be used as a secondary source of data, can direct theoretical sampling and can be used as a supplementary validation tool. However, literature should not constrain one’s creativity in developing concepts and relationships, although these must be checked against the primary data. As our goal was not to create a whole new theory, we only used open and axial coding to identify the main categories and to make connections between categories, hereby identifying causal conditions, context, strategies and intervening conditions (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

The end result provided us with more in-depth knowledge about portal implementation projects: We could identify the main players (portlet supplier, portlet package supplier, porta integrator, portal vendor, portal provider and portal user) together with their relationships in a multi-layered portal value chain, as well as the stages of the implementation process on which we later needed to perform the CSF analysis. In addition, we were able to generate concepts dealing with key problems related to the complexity of portal implementation projects. For example, we found an increasing focus on processes; a lack of use of standard tools and work practices within the stage of analysis; a high number of self-developed portlets; a lack of standards complicating the integration of portlets, etc. Various influencing factors lead to this complexity: first, the variety of potential areas of application for portals and sub-portals; second, the amount of portlets, which have to be integrated into the portal; and finally, the different views on portals, which have to be considered during the implementation of a porta solution.

The explorative nature of the state-of-the art analysis helped to provide further detail to our research agenda by focussing on CSFs across the stages of implementation, as well as their potential support by tools to manage these factors. Later on it became obvious that particularly the ‘thick’ description became an important source to explain the analysis results of the later research phases.

## CSF identification

In the preliminary study described earlier, we had asked the respondents to identify the most important CSFs. In addition, we reviewed literature on scientific and practitioners’ papers and case examples (16 in total). These cases were collected via the internet, from technical reports by portal vendors and from consultants. Using open and axial coding from grounded theory, we derived a three-level CSF model consisting of six main categories, with the 21 CSFs as subcategories, together with their underlying phenomena (74) as indicated in the data (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). In addition, we refined our inventory of CSFs by comparing these with CSFs in other IS implementation projects, e.g. ERP projects (e.g. Somers & Nelson, 2001; Zhang et al., 2003). In the style of Esteves & Pastor (2004), we were able to classify CSFs according to different perspectives. The technological perspective refers to technical aspects related to the particular portal package, whereas the organizational perspective is related to concerns like organizational structure, culture and business processes. Here, with regard to the numbe of CSFs mentioned, we identified a good balance between organizational and technological factors, but with a tendency towards organizational CSFs. It is also notable that in portal projects, short-term technological factors play an important role.

Referring back to phase one of our research agenda, we were able to link the CSFs back to the state-of-the-art analysis. This was useful in two regards. First, this helped us to determine similarities or patterns of commonality between different descriptions of CSFs in the case studies and the literature. Second, referring back to particular statements in interviews gave us the opportunity to triangulate several sources of information and deepen our understanding of CSFs in portal implementation projects.

## CSF analysis

In order to measure the relevance of the CSFs identified, we asked our target group to rank the CSFs in general as well as across the stages of portal implementation projects. The mailing of 49 surveys resulted in a total response of 10 usable questionnaires. As already mentioned, we had chosen German portal integrators as our target group, having in mind that this group seems to be the most experienced one to evaluate CSFs because of their experiences in multiple portal implementation projects. We analyzed the means and standard deviations fo our 21 CSFs in descending order of importance. The CSFs were rated according to 1 = critical, 2 = very high, 3 = high, 4 = moderate and 5 = low. We then analyzed the different rankings in order to:

1 Analyze different categories of CSFs (strategic, tactical, organizational, and technical).

2 Examine the relationship between the CSFs identified.

3 Analyze CSFs in general and across the different stages of implementation (analysis, design, implementation, introduction, evolution).

4 Relate findings to similar CSF research projects (e.g. Somers & Nelson, 2001).

User acceptance was regarded to be the most important CSF (in general and in the introduction stage). Referring back to the interviews from phases one and two, we were able to identify two possible reasons throughout the implementation process. First, in a prototypingoriented implementation process, the involvement and feedback of the user is seen as crucial. Second, the subsequent process of getting end-users to accept and adopt the portal in their daily work processes seems to be even more challenging and important. The selection of the appropriate portal package was rated as the second most important CSF. Apparently, selection is seen as a strategic decision determining all subsequent measures, e.g. the selection and customizing of portlets and the evolution of the portal. Here, once more we were able to analyze and assess our results with the results from earlier phases, explaining that flexible and open platforms, as well as the development of standards for the development and integration of portlets into portals, can each help to mitigate the consequences of this strategic decision. We were surprised to discover that the CSF process and application integration were stil regarded as very important, even in the evolution phase. After referring back to the transcripts we were able find a possible explanation for this from several portal integrators. Essentially, they held the view that even after implementation the portal could still be adapted to underlying business processes, the reason given being that portals provide better possibilities for customization by, e.g. the definition of roles, personalization, the flexible deployment of (often interacting) portlets, or the definition of ad hoc workflows to support very flexible processes.

## CSF management

In order to suggest measures to manage and, finally, to improve the entire implementation process, we then analyzed the top five CSFs according to how well supported they are by available methods and tools. In order to refine our findings, we asked selected experts (porta integrators) and consulted the available literature, i.e. white papers from portal vendors and case studies. We found out that, at present, neither integrated tools to select portal components nor tools to support the whole portal development process are available. Moreover, most existing tools are product specific and not suitable for other portal platforms. Hence, when planning, customizing and implementing new portals, organizations have to start from scratch. As a result we decided to design and develop the software toolset ‘PortalAccelerator’ to support the core implementation stages. This toolset aims to help define the portal strategy, to incorporate best practices, and in particular to support requirements analysis, portal prototyping and the installation of the portal package, together with the customization of its building blocks (portlets, navigation structure, portal design).

## Critical reflection

This section summarizes our experiences and in particular points out the strengths and weaknesses of our research agenda that in turn influenced the subsequent CSF research project. Certainly, the strengths of this research include the adaptation of the CSF research framework in combination with a multi-method approach and the use of coding procedures from grounded theory.

The four-phase research framework was very helpful in structuring the overall research process. A research question could be assigned to each phase and this flexibility guided the selection of the corresponding research method. Using a multi-method approach gave us the flexibility to focus on the corresponding research question and to select the appropriate research method without being restricted to one research paradigm. In addition, the triangulation of different data sources between and within the four phases helped us to confirm and extend our perspectives and provided us with new insights. In order to add precision and rigour to the analytical process, we applied coding procedures from grounded theory. Not only during the state-of-the-art analysis, but also during the CSF identification phase, we applied open and axial coding, particularly when developing a list of CSFs by grouping together similar CSFs. The coding was based on memos that have been written up, coded and stored in a repository. We found this procedure to be very efficient in terms of backtracking to single statements in later stages of the research.

However, we identified the following limitations (structured according to the research phases):

## State-of-the-art analysis

Some issues in portal implementation projects had already been identified after five interviews. Therefore, it would have been better to follow a strict grounded theory approach by continu ously adapting the interview guideline during the interview process and to sample unti theoretical saturation of each category was reached (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

## CSF identification

We used an existing classification of CSFs instead of developing our own classification that could have emerged during the analysis. However, because of the balanced assignment of the identified CSFs to the four categories, we found this framework to fit well with our data. We analyzed the CSFs purely from a portal integrator’s perspective. In addition, we focussed only on managers of portal projects, neglecting the perspectives of other stakeholders, e.g. portal developers and users. In particular, further comparison with the client’s perspective could have revealed more interesting issues, providing a fuller picture of portal implementation projects.

## CSF analysis

The small sample restricted the use of sophisticated statistical methods for the interpretation of our data. Nevertheless, the ranking was able to point out some important issues and should be seen as an initial step towards a more comprehensive quantitative analysis of CSF. Distance values could be extremely useful in interpreting the different rankings. Although the general evaluation of CSFs is interesting, examination of CSFs across the stages of implementation was found to be much more significant. In this light, we would also recommend focussing on CSFs ranked lowest and relating these to those ranked highest. In addition, the comparison of CSFs to other similar research revealed various other important issues on which to focus.

## CSF management

There are also limitations with regard to the management of CSF across the stages of implementation, i.e. by the use of methods and tools. The development of the tool ‘PortalAccelerator’ was based on the results of the earlier phases. However, in this phase we only validated our findings in selected expert interviews. Because of the limited literature, it was also not possible to enhance or confirm our management implications. Also, we have not conducted pilot or confirmatory case studies or used the ‘PortalAccelerator’ within an action research approach.

## CASE TWO: CSF FOR OFFSHORE SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS

With regard to offshore software development, some German associations and the research institutes of large-scale German enterprises have already published practical guidelines on the implementation of IT offshoring projects. However, little research has been carried out in regard to offshore software development from a German perspective in general, and in particular, looking at the CSFs of such projects. Even though some research studies concentrating on the CSFs of offshore software development projects from a U.S. client as well as from an Asian and European provider perspective already existed, we did not know if the results of these studies can be transferred to German-speaking clients. ## State-of-the-art analysis

In an effort to analyze the state-of-the-art in CSF research in the field of offshore software development, we carried out a comprehensive literature review. Because of the limited number of publications in the field of offshore software development, we did not limit our literature review solely to offshore software development; rather, we scanned key IS conference proceedings (AMCIS, ECIS, HICSS, ICIS) and journals (CACM, HBR, ISR, MISQ, SMR) produced from 1997 to 2004, books on the subject of IT outsourcing (in a broad sense) as well as scientific online databases in order to find publications dealing with the (critical) success factors of IT outsourcing projects in general. Based only on our keyword search within the mentioned conference proceedings and journals, we were able to identify more than 600 articles and conference papers related to IT outsourcing (in a broad sense), although not all of the identified publications focussed on this topic. After carrying out an additional manual search, we were able to identify 156 publications dealing solely with IT outsourcing (in a broad sense) within the selected IS conferences and journals.

On the basis of this search of the relevant literature, we identified articles and othe contributions that explicitly concentrate on the (critical) success factors of relevant projects in an IT outsourcing context. As a first step, these studies were classified according to the research field (IT outsourcing, IT offshoring, offshore software development), perspective (client, provider), and research method applied (literature research, interviews, case studies, experience). As a second step, the identified studies were compared to one another with respect to their specific content.

In total, we found only four CSF studies that focus explicitly on offshore software development. However, in these studies, the CSFs are either insufficiently described or reflect a provider perspective. In addition, with regard to the applied research method, it became apparent that only two of the four offshore software development CSF studies rely on literature research. One mentions interviews as their primary source of information. Furthermore, only one study examines the relevance of the identified CSFs, and none of the studies provide a more sophisticated management analysis in regard to the proposed CSFs.

## CSF identification

In order to develop an emergent theory of CSFs, we followed a qualitative research approach using concepts from grounded theory. Our use of the grounded theory method followed the process proposed by Pandit (1996), comprising the following phases:

1 Literature review: Analysis of the content of the identified CSF studies in regard to the identification of relevant CSFs.

2 Preparation of questionnaires: (1) Formulation of general and specific questions for interview guidelines; (2) adaptation of specific questions from interview guidelines following a firs data evaluation; (3) formulation of verification questionnaire after a second data evaluation.

3 Data collection: (1) Conducting of ten interviews; (2) conducting of twelve more interviews; (3) mailing of the questionnaires for verification purposes.

4 Data analysis: (1) Iterative analysis of interview data by means of the open coding process; (2) evaluation of the verification questionnaires.

5 Comparison with related studies: Extension of the preliminary CSF list by comparing this lis with similar lists of CSFs (in the IT context) and a list of issues and problems (in the offshore software development context).

It has to be noted that the second, third and fourth research phases were not conducted strictly sequentially, as the process of grounded theory building ‘is an iterative process’ (Pandit, 1996). For example, after analyzing the first set of interviews, some questions have been omitted where previous responses had provided sufficiently detailed information to answer certain questions. New questions were inserted to follow new paths of investigation where there was a need to explore further details.

In order to arrive at a comprehensive understanding of how to implement successfully offshore software development projects, a variety of company perspectives (client, provider, and consultancy) needed to be canvassed, and this was taken into account when selecting interview respondents. While the majority of the participants (59%) were employed by offshore software development client companies. 27% worked for IT consultancies and 14% for offshore software development providers. All of the interview partners had already imple mented several offshore software development projects, predominantly in India (64%), and had taken on leading roles in these projects. To complement the expert interviews, participants were provided with the already developed list of CSFs and a brief description of each CSF in order to verify the results of the interviews as well as to prevent misinterpretations.

Based on the literature review, conducting interviews with experts in the field of offshore software development, and the subsequent verification of the compiled results, we derived a list of CSFs for the successful implementation of offshore software development projects from the perspective of German-speaking companies. Our research results showed that a classifi cation of the CSFs into ‘internal vs. external’ and ‘static (suitability) vs. dynamic (management) factors enabled a balanced assignment of the identified CSFs to four categories: Internal suitability factors relate to offshore readiness on the part of the client; internal management factors relate to the planning of an offshore software development project; external suitability factors relate to the selection of an offshore provider; and external management factors are related to the implementation of an offshore software development project.

## CSF analysis

In an effort to analyze the identified CSFs in more detail, we prepared a web survey. In line with Esteves (2004), the reason for using this method was the fact that it was the easiest, fastest and cheapest way for us to access experts in the offshore software development field. In total, 103 company representatives with relevant project experience participated in the online survey. The majority of the respondents worked for an offshore software development client company (39.4%), had implemented offshore software development projects in Asia (69.9%), and had taken on a leading position within their companies’ offshore software development projects (90.3%). Regarding offshore software development project experience, more than half of the participants (52.5%) stated that they possessed more than three years’ experience with offshore software development and had implemented more than three such projects within the last 3 years; this corresponds to high levels of project experience.

The assessment of CSFs was carried out by means of a Likert scale with values from 1 to 5, where 1 was equivalent to ‘not relevant’ and 5 equivalent to ‘significantly relevant’. We analyzed our list of 29 CSFs with regard to their relevance, phase specificity and level of influence (for details, see Wiener (2006)). In this context, we examined all three aspects of the analysis both in general (by incorporating the answers of all participants) and according to selected dimensions of the analysis (by only incorporating the answers of the respective participant groups). For the latter, we classified the participants along nine dimensions and examined the significance of the assessment differences between the individual participant groups (if statistically applicable).

The results of our online survey show that the participants consider ‘definition of clear project goals’, ‘continuous controlling of project results’, ‘ensuring of a continuous communication flow’, and ‘high quality of offshore employees’ as the CSFs most critical to the success of an offshore software development project. In addition, with regard to our two-dimensional CSF model, it becomes apparent that the external management factors represent the most relevant CSF group, followed by the internal management factors. The great majority of the identified CSFs can be classified as cross-phase (20 factors). With regard to the level of influence, it can be observed that the majority of the 29 CSFs can directly be influenced in the short-term (12 factors). The analysis of the assessment differences between the three company perspectives confirmed differences in perception of our CSF list by the respective participant groups. In total, these groups assigned 10 of the 29 CSFs to different levels of influence. For example, major differences were able to be identified for ‘sustained management support’; whereas OSD clients found that this CSF can be influenced in the short-term, both consultants and offshore providers thought that this CSF could only be influenced in the long-term.

## CSF management

In an attempt to arrive at suggestions for the management of the developed CSFs, we first analyzed the content of the CSF studies identified within the state-of-the-art analysis. However, as we believe that an understanding of the management of CSFs for offshore software development projects cannot be achieved without considering the organizationa context (Yin, 1994), we decided to extend our research by conducting two case studies (a pilot case study and a confirmatory case study), one with a large-scale Swiss enterprise and another with a medium-sized German enterprise, each encompassing the client and provider perspective. A detailed description can be found in Wiener (2006).

For both case studies we conducted five interviews with different members of the offshore software development projects under investigation. Here, all of our interview partners took on leading roles (project managers and coordinators) within the examined offshore software development projects. In order to better understand the management of CSFs, we also interviewed the offshore providers’ single point-of-contact who worked permanently on the client site. Complementing the interviews, we were able to observe project members during their daily work and analyze project documents, such as presentations, project plans or charts. In this context, the main goal was to gain as much project-related background information as possible. As in the CSF identification phase, to ensure the open nature and authenticity of the statements, we decided to keep written records of the relevant contents, rather than record them (Urquhart, 2001). In order to verify the initial data interpretations by the research team, the participants of both case studies received a structured summary of the interpretations via e-mail.

Field notes, commonly known as memos (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), were created immediately after each interview. Subsequently, interview data were triangulated with the documentation accumulated so far. After having identified the most important CSFs for both the client and the provider company, we analyzed these factors with regard to their potential support by the application of methods and tools. After having carried out both case studies, the studies were compared to one another. Based on the identified similarities and differences, fina conclusions concerning the management of CSFs in offshore software development projects could be drawn.

In total, we identified 148 management activities carried out by the case study partners, which had a significant influence on our list of CSFs. More than one-third of these activities were confirmed by both client companies. Focussing on the top seven CSFs of offshore software development projects, all rated as significantly relevant for the success of an offshore software development project within the online survey, we derived key management activities from the comparison of the conducted case studies.

We also explored similarities or context-specific differences on how CSFs are ranked in the case studies compared to our results from the online survey in the analysis phase. Even though these results have to be carefully interpreted, the results strongly suggest that the importance of some of the CSFs is dependent on the underlying context (e.g. risk awareness, type of software to be outsourced). A further analysis of case data revealed that some challenges could be attributed to their unique project context and were closely related to the CSF management activities carried out. In particular, this shows the importance of looking at interrelationships between CSFs and corresponding activities (Remus & Wiener, 2009).

Although major weaknesses of the first project have been solved satisfactorily we are aware of the limitations of the second research project, i.e.

## State-of-the-art analysis

We conducted a comprehensive literature review covering not only the core area of success factors in the field of IT outsourcing and offshoring, but also general literature on IT outsourcing resulting in more than 600 publications. An add-on explorative stage with interviews could have enriched our findings.

## CSF identification

It has to be noted that, with 29 factors, the number of identified CSFs is large, making it necessary to filter the most critical ones by taking into account situational and contextual factors. Furthermore, by using only open and axial coding during the identification of the CSFs, we have not fully exploited the methods of grounded theory.

## CSF analysis

There are some limitations with regard to the statistical interpretation of the data: In order to collect more precise data, the participants of the online survey were allowed to specify multiple answers within three of the nine analysis dimensions. This procedure resulted in interdependencies between the corresponding dimensions, which made it impossible to analyze the significance of assessment differences within the respective dimensions.

## CSF management

We only conducted two case studies. As the perception of CSFs is highly context-dependent, additional research would be necessary in order to explore further the management of CSFs in different project contexts. Furthermore, in contrast to the first project, the development of integrated management methods and tools for the identified CSFs, or clusters of those, was not part of this research project. However, the results of this study, i.e. the developed CSF list and classification could serve as a starting point for the development of such methods or tools.

## FINAL DISCUSSION

In this discussion we want to refer back to our initial question and critically reflect on the application of the multi-method research approach for similar research projects in the future. In particular, we want to point out differences between the two research projects that led to variations in the multi-method research framework adopted (Table 5).

Both research projects dealt with IS projects. In order to analyze stage-specific CSFs, it was possible to structure the project in different stages (although the resulting stages selected turned out to be different). Both research projects were able to draw on a large amount of literature on CSF research, although the literature was from different contexts. As a result of the differences between the projects, certain requirements had to be met and adjustments had to be made to apply the research approach to the new case. Whereas in the first project the focus on CSFs evolved during the exploration of the state-of-the-art, the research goals of the second project were directly targeted at CSFs. Another difference was the nature of the literature review in the first phase of research. Because of the lack of existing literature in the field of portal implementation projects, we had to develop our own theory, whereas in the second project we could draw from a large amount of literature. Both projects had a strong focus on qualitative research methods. Quantitative methods only were used in the analysis of CSFs. We also had access to two companies conducting an in-depth case study in order to develop and confirm the management implications for our developed CSF list.

The main limitations of the first CSF project were addressed as follows: First, we were able to profit from the experiences with grounded theory and could then develop our own CSF model by using coding procedures from grounded theory. Second, we enriched the existing view of CSFs by introducing multiple perspectives that considered client companies, IT and offshore software development providers. We could then classify the participants along nine dimensions (company perspective, company size, industry, region of destination, organizational form, project type, project size, project experience and position of employee) and examine the significance of the assessment differences between the individual participant groups. Third, the second CSF project had a much higher sample rate, making it possible to use more sophisticated statistical methods. Finally, the robustness of the last phase of research (CSF Management) was improved by conducting both pilot and confirmatory case studies.

## Benefits of the multi-method design

Referring back to the initial statement made in the beginning that multi-method approaches have to show that they (1) improve trustworthiness; (2) support creativity; and (3) widen the scope of the study, we will finally point out how these criteria were met:

## Trustworthiness

In particular, when evaluating the last CSF project, trustworthiness was significantly improved. As our CSF research approach primarily comprises qualitative research methods, we agree with Lincoln & Guba (1985) that conventional criteria are not appropriate for evaluating the trustworthiness of qualitative studies and instead use the following criteria as an alternative:

Credibility refers to the question of ‘truth value’ (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Miles & Huberman, 1994) and aims to ensure that the unit under study was accurately described (Esteves & Pastor, 2004). In order to maximize research credibility, a frequently used technique involves persistent observation and triangulation, external checking of the inquiry process by peer debriefing, refining the emerging results by negative case analysis, as well as ‘member checks’, which is the verification of data, categories, interpretations and conclusions with participants (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). With regard to the credibility of the research results, we carried out a comprehensive literature review (on both IT outsourcing in general and CSFs of IT outsourcing projects in particular, over a time period of more than 1 year, resulting in more than 600 publications), conducted more than 30 expert interviews, conducted an online survey with more than 100 participants, as well as carried out two case studies, triangulating data and considering different perspectives during data collection.

Transferability refers to the applicability of the findings in other contexts (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). Maxwell (2005, p. 115) emphasizes that ‘the generalizability of qualitative studies is usually not based on explicit sampling of some defined population to which the results can be extended, but on the development of a theory that can be extended to other cases’. In terms of the transferability of the research results, we provided a detailed description of the research contexts, included our findings from the conducted literature reviews, transferred findings from previous research phases and paid attention to the fact that our results are represented by data from all perspectives.

Dependability examines the stability of the research results over time, across researchers and methods (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In our study, we used all categories emerging from the data and regularly aligned the further research process.

Confirmability is a synonym for objectivity (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In this context, researchers need to show that their results ‘are rooted in contexts and persons apart from the evaluator and are not simply figments of the evaluator’s imagination’ (Esteves & Pastor, 2004, p. 79). We compared our results with those of the literature reviews and related studies respectively, verified our initial interpretations with the corresponding participants as well as included their feedback, and applied statistical measures.

## Creativity – discovering fresh or paradoxical factors

Conducting a broad state-of-the-art analysis gave insights into related areas and stimulated further work, i.e. to focus on (specific) CSFs. Considering additional perspectives (clients, supplier and consultants) showed different interpretations of CSFs. Using alternative research methods led to additional interesting aspects or confirmed already identified aspects. One example was looking at the context dependency of certain CSFs by using case study data.

## Expansion – widening the scope of the study

The rich description of the research context contributed considerably to the correct interpretation and the possible transformation of the results to another context. This was especially true for case studies and the use of grounded theory. The comparison of CSFs with regard to the type of IS project (e.g. offshore software development, ERP, portal implementation) and different perspectives (e.g. German clients, US clients, Indian suppliers) widened the scope and led to interesting results.

## Limitations of the overall multi-method design

Alongside limitations specific to each research project presented here, the use of a multimethod design for CSF research in general has limitations that lie within the nature of CSF research and the integrated use of quantitative and qualitative methods. Some general limi tations to CSF research are already mentioned in the Theoretical Background section, and include the wide variation in the definition of ‘success’ and its difficulty in measuring success in financial and non-financial terms, Some of the most noted problems are the lack of a theoretical basis, potential for interviewer bias and the absence of established, consisten procedures for identifying CSFs and carrying out CSF studies (Boynton & Zmud, 1984). All sorts of cognitive bias could occur, including the hesitancy of project team members to discuss their failures, blunders or steps that were unsuccessful, and ‘short memory’, where the view from the end of a project is quite different from the views during the project (Markus et al., 2000). However, careful selection of interview partners, taking on multiple perspectives (Keil et al., 2002) and triangulating data may mitigate these problems.

On the other hand, we can also identify other limitations that mainly lie in the applicability of multi-method research as proposed in this article. The qualitative ‘drive’ of our multi-method approach, in particular the ability to triangulate different sources of data, both quantitative and qualitative, requires a well-rounded researcher. At the same time, the use of grounded theory necessitates additional research skills and, above all, experiences in past grounded theory projects. One of the strengths of this approach is that following the four-phase structure will lead to a comprehensive picture of CSFs. However, the rigorous use of different methods and the triangulation process requires extra time and effort, thus extending an already lengthy research process.

## CONCLUSION

We will conclude with implications of our research for practitioners and IS researchers. Alongside gaining insights into two practical examples for multi-method research, we believe that IS researchers can gain new insights into conducting CSF research. Particularly, CSF research should not be limited to the identification stage. Rather, it should be extended to CSF analysis and management. Our research agenda can thus be seen as a further step towards a more holistic, systematic and adaptable approach to CSF research. We recommend adopting a multi-method research approach using phase-specific qualitative and quantitative methods. In line with Tashakkori & Teddlie (2003), we could show that using a multi-method approach can increase trustworthiness, discover fresh or paradoxical factors and widen the scope of the study.

Finally, based on the four-phase approach for CSF research, we were able to make recommendations for each individual research phase. We were also able to identify implica tions for the developers of tools for IS research. In particular, the application of differen research methods (method triangulation) as well as the use of different data sources (data triangulation) will require an integrated toolset for managing the whole research process, especially for referring back and forth between results.

Practitioners, i.e. managers of IS projects and those who are involved in portal or offshore software development projects, can use the results of CSF studies when coordinating man agement activities over the course of an IS project. IS implementers might use the CSF project phase level scheme outlined to concentrate their activities on areas specific to the correspond ing stage of the IS project. We believe in particular that the comparison of CSFs from different IS projects in different settings will reveal interesting results, even though extra care needs to be taken when interpreting these results. A single CSF or set of CSFs could be compared according to its/their dimensions or, if suitable, compared according to project phase level, provided that phases can be mapped. This suggestion was first indicated by the comparison with CSF studies on ERP implementation projects, which provided a new perspective on the technology life cycle of ERP systems versus enterprise portals and their corresponding CSFs. Using the four-phase CSF research framework, together with a multi-method design, may well be a promising starting point for exploring and explaining these differences.
