# Understanding documentation and reconstruction requirements for computer-assisted decision processes

Peter Bajcsy <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Rob Kooper <sup>a</sup>, Sang-Chul Lee <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> National Center for Supercomputing Applications, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, IL, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Computer and Information Engineering, Inha University, Incheon, Republic of Korea

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 August 2009 Received in revised form 24 August 2010 Accepted 26 August 2010 Available online 19 September 2010

Keywords: Preservation decisions Information granularity Evaluations

## a b s t r a c t

Challenges related to documenting and reconstructing computer-assisted decision processes include the selection of information granularity, design of information gathering and reconstruction mechanisms, evaluation of reconstruction value, and storage and computational costs. This article surveys these challenges and explicates an approach to designing and prototyping an evaluation framework for decisions based on image inspection. The framework explored here allows users to analyze storage and computational costs of information gathering as a function of information granularity and then assesses the potential value of decision process reconstructions. We illustrate how evaluations of decision process reconstructions could potentially improve our understanding of future archival needs by simultaneously documenting, preserving and reconstructing computer-assisted decision processes, and evaluating and forecasting computational and storage requirements of the documentation and reconstruction processes over time.

## 1. Introduction

In today's world, the challenges associated with preservation of historical documents, artifacts and various collections are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and of immediate concern. Today's challenges could have been mediated to some degree by a better understanding of technological changes originating in past decision making, be it in hardware allocations or software development. The question for us at present is whether we could understand the preservation and reconstruction needs of tomorrow by forecasting speci<sup>fi</sup>c uses of technologies and possible technological changes.

We focus on the use of computers as a technological tool for decision making. Documenting, preserving and reconstructing these computer-assisted decision processes are of paramount importance, since many of these decisions impact our society in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ways. Thus, gathering information about computer-assisted decision processes and providing enough information for their reconstruction in the future become of interest to archivists, librarians, and historians. This goal is clearly stated in [34]: “the essential goal in preserving records is to enable reconstruction of past events, activities, decisions, and states of affairs from recorded information accumulated in the course of action.…. The role of archivists and of archives is to deliver the building materials which others use to reconstruct the past.” Documenting and preserving computer-assisted decision processes are different from the preservation of paper documents [31] and depend not only on electronic records created and their changes over time but also on computer technology and its changes over time [3,32].

In addition to the need to document, preserve and reconstruct computer-assisted decision processes, there is a demand for evaluating and forecasting computational and storage requirements of the documentation and reconstruction processes over time. At its broadest, as the use of computers for computer-assisted decisions becomes more ubiquitous, we ask: “what are the computational and storage requirements on gathering provenance information and on reconstructing critical decision activities?” The answer to the above question opens new questions such as: “What are the information granules that one should preserve? What software mechanisms could be available for information gathering? How should we represent and store gathered information? How do we reconstruct critical decision activities later in time? What types of questions could be answered about past decisions?” This article suggests a design for a prototype evaluation system as a test bed for performing tradeoff studies related to the questions above and for supporting archival decisions about how to document and preserve computer-assisted decision processes.

## 1.1. Motivation and problem definition

In designing a test bed for performing tradeoff studies about computational and storage requirements needed for documentation and reconstruction of computer-assisted decision processes, the possible application of our work includes supporting archivists, librarians, and historians at any institution where critical computerassisted decisions have to become electronic records available for future reference. Our ultimate goals are to gather all observable variables about decision making processes today so that (a) a decision making process could be documented properly in the future; (b) audits and authentications of records could be executed; and (c) accountability of decisions could be achieved. Our overall objective then is to allow users to analyze quantitative measurements of computational and storage requirements as a function of documentation and reconstruction variables, and then map them into <sup>fi</sup>nancial costs of hardware, software and labor investments compared with the information value delivered to a consumer at the time of decision process reconstruction. More simply, we provide analytic metrics to assess the feasibility of particular hardware, software, and personnel con<sup>fi</sup>gurations for long term data preservation and use.

## 1.2. Illustration example

The evaluation framework is illustrated as part of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c example of image inspection based decisions. We selected this general class of computer-assisted decisions based on considerations about (a) computer technology and data characteristics supporting decision processes, and (b) the types of questions that one would want to answer about decision processes. In this case of image inspection based decisions, a computer assists decision makers by accessing and displaying digital images inside of an image viewer, and then letting users visually inspect images using navigation functionalities of the image viewer. From the wide spectrum of computer technologies, we selected (a) Java to be our programming language for writing the evaluation framework and (b) images in widely used image <sup>fi</sup>le formats. The Java programming language is widely available on many different platforms and the code generated is not dependent on any particular operating system (OS) and hardware combination. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation for the Java language is an open standard (http://jcp. org), and there are multiple open source implementations available. The open speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and open implementations together allow the use of an older open source implementation (if there were an issue with backward compatibility) or the creation of a new interpreter of the byte-code base on the open speci<sup>fi</sup>cation in the future. Java's common usage and richly documented history make it suitable for long term preservation. Similarly, we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally looked at those image formats that are well documented and have an open and welldocumented speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and (multiple) open source implementations, allowing future systems to be able to load those images.

In privileging Java and commonly used <sup>fi</sup>le formats, we recognized that questions of future interests are pertinent to the computerrendered content of an inspected image to a decision maker. Example questions included, but were not limited to: “Was the <sup>fi</sup>le viewed? Did the <sup>fi</sup>le contain relevant information? Was the image viewed at the appropriate magni<sup>fi</sup>cation and spatial location? Was image viewed using adequate intensity brightness and color bands?” The software that supports the actual computer-assisted decision process is called an image reviewer and its user interface is presented in Fig. 1. As a whole, the image inspection tool allows users to load, manipulate and view images with their associated information. A more detailed description of the software can be found at http://isda.ncsa.uiuc.edu ip2learn/IP2LearnManual.pdf.

## 1.3. Related work

For the purposes of decision process documentation, preservation and reconstruction, the information about computer-assisted decisions refers to (a) the origin of data sets, processing tools and computational environments (hardware and software), (b) the sequence of processing steps including user interactions with data sets, and (c) the human behavior while performing the decision activities. In the literature, several terms and problem formulations have been used to describe information gathering about computerassisted decision making processes. The term lineage has been used, for example, to refer to the origin and subsequent processing history of a data set for computer-based processing with limited human interventions [4]. The computer-based processing (also called insilico processing [18]) is loosely de<sup>fi</sup>ned as (a) script- or programbased, (b) query-based, (c) work<sup>fl</sup>ow-based, or (d) service-based. The terms data provenance and data pedigree have been also used for query- and service-based processings [6]. Other terms, such as derivation history, data set dependence, execution trace, log/logbook, <sup>fi</sup>liation, data genealogy, data archeology, and audit trails, are referenced in the literature too (see the survey paper [4]). In our work, the decision process provenance is gathered only about the information presented by a computer to a user (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as computer centric information [25]) due to the privacy policies on monitoring human behavior in the government.

There is a large body of literature focusing on the preservation of electronic records [13,30], knowledge management [15,20,23] and reproducible research [33]. One of the basic problems in the previous projects such as reported in [4,7] is provenance information gathering and management of electronic records. It is a foundation block of knowledge management (KM) and decision support systems (DSS) that aim to get the right knowledge in the right form to the right persons at the right time [20]. Solutions with provenance gathering and management capabilities [4] have been built in multiple domains including environmental engineering [24], hydrologic and climate modeling, molecular biology [16], physics, and chemistry [28]. It is known that provenance management systems have to deal with large amounts and a wide variety of provenance information and hence the system has to be scalable, general and customizable, to include trust, security and preservation [17], as well as to provide automatic and systematic provenance gathering [16]. For instance, applications where a document has to be re-assembled from document fragments [21] or their original document format (e.g., PowerPoint, Word, WordPerfect, Excel, etc.) has to be determined from a scanned bitmap image [12] require provenance gathering and/or very elaborate analyses.

A paucity of standards, components and techniques for recording provenance creates signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges [17]. The issue of standards might not have been addressed yet because the <sup>fi</sup>rst provenance systems were reported only about a decade ago (Geolineus in 1993 to track ArcInfo GIS operations, GOOSE in 1994, and Geo-Opera in 1997 [4]). The lack of standards in semantic and syntactic descriptions about data leads to dif<sup>fi</sup>culties when the data sets are used, stored and retrieved. The issue of components and techniques for recording provenance has been approached from the perspective of a single human (Gordon Bell and his MyLifeBits [7]) or a community (collaborative environments and experiment management [4]). Nevertheless, there is not a provenance management system that would meet all above requirements, as well as speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements de<sup>fi</sup>ned by multiple application domains.

Our focus on computational and storage costs versus reconstruction value evaluations could be related to the previous work on estimating the cost of a preservation lifecycle, for instance, in the LIFE project [11,26]. According to the LIFE project, the digital preservation cost is an accumulation of many costs according to the Generic LIFE Preservation Model [26]. Following the preservation model, a total lifecycle cost is estimated as $\mathtt { L } { = } \mathtt { C o s t } ( \mathtt { A q } + \mathtt { I } + \mathtt { M } + \mathtt { A c } + \mathtt { S } + \mathtt { P } )$ , where L is the complete lifecycle cost over time 0 to T, Aq is Acquisition, I is Ingest, M is Metadata, Ac is Access, S is Storage, and P is Preservation. Each component consists of one to six elements corresponding to preservation activities. The costs were evaluated based on 0.5 million individual <sup>fi</sup>les and led to an estimate of £19 to store and preserve an e-monograph in year one. Our evaluation framework differs from the LIFE project by focusing on (a) decision processes instead of on e-monographs, (b) computational and storage costs that are parts of almost all contributing costs according to the Generic LIFE Preservation Model, and (c) information granularity, meta-data representation and computational aspects of decision processes. Our evaluations could be viewed as contributing factors to the following elements of the Generic LIFE Preservation Model: Aq — obtaining, I — deposit, M — characterization, Ac — mechanism, S — bit stream storage, and P — quality assurance.

## 1.4. Approach

We formulate the problem of designing an evaluation system for predicting computational and storage costs of computer centric information gathering, management and computer-based activity reconstruction as a function of information granularity and of the information value to the consumer of computer-based activity reconstruction. We approached the design problem by dividing it into two sub-problems:

(1) Research automated documentation methods about computerassisted decision making activities (information gathering mechanisms and related data management options).

(2) Research automated reconstruction methods of computerassisted decision making activities and the methods supporting tradeoff evaluations of the reconstructed information value as a function of information granularity.

These two research problems could be mapped into basic questions related to the computer-assisted decision processes shown in Fig. 2. Our approach to the <sup>fi</sup>rst research problem (and the questions in the upper half of Fig. 2) was to identify and prototype methods to monitor executions of various software functionalities. Next, we summarize and visualize execution statistics in real-time, enable gathering information about various aspects of execution, organize the gathered information and store the data for future usage. Similarly, our approach to the second research problem (and the questions in the lower half of Fig. 2) was to identify and prototype methods to retrieve information, reconstruct executions of software functionalities (decision process activities), and present the reconstructions to a user in order support tradeoff evaluations with respect to a set of questions about information value and the computational and storage costs.

## 2. Evaluation framework

Two components of an evaluation framework include documenting and reconstructing decision making activities corresponding to the sub-problems de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Section 1.4. We focused on identifying categories of general methods for documenting and reconstructing decision process activities that should be included in a test bed for performing various tradeoff evaluations.

For the class of image inspection based decisions, the evaluation framework consists of the following key software capabilities: (a) userdriven image inspection based decisions (termed image reviewer), (b) automated information gathering and data management (identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as event tracker), and (c) automated reconstruction of computerassisted decision process activities presented to users for tradeoff evaluations (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as event reviewer). These functionalities enable us to gain knowledge about the tradeoffs between computational and storage costs and reconstruction value as a function of information granularity. The problem of information preservation has not been included as it requires studying trends in storage media, computer technologies, and preservation tools which are outside of the scope of our work and have been studied and discussed by other investigators [8,19] or approached in pilot projects [11].

## 2.1. Documentation of computer-assisted decision making activities

The software for documenting decision making activities gathers and stores information about image inspections. In the next sections, we describe (a) types of information granules about image inspection tasks, (b) information gathering mechanisms, (c) information representation and storage, and (d) real-time visualization of gathered information.

## 2.1.1. Information granularity

In general, information granularity is the answer to the question “What should be extracted about decision processes?” The information granularity depends not only on the anticipated future queries about decisions but also on observable variables available to the computer that assists during a decision process. Given the class of image inspection driven decisions, the image inspection tool can report only information collected by its Java components, such as the image frame and image panel.

The execution information accessible from the image frame corresponds to events such as when a window was created, closed, shown, hidden, or a window snapshot was taken. The image frame does not hold the image content which is stored in the image panel. Thus, it is the image panel that has access to information about image manipulation operations depending on what functionality has been developed and added to the image panel. The modular architecture of the underlying software allows us to add and remove any menu functionality for evaluation purposes. In the current software prototype, the following information can be selectively gathered: new image, change crop, change auto zoom, change zoom factor, change intensity gamma correction, use of RGB or grayscale display, change of selected displayed grayscale band or change of the assignment of bands to red, green and blue (RGB) display channels, display image using pseudo RGB colors where the color is interpreted from high values (red) to low values (blue), change the use of image derived min and max values for intensity scaling, change the use of user de<sup>fi</sup>ned min and max values for intensity scaling grayscale scale, add annotation, remove annotation, allow selection of sub-areas, change selection of sub-areas, mouse clicked, and change visible region by scrolling the slider bars. There are altogether <sup>fi</sup>ve information granules gathered from the image frame and 21 information granules collected from the image panel that could be used for evaluation purposes.

The choice of information granules directly impacts archival costs of preservation and information value of reconstructions. There is a central processing unit (CPU) usage and storage cost associated with gathering information that maps into the cost of information retrieval and reconstruction and leads to some reconstruction value. The increasing amount of gathered information does not proportionally increase the cost and the reconstruction value. Gathering a copy of an entire computer and its hard drive every CPU clock tick, for example, would lead to extremely high computational costs (CPU and storage of the tracking application would have to be far superior to the computer supporting decision processes) and 100% reconstruction value. However, less intensive information gathering mechanisms would reduce the computational costs tremendously while still keeping the reconstruction value high. Within this consideration, a good de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a reconstruction value is critical for wise selections of gathered information granules. The granularity of gathered information becomes one of the evaluation parameters chosen by a user.

## 2.1.2. Information gathering mechanisms

In general, two questions arise when it comes to information gathering about computer-assisted decision processes. First, at what computer system level should one gather information? Second, what gathering mechanism should one use? Previous inquiries [1] reveal that computer system levels refer to the layers from where information could be reported. For instance, in our case it could be the image inspection tool (user program), a software integration environment for running the image inspection tool (middle layer such as a work<sup>fl</sup>ow engine), a java compiler that could embed information reporting to the image inspection tool (custom compiler layer), or operating system or hardware system interface [9]. In our current approach, we used a user program (the image inspection tool) to report provenance information.

Given the user program level of information gathering, the second question was addressed by exploring three different mechanisms. These three mechanisms include the aspects of when and how information should be gathered. They are based on (a) listening to events generated by graphical user interfaces (GUI) (e.g., mouse movement, key board strokes), (b) intercepting messages between software components performing image operations (e.g., decomposing the image inspection functionality and embedding it inside of a work<sup>fl</sup>ow environment), and (c) inserting logging statements inside of software components (e.g., report a variable value when it changes). Each of the mechanisms has individualized pros and cons as described in previous explorations [1].

In the prototyped evaluation framework, we experimented with two information gathering mechanisms. First, we used an event manager that listens to any changes inside of the application (inside of image frame and of image panel). It is a mechanism that utilizes object-oriented programming for event interception and minimizes insertions of logging statements. In general, insertions of logging statements are very costly for legacy code since one has to learn the legacy code often without any documentation, decide at which places information should be collected, and then insert logging statements and recompile the code. Therefore, our code design for provenance gathering incorporates foreseen needs for extensions of the image inspection tool, as well as the needs for the number and type of provenance information granules. The current mechanism allows us to gather <sup>fi</sup>ve information granules from image frame and 21 information granules from image panel. However, it does not allow us to gather information about any events inside of pop-up dialogs, for instance, intermediate information presented during gamma intensity adjustment or band selection in pop-dialogs, or scrollable information presentation in image information dialog showing image dimensions and properties (see Fig. 3). While the <sup>fi</sup>nal gamma and band selection values are gathered, the intermediate values before closing each dialog are not reported. In order to solve this problem, snapshots of pop-up dialogs can be optionally taken at regular time intervals to preserve the information rendered by the computer.

Second, we have also designed an information gathering mechanism that would record only mouse movements. This approach is similar to the approach based on listening for events generated by graphical user interfaces (GUI). The intention is to reconstruct the entire decision process by re-executing mouse movements and mouse clicks in the same order. In this case, we do not record any longer the computer interpretation of the user's intent (e.g., File Open) but only the actions of the user (e.g., mouse click at location (x, y)). By preserving mouse movements only, we do not know what a mouse click meant, if it was a <sup>fi</sup>le selection (and if so then what <sup>fi</sup>le was selected) or just a click on the image. In order to reconstruct decision making activities based on only mouse movements the entire computer environment has to be intact including not only the <sup>fi</sup>le system for input <sup>fi</sup>le access and any existing software but also display resolution, mouse speed and hardware characteristics. Furthermore, from the reconstruction viewpoint, answering questions about decision processes can be accomplished only by viewing a replay of the entire mouse movement sequence and not by querying a provenance information repository. The gathered information about mouse movements would not contain information about, for instance, what tool was used and what parts of an image were displayed.

The purpose of these options for information gathering is to explore the cost versus value tradeoffs while considering simultaneously documentation and reconstruction. One would like to know, for example, whether it is more cost-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient to document and reconstruct from (a) a sequence of snapshots (a movie) of all rendered information during a computer-assisted decision process (high cost of data storage, low cost of reconstruction using a cross-platform image viewer, high value of seeing all information rendered on a screen), (b) a sequence of operations and changes inside of the image inspection application (cost and value vary depending on user's choice) or (c) a sequence of mouse movements together with the computer environment (high cost of preserving an intact computer, low cost of data storage, high value of seeing all mouse-driven decision activities on a screen). We will illustrate an example of such evaluation study for the case (b) in the Experimental section.

## 2.1.3. Information representation and storage

Representation and storage of gathered information can be approached as a meta-data content management problem, where the content in our case is the gathered information together with all provenance data descriptors. Our objective is to represent and store information in such a way that questions about decision activities could be easily answered and various pieces of information could be retrieved. In order to achieve automatic content handling during decision process reconstructions, a structural format and the meaning of the various attribute terms in the format must be standardized (or at least agreed upon) [10].

We explored two meta-data structures for provenance information representation and three storage formats. The two meta-data structures consist of (key and encoded value) or (subject, predicate, object). The former one is a standard encoding for any meta-data information following the W3C's eXtensible Metadata Language (XML) format and stored in the chronological order (http://www. w3.org/XML/). Fig. 4 shows an example of such a representation. The latter one is based on the W3C's Resource Description Framework (RDF) representation (http://www.w3.org/RDF/) and has been viewed as the structure suitable for semantic integration [2]. We included the RDF representation because users less familiar with the content management problems could experiment with the two metadata representations and quantify the cost-value tradeoffs between storage size and the informational retrieval value when multiple vocabularies for key terms would be referenced from an XML <sup>fi</sup>le or the questions of future interest would include relationships of events.

Among many information storage mechanisms, we have investi gated the use of uncompressed and compressed forms applied to provenance meta-data information representations. Our current implementation reports results for compressed XML <sup>fi</sup>les using gzip compression algorithm and it is also capable of sending uncompressed information to a data base.

We chose the most naïve meta-data representations (XML and RDF) and the most widely used compression tool such as gzip purposefully. When the evaluations are pertinent to forward looking archival issues of <sup>fi</sup>les and technologies (hardware and software) then the most widely used meta-data representations and compression techniques are likely of interest. Other representations and compression techniques could be added as options to the evaluation framework.

## 2.1.4. Real-time visualization of gathered information

A single instance of the event tracker provides four views of the information gathered, such as (1) a temporal sequence of gathered information, (2) a summary of gathered information by information type, (3) a currently viewed image sub-area in the entire image being inspected, and (4) a visualization of multiple line graphs representing the disk space occupied by the gathered information over time per information type.

Multiple instances of event tracker can be executed simultaneously to evaluate disk space storage requirements for different selections of information granularity or for having all four views of the information being gathered. Fig. 5 illustrates these four views from top down on the left side.

## 2.2. Reconstruction of computer-assisted decision making activities

We assume for now that information retrieval from a compressed <sup>fi</sup>le or a database has been accomplished and the future reconstruction could start with all decompressed information about a computerassisted decision process. Given the data, how could one reconstruct computer centric decision process activities in order to construct the most authentic renditions? While not knowing all future questions of interest, we seek an understanding of reconstruction limitations from the image inspection data sets in particular.

## 2.2.1. Classification of retrieved information

Besides the technical challenges of decision process reconstruction, the problem becomes even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult when additional scenarios are considered: (a) the input information might be incomplete, (b) information gathering software or input data might have been compromised or (c) the input data might be in an unknown binary format at the time of reconstruction. We propose to classify information gathered using previously described provenance gathering mechanisms into three categories. First is the ‘interpreted’ information that is retrieved as meta-data about decision activities, e.g., descriptor ‘zoom’ corresponds to some low level computer operations with image values rendered on a screen. The information is represented as ASCII text characters that are easy to read by a human (paper or digital version) as seen in Fig. 4. Second is the category of ‘raw’ information that corresponds to byte representation at the level of the computer program (image inspection tool) reported by a programming language (java in our case) as a base64 encoded streams. Third is the ‘snapshot’ category that provides a screen capture at a sampled time of an active window on the computer screen. It is represented as a temporally ordered sequence of images. The issues related to information authenticity, reliability and validity [27] would be addressed as part of a complete preservation solution.

## 2.2.2. Reconstruction mechanisms

From an evaluation perspective, exploring reconstructions as a function of information granularity to assess the real value (ability to pose and answer questions) is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant importance. Due to the fact that all decisions occur over a period of time, we prototyped reconstructions as temporal replays of decision process activities.

Given all the gathered information, the activity replay can still <sup>fi</sup>lter out subsets of information based on the retrieval categories to simulate the cost-value tradeoffs of reconstructions. Fig. 6 shows the front end of the evaluation component called the event viewer. There are currently four reconstruction mechanisms based on name events, ‘interpreted’ information, ‘raw’ information, and ‘snapshot’ information. When reconstruction based on event name is executed, then only time and ‘event name’ information are available for the reconstruction and the event viewer presents only textual description of activities.

Similarly, when reconstruction is based on ‘interpreted’ information, time, event name and interpreted information are displayed, as well as an additional empty frame with the location and size depicting the sub-area of the image the user was inspecting. The choice of reconstruction based on ‘raw’ information adds to the previous reconstruction type the raw information (shown in Fig. 6 at the bottom left as encoded and decoded information) and the image content for location and size overlay of an inspected image sub-area. Finally, when reconstruction based on ‘snapshot’ information is selected then a sequence of jpg format snapshots is presented as a movie in the event viewer window on the right side.

## 2.2.3. Reconstruction value and its cost

The problem of measuring a real value of any decision process reconstruction is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, as the main focus of the past research has been on understanding how decisions are made [22] and how to manage them [35]. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culties have been encountered in designing tools (a) to examine decision making processes in some domains, such as criminal justice [5], (b) to simulate decision making according to government guidelines (e.g., response to pandemic in<sup>fl</sup>uenza [14]), or (c) to measure and improve crisis decision making (e.g. public health decisions) [29]. According to our knowledge, there are not any existing tools for measuring the value of any decision process reconstruction.

Based on our understanding, the real value of any decision process reconstruction depends on (a) a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization, (b) the time when a reconstruction is evaluated, as the organizationally-recognized value might change over time, and (c) the metric used for measuring the value. We believe that an organization interested in documenting and reconstructing decision activities later in time would be able to map questions about decision activities to organizationally-recognized values and then to recordkeeping requirements. While we are not providing any guidelines for establishing organizationally-recognized values, we posit that reconstructing all decision activities and/or all information presented to decision makers on a computer screen would have a high value. This labeling of high reconstruction value is implicit in our experimental evaluations.

The evaluation framework provides means for assessing a user de<sup>fi</sup>ned reconstruction value but does not explicitly report a quantitative number. Nonetheless, the evaluation software can report the cost of reconstruction. For example, a replay of ‘snapshot’ information is inexpensive in terms of reconstruction (an image viewer is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient) but expensive in terms of storage. On the other hand, a reconstruction from ‘raw’ information requires much less storage but more investment in building the reconstruction software. Finally, the cost of a reconstruction from ‘interpreted’ information is very low (a text viewer) but the value might be limited.

## 3. Experimental evaluations

In this section, we evaluate a simple image inspection based decision. Our experiment was performed on a generic PC with Java Development Kit (JDK) version 5 on Windows XP with Service pack 2 installed. We established a typical decision task where a user observes an aerial image and searches for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c target with certain characteristics known only to the user. Table 1 shows the speci<sup>fi</sup>c sequence of the decision process to <sup>fi</sup>nd a house in an aerial image.

![](/api/attachments/C24R9QCB/fulltext/images/096e2ca1027ca55d52af3a3b62708e804b84c14a61175f1b908657d2d677dfc4.jpg)  
Fig. 7. A plot of hard disk space needed for saving all information granules over time.

The sequence of image operations included loading a <sup>fi</sup>le, zooming to 50% so that the entire image could <sup>fi</sup>t into a viewable window, adjusting the gamma intensity value for better visualization, and then zooming to 250% so that with the help of a scroll bar the house location can be found. Finally, the located target is marked as a point of interest. In general, the sequence of steps and its complexity would become evaluation variables.

We launched four instances of event tracker to gather information at different granularities. The four instances gathered (a) only ‘interpreted’, (b) only ‘raw’, (c) only ‘snapshot’ or (d) all information. Among the 21+5 information granules, only 10 were used during the above described computer-assisted decision process (see Fig. 7). Note that the software automatically creates one record (row in a sequence of events) about each user–computer interaction except from the image pane scrolling activity (“Change visible region”). The software creates multiple records over time as the scroll bar is moved and the view area of the image changes. The exception is imposed in order to know what part of an image was viewed on a computer screen.

When all information was recorded (labeled as ‘everything’) then Change Visible Region (59.55%) and New Image (29.88%) events dominated the storage requirements of about a total of 3,600,000 bytes. When only ‘interpreted’ information was recorded then Change Visible Region (70.26%) event required the most storage space and the total space was only about 10,600 bytes. When only ‘raw’ information was recorded then New Image (97.95%) event dominated the total of 1,000,000 bytes of storage. Finally, when only ‘snapshot’ information was recorded then Change Visible Region (54.74%), Window Snapshot (27.26%) and Change Zoom Factor (11.30%) were the main contributors to the storage requirements from the total of 547,000 bytes.

Based on the quantitative results, we concluded that among all image operations the Change Visible Region event requires the most storage since it was the most frequently used during our experiment. When storing New Image data in the gathered ‘raw’ information, the ‘raw’ information size becomes much larger than the meta-data about image operations. This would be also true when only mouse movements are recorded. It should be also observed that since the ‘snapshot’ information included only rendered images (sub-areas or preview images at coarse resolution) and periodic snapshots of the Gamma popup dialog, it led to a smaller storage requirement than the one for ‘raw’ information. This observation opens a question about the possibility of rendering more from the ‘raw’ information than what was presented during a decision process. In contrast, the ‘snapshot’ pictures could provide only information about what was rendered during a decision process. The evaluation results could also be used for estimating relative storage and execution time requirements. The ratios for ‘only interpreted’ to ‘only raw’ to ‘only snapshot’ to ‘everything’ are (a) approximately 1:100:55:360 for storage requirements and (b) about 1:3.46:16.49:81.42 for execution time requirements.

These computational requirements for information gathering and preservation can now be evaluated from the reconstruction perspective using the event viewer component of the evaluation framework. For instance, given the data sets gathered during the task of <sup>fi</sup>nding a house of certain characteristics in a collection of images, we posed the following two questions of future interest: (1) “What were the house characteristics based on which the image analyst found the house?” and (2) “Was one part of the decision process an inspection of the left lower image corner at the zoom level equal to 250%?” Answering the <sup>fi</sup>rst question requires reconstructing all image sub-areas inspected by an image analyst (and perhaps looking at all houses in the image) and comparing them with the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed house. It is apparent that at least the ‘raw’ information is needed from the information value perspective regardless of the cost. In contrast, the second question might be answered by inspecting the information labeled as ‘only interpreted’ since Change Visible Region and Zoom Factor interpreted events can be extracted at a low cost of preservation and reconstruction.

## 4. Summary

We have researched and prototyped an evaluation framework called “Image Provenance To Learn” (IP2Learn) that is designed for a class of computer-assisted decisions based on image inspection. Our paper addressed several fundamental issues related to documentation and reconstruction of computer-assisted decision processes. Many of these issues and basic questions were turned into parameters of an evaluation framework including information granularity, information gathering mechanism, visualization type for gathered information, information representation and storage, reconstruction mechanisms, and evaluation metrics. A simple experiment using the evaluation framework was presented to illustrate how quantitative values could be obtained for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of understanding the cost-value tradeoff. The software is available for downloading from the NCSA ISDA web page at isda.ncsa.uiuc.edu/download.

The problems related to understanding and forecasting preservation requirements for computer-assisted decision making processes are, in general, very complex. A future direction for our research includes, but is not limited to: exploring a concrete class of anticipated questions of future interest; environments for automated gathering of provenance information; content management problems when using distributed image resources; and reconstruction evaluation approaches.
