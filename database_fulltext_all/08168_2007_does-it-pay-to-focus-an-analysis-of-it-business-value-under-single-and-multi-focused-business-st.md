# Does IT pay to focus? An analysis of IT business value under single and multi-focused business strategies

Paul P. Tallon

Wallace E. Carroll School of Management, Fulton Hall 452A, 140 Commonwealth Avenue, Boston College, Chestnut Hill, MA 02467-3808, USA

Received 14 August 2006; revised 17 January 2007 Available online 29 May 2007

## Abstract

Previous research finds that firm performance is highest when firms maintain a singular strategic focus as opposed to a multi-focused strategy. Yet, from an IT perspective, there is still some debate as to whether IT business value or the contribution of IT to firm performance is also maximized when firms maintain a single-focused strategy. Using the notion of value disciplines to model strategic foci, we find in a matched survey of executives in 241 firms that IT business value is highest in firms with a multi-focused business strategy and lowest in those with a single focus. We also find a relationship between strategic foc and the primary locus of IT value within the value chain for all focus-types except those emphasizing operational excellence. If all firms are using IT to reduce operating expenses, operationally excellent firms may find it increasingly dificult to sustain a low-cost advantage over time through IT. - 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Value disciplines; IT Business value; Strategic focus; Stuck in the middle; Value chain

## 1. Introduction

Despite more than a decade of research into the business value impacts of information technology (IT), executives remain skeptical of whether firms are realizing an adequate return on their IT investment (Carr, 2003; Luftman, 2004). Further complicating the task of IT business value analysis is the fact that as business strategies grow more complex with broad product lines, global operations, and diverse modes of competition, it becomes more dificult to ensure alignment between IT and business strategy (Chan et al., 1997; Palmer and Markus, 2000) and to assess IT business value against goals for IT (Tallon et al., 2000). Facing limited resources and competing strategic priorities, firms have become more selective in their use of IT even as the demands for IT resources have grown (Melville et al., 2004; Wade and Hulland, 2004).

At the same time that firms are struggling to manage the allocation of IT resources and the many applications that has evolved over time, the strategic management literature finds that firms with a single focus underlying their business strategy have higher performance than firms with multi-focused strategies (Dess and Davis, 1984; Robinson and Pearce, 1988; Zahra and Covin, 1993). Porter (1980) refers to firms with multi-focused strategies that combine elements of low-cost leadership, innovation, and niche as stuck in the middle; their lack of focus, indecisiveness, and desire to be all things to all people breeds confusion in the marketplace and leads to mediocre firm performance at best. While this might lead firms to adopt a single focus, as seen in such firms as Ryanair (low-cost leadership), 3M (innovation), and Cartier (niche), the changing nature of competition has forced firms to diversify their strategic risk through multi-focused strategies. Thus, Delta, United, KLM, and Air Canada have created low-cost subsidiaries to target specific routes while Merrill Lynch has added an online, low-cost, self-service division to supplement its range of high-priced investor services. Similarly, Charles Schwab and other discount brokers have acquired private trusts and boutique financial services providers in an attempt to compete directly with their full-service rivals. While the literature might warn against becoming stuck in the middle, as Delta learned in mid 2006 when financia losses forced it to discontinue its nascent low-cost division, uncertainty and competitive rivalries are forcing firms to rethink and broaden their strategies. For many firms, it would seem that being stuck in the middle with multi-focused strategies is no longer an option – it is perhaps a matter of survival.

From an IT perspective, it is logical to ask if the same issues that cause stuck in the mid dle firms to sufer mediocre performance – channel and pricing conflicts, confused customers – might also translate into weak payofs from IT. Quite simply, could anecdotal claims that IT is not creating suficient value be linked to the growing complexity of business strategies? One can say, for example, that IT business value might sufer if firms are forced to allocate a shrinking IT budget across a number of potentially competing strategies. Diverse strategies can mean fewer opportunities for economies of scope and the possibility that some IT projects may be under-funded or shelved for reasons of political expediency (Austin et al., 2005; Dewan et al., 1998). However, it could also be argued that in an era of shared infrastructure, web services, and flexible technologies, more IT business value – not less – might be garnered by firms using multi-focused strategies (Weill et al., 2002). For example, Abercrombie and Fitch have achieved significant value by leveraging a shared IT infrastructure and applications to support their Hollister and sportswear brands.

In this paper, we use a multi-theoretic lens to argue that, while firms with multi-focused business strategies may be vulnerable to lower firm performance, IT business value at a process and firm-level can, paradoxically, be higher than in firms with a single-focused strategy. As later revealed, data from matched surveys of business and information systems (IS) executives at 241 firms verify that IT business value is, indeed, higher for multi-focused firms. The significance of this result is that if firms must become stuck in the middle in order to cope with uncertainty or competitive expediency that the value created by IT can ofset some of the downward pressure on firm performance. Just as we have observed in other areas of the literature that IT can sustain an inefective hierarchy where a market mechanism may be the preferred contractual form (Malone et al., 1987), IT can sustain stuck in the middle firms as they try to compete. Value can come, for example, from the IT-based coordination mechanisms that allow firms to manage disparate activities. Our contribution to the literature, therefore, is not only to extend our understanding of IT business value but to create insights into how the choices shaping business strategy in today’s world can afect the extent of IT business value.

In the next section, we assess various theories of business strategy to show how IT business value is related to strategic foci. After outlining our primary hypotheses, we review our data and measures. We specifically evaluate IT business value in terms of IT impacts at the process-level, thus providing a more detailed analysis than firm-level measures alone. After presenting our analysis, we discuss our results and their implications for research and practice. Finally, we assess future research opportunities and conclude.

## 2. A theory of strategic focus

Chandler (1962) defines business strategy as ‘‘the determination of the basic long term goals of an enterprise, and the adoption of courses of action and allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals’’ (p. 13). Over time, several typologies have evolved to denote the myriad strategies that firms have tended to pursue. For instance, Miles and Snow (1978) were among the first to classify strategy into broad headings (prospector, defender, analyzer, and reactor). Later, Porter (1980) outlined a set of generic strategies (low-cost leadership, diferentiation, and focus) to help firms manage the competitive forces in an industry. Recently, building on the generic strategies proposed by Porter, Treacy and Wiersema (1995) use anecdotal and case study analysis to describe three value disciplines (operational excellence, customer intimacy, and product leadership) that firms can use to create value for their customers and markets.

While empirical studies have reported that it can be dificult to classify firms within these various typologies, the dominant conclusion is that it pays to pick a focus and to execute against it. For example, firms that focus on product quality and innovation – such as prospectors in the Miles and Snow typology – outperform all others (Hambrick, 1983). Those that focus on cost eficiency or that lack a clear focus (if they have multiple foci) have the lowest performance (Dess and Davis, 1984; Robinson and Pearce, 1988; White, 1986). Equally, Treacy and Wiersema (1995) imply from anecdotal evidence that firms that do not excel at one of the three value disciplines are ‘‘in a muddle . . . [pursuing] hybrid operating models that are neither here nor there, and that consequently cause confusion, tension, and loss of energy’’ (p. 45).

There are several reasons for why firms with narrowly-defined or focused business strategies tend to have higher firm performance. Firms that diferentiate themselves on some dimension of value such as superior customer service or product and service variety can command a price premium from customers. Toyota, for example, has been able to charge full-price for their hybrid Prius model (a product innovation) while Dell has higher margins due, in part, to their ability to customize product oferings (diferentia tion). Firms that compete on the basis of cost are more likely to use standardization or mass production as a way to stay competitive. With little opportunity to earn a pre mium from product diferentiation or customer service, new entrants and price wars can damage the profit margins of low-cost leaders (Porter, 1980). In efect, low-cost leaders face a vicious cycle of falling prices and shrinking margins. Thus, while it pays to focus strategy on an area of specialization, the consensus is that low cost is not a viable focus by itself and so it needs to complement one of the other foci in order to be truly efective (White, 1986).

## 2.1. IT and business strategy: searching for value

Since researchers successfully dispelled the IT productivity paradox (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996; Dewan and Min, 1997), there has been a significant body of work attesting to the positive link between IT investment and firm performance (Kohli and Devaraj, 2003; Melville et al., 2004; Piccoli and Ives, 2005). As such, Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1996) posit that, ‘‘the next round of work should focus on identifying the strategies which have led to large IT productivity’’ (p. 557). The contribution of such strategies is evident in the fact that firm idiosyncracies, of which business strategy is a quintessential form, account for half of the productivity gains imputed to IT (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1995). Research on strategic alignment or the fit between IT and business strategy is useful in explaining how diferences in business strategy afect this relationship between IT and firm performance. To the extent that firms can align IT to business strategy – assigning resources to activities that are central to business strategy or using IT in a way that supports the strategy – IT will make a greater contribution to firm performance than if IT is at odds with the business strategy (Chan et al., 1997; Palmer and Markus, 2000). However, this stream of research has not sought to examine if IT business value is in any way related to the type of strategy that a firm employs and whether that strategy is single-focused or multi-focused. Only Sabherwal and Chan (2001) and Chan et al., 2006) have examined diferences in business strategy, finding a positive correlation between strategic alignment and perceived firm performance for prospectors and analyzers using the Miles and Snow typology but not for defenders. However, this does not say if there are diferences in IT business value between strategies, and so we next provide a theoretical rationale for how IT business value may be related to strategic focus. Our arguments will suggest that stuck in the middle firms with multi-focused business strategies are better positioned to realize higher IT business value than firms with single-focused strategies.

Using Porter’s (1996) argument that superior performance is shaped by operational efectiveness, (performing similar activities better than competitors), strategic positioning (performing similar activities in diferent ways or diferent activities), or a combination of both, Tallon et al. (2000) find a link between strategic intent for IT and IT business value. Firms whose IT goals reflect either operational efectiveness or strategic positioning realize lower value from IT – measured on a process-by-process basis – across the value chain than firms with a simultaneous focus on both operational excellence and strategic positioning. Broad goals for IT, therefore, translate into higher IT business value. Consistent with previous research on strategic alignment, firms with broad or multi-focused business strategies will have multiple goals for IT (Chan et al., 1997). Thus, as seen in a financial services context, relationship or one-on-one banking relies on data mining to discover cross-sell opportunities but IT must also reduce customer service costs in order for relationship banking to scale (Booz-Allen-Hamilton, 2006). As banks such as ING Direct, HSBC, and E-Loan have broadened their strategies to target new customer segments, their strategic goals for IT have also broadened (Leenders et al., 2002). Alignment theory would suggest, therefore, that firms with multi-focused business strategies will realize higher value from IT on account of their broader goals for IT.

A similar argument can be found in the unfolding theory of dynamic capabilities that has emerged from the resource-based view of the firm (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000; Teece et al., 1997). While core IT resources such as hardware, software, networks and IT skills can be easily replicated or procured in factor markets, the way that IT is imbedded into business processes or combined with complementary resources such as strategic foresight or knowledge is much more dificult to replicate (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1998; Mata et al., 1995; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997; Ray et al., 2005). Sambamurthy et al. (2003) find that IT capabilities around adaptiveness shape agility which, in turn, afects the number of competitive actions that a firm can undertake; the number of competitive actions directly underscores the complexity or multi-focused character of business strategy (Miller et al., 1996). Byrd and Turner (2001) show that capabilities based on software modularity and hardware integration – key components of a flexible IT infrastructure – afect a firm’s ability to use IT for competitive advantage. As we link these arguments back to stuck in the middle firms, we note from previous research that multi-focused strategies are a classic feature of firms in volatile markets (Miller and Chen, 1996). Sambamurthy et al. (2003) further argue that a firm’s ability to be agile across a broad range of competitive activities is conditional on holding digital options – a concept emanating from research on real options that recognizes how flexible IT gives firms the option to move in diferent ways. Combining these arguments, we see how firms with multi-focused business strategies may be able to use flexible or adaptable IT to realize higher IT business value than single-focused firms. Thus:

H1. IT business value, as measured at either the process or firm-level, is higher in firms with multi-focused business strategies than in firms with a single-focused business strategy.

## 2.2. The locus of IT business value

Perhaps not surprisingly, the strategic management literature shows that the activities undertaken by firms vary based on their business strategy. Firms that espouse a low-cost strategy place a great deal of emphasis on procurement and availability of raw materials, speed, and production eficiency – less so on customer service, advertising, and product breadth; those favoring diferentiation as their primary strategy focus more on innovation, product development and brand management, while those pursuing niche focus more on customer service, sales, marketing, pricing, and customization (Dess and Davis, 1984; Robinson and Pearce, 1988; Segev, 1989). The generic value chain uses a similar argument in identifying how firms create value by focusing on particular processes that are essential to their chosen strategy (Porter, 1985). What this means, however, is that based on the particular strategy chosen by a firm, some processes in the value chain will be more important than others. Returning to our examples of firms with a single strategic focus, Ryanair, Europe’s low-cost airline, pays a lot of attention to operations in order to strip costs out of as many activities as possible but cares relatively little about customer-facing processes since they do not diferentiate between customer segments in the same way as British Airways, Cathay Pacific, or United.

We can use this argument to make a similar claim for where IT business value will most likely be found in firms. For example, when a firm emphasizing a low-cost strategy concentrates its IT in processes that are critical to that strategy, we would expect IT business value to be higher in those processes than in more peripheral processes. This argument is consistent with prior research on strategic alignment. Palmer and Markus (2000), for example, identify processes where diferent business strategies call for greater or lesser con centrations of IT use. As noted in past research, IT use is a key determinant of IT business value (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Lucas, 1975). Based on the strategic management litera ture, for firms focusing on low-cost leadership, we would expect value from IT to be greatest in supplier relations, and production and operations. Equally, firms that favor a diferentiation strategy would expect greater IT business value in product development and R&D processes, while those pursuing a niche strategy would expect to see IT business value concentrated in sales, marketing, and customer relations processes. Thus:

H2. Firms that focus on a low-cost leadership strategy will see IT business value concentrated in processing dealing with supplier relations, production, and operations.

H3. Firms that focus on a differentiation or innovation strategy will see IT business value concentrated in processes dealing with product development and R&D.

H4. Firms that focus on a niche or customer strategy will see IT business value concentrated in processes dealing with marketing, sales, and customer relations.

## 3. Data collection and analysis

To test these hypotheses, in 2002 we surveyed 1600 firms, randomly drawn from a population of 2826 publicly-traded firms, identified in S&P Compustat as having 2001 sales in the range: \$100 million to \$3 billion. In order to contain respondent bias, a matched survey methodology was adopted (Podsakof et al., 2003). First, a senior business executive was chosen to report on perceived measures of IT business value at the process-level; these measures are discussed in detail in the next section. Potential respondents were identified using Hoovers, a subscription-based website that lists function-level managers and senior executives in each firm; preference was given to persons whose title suggested responsibility for strategic planning, business development or strategic management. We selected one such executive per firm. CFOs were used as default respondents if there were no identifi able individuals with strategy oversight. Second, we identified a CIO or senior IT executive in each firm using the Spring 2002 Directory of Top Computer Executives published by Applied Computer Research. This second part of the match was used to identify business strategy, IT spending, and details of IT infrastructure. To safeguard against the possibility that IT executives may not be the most qualified individuals for reporting business strategy, additional steps were taken to validate the strategy data given by CIOs; these additional steps are discussed in detail later.

From this sub-sample, matched responses were received from 241 firms yielding a 15% response rate. This response rate is on a par with matched surveys elsewhere in the IS literature, such as a 13% rate reported by Sabherwal and Chan (2001). An analysis of variance on financial data drawn from Compustat (total assets, sales, and net profit) finds that the 241 firms in our sample are representative of our overall population. We also compared financial data for firms that replied during the first and last month of data collection; no significant diferences were noted. A random analysis of 100 non-responsive firms revealed that targeted respondents were traveling, had insuficient time to devote to the survey, or their firms had a policy precluding participation in academic surveys. These 100 firms were not significantly diferent from the firms in our sample or population. Table 1 reports a summary of our matched sample and respondents; average revenue (in 2001) for the firms in our sample is \$798 million (variance: \$1,916 million).

## 3.1. Measuring business strategy

While there are several established ways of measuring business strategy, self-typing continues to be among the most popular. A well-known weakness with this approach, however, is that respondents can sometimes be torn between two strategy types (as might be expected of firms that are stuck in the middle) and so asking respondents to pick just one type introduces a degree of error into the measurement process. Alternatively, other researchers have advocated the use of Likert scales to tease out diferent elements of a strategy (Venkatraman, 1989); cluster analysis can then be used to reveal strategic groups that share key elements of strategy. However, it can by dificult to interpret or label these clusters in order to capture the unique idiosyncracies of their member firms (Dess and Davis, 1984; Zahra, 1996). Recognizing that both approaches have their respective shortcomings, we opted to use self-typing but to build a series of checks into our analysis to confirm that what executives were reporting was accurate and meaningful.

A potential solution is to use the Treacy and Wiersema (1995) typology of operational excellence, customer intimacy and product leadership – labels that are based on extensive case-based research, have meaning in the eyes of key executives, and more importantly map to Porter’s typology as follows: low-cost leadership (operational excellence), niche (customer intimacy), and diferentiation (product leadership). As noted by Treacy and Wiersema (1995), ‘‘operationally excellent companies deliver a combination of quality, price, and ease of purchase . . . they are not product or service innovators, nor do they cultivate one-to-one relationships with their customers. [They] don’t pursue transactions; they cultivate relationships’’ (p. 40), while product leadership firms ofer ‘‘products or services that expand existing performance boundaries. . . [They aim] to provide markets with leadingedge products or useful new applications of existing products or services’’ (p. 37). To collect data on these value disciplines, we asked respondents to allocate 100 points among the three value disciplines with the greatest allocation of points going to that discipline that indicates the main focus of their business strategy. This method allows executives to identify a clear and unambiguous focus if appropriate, for example 100–0–0, or a more mixed focus such as 50–30–20 or 33–33–34. This approach to identifying business strategy has been tested in past research and has been found accurate and reliable to the extent that executives in the same firm assign points similarly (Tallon and Kraemer, 2006).

## 3.2. Measuring IT business value

While IS researchers have tended to view IT impacts purely in terms of how IT has contributed to firm-level metrics such as profit, productivity, market capitalization, sales, etc. (Dehning and Richardson, 2002), there is a growing acceptance of the need to review IT impacts at the process-level by virtue of the argument that the first-order impacts of IT spending occur at the process-level (Barua et al., 1995; Barua and Mukhopadhyay, 2000; Melville et al., 2004). While there tends to be a lack of objective process-level metrics – if data are not collected or firms are unwilling to share it – perceptions have emerged as a useful barometer of IT business value (Tallon and Kraemer, 2006). The belief that perceptions are distorted and biased to the point of being irrelevant has also been disproved by research that finds a significant positive correlation between objective and perceptual measures of IT business value (Tallon and Kraemer, 2007).

Building on prior research by Mahmood and Soon (1991), Mooney et al. (1995), Sethi and King (1994), and Tallon et al. (2000), Tallon and Kraemer (2006) construct a multiitem instrument to evaluate executives’ perceptions of IT business value at various processlevel points along the value chain. For the purposes of this study, we adapted 25 of the 44 items tested in their research to represent the five primary processes in the value chain: supplier relations, production and operations, produce and service enhancement, marketing and sales support, and customer relations (five items per process). These items were added to the business executive instrument with the following introductory question: How much impact has IT had on your company’s performance in each of the following areas? Please limit your appraisal to value already realized rather than value expected in the future. Respondents used a seven-point Likert scale anchored on ‘‘low IT impact’’ and ‘‘high IT impact’’. A complete list of all items appears in Appendix A.

## 3.3. Construct validation

In order to validate the IT business value items, we first undertook an exploratory factor analysis. Using principal components analysis with varimax rotation, a six-factor structure arose explaining 71.2% of the total variance. All items factored under their expected process headings. Harmon’s one-factor test, recommended by Podsakof and Organ (1986), confirms that our factor structure is not dominated by one factor and so common method bias is not a key issue in using these IT business value items. Factor scores and descriptive details for all items are shown in Table 3. Lastly, internal consistency or item reliability – measured by Cronbach’s a – is consistently above a suggested minimum of 0.80 (Nunnally, 1978).

We also reviewed all items for convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity tests if the indicators of a factor correlate higher among themselves than with indicators of another factor, while discriminant validity determines if the indicators of a specific factor load higher on that factor than on any other factor. To test both forms of validity, the correlation between each factor-pair must be less than the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each factor, while the AVE should also be above a recom mended minimum of 0.50 (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). As shown in Table 4, all factors pass both tests, showing that our factor structure is valid. However, it can also be noted that some of the correlations in Table 4 seem high – two of the correlations are above 0.50. These data do not negate our earlier factor analysis. Rather, correlations of this magnitude reveal the likely existence of a second-order factor, which in this case can be construed as a firm-level proxy for IT business value.<sup>2</sup> This view is consistent with the generalized IT business value model outlined in Barua and Mukhopadhyay (2000) where value percolates from the process-level up to the firm-level. However, rather than try to aggregate IT business value from the process-level upwards, our sense is that if the first-order impacts of IT materialize at the process-level, then it is appropriate to continue working with a more disaggregated set of process-level measures.

## 3.4. Strategic focus and IT business value

In order to determine from our value discipline measures whether firms maintain a single focus or a multi-focused business strategy, we needed to apply a series of rules to the data. Since respondents had the option to allocate points to any of the three value disciplines, we first decided that for firms to have an unambiguous and clear single focus that one and only one discipline should have received an allocation of 50 points or higher. This rule yielded 167 single focus firms as follows: 96 operationally excellent (OE); 38 customer intimate (CI), and 33 product leaders (PL). While this means that the remaining 74 firms in our sample would be classified as stuck in the middle, we decided to further analyze this residual number to isolate firms pursuing two foci. Part of our motivation for doing this was based on data in White (1986) who found that some firms liked to combine a diferentiation or niche strategy with low-cost. To uncover such dual-focus firms, we applied a second rule of thumb that identified any firms that, while not having a dominant single focus, had allocated 80 or more points across any two foci. Of the 74 stuck in the middle firms, this rule generated: 5 (CI + PL), 21 (OE + CI), and 6 (OE + PL); the remaining 42 firms can, therefore, be truly described as stuck in the middle with allocations such as 35– 35–30. Since these rules of thumb can be criticized as being somewhat arbitrary, we used discriminant analysis on the raw data supplied for each value discipline. As noted in Table 5, this analysis correctly predicts 90% of all classifications and 100% of the 42 firms that, by the end of our analysis, had been definitively branded as stuck in the middle.

As a further face validity check on these strategy measures, we extracted financial data from S&P Compustat for 2002 and then ran a series of correlations between these data and the raw data on the three value disciplines collected from our surveys. The financial metrics used for this analysis were margin and asset turnover (a test of profitability and productivity for low-cost leadership), return on equity or ROE (a profit measure linked to customer intimacy), and sales markup (a measure of how much price premium is added to production costs to obtain the sales price – high markup is synonymous with product leadership). As seen in Table 6, an interesting set of face validity results emerge from this analysis. First, a correlation of -0.125 between OE and profit margin shows that the more that firms focus on low-cost leadership, the lower their profit margin; this erosion of profits is confirmed by a correlation of -0.116 between OE and ROE, and -0.182 between OE and markup. Yet, OE firms are most efective in terms of asset turnover (sales divided by total assets) – a measure of productivity showing how total assets (inputs) are leveraged to generate sales (outputs). To assess the first hypothesis that IT business value will be highest in multi-focused firms than in single-focus firms, we first computed a proxy measure of IT business value in each process by averaging the individual items loading on each process, as seen in Table 3. We also created a proxy for firm-level IT business value by averaging across the five process-level averages. Next, using the assignment totals from the second column of Table 5, we used a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to ascertain if there are significant differences in these process and firm-level measures across the various focus types. As 11 dual focus firms could not be verified through discriminant analysis, namely ${ \mathrm { O E } } + { \mathrm { P L } } \left( N = 6 \right)$ and $\mathrm { C I } + \mathrm { P L } \left( N = 5 \right)$ , we decided to approach this aspect of our hypothesis testing in several ways. As shown in Table 7, we ran ANOVA first on two groups to compare singlefocus firms with all others, then on three groups made up of all single-focus firms, dual focus firms, and all remaining stuck in the middle firms and, finally, on the seven groups in the second column of Table 5 that give the most detailed breakdown of our focus types.

Before reviewing the results in Table 7, it is worth noting that ANOVA assumes equal variances across the groups under review. Keppel (1991) claims that F tests can be ‘‘relatively insensitive to the presence of variance heterogeneity, except when unequal sample sizes were involved’’ (p. 98). For the three versions of ANOVA identified in Table 7, the problem of unequal sample sizes is particularly acute in the final panel where two groups, $\mathrm { O E + P I }$ and $\mathrm { C I } + \mathrm { P I }$ , have sample sizes of six and five, respectively. It is still appropriate to retain each group and to be confident in the ANOVA results if Levene’s statistic – a widely known test for equal variances – fails to reject the null hypothesis that variances within groups are not significantly diferent. In applying this test, we found an insignificant result $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ for IT business value in each process and overall. Accordingly, group size diferences do not appear to have biased the analysis in Table 7.

In reviewing the results in Table 7, an interesting and consistent pattern emerges. Beginning with the top panel, for each process, multi-focus firms realize higher IT business value than single-focus firms, with several processes showing statistically significant diferences. A similar pattern occurs in the second panel when comparing single focus, dual focus, and stuck in the middle firms. Single focus firms achieve lower IT business value in several processes, most notably involving production and operations, product and service enhancement, and customer relations – areas where one might expect single-focused firms to have higher IT business value. When all seven groups are examined in the final panel, stuck in the middle firms are again, with the exception of the six OE + PL firms, the highest in terms of perceived IT business value across all processes of the value chain. These findings indicate strong support for H1 showing that, contrary to the strategic management literature where single focus firms are shown to achieve greater firm performance, in terms of IT business value, single focus firms realize significantly less value from IT than multifocused firms that are either dual focused or stuck in the middle.

A review of the data in the lower part of Table 7 indicates that for operationally excellent firms, for example, the primary locus of IT business value lies in production and operations as expected. Similarly, customer intimate firms report high IT business value in customer relations. One possibility is to use correlation analysis to see if strategic foci and processlevel IT impacts move in unison. If we believe, for example, that product leaders will see IT impacts concentrated around R&D activities in product and service enhancement, then the more that firms focus on being a product leader, the more likely we should be to see a commensurate increase in IT business value in this area. A series of regression analyses yield a similar set of results but as our goal is not to explain variance in the dependent variable but simply to test associativeness, correlation analysis is suficient; the result of this correlation analysis appears in Table 8.

In addition to Pearson correlations, we also reviewed partial correlations in order to partial out the efects of firm size which might, by virtue of scale efects, cause IT business value to be higher regardless of strategic focus, and rates of IT spending (IT spending as a percentage of sales reported in the CIO/IT Executive survey) that can also afect higher IT impacts independent of strategic focus. An interesting pattern of results emerges from this analysis with Pearson and partial correlations being numerically similar across all processes. We had argued in H2 that operationally excellent firms would see IT business value concentrated in supplier relations, and production and operations. Our results in Table 8 do not support this contention; in fact, the opposite seems to hold. Operationally excellent firms perform no better, nor no worse, than other firms in terms of creating IT business value in these areas. What are equally interesting are the significant negative results in each of the remaining processes (product and service enhancement, marketing and sales support, customer relations). A negative correlation indicates that as firms place a greater emphasis on operational excellence, IT impacts begin to decline in these other areas. This is not unexpected to the extent that firms are forced to draw down IT resources from processes that are not central to their cost or eficiency-driven strategy. However, an absence of significant positive correlations in supplier relations and production and operations – processes that are vital to their strategy – is troubling indeed for it shows that IT may not be the best way to sustain firm performance if pressure to lower prices erupts into a broader price war. If all firms, regardless of strategic focus, are using IT to reduce operating costs, then firms that compete purely on the basis of cost may find it increasingly dificult to achieve diferentiation in the marketplace. We saw previously in Table 6 that operationally excellent firms are already under enormous pressure from falling margins, lower ROE, and declining sales markup. While research has shown that IT can reduce labor and operating costs (Alpar and Kim, 1990; Rai et al., 1997; Shin, 1997), our results suggest that at some point, further cost benefits may be infeasible, forcing these firms to rethink or perhaps expand the focus of their business strategy away from pure operational excellence. In summary, therefore, H2 is not supported.

## 4. Discussion

An important question raised by our analysis is why do single-focus firms fare better than multi-focused firms in terms of financial performance metrics (reported in Table 6 and consistent with previous research in the strategic management literature) but yet fail to see significantly higher business value from IT? The resource-based view of the firm ofers a theoretical rationale for why this might be.<sup>3</sup> Focusing on just one area of strategy such as low-cost leadership, diferentiation or niche can lead to highly specific IT investments as firms push for increased economies of scale through IT. A likely downside of focused IT investments, however, is the potential for inflexibility through rigidity traps which, in the event of market change, could impose a cap on IT impacts (Bharadwaj, 2000). Diversification, not just in terms of product or service variety but the types of competition used by firms, can place significant coordination pressures on managers to leverage IT investments for use in other contexts. Dewan et al. (1998) find that the rate of IT spending increases with related diversification. Single-focused firms are less inclined to diversify – for them, competition is about execution in and around a narrow band of activ ities. The resource based view recognizes that part of the value of IT comes from using IT in diferent ways. Lacking in diversification, single-focus firms are more likely to be homogeneous in their use of IT; homogeneity may be particularly prevalent among low-cost leaders where IT is used to reduce costs through task automation and increased productivity. The net result is that while single-focused firms can still realize business value from IT, the specialized nature of their IT will likely limit their use in other contexts. The resource based view would also argue that whatever benefit single-focused firms generate from IT could be competed away if their IT resources can be replicated. The complex nature of strategy in multi-focused firms may make it less likely that competitors will be able to discern how IT is used in order to replicate it fully; this can help to protect (at least in the short term) some of the business value that such firms realize from IT.

As argued earlier, multi-focused firms may also be in a position to leverage shared infrastructure and applications across multiple strategies (Weill and Broadbent, 1998). Even if multi-focused firms need higher levels of IT spending – although we found no evidence to support this – there are more ways to use the same investments to generate value whether through reduced costs, customized products and services, or superior levels of customer service. Research is also beginning to pay closer attention to how IT afects strategic agility or ambidexterity in how firms create options for responding to unexpected market change (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Weill et al., 2002). Strategy in multi-focused firms is complex in terms of the number and variety of competitive actions (Miller et al., 1996). Strategic complexity is often a response to growing environmental volatility and uncertainty as to how to compete in the marketplace. Multi-focused firms do not want to limit their options by confining IT to just one area of competition – they must be able to compete across a broad front. Certainly, firm performance may sufer if competition forces firms to cut prices or provide a higher level of service for the same price. For multi-focused firms, these issues could arise simultaneously, as was the case in the brokerage industry after the dot-com collapse in 2001. Even if financial performance declines, an ability to use IT for greater coordination and adaptation across a broad spectrum of activities could at least compensate for some of the downward pressure on firm profitability.

A more troubling aspect of our results concerns the fact that operationally excellent firms, despite their proclivity towards low cost competition and an emphasis on driving eficiencies through production processes, are unable to dominate other firms in using IT to create a greater level of performance in these processes. Treacy and Wiersema (1995) argue that a key challenge to sustaining a strategy of operational excellence is ‘‘to shift to the next generation of ‘no frills’ standardized assets to achieve the next level of eficiency’’ (p. 192). In trying to sustain a cost advantage, operationally excellent firms may face growing encroachment from customer intimate or product leadership firms who are equally efective at using IT to reduce costs but who also have an alternative way to diferentiate their products and services besides cost. Thus, in the airline industry, there has been some criticism of Ryanair who, in an attempt to further reduce costs, have started to charge for wheelchair access, use non-reclining seats, and remove seat back pockets and in-flight magazines. As Southwest Airlines has discovered, moving to the next generation of no frills entails some degree of customer diferentiation, prompting consideration of how IT can boost the quality of passenger service rather than using cost as an excuse for eliminating service entirely (Kontzer, 2005).

Recognizing that the question of how to generate value from IT remains foremost in the minds of IS executives (Luftman et al., 2006), our analysis shows that while past studies might admonish firms for becoming stuck in the middle by failing to select a clear focus (Porter, 1980; Treacy and Wiersema, 1995; White, 1986), IT can help to ofset some of the sources of confusion and tension that are said to limit firm performance. Our analysis has implications for these multi-focused firms in that while we do not condone ‘‘muddling through’’ (Treacy and Wiersema, 1995), the higher IT business value that dual focus and stuck in the middle firms report (Table 7) may ofer some sense of relief. Our results also have implications for research in that, supplementing what we already know about alignment between IT and business strategy, the nature of business strategy is also a factor in firms’ ability to realize value from IT investment. While we do not assess whether the particular strategy that a firm has chosen is appropriate for its environment, the fact that strategy is important provides insight into analyses elsewhere in the literature as to the role of work practices. Practices such as TQM, team work and employee involvement are seen as important to IT impacts (Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1997; Melville et al., 2004; Ramirez et al., 2002). Our results may help to explain why these practices matter, to the extent that they assist with the execution of business strategy.

We contribute to the literature on IT business value in two ways. First, contrary to what we might expect based on the strategic management literature, firms with a narrow strategic focus realize less value from IT than those that espouse a broad or multi-focused strategy. For these stuck in the middle firms, IT seems critical to being able to simultaneously pursue multiple business goals. Second, we extend research on IT impacts at the process-level by showing how the level and locus of IT business value is sensitive to the type of strategic foci underlying a business strategy. Related to this, we show that a strategy based on operational excellence – equivalent to low-cost leadership within Porter’s (1980) typology – is unlikely to be sustained through IT. While IT can generate eficiency gains for these firms, those with rival strategies can garner similar returns. Echoing past research that depicts a low-cost strategy as unsustainable unless it can be combined with a diferentiation or customer focus (niche) strategy (Dess and Davis, 1984; White, 1986), IT can support but will unlikely sustain a low-cost strategy. Instead, firms that are inclined towards innovation or niche strategies appear to be developing a low-cost presence as noted in the airline industry and increasingly among securities firms in the bro kerage industry.

## 4.1. Limitations and future research

There are several limitations in this study that could possibly undermine our results. First, we use perceptions of IT business value at the process-level in lieu of objective measures. There has always been a question as to the accuracy of perceptions out of an abundance of concern for bias and error. Although recent research shows that executives perceptions of IT business value are more fact that fiction, there is always a possibility that what executives perceive by way of process-level IT impacts is not what flows to the finan cial statements (Tallon and Kraemer, 2007). Second, we use self-typing to identify strategy using value disciplines. One could argue that the measures used are overly simplistic or incapable of accurately reflecting the complexity of business strategy in modern firms. Third, although we use matched surveys, we measure both IT business value and business strategy through the eyes of just one person in each firm. While there is some evidence in past research of consensus among executives in the same firm on both IT business value and strategy (Tallon and Kraemer, 2006), we did not have data to test if consensus existed in this research. Fourth, our data are cross sectional and so we cannot say if changes in strategy over time have any impact on IT business value. Lastly, our sample involved small to medium size firms with sales in the range \$100 million to \$3 billion (in efect, omitting all Fortune 500 firms which were the focus of a separate study) and so we caution against generalizing our findings to firms outside this range.

Besides addressing these limitations, future research could explore the role of strategic alignment in multi-focused firms in order to identify if alignment is instrumental in allowing these firms to achieve a higher return on their IT investment. There is some evidence from previous studies that firms with multi-focused strategies employ more flexible IT to coordinate their activities (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Weill and Broadbent, 1998), and so as noted earlier, flexibility may be the reason why multi-focused firms have higher IT impacts. Future research could investigate this argument in more detail. Finally, we did not look at the role played by management in setting or executing business strategy. For firms that are stuck in the middle, it may be appropriate to review the interaction between IS and business executives to see if closer cooperation is contributing to the higher IT impacts reported by these firms (Bassellier et al., 2003).

## 5. Conclusion

When we began this research, it was to resolve if diferences in strategic foci had an impact on IT business value. In the post-productivity paradox era, as researchers turn to uncovering how, rather than if, IT contributes to firm performance, knowing that strategic choice afects the level and locus of IT impacts within the value chain helps firms to know where IT resources should be located to help in the execution of their business strategy. The strategic management literature has shown on several occasions that it pays to pick a single focus rather than trying to half-heartedly pursue a multi-focused strategy. In this research, we show that broader is better when it comes to IT business value. For firms whose environments require multi-focused strategies, IT emerges as an important source of value. We argued previously that in an era of growing economic uncertainty, globalization, and fickle consumers, a stuck in the middle strategy may be the only way for firms to survive. Firms are increasingly trying to be all things to all people whether in financial services, airline travel, retail or auto manufacturing. Interestingly, IT may be the key to allowing a firm to muddle through, perhaps prolonging a situation when a firm cannot decide what it wants to be or how it should compete, but also giving it the option to learn how markets behave before deciding to pick a single focus. As researchers move towards a better understanding of how IT provides value, these insights could prove useful as the breadth of explanatory variables and moderating relationships is expanded.
