## Abstract

Information technologies can transform an organisation’s capacity to act effectively and achieve its performance aims. This theoretical paper presents a framework, adapted from the work of the semiotician A.J. Greimas, for qualitatively analysing this shaping of organisational behaviour and effectiveness around the use of IT. This framework permits various forms of capability or constraint in organisational action to be distinguished, in terms of the functional capacities of IT systems or key dimensions of social structure. A multi-faceted consideration of positive and negative outcomes of IT-based activities is thus supported. The use of this framework is demonstrated through brief case examples that illustrate its utility in providing a dynamic perspective on organisational performance and alignment in the use of IT. This theoretical framework equips IS interpretive studies to analyse the shaping of IT-based organisational activity from a standpoint of structures of behaviour or significance in a context of purposeful action.

## Introduction

IT resources or assets have been seen to enable better performance only when leveraged in tandem with complementary human abilities in an organizational context (Powell and Dent-Mitcallef, 1997). The IS field thus requires theoretical frameworks that can clarify the effects of IT in relation to the particular context of social or organisational behaviour in which its use is embedded (Lee, 1999).

This conceptual paper proposes and illustrates a new framework for carrying out a qualitative analysis of ITbased organisational functioning in terms of social dimensions of behaviour. This framework is intended to complement, not supplant, past frameworks on the shaping of organisational action in the use of IT (e.g. Orlikowski, 2000). In particular, it aims to treat certain aspects of the constitution of organisational capability or effectiveness that past IS theorising has not sufficiently addressed, namely: (i) the way organisational performance in the use of IT is shaped by conflicts or contradictions that arise in the course of action; and (ii) the characterisation in behavioural terms of the form or nature of alignment in IT-based organisational action.

This framework’s theoretical underpinning is adapted from the work of a leading French semiotician, Greimas (1987), on the structural organisation of human behaviour. Some justification is necessary for turning to Greimas’ work to augment the theorising of IT-based practices. Greimas, adopting a direction common among European semiotic theorists, worked on the perspective that semiotic theory is not merely restricted to a study of text/communication, but encompasses the more global phenomena of social behaviour or cultural practices (Eco, 1973). Gottdiener (1995, 17) notes this: ‘Greimas extended his interpretive paradigm to include the structure of social action, creating a general semiotic approach to all of culture’. In light of a recommendation by Orlikowski and Robey (1991) that IS studies should be informed by a theory of social organisation or structure, Greimas’ (1987) ideas were found to be relevant and useful for illuminating the nature of IT-based practices. This paper will demonstrate that there are key benefits to be derived from using his semiotic framework.

This framework will interest IS researchers concerned with the capability of IT systems to mediate or alter organisational functioning or performance. It may also benefit practitioners involved in evaluating the role of IT tools in their companies. The paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews theoretical understanding on the shaping of IT-based organisational effectiveness, and requirements for further development. The new framework is then introduced and illustrated, and its utility subsequently discussed.

## IT-based organisational effectiveness

This conceptual paper seeks to contribute to existing theoretical literature in the IS interpretive research tradition (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991), that illuminates the shaping of IT-based organisational activities from a behavioural or processual standpoint, and in relation to the significance or meanings that organisational actors ascribe to IT systems and their features of use.

The IS field has witnessed the rise in the past two decades of a significant body of such research studies, which, using analytical frameworks such as structuration theory or actor-network theory, emphasise an action-oriented, contextually situated understanding of IT use in organisational environments (Suchman, 1987; Monteiro and Hanseth, 1996; Ciborra, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000). Shulman (1999) asserts, however, that while such studies have produced rich insights on the structuring of human action in the use of IT, they offer little guidance on understanding how effective organisational performance is enabled by IT. There thus appeared to be a key need for developing a behavioural framework aimed at extending theoretical analysis of IT-based action in IS interpretive research to the study of organisational capability and effectiveness.

The role and impact of IT in shaping organisational effectiveness is a broad topic with multiple facets and issues. It has therefore been conceptualised in prior IS literature through numerous frameworks that have addressed different themes, such as strategic advantage, organisational transformation and alignment. A way of appreciating this large body of theorising effort is to distinguish the type of approach in conceptualisation.

On the one hand, certain past studies have proposed ‘stage models’ of IT-enabled capability, in which IT is envisioned to increase organisational capability or effectiveness via a set of incremental stages (Willcocks and Sauer, 2001; Ross, 2003). On the other hand, the majority of past IS theorising on organisational effectiveness has taken the form of ‘factor models’ that identify key structural components or attributes responsible for shaping alignment or performance (e.g. Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). While both factor- and stage-based frameworks have contributed to current understanding of IT-based organisational effectiveness, there remain certain key areas that are under-developed and appear to invite a different approach in conceptualisation.

Numerous IS studies have exposed situations in which IT systems have hindered organisational efforts at alignment or transformation (Broadbent et al., 1999; Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001) or produced key disruptions and negative displacements in social relations and work environments (Bloomfield, 1995). Moreover, the emergent nature of organisational functioning has meant that IT use often engenders unanticipated consequences or vulnerabilities (Galliers and Baets, 1998). However, many past IS frameworks of IT-based effectiveness may be seen as having tended to represent only the positive, enabling effects of IT in their conceptual models or terminology, while not explicitly highlighting its negative or inhibitive effects. There thus seems a need for explicit representation of negative dimensions of IT use, in addition to positive. IT tools can invoke a mixed bag of outcomes in organisational contexts that warrant adequate theoretical representation.

The negative effects of IT are often due to the contradictions, inconsistencies or conflicts that arise in particular historical and social contexts of IT-based activity: this is an under-developed area of IS research in strong need of further specification (Truex, 1991; Robey and Boudreau, 1999). The existence of opposing forces and priorities that shape organisational action and effectiveness invokes a strong need for theoretical frameworks to treat the form of alignment between IT and organisational elements in behavioural and context-specific terms (Robey and Boudreau, 1999). However, past research on IT-based organisational alignment and effectiveness has predominantly involved the derivation or use of generic factor models (e.g. Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993), that do not offer the conceptual tools for characterisation on such terms.

These past shortcomings may be seen as have restricted the means for analysing how IT use shapes organisational capability or effectiveness behaviourally. A theoretical framework is therefore introduced next to address these inadequacies.

## The modalisation framework

A central concept in this new framework is modalisation. Modalisation is defined as the modification of an organisation’s capacity to carry out desired actions, or achieve a desired state of accomplishment (i.e. the modification of the ability to act or to be). The focus here is on the modalisation of organisational action through the use of IT systems or information-based practices. Organisational action is seen to be shaped along multiple ‘modalities’ that denote various behavioural dimensions in which such action is altered or mediated by social and technological factors/resources.

Common social structures or factors by which organisational action is modalised include legitimisation, power or knowledge. The use of IT tools represents technological resources by which organisational action is modalised. It should be noted generally that there are both positive and negative modalities (i.e. positive and negative behavioural dimensions by which organisational competence or capacity for action is modified). For example, the use of IT may variously facilitate or impede the actions of staff (Orlikowski, 1992), that is, IT enables action vs IT constrains action. This framework supports analytical discrimination of various modalities by which to analyse organisational functioning.

## Theoretical foundations

For Greimas (1987), human actions and socio-cultural practices may be ordered into incompatible and compatible behavioural dimensions (or properties), by structuring them in terms of ‘systems of signification’ (systems of significance, meaning or value). He formulated an analytical schema to express a system of signification. This schema, which is also known as the ‘semiotic square’ (Greimas, 1983; Jameson, 1987, XIV), is depicted in Figure 1.

This schema enables a more comprehensive analysis of structural opposition of significance or value by permitting an enlargement of their implications (Jameson, 1987). The upper corners, S1 and S2, are ‘contrary’ or opposite positions of the significance of a particular activity or concept, S. A basic example of this could be: S denotes the concept ‘absence of colours’. The two opposing terms that make up this concept might then be ‘black’ (S1) and ‘white’ (S2). The lower two corners, –S2 and –S1 (or ‘Not S2’ and ‘Not S1’ respectively), are generated by forming negatives, in a traverse direction, of opposing terms on the upper axis (i.e. S1 and S2). Thus, –S2 would denote ‘non-white’, while its opposite, –S1, ‘non-black’. These represent the ‘contradictory’ positions of the two terms on the upper corners (i.e. –S1 is the contradictory of S1; –S2 of S2). Together –S2 and –S1 make up opposing positions of –S, which may be seen as denoting the concept ‘presence of colour’. It might be seen at once that those two lower terms, –S2 and –S1, identify ‘implicative’ positions not accounted for by the binary opposition on the upper axis. For example, ‘nonwhite’ encompasses much more than ‘black’ (i.e. a range of colours).

This schema can thus permit an understanding of concepts or social practices to be enlarged beyond binary oppositions, that is, to include also the consideration of associated value-positions beyond a narrow either/or dimension. Schleifer (1987, 25–27), a major commentator on Greimas’ work, moreover presents an alternative version of the configuration of value-positions on this schema. The upper left corner is changed to represent the concept S itself, ‘absence of colours’ (i.e. no colours), while the upper right corner becomes its negation, Non-S (i.e. all colours). The bottom left corner is then changed to ‘neither S nor Non-S’ (i.e. colourlessness), while the bottom right corner becomes ‘both S and Non-S’ (i.e. colouredness). As Jameson (1987, XIV) observes, the schema is dynamic: the positionality of significance on the schema can be altered to expose alternative layers or ‘ensembles’ of significance.

The schema may be used in IS research to clarify or enlarge the understanding of IT-related concepts or activities. For example, the notion of ‘telecommunicating’ might initially be represented by the polar opposite meanings of ‘work in office’ (S1) and ‘work at home’ (S2). This schema permits that immediate significance to be expanded, however, to encompass the following meanings: ‘not work at home’ (–S2) and ‘not work at office’ (–S1). It can be instantly perceived from this operation that the spatial dislocation implied by the notion of telecommunicating encompasses more than solely the idea of working from home (as opposed to an office): it also implies other venues or spaces in which work may be carried out remotely through the use of networked computer systems.

Figure 1 A signification system (adapted from Greimas, 1987).

This schema is used in two ways. Firstly, as a ‘configurational model’, the various nodes on the schema may be used to depict contrasting themes or value positions relevant to an understanding of an organization’s activities and use of IT. This manner of use is ideal for highlighting incompatibilities or tensions seen as fundamental to the shaping of IT-based activities. Secondly, this schema can be used as a ‘transformational model’, in which various nodes are used to represent key points in a sequence of change. The initial state of an organisation or a social practice is designated by a particular node (e.g. S ), while the intermediate and final states of transition which this entity undergoes is denoted by others (e.g. S<sub>2</sub>, –S<sub>2</sub>).

Jameson (1987), the foremost commentator on the use of this schema to support interpretive studies in social science research, encourages researchers to appropriate its use to generate new configurations of significance (or clusters of meaning) that provide fresh understanding of social or organisational practices (e.g. use of IT). Jameson (1981, 254) points out that strict logical accuracy in labelling its nodes or axes (i.e. ‘a concern to compare only comparable entities or oppose only terms of the appropriate category’) is not as essential or as important as the ability to creatively frame the polysemic nature of meaning (i.e. the multiconnotative or associative nature of significance) for surfacing insights or useful perspectives. One may depart from a purely programmatic observance of the structural oppositions in the canonical version above, to capture the dialectical nature or the elusive properties of significance in various contexts of human endeavour (Jameson, 1987).

The use of this schema to support analyses of organisational or social practices in the use of IT may require the exercise of a certain degree of interpretive skill on the part of the researcher, as well as significant prior grounding in understanding the domain of behaviour under study, before useful insights may crystallise. This schema has been used by a few non-IS studies of cultural practices (e.g. Fleming, 1996). Its presentation here increases the range of analytical resources IS studies can draw upon to illuminate the complex nature of organisational functioning and IT use.

## Modalities of IT use

A central modal system is proposed here to support the analysis of technology use in organisations. This modal system, depicted by the schema in Figure 2, captures the salient behavioural dimensions or outcomes associated with the use of IT systems. Termed as the ‘modalities of IT capacity’, this schema designates the facilitative and inhibitive effects of IT use on the undertaking of work activity. It permits the consideration of the enabling capacity of IT systems to be enlarged to encompass their preventive capacity, as follows.

The upper corners of the schema denote contrary value polarisations regarding the enabling capacity of IT systems, that is, IT enables action vs IT does not enable action. In other words, IT systems either support or constrain an organisation’s ability to carry out particular actions and achieve desired states of performance. The lower two corners of the schema account for the converse side of this enabling capacity: they denote positive and negative values of the ‘preventive’ capacity of IT systems in organisational contexts. The lower left corner (i.e. IT enables to avoid being inhibited) designates the positive capacity of IT to prevent or do away with certain restrictions. For example, the implementation of IT-based automatic cheque processing by a bank eliminates the inefficiency of expending valuable time/effort processing cheques manually. The right lower corner represents the opposite – the inability to prevent restrictive or undesired outcomes. In other words, this zone denotes various inhibitive or paralysing properties related to the use of IT systems.

Two types of inhibitive capacity might be suggested: procedural and dispositional. The first relates to situations where IT systems possess inflexible features, so that the inability to modify or to configure them blocks users from desired actions. For example, an ERP system intended to strategically re-engineer a firm’s processes could introduce a degree of rigidity in its workflows that curtails their effectiveness. Another example of such procedural inhibition is when workflows are subjected to disruptions as a result of problems with the reliability of their technology platforms. The second type of inhibitory capacity (i.e. dispositional) involves situations where an organisation’s ability or integrity is compromised by lack of IT functionality. This might occur, for example, when an IT system introduces a capacity for its users or benefactors to take advantage of a company in ways not previously envisaged (e.g. mild abuses of the system, or pernicious attacks like fraud, security breaches), while not equipping the company with the ability to stop (or to repair the effects of) such behaviour.

Various characteristics of the use of IT systems might thus be mapped against this schema to identify positive and negative dimensions of behaviour by which they affect an organisation’s ability (or that of its members) to take action and achieve desired aims in a particular context. It should be noted that the labelling of this schema (like all the other schemas presented in this paper) is not prescriptive, that is, it is not fixed, but is contingent on the context of a study. This schema (Figure 2) can be re-labelled to reflect the particular themes of the analysis being conducted (i.e. the various nodes or axes can be renamed to express particular criteria used as the basis of evaluation).

## Illustration of application

One example of the application of this schema is found in a case study of customer service operations in a large retail firm (Corea, 2006). The details from the case are summarised below to illustrate an analysis of the modalities of IT use.

The firm, HealthCo (a pseudonym), runs an extensive chain of pharmacy stores that sell a wide range of health and beauty products. The company introduced IT-enabled call centre operations in the late-1990s, as part of a strategic re-orientation towards higher service quality and promotion of customer loyalty. The customer service representatives (CSRs) at the call centre who answered calls from customers used a range of IT systems to record, retrieve and manage information related to customer inquiries or complaints, including a customer relationship management (CRM) system. However, fulfilling the expectations of customers contacting the centre proved challenging because of difficulties associated with the centre’s information resources and use of IT tools, and the variability of its customer call issues and call patterns. As illustrated above, the schema serves to enframe and illuminate both positive and negative dimensions of IT support for customer service efforts. ## Illustration of application

An example of the use of this schema is presented next, summarised from a case study by the author on the customer service operations at the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC), a full account of which will be reported in a future publication. The BBC is a major national broadcasting and media institution in the United Kingdom. It transmits programs on numerous television and radio channels, ranging from news to entertainment to educational documentaries. The BBC’s operations are mainly funded by license fees paid by its customers (or audiences), the general public.

From its inception in 1927, the BBC’s activities have been fundamentally oriented towards upholding the ideals of ‘public service broadcasting’. Its activities are aimed at impartially and universally benefiting the nation’s population. It is committed to offering as much variety in its programming as to cater for the tastes and interests of all audience members, with provisions for disadvantaged minority groups. The BBC also maintains editorial and socio-political independence: its broadcasting output seeks to avoid any bias that may promote the interests of one group or individual over that of others.

Major changes in the BBC’s operational environment in the 1990s, however, led to key changes in its strategy and operating model. The generous government funding that it used to enjoy in prior decades declined sharply, while operating costs rose considerably. Moreover, new broadcasting companies emerged in the TV and radio industries, inducing serious commercial competition. A higher portion of the BBC’s funds had now to be obtained by market action: new viewers and listeners had to be won over and current ones maintained in the face of competing claims. As a result, the BBC’s strategy from the mid-1990s onwards was changed to a central focus on its public accountability to its licence-paying audience, that is, being more answerable to the concerns of its viewers and listeners.

This new strategic aim of accountability to its audience led to a restructuring of its customer service operations. Till the late 1990s, the BBC’s service operations had been run in a decentralised way by regional units throughout the UK, who handled inquiries and complaints from local audiences. In 1999, however, it centralised its customer service operations around the introduction of a new call centre, which is referred to as Centre A in the upcoming accounts. Staff at this centre handle ‘unsolicited’ contacts from audience members, that is, inquiries, feedback or complaints regarding TV/radio programmes (around 1 million calls a year). The BBC also has a second, older call centre in operation (Centre B), which handles a separate stream of ‘solicited’ contacts, that is, calls/emails invited from audiences during/after certain TV/radio programmes (about 800,000 calls a year).

Moreover, from 1999, the BBC outsourced the running of its two IT-enabled call centres to an established services vendor firm, ServeCo (a pseudonym). ServeCo has a strong track record as a services operator, and was seen as a strategic partner in helping the BBC improve its customercontact operations and, correspondingly, its accountability to its audience. The management staff of the BBC’s customer service operations (CSO) department, that planned the restructuring and the outsourcing contract, are responsible for ensuring ServeCo’s compliance in its commitments.

Achieving its strategic aim, however, has proved a challenging task for the BBC. The restructuring of its service operations has been conditioned by fundamental conflict between two institutionalised approaches to the goal of accountability: the public service model, which stresses critical detachment and altruistic consideration of the interests of all audience members as a whole (while upholding values such as fairness, impartiality and consistency), and the commercial customer service model, which places emphasis on catering to the interests and concerns of particular segments of the audience, for reasons of profit or self-advantage. The BBC needs to reconcile these models, as keen competition and cost pressures have forced its need to function as a commercial operator while maintaining traditional public service ideals. In particular, the relationship between the management staff of the BBC’s CSO department, who are strongly attached to public service ideals, and management staff of ServeCo, who are strongly accustomed to the commercial service model, has witnessed tension as a result.

The complexity of BBC’s strategic performance aim can be appreciated through the ‘modalities of performance’ schema in Figure 5, which depicts various dimensions of the strategic aim of accountability in the shaping of service performance. The BBC’s service operations had been restructured with the aim of taking fair, equivalent account of the concerns of all audience members, and responding to them in a consistent manner. The establishment of Call Centre A has provided audience members with uniform access to inquiry and feedback services (i.e. one number to call on all matters). The centralised capture of audience feedback, and data on audience interactions, in the CRM database at Centre A, and its use in generating regular reports disseminated to senior management and TV/Radio programme staff on the company’s intranet, has enabled more effective, large-scale awareness of the BBC’s broad audience than had existed previously (i.e. under the decentralised operations). A large amount of standardised carefully vetted information for answering audience inquiries made available to CSR staff on the CRM and other systems (e.g. Outlook, intranet, internet website) has enabled consistency in content and quality of service responses. This dimension of accountability is that of the public service ideals of fairness, consistency and universal benefit.

The preceding dimension is complemented by another in BBC’s public service ethos: impartiality or critical detachment. This has meant that policies and work practices instituted for handling audience complaints or comments at Centre A are designed not to take selective account of the interests of particular audience members, that is, not giving preferential treatment to particular audience groups or deferring to subjective viewpoints. Responses to audience complaints are made with reference to standards of disinterested objectivity and fairness. The use of IT functionality to produce reports that categorise and rank audience data (e.g. the daily issues report), or to prioritise and support complaint-handling activity, are examples of this effort at preserving objective treatment or detachment. This public service idea of accountability, upheld in an attitude of autonomy and independence from the vested interests and biased views of particular audience members or segments, is also reflected by the dissatisfaction of the BBC’s CSO management over its perceptions that CSRs at Centre A (run by ServeCo) are too sympathetic to audience preferences or complaints.

At the same time however, customer service operations at BBC have been shaped by commercial pressure from the intensified industry competition and loss of government subsidies. This has resulted in dimensions of accountability in work organisation opposed to the public service model, that is, that reflect the commercial service model, where actions are driven by economic motives or self-interest. The management approach that ServeCo, the outsourcing vendor, has instituted in its running of the BBC’s call centre operations reflects this thrust. Certain aspects of the service operations are designed to take selective account of particular audience segments, such as a major effort by the audience research team at Centre A to exploit the datareporting abilities of the CRM system and other tools (querying software, data mining) to improve the knowledge of BBC programme makers regarding their audience tastes and interests, so that TV/Radio programmes can be better tailored to gain the patronage of viewers/listeners (licencefees, income from broadcasts). This is also exemplified by efforts at Centre A to profile individual audience segments, so that the presentation of information by CSRs or corporate web-pages can be differentially geared to satisfy particular needs and preferences.

Another dimension of commercial service accountability is in contradistinction to the public service ideal of universal and fair treatment: not to take equivalent account of all audience members. This may be seen as reflected by an emphasis of CSRs on friendly banter and rapportbuilding during call interactions with audience members, as well as expressing sympathy with their grievances. From the standpoint of ServeCo management, this is viewed as essential for establishing loyalty among audience members, and raising their satisfaction with the centre’s services (i.e. a primary service target in the outsourcing contract). From the viewpoint of the BBC’s CSO management staff, however, spending too much time in interaction with some callers prevents quick response to others waiting on the line (i.e. not giving them an equivalent chance of service). Moreover, because of this emphasis on establishing solidarity with callers, the CSRs at Centre A are seen by the CSO staff as not exercising sufficient discernment in reporting feedback on complaints in the CRM system (e.g. when CSRs duly report what is considered ‘unnecessary’ or invalid feedback by the CSO staff, such as politically biased or racist viewpoints, or viewpoints that are unfair to the interests of other audience groups). This dimension of accountability may also be seen as reflected in the reluctance of ServeCo management to release CSRs at Centre A to handle the significant number of excess calls often experienced by Centre B, which can be diverted to them via the automatic call distribution (ACD) software (note: this is explained in the next section).

Through the representation of the BBC’s strategic aim in these multi-dimensional terms, this schema (Figure 5) not only clarifies the contested nature of performance but can also serve to promote management understanding. For example, the tension and disagreement between the CSO and ServeCo management may be seen to stem from opposing emphases in efforts to improve information management practices and IT-based service performance. These opposing trajectories in the focus of the CSO staff and ServeCo staff are, respectively, depicted in Figure 5 by the solid arrow line and the broken arrow line.

The ‘point of departure’ for the CSO management has been the decentralised service operations of the past that were marked by inconsistent and fragmented responses, in which the concerns of only particular audience members were addressed. Through the use of centralised services (based on integrated IT systems and standardised information practices, and strict observance of policies), the CSO staff are moving towards realising a public service notion of accountability. In contrast, the ‘point of departure’ of the ServeCo management has been their direct knowledge of a comprehensive range of audience concerns and perferences which they have gathered (captured in the CRM system or held by their staff), as well as their motivation and expertise as a successful commercial service operator. By exploiting their resources and experience within the legitimacy of their contractual stipulations, they are moving towards heightening the commercial viability of BBC (and correspondingly, the value of their own services) by helping tailor programme production and service provision.

In summary, the BBC’s endeavour to achieve its strategic aim of accountability through high-quality IT-based audience contact operations has been shaped or entangled by the conflicting injunctions of two institutional codes of service. Achieving successful performance in these operations may be seen to depend on the BBC’s ability to harness a fruitful synergy or dialectical interplay between these opposing trajectories of innovation. Thus, as demonstrated here, the application of the modalities of performance schema can help clarify multiple behavioural dimensions of performance, and aid management sense-making efforts in reconciling opposing priorities or emphases.

## Modalities of action

Organisational action and the use of IT are shaped by various social structures or resources specific to the context (Orlikowski, 2000). It is beyond this paper’s length to capture or illustrate all such facets as relevant modal systems. However, certain important modalities of action can be addressed here as an initial starting set: legitimisation, power and motivation. These are depicted in Figure 6.

## Legitimacy, power and motivation

Legitimisation is a significant aspect of the shaping of organisational activity and use of IT (Kling and Iacano, 1984; Avgerou, 2000). This key dimension of organisational functioning encompasses such elements as formal policies or stipulations that govern the behaviour of organisational members. It also includes social ‘institutions’, which refer to ingrained premises, patterns of expectation or regulative structures that provide the basis of validity for the way organisational members perceive and act in the use of IT (Scott, 1995; Avgerou, 2000). The forces of legitimisation that induce normative or cognitive pressures on social actors to think or act in particularly acceptable ways may be classified in their effects by the generic dimensions of modality depicted in Figure 6a, differentiated in a fourfold way: (i) having to act; (ii) having not to act; (iii) not having not to act; and (iv) not having to act. These dimensions of legitimisation correspond to those identified in Greimas (1987): (i) prescription (i.e. having to do); (ii) prohibition (i.e. having not to do); (iii) permission (i.e. not having not to do); and (iv) optionality (i.e. not having to do).

Power is another fundamental aspect of organisational work practices based on the use of technology systems that can wield a considerable influence on their outcomes (Markus, 1983; Bloomfield, 1995). One potential way of analytically distinguishing the capacity or effects of power structures on human agency in organisations is (Figure 6b): (i) causing to act; (ii) causing not to act (e.g. repressive power); (iii) not causing not to act (e.g. liberating power); and (iv) not causing to act (e.g. immobilisation or powerlessness). Such designation can also be qualified to distinguish other differences in forms of power, such as the discrimination between authority and influence (causing to act vs to believe), or punitive control and persuasion (forcing to do vs making it attractive to think).

Yet another salient aspect of organisational functioning is motivation (Mumford, 1999), which denotes the extent staff members feel congruent with behaviours required for effective job performance or use of technology systems. Key dimensions of modality pertaining to motivation can be characterised as follows (Figure 6c): (i) wanting to act; (ii) wanting not to act (i.e. resistance); (iii) not wanting not to act (i.e. receptiveness); and (iv) not wanting to act (i.e. indifference). This schema may be used to evaluate the extent stakeholders have been mobilised for a project of ITbased organisational change.

## Illustration of application

A brief example is presented next to illustrate the analysis of modalities of action. This example is drawn from the context of the same case of the BBC’s customer service operations described in the previous section on performance modalities. The ‘modalities of legitimisation’ schema is used here to clarify an analysis of a contested implementation of IT-enabled ‘virtual’ call centre integration in the BBC’s operations.

As noted earlier, in 1999, the BBC outsourced the running of its two call centres to ServeCo. In its outsourcing contract, the BBC had stated five primary objectives and several service level targets for ServeCo to fulfil. One of the objectives was for ServeCo to carry out the integration of the two call centres and form a single, ‘virtual’ call centre. Other objectives set for ServeCo included achieving consistency in the quality of service response across all channels, as well as develop the BBC’s accountability to its audience. This latter objective involved meeting certain formal service targets: (1) to raise audience satisfaction levels from a past average of 66% to 90% by mid-2000, as well as maintain this after that; (2) to observe targets for length of call (e.g. 2 min 30 s) and speed of response (e.g. 90% calls answered within 20 s). These targets only applied to the new Call Centre A, which had been the focus of improvement in the restructuring efforts. No service targets were set for the older Centre B: only a general expectation that ServeCo would run it more efficiently. The management staff of the BBC’s CSO department were responsible for overseeing and ensuring ServeCo’s compliance.

On ServeCo’s takeover, the two call centres were integrated at a technological level, permitting them to operate as a single ‘virtual’ call centre. Besides sharing the same CRM database, both centres also share a common communications infrastructure, which means that calls routed to Centre B can be easily switched to and handled by the staff at Centre A, and vice versa, through simple realtime configuration of each centre’s ACD software. This permits suitably trained staff at one call centre to assist their counterparts at the other, by taking on excess calls arriving at the latter when it faces too great a surge.

The aim envisioned by the BBC, in its contractual stipulation for the centres to be integrated, had been for Centre A to provide such support (i.e. help Centre B cope with excess calls it faces frequently) almost on a daily basis. Centre B experiences alternating periods of excessive and light call volumes, as the audience members contact it in mass reaction to certain programmes aired on TV/radio (e.g. on emotive topics). In contrast, Centre A receives a much more uniform rate of call arrivals. Owing to these differing traffic patterns, an average of 15,000 calls a month are lost at Centre B (i.e. callers hang up rather than waiting for an answer), compared to less than a 100 at Centre A. The BBC thus intended ServeCo to set up virtual call centre functionality to reduce this disparity, and increase response rates at Centre B. However, the extent of integration envisioned by the BBC in the outsourcing contract’s stipulations did not materialise. Instead, it led to conflict over the interpretation of contractual stipulations. The differences in the disagreement between the BBC and ServeCo may be mapped and clarified in reference to the modalities of legitimisation shown in Figure 7.

The ServeCo management were seen by the BBC management as not releasing enough CSR staff at Centre A to handle the excess calls faced at Centre B (contrary to contractual requirements to integrate the operations of the two centres as well as develop consistency of responses). The BBC management had wanted such integration to mean not only full integration at a technological level, but also significant integration at the workflow level, that is, for staff at Centre A to take on the excess calls at Centre B as much as possible. The contract’s stipulations, however, appear to have contributed to ServeCo’s reluctance to execute a merging of two workflows to that desired extent. It had to do with the way that the BBC has measured performance, by setting service level targets (e.g. length of call, speed of response) for Centre A but not Centre B. This is perceived by BBC management to have given ServeCo an ‘excuse’ not to pursue a full integration, that is, it undermined their motivation to do so.

The nature of calls arriving at Centre A allow for their resolution at a quick pace of turnover, and for CSR activities to be carried out within standard, computermonitored time-based targets. However, calls handled at Centre B do not fit such a mould. They often involve requests by audience members for assistance with socially sensitive issues that take longer than the typical call at centre A to resolve. In addition, the requirement for ServeCo to raise customer satisfaction levels at Centre A lent added impetus to their focus on having that centre’s staff focus on handling their own stream of calls, rather than diverting effort to handle calls from Centre B. Thus, it has been easier or more feasible for ServeCo to maintain the prescribed targets by having Centre A concentrate on its own calls (rather than jeopardise the ability to meet targets by taking on calls from Centre B).

Moreover, from the standpoint of the ServeCo management staff, they had already met that stipulation to join the operations of the two call centres by integrating them at a technology level, so as to allow backup coverage between the two centres in exceptional circumstances. Furthermore, they placed high priority on having to achieve prescribed service targets. Another factor that may have contributed to the tendency of the ServeCo management to keep the two workflows unmixed has been the difficulty they had faced in raising the productivity of staff at the older Centre B (whom they ‘inherited’ when they took over), in contrast to staff at Centre A (whom they managed from the start). Since the contract had not explicitly prohibited them from keeping the two workflows functionally separate, but did prohibit them from falling below the service targets, they felt compelled to focus on those maintaining performance levels at Centre A. The degree to which the workflows of the two centres are integrated on a day-to-day basis was viewed by them as an optional matter, to be decided at their own discretion.

Moreover, ServeCo has been generally successful in meeting prescribed service targets and the customer satisfaction level. The approach they have applied in running the centres has also brought significant benefits to various BBC units (e.g. use of data from the CRM system to raise programme makers’ understanding of audience interests). This success may be seen to have given the ServeCo staff an implicit degree of endorsement or ‘permission’ to persist in their own approach to managing those two workflows. Thus, in summary, the context of legitimacy shaping the outsourced management of these call centre operations has been characterised by the conflicting and implicative interpretations of different parties of social actors, mapped against the modalities of legitimisation.

The preceding example thus illustrates the discrimination of modalities of action in IT-based organisational functioning. The contested nature of legitimacy involving the actions of ServeCo staff in implementing virtual call centre integration for the BBC was clarified in terms of multiple implications or nuances of behaviour. The use of the schema served to highlight the conflict or dilemma that can arise when contractual specifications in outsourcing relationships are not carefully considered (Willcocks and Kern, 1998).

Thus, in summary, the above-described facets of organisational functioning or IT use comprise an initial set of modal systems. This framework presented here is intended only as a provisional inventory of modalities: one that is neither closed nor organised as a definitive taxonomy. IS researchers or practitioners may modify or extend these schemas to identify various competencies, aptitudes or orientations in organisational domains. The complex nature of IT-based action entails that a comprehensive analysis of organisational effectiveness can include behavioural aspects not listed here, for example, knowledge, autonomy, leadership, etc. The initial formulations illustrated above nevertheless indicate how this framework encourages such analyses in a multi-dimensional way.

## Discussion: utility of approach

This framework is aimed at augmenting IS interpretive research on the shaping of IT-enabled organisational capability and effectiveness from a behavioural standpoint. Its potential contribution to IS theorising may be seen to lie in the following areas.

## Capturing organisational complexity

This framework is geared at a conceptual level to foster recognition of negative dimensions or outcomes of human action and IT use, in addition to positive ones. It thus offers a way to arrive at a more comprehensive understanding of the way organisational capability or effectiveness is shaped around the use of IT tools in a ‘purposeful’ context of activity, that is, in relation to performance aims or desired states of behaviour.

By enabling the representation and analysis of organisational action and IT use in counter-factual terms, this framework also helps to highlight the contradictions, conflicts or dilemmas that have to be addressed to achieve effective performance. IS studies have evoked strong recognition of organisations as strained by incompatibilities or tensions (Mathiassen and Nielsen, 1990; Bloomfield, 1995). This framework thus fulfils a current need for conceptual tools to assess IT use accordingly, in terms of contradictory or oppositional dimensions (Truex, 1991; Robey and Boudreau, 1999).

This framework adds to other theoretical resources that IS interpretive research studies can use to investigate the socially situated, behavioural shaping of organisational agency around the use of IT. While such studies have tended to focus on elaborating the shaping of organisational action in terms of social structure or resources like legitimacy, power and knowledge (Monteiro and Hanseth, 1996; Ciborra, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000), this new framework extends a behavioural standpoint to the elucidation of ITbased organisational effectiveness. In other words, it can help illuminate organisational functioning and IT use not only in terms of the shaping of action but of performance as well (i.e. regarded as the achievement of envisioned strategic aims or desired states of competence).

In particular, by supporting explicit representation of both positive and negative dimensions of IT use or social action, this theoretical framework allows for the complex nature of performance in organisation to be recognised. There sometimes appears to be a tendency in popular business literature on organisational/IS innovation (e.g. ‘cookbook’ approaches) to assume that strategic organisational aims are uncomplicated or clear-cut in nature, and therefore only need to be executed in a straightforward way. As was seen in the earlier example of the BBC’s operations, however, strategic aims like ‘accountability to customers’ are not necessarily simple or uni-dimensional in their implication, but may have multiple behavioural dimensions that are oppositional or complementary in relation, which this framework can help identify. It is useful to take into account both negative and positive dimensions of strategic action, since organisational performance aims can then be pursued with the awareness of contradictory outcomes that can arise in place of (or in conjunction with) the achievement of such aims.

This framework may also be seen as contributing towards developing the focus on signification central to studies in the interpretive research approach (Markus and Robey, 1988). It furthers the theoretical ability of IS interpretive research to represent the shaping of IT-based practices from a standpoint of structures of significance/ meaning, through its adaptation of analytical tools from the field of semiotics (Greimas, 1987), that specialises in the study of signification. The socio-semiotic perspective thus embodied here may be seen as advantageous (Klein, 1996), helping disclose of behaviour in a context of action. As illustrated earlier in the examples, this framework can enable the complexity of organisational functioning or IT use to be understood in a systematic way.

## Alignment from a behavioural standpoint

Numerous studies in the literature have emphasised the need for organisational and IT-related components or process to be strongly aligned, or in a state of fit, as a pre-condition for organisational effectiveness and the achievement of strategic aims (Powell and Dent-Mitcallef, 1997). As a result, many factor-type frameworks on ITorganisational alignment have been proposed (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993; Boar, 1994), giving rise to numerous quantitative studies measuring the degree of fit (Bergeron et al., 2001). However, such factor-based models may be seen as inadequate in addressing the issue of alignment in IT-based organisational action, for two reasons.

While they have done much to identify the components or factors that need to be aligned (i.e. what IT and organisational elements need to be in a state of fit), they do not offer adequate conceptual tools to represent, in behavioural terms, the form of alignment (i.e. how, or towards what purpose, social and technological elements are inter-related in a specific context of action). Moreover, recent studies have shown the nature of alignment to be a dynamic process shaped by the actions of organisational actors, rather than a static relationship of fit (Truex et al., 1999; Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001).

These developments have invoked the need for a behavioural or process-oriented perspective on alignment, that recent IS interpretive studies have taken up. For example, studies adopting actor-network theory (Monteiro and Hanseth, 1996) have highlighted the role of political activity and material inscriptions by various stakeholders, in negotiating interest-laden alignments between IT and organisational elements. Other studies (Ciborra et al., 2000) stress the role of improvised behaviour and institutional contexts in shaping alignment between IT and organisations, or creating ‘drift’ (misalignment) between their planned and actual integration.

This new framework may be seen as contributing towards this development of a behavioural representation and analysis of alignment. It enables the alignment between social and technological elements/features in organisations to be comprehended in relation to behavioural characteristics of action (legitimisation, power, motivation) or performance aims. Moreover, it fosters simultaneous, multi-dimensional consideration of both alignment (e.g. IT enables to act, IT enables to avoid being inhibited in action) and misalignment (e.g. IT does not enable to act, IT does not enable to avoid being inhibited).

Correspondingly, it may be asserted that the notion of alignment implicit to this framework is not solely that of complementarity (i.e. a fit between matching social and technical elements), but one that embraces incompatibility at the same time. Alignment is represented here as a contested state of tension among various different factors, priorities or interests: a strained complex of opposing dimensions of behaviour, in unstable adjustment. This was seen in the earlier example of the BBC’s operations, where the alignment between BBC’s and ServeCo’s management of service activities involved an uneasy accommodation of differing interests or interpretations. In such circumstances alignment is rarely rigorous or mathematical.

This framework thus permits alignment in IT-based activities to be represented in behavioural terms as a dynamic state in constant negotiation by stakeholders, and open to the shaping of emergent circumstances (Truex et al., 1999). However, while promoting qualitative understanding of alignment, this framework does not incorporate quantitative measurement of such (i.e. metrics), nor does it identify particular factor-type criteria of appraisal. The balanced business scorecard approach (Kaplan and Norton, 1992) is one technique that addresses such requirements, enabling quantitative evaluation of alignment from a standpoint of multiple sets of criteria like financial health, customer orientation, process performance and innovation/ learning. In contrast, the multidimensionality of this paper’s framework is structured or nuanced in behavioural terms. It promotes functional understanding based on the socially shaped (or context-embedded) nature of alignment in IT-based organisational action.

## Conclusion

The theoretical framework presented here provides analytical tools that IS studies can adopt, to incorporate a more inclusive account of social dynamics, and highlight the inconsistencies or conflicts that pertain to the use of IT and the shaping of organisational effectiveness in particular contexts.

This framework enables researchers or organisational practitioners to discriminate multiple behavioural dimensions of technology-based activity, by their representation as systems of modality. The conditioning of organisational action or competence around the use of IT tools can thus be systematically analysed in terms of various capabilities and constraints. There appears to be scope for further development and refinement of this framework. Future studies can enhance its utility via case-based empirical substantiation or theoretical extension: aimed at establishing a repertoire of modal schemas to illuminate comprehensively and insightfully the nature and outcomes of IT-based practices in society.

## Notes

1 The concept of modalisation central to this new IS framework is adapted, in an analogous sense, from the notion of modalisation used in semiotics/linguistic studies (Greimas, 1987 chap. 7), where it refers to the modification of a basic linguistic statement of being or action (e.g. Bob is rich) by a modal expression like ‘wanting’, ‘having to’, ‘being able to’, ‘knowing how to’ (e.g. Bob wants to be rich), that alters its meaning-effects. Thus, by analogy, an organisation’s behavioural ability to be a certain desired state of achievement (or to carry out certain actions) is modified by certain social and technological features or resources (e.g. power, legitimisation, IT use) that have modal value i.e. they affect organisational competence instrumentally.

The underlying assumption here that a theory of a linguistic action is analogously compatible with a theory of organisational action and technology use, has been adopted before by IS researchers (Liebenau & Backhouse, 1990; Taylor, 1993; Klein, 1996; Stamper, 1997). It is also consistent with a current trend in sociological studies to lean on linguistic models for the elaboration of reality and organisational behaviour (Lopez and Scott, 2000) – based on the insight that social practices are not simply material phenomena but phenomena with meaning/ significance.
