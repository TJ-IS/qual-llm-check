# An analysis of consumer training for feature rich products

Fang Fang <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Xiaohui Xu <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, California State University at San Marcos, United States

<sup>b</sup> Technology and Operations Management Department, California State Polytechnic University at Pomona, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 29 October 2009 Received in revised form 11 June 2011 Accepted 19 July 2011 Available online 28 July 2011

Keywords: Feature rich product Consumer training Learning costs Two dimensional model

## a b s t r a c t

Many products now are loaded with an enormous number of features. It creates a great challenge for consumers to fully explore functionalities of those products. A recent trend is that manufacturers that market feature rich products start to provide consumer training programs. In this paper, we study the motivation of a manufacturer to offer training to consumers and how the training related costs affect his pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We use a two dimensional consumer heterogeneity model to capture the consumer's learning cost and valuation of features. We show how training shifts consumer's purchasing decisions and the conditions under which the manufacturer is motivated to offer training. Two speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations are investigated: when training is required and when training is optional. We show that if the manufacturer can effectively and cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently deliver the training program, then optional training can make the product more attractive and improve the manufacturer's net gains the most. Our results also indicate that the availability of online educational tools is one of the reasons for recent trend in consumer training.

## 1. Introduction

It is not uncommon to hear consumers complain about how hard to learn and to use a product they have purchased. One reason for such frustration is that the product is loaded with a lot of features due to the manufacturer's desire to provide consumers with a more useful or desirable bundle to promote sales. This strategy is widely visible in electronics and information technology. Nowadays cell phones are used as cameras, MP3/video players, portable web surfers, pocket organizers, calculators, <sup>fl</sup>ashlights, and so much more. As it turns out, though, most people are getting overwhelmed by all these features. According to a survey conducted by a mobile phone recycling company, 61% polled said that there are too many unnecessary features on their mobile phones today compared to the devices 10 years ago.<sup>1</sup> One extreme example is iPhone, which has a 124-page manual plus a numerous number of features/applications you can download from Apple App Store to extend the functionality of the handsets. Although each additional feature provides another reason for consumers to purchase a product, it also increases the time needed to fully explore the product, and even lead to negative emotional reactions, or so called “feature fatigue”, e.g. [10].

Obviously the overwhelming number of features can burden a consumer and lead to negative utilities that reduce the possibility of purchase. Thus, in consumer's purchasing decisions for feature rich products, learning costs should be considered in addition to the value associated with product capability. In this study, we introduce a two dimensional model that captures the positive utility a consumer can derive from features packed into the product and the negative utility due to time (and efforts) needed to learn to use the features. Consumer can differ in two ways. Some consumer may value functionality higher than a lot of others and each additional feature increases his/her welfare more than it does to others. Also consumers vary in terms of learning capabilities, which determine the total learning costs (time and/or efforts) in order to utilize the product. A consumer with high evaluation toward product functionality and rich expertise in using the product is willing to pay top price for the product, while a consumer that assigns low value on features/functionality of the product and has dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in learning is most likely to be priced out. Consumers with high evaluation and high learning costs and those who do not value the product much but can learn fast and easily are market segments that lie between aforementioned two extreme types of consumers. The introduction of the learning capability of consumers has enriched the traditional framework for modeling consumer heterogeneity.

Recently, in industries that make feature rich products, more and more companies are providing training to consumers and claim the practice a critical step to provide value to consumers. Mathematica is a feature rich computational software program developed by Wolfram Research and widely used in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c, engineering, and mathematical <sup>fi</sup>elds and other areas of technical computing. Wolfram Research offers free tutorials on its website and provides online lessons to users. The company routinely sends emails to users announcing the time and topics of the online seminars, which are designed to help users fully explore the capability of the software. A lot of software and consumer electronics manufacturers that offer training and certi<sup>fi</sup>cation programs even charge a fee for educating consumers about their product features. The most famous ones include SAP, Microsoft, and Apple. Another example is that Freddie Mac provides free online or face to face training sessions on complicated mortgage products like Home Possible Mortgages, mortgages for newly constructed homes, and super conforming mortgages.<sup>2</sup>

As training becomes a common practice in the business world, in part due to the ease and cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of online training, it is of interest to investigate how training, as a vehicle to facilitate consumer learning, affects consumers' purchasing decisions and a product's marketing strategy. One obvious consequence of product training is that it reduces the pain of learning for a lot of consumers. Thus these consumers are willing to pay more as their learning costs are lowered. This provides opportunities for companies to charge more and earn higher pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. It is well known that a diverse market prevents companies from obtaining all consumer surplus, and hence restricts its pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Training with its <sup>fi</sup>xed time requirement and <sup>fi</sup>xed number of features covered can, to some extent, unify a diverse consumer pool and improve the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Without training, a consumer maximizes her utility by choosing how many features to learn. However, with training, consumers will learn at least the features demonstrated in the training and spend the same amount of time learning these features during training. This gives a common component in consumers' utility functions and thus can, to some extent, allow companies greater ability to exploit the consumers. Nevertheless, a diverse market also lessens the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the training, as consumers with strong learning capability will <sup>fi</sup>nd standardized training unattractive.

The paper is organized as follows: First, related literature is examined and our contribution is explained in Section 2. Then we set up a two dimensional consumer purchase decision model and illustrate consumer behavior when no training is available in Section 3. Then training is introduced to our model and manufacturer's pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization problem is solved in Section 4. Finally, a discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings and extensions is provided in Section 5.

## 2. Related literature

Our paper draws upon literature on how to model consumer heterogeneity. This is the basis for a lot of research in economics, marketing, supply chain management, among many others. Usually, it is assumed that each consumer has a <sup>fi</sup>xed value toward the product and this value follows a certain distribution, either discrete or continuous. Some classic examples are provided here. In competitive analysis, the famous Hotelling's “linear city model” [6] shows that in many markets it is rational for producers to make their products as similar as possible. Another famous spatial model in competition literature is Salop's circular city [11]. In the research of product line strategy of a monopolist, [9] assumes a uniformly distributed consumer base while [12] takes a discrete approach. Our model uses a two dimensional uniform distribution to capture the consumers' two relatively independent characteristics. We allow consumers to decide how many features to learn based on these two characteristics. The number of features a consumer chooses to use will directly affect her willingness-to-pay for the product.

In addition to the additive form of consumer value function related to the feature choices, we have consumer feature learning costs as a second dimension in the consumer behavior model. There is a recent trend in connecting consumer learning to various strategic issues. For example, [3] shows that consumer learning of the product leads to periodical price promotions. [4] indicates that consumer learning has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the optimal service level of two competing retailers. [2] examines the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of virtual learning online on brand attitude, product knowledge, and purchase intention. [7] studies a situation where consumers can learn about the quality and quantity of a service. All these papers develop their own consumer learning models and all are different from ours. We model consumer learning from consumer utility perspective and adopt a convex cost function that is similar to what is used by [10], the paper that coins the term “feature fatigue.” Furthermore, our paper studies the consumer learning with the help from a training program offered by the manufacturer, which is a topic less investigated in the existing literature.

Offering training is essentially a way to bundle more value into the product package. Literature on bundling has been proli<sup>fi</sup>c. [1] studies a dynamic bundling strategy and [5] examined the strategies of bundling information goods with decreasing value. Our product training can be considered partly information goods with decreasing value since the more features leant has a decreasing marginal utility to the consumer. We expand the "to-or-not-to-bundle" question studied in the previous literature and solve the problem of how much to bundle when utilities from additional features decrease. As remote learning is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient option for customer training, our paper also relates to study on the effectiveness of online learning, such as [8].

## 3. An analytical model of consumer learning

A manufacturer is releasing a new product with N features. Assume that there is a continuum of potential consumers differing in two ways. First of all, consumers may value the features differently due to their distinct needs and interests. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne v∈[0, 1] as a consumer's marginal valuation of a feature. Second, we assume that consumers vary in terms of feature learning capabilities, which may be a result of past experience and educational/professional background. We assume that each consumer needs a certain amount of time, t∈[0, 1], to learn how to use a feature. Thus if a consumer chooses to learn n (n≤N) features, she will spend a total time of T=n t learning those n features. In this paper, we use learning time alone as consumers' learning costs. However, our model can easily include other types of learning costs, such as psychological costs when a consumer struggles through the learning process. That is, t can be explained as both the time and mental efforts a consumer pays in order to learn a speci<sup>fi</sup>c feature.

Consumers, each represented by a parameter pair (t, v), are distributed uniformly over the unit square (see Fig. 1), with a total mass normalized to 1. If a consumer with parameter (t, v) has purchased the product and chooses to learn n (n N) features, she will incur a non-negative learning cost c(T), where T=n t is the total learning time. We assume that the cost function satis<sup>fi</sup>es $c ( 0 ) = 0 , c ^ { \prime } ( x ) > 0$ for xN0, $c ^ { \prime } ( 0 ) { = } 0 ,$ , and $c ^ { \prime \prime } ( x ) { > } 0$ for $x \ge 0 .$ Thus by assumption the learning cost function is convex and increasing in total learning time. Note that the assumption $c ^ { \prime } ( 0 ) = 0$ is not that restrictive as it looks. We can lift this assumption, which will not change the main results in this paper but add a lot of discussion of trivial boundary cases. Furthermore, in order to ensure interior solutions, we impose that $c ^ { \prime } ( N ) { > } 1$ . Note that here N 1 is the learning time spent by the least capable feature learner $( t { = } 1 )$ to master all available features. The condition indicates that the marginal learning cost of learning more than N features is high for those consumers and is imposed to avoid extended discussion of trivial boundary cases.

## 4. When a training program is offered

In this section, we discuss a scenario where the manufacturer offers a training program that covers $n ^ { \circ } \leq N$ features. In business to business (B2B) application software industry, training programs are usually part of product offerings. Almost all ERP products come with training programs that aid functional personnel of clients to fully utilize the software. In integrated circuits (IC) design, the design tool developers always provide training sessions for customers. Customers send their IC design engineers to learn the latest advanced design and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation software. In most of the above cases, training is required. In business to consumer (B2C) world, the training is usually optional. Apple and a lot of other device manufacturers provide optional training both online and in store. In this section, we will study both cases separately and then compare them to the no training case. The required training case, though not applicable to consumer electronics products, provides strong insights in the analysis of the optional training case. In Section $4 . 1$ , we investigate the scenario when training is offered to every customer. In Section 4.2, we look at the optional training program.

## 4.1. When training is required

## 4.1.1. Consumer purchase decisions

## 5. Discussion

Training is an important way to increase the value of a feature rich product, especially when a lot of consumers are not familiar with the product or the technology. A well-designed training program can give many consumers a jump start and eliminate their worries of possible frustration. Furthermore, with training programs, a certain number of consumers can explore more features than if they learn on their own. So a training program can possibly make a consumer learn more features with less time, thus the value of the product is certainly higher to this consumer. As a result, the manufacturer can charge a higher price and make more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

By designing a training program with n<sup>∘</sup> features that takes t<sup>∘</sup> time per feature, all consumers that purchase the product will use at least $n ^ { \circ }$ features and spend at least n<sup>∘</sup>t<sup>∘</sup> time on learning. This restricts consumers' choice and, to some extent, mitigates the problem with consumer diversity. This is also a reason that training can help a company's bottom line when most of their consumers need some help in learning.

However, the designing of a training program is quite challenging as the program has to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient enough to help most potential consumers. If a lot of consumers regard a training program as a burden instead of a learning aid, then it is not bene<sup>fi</sup>cial at all for the manufacturer to offer it in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. If this is due to the lack of expertise in delivering a training program (i.e., t<sup>∘</sup> is high for the manufacturer in our model), then outsourcing the training may be an option worth exploring. Many learning process outsourcing companies provide high quality services and comprehensive training capabilities, including 3D multimedia and interactive web delivery. If the fees paid to these consulting services can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the increase in the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, then contracting with these service providers can lower $t ^ { \circ }$ and make training a critical component in product offering.

In this paper, we assume that learning is perfect. That is, all the customers who go through the training program will be able to learn all the $n ^ { \circ }$ features. However, in reality, learning is never perfect. Customers may not be able to learn all the n<sup>∘</sup> features after the training program. If we assume that a $n ^ { \prime } { < } n ^ { \circ }$ <sup>∘</sup> features are actually learnt, then it is not dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to conclude that training is even less favorable. Required training will hurt manufacturer more and there will be less customers who will choose the training program if training is optional. In this case, the manufacturer will have even less incentive to offer training program. The optimal number of features covered in training will decrease.

Another alternative to the manufacturer is to charge training separately and allow customers to choose whether to pay for it. In comparison, our current optional training case can be considered as a special case where the training charges are arbitrarily small. As training cost increases, the customers will become less willing to pay for training and hence training will have less impact on the result. As a result, only those with a high training cost t will purchase the training. However, the manufacturer should be able to earn a higher, or at least equal, pro<sup>fi</sup>t by charging training separately, since the optional training case is essentially adding a constraint of zero training charge in the maximization problem.

Our results also show the interrelationship between the training related costs and training capability of the manufacturer. If training can be provided relatively cost-effectively, then the manufacturer with less training capability can still improve pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by providing training. This explains why consumer training prevails in recent years. With the rapid penetration of the Internet, companies can deliver training online almost with zero marginal cost. The reduction in training related costs makes training a pro<sup>fi</sup>t increasing tool for companies. Furthermore, we show that if the training related costs are low, then the manufacturer would cover more features in the training, although he needs to fully realize that a long training program may burden some consumers. Another <sup>fi</sup>nding of the paper is that more features may be covered in a voluntary training than in a required training.

Our model assumes uniform distribution. There are other forms of consumer distributions that would be of interest to investigate in the future. In our model, each consumer derives the same value from each feature and spends the same amount of time learning each feature. Although we allow diversity among consumers, this is an assumption that may not be realistic but good enough for us to derive some basic intuitions. Future studies can explore other models with the assumptions relaxed. Finally, our framework provides foundations for future empirical study to verify the interrelationship between training costs and training capability thresholds.
