# Examination of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and interpersonal trust on continuous use intention: Evidence from experienced online community members

Jacob Chia-An Tsai<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Shin-Yuan Hung<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Yunlin University of Science and Technology, 123 University Road, Section 3, Douliou, Yunlin, 64002, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, Chia Yi62117, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online community Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Interpersonal trust Experienced members Continuous use intention

## A B S T R A C T

Members’ continued intention to use a community service is rooted in social identity. Past research has examined social identity but ignored the development of interpersonal trust through community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Drawing on social identity theory, one can conclude that community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is the foundation of a member’s con tinuous use intention through interpersonal trust. Data come from responses to a two-stage survey of experi enced members of an online game-based community. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the belief that the identity of the community will strengthen long-term relationships within it. The projection of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on continuous use intention is tied to interpersonal trust.

## 1. Introduction

Companies strive to <sup>fi</sup>nd new approaches to maintain relationships with their current customers, and thus turn them into committed members [20,57,117]. Practitioners and researchers have studied the structural mechanisms that attract customers to the company’s Busi ness-to-Consumer (B2C) community to increase their shares of the so cial commerce market [27,44,117]. Ren et al. [97] suggest that a major obstacle to online community success is engaging its members, because potential members have the full freedom to switch between alternative communities without having too many costs and restrictions. Un fortunately, most newcomers may not be ready to fully engage in the community, could easily misinterpret the shared culture, and might also refuse to commit to a long-term membership. They are vulnerable and at the far end of the loyalty spectrum, thus needing time to become active and contributing members of the community. However, experienced members, as major participants in the community, are likely to be the most engaged users due to their interest and knowledge. They better understand how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively engage into the community because they have devoted more time to build relationships and accumulate a wide range of local knowledge to evaluate other members contributions [109].

The experienced members’ participation in the community not only helps the communal development of long-term relationships with newcomers, but also establishes a collective identity by sharing a common history with them. Primarily they follow a consistent pattern that is more suitable to answer the proxy measure of loyalty-driven behavior as it relates to continuous use intention [71,85,125]. A better understanding of how the experienced members develop continuous use intention allows the community to realize their behavioral patterns and to extend their networking to attract more potential members. Many B2C communities have invested in user retention programs to protect against low switching costs and low entry barriers as well as limited di<sup>f</sup>erentiation in e-commerce [24]. This perspective has also been recognized by consumer behavior literature, because a company’s retention ability can ensure the long-term continuity of exchange between the company and their regular consumers who identify with the company product, which is critical for any company’s survival [11,12].

The literature on online communities suggests that members’ loyalty-driven behavior is rooted in identity and attachment therein [65,95,111,117]. The experienced members develop shared characteristics based on a common interest as they involve themselves with the community’s shared values and knowledge over a long period of time [109]. They possess local wisdom and learning based on years of accumulated observations of members’ behavior, which, in turn, results in a better ability to structure their own category-based assessments toward the entire community and to recognize the distinctiveness between the target community and others. They de<sup>fi</sup>ne themselves through the groups with which they are willing to belong [15,112]. Ingroup identity not only simulates loyalty-driven behaviors toward the group but also leads to ingroup favoritism [4,15,112]. This is because the ingroup members’ de<sup>fi</sup>nition of who they are is partially dependent on who they exclude from the group. A comparative approach is necessary to distinguish the groups to which certain members belong, which may result in ingroup favoritism [15,112].

Ingroup favoritism is featured as interpersonal trust in this study, which includes cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust toward the members of the community. Through interpersonal trust, one delineates the cognitive trust of believing in the community’s abilities, and the affective trust of investing emotional attachment to it. Cognitive trust leads members to believe in the quality and creditability of their fellow members, thus persuading them to use the community for a desired outcome. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust leads them to believe that their fellow members are not being sel<sup>fi</sup>sh by only taking care of themselves. As they embrace the a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, emotional investment in the social network encourages them to continually participate in this networking. Therefore, developing both cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust are not mutually exclusive but can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contribute to the continuous intention to use the community.

The challenge proposed by social identity theorists is that once a common identity is established, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to sustain, and it does not lead to continuous use intention automatically [88,97]. To investigate the relationship between social identity and continuous use intention, researchers mostly examined the role of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and group attachment [97]. However, they ignored the development of interpersonal trust from the experienced members’ perspective, which is very valuable. It examines how members who identify with the community will connect with the other members and how it eventually translates their shared identity into continuous use intention. Although information systems (IS) researchers have devoted several studies to further the general understanding of social identity and trust in the online community, existing IS studies largely consider cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust to be a single entity, thus paying little attention to the importance of building the causality between them. They have not yet examined how experienced members base their identity on inherently di<sup>f</sup>erent trusts toward the community.

Recent IS studies also suggest that cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust have di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects on use intention, and the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of identity on either cognitive trust, a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, or on use intention is somehow contradictory to the theory [19,95,111]. Many prior trust studies have excessively focused on website subscriptions, such as those between members and the community [33,66,122], or online sellers and the website [56,87,117], rather than on the self and the community. In addition, whether interpersonal trust can be empirically distinguished into cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trusts in the context of an online commu nity is unclear, and if so the relationship between them should receive more attention. Drawing on social identity theory (SIT), the objective of this study is to interpret how community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation relates to continuous use intention by examining cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust from the experienced members’ perspective. We argue that experienced members will consider community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to develop the psychological importance of cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust di<sup>f</sup>erently when considering future engagements.

The following section includes the theoretical background, hypotheses, and research model. The research method and sample collection are outlined, as well as a survey of one famous Taiwanese gamebased community, Bahamut. The data collected are then analyzed and interpreted. Finally, the implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are discussed, and suggestions for associated participants and researchers are provided.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Social identity theory

SIT explains how individuals develop positive perceptions toward their membership in a social category to which they attach signi<sup>fi</sup>cant emotional value. Self-categorization and social comparison are the most important processes in formulating identity [106]. Self-categorization forces individuals to place themselves in a particular category and exist signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in relation to other opposing categories. Social comparison enables an individual to cognitively and a<sup>f</sup>ectively evaluate the category to which they belong, thus providing a feeling of superiority and thereby justifying their belonging. Ashforth and Mael [4] de<sup>fi</sup>ne distinctiveness, prestige, and the salience of outgroups as the essentials of social identity formation. Distinctiveness di<sup>f</sup>erentiates the social category from others and provides a unique collective value. The in-group’s superiority creates prestige, by which the individuals evaluate themselves more positively than out-group persons. Out-group threats and competition usually enhance intergroup discrimination, thus leading to a stronger defensive and negative evaluation toward such individuals. Social identity encourages individuals to support and to be loyal to the social category, as well as to behave cooperatively and altruistically toward ingroup individuals and adhere to collective norms [4,106].

Pan et al. [88,p 73] suggest that social media users’ continuous participation “depends not only on personal factors but also on their conceptions of themselves that originate from other members.” The formation of social identity involves the processes of self-categorization and social comparison, which is the perception of a person in terms of his or her membership within a category rather than as an independent entity. The implicit assumption in SIT is that memberships are aggregated to establish common identity and thus create an enduring ingroup relationship [4,106]. We depict the relationships between self and interpersonal concepts in Fig. 1. For example, an individual in a game-based community (Bahamut) de<sup>fi</sup>nes himself in terms of the membership with which he classi<sup>fi</sup>es himself. Based on the shared characteristics of interpersonal relationships with group members, he perceives himself as an actual or even symbolic member of the community (I am a Bahamut gamer). As a result, an individual in a particular social category will likely promote his or her in-group favoritism toward a group of members who have been classi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the same social category [4,106].

This study de<sup>fi</sup>nes community identification as an individual’ perception of a community of members resulting from the individual deriving part of their own positive categorization from membership within the said community. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation allows members to exert e<sup>f</sup>ort on behalf of the community and to distinguish their ingroup from out-group membership. Members tend to make comparisons between in-groups and out-groups and view in-group members more positively in order to establish the distinction. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Turner [112] claims that individuals always prefer to seek a positive identity, which indicates that they will likely perceive their social category more positively than other categories and will have more favorable evaluations toward in-group individuals. Trust is a prominent aspect of ingroup favoritism, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the belief that each in-group individual will bene<sup>fi</sup>t the entire category [114]. Self-categorization and social comparison processes lead members to evaluate their category positively, which makes them feel safe and elicit positive emotions toward the ingroup individuals. In contrast, a negative evaluation of ingroup individuals will eliminate any expectation of in-group trust. Thus, ingroup members are likely to be perceived as more trustworthy, and the absence of trust toward out-group individuals is a typical type of discrimination and prejudice [32,114].

Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is present when team members relate well with their partners, and it enables the development of social connections as they understand mutual interests [119]. The development of trust by increasing the extent of in-group identity has been discussed by prior behavioral studies [43,80,120]. If in-group identity is structured in working teams, previous studies suggest that team members will contribute to the team mentality more than required, and will consider their role in broader team situations [43]. That is, individuals with a close categorical relationship in a social network will provide the knowledge and a<sup>f</sup>ects necessary for each other to constitute both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust [100]. Second, close categorical relationships provide the foundation for mutual understanding, as individuals can make their own judgments on whether the others are reliable or have the integrity related to establishing cognitive trust [100]. Last, individuals with close categorical relationships can provide support and consolation to others, thus showing a<sup>f</sup>ection toward each other for developing a<sup>f</sup>ective trust [47].

## 2.2. Trust

An online community is a virtual place for members to interact and share experiences, thus developing partnerships with each other over time [121]. The members are normally strangers to one another [98]. They develop their relationship and emotional attachment to the community through interaction [38]. Generally, trust refers to a subjective probability derived from an individual assessment of another agent performing a particular action [78]. Hsiao and Chiou [50] stand for the sociological perspective of investigating how a game player’s position in an online gaming community leads to the continued use of the said games. They claim that community trust is particularly important, as it represents an expectation that there will be less opportunistic behaviors between members.

The central statement of trust is that trust encourages members to collectively perform transactions and mitigate risk when they interact with each other. Trust is even more important in the online community, which it is designed for strangers to interact and allows extant member to connect with unknown members from anywhere with common interests. The development of trust among members is based on how many social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and how much support they obtain from the community [16,38]. There is no guarantee, however, that the membe can always obtain the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from anonymous members. As a result, members must take a certain degree of risk in trusting that anonymous members will behave in mutually supportive ways and respond as expected [77,96].

A growing body of studies has discussed the importance of trust in a border online context including online marketplace, e-commerce, mcommerce, social network sites, and social commerce. The di<sup>fi</sup>culty of building trust has received much attention from behavioral research in the IS community through their study of how to form trust and to assert its potential e<sup>f</sup>ect on user commitment, participation, purchase intention, and use intention (see Appendix A). Although higher degrees of trust in the online community have been proven to increase individual intentions such as self-disclosure, purchase, and intention to buy and repurchase, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trust on individual intentions seems inconsistent across di<sup>f</sup>erent studies. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some studies show trust as having a strong e<sup>f</sup>ect on behavioral intention [10,56,65,69,75,92,111,115,116], whereas others demonstrate little e<sup>f</sup>ect [87,89,117,122], or even no e<sup>f</sup>ect at all [1,33,66,75,122].

A review of trust from past literature also indicates that trust has been conceptualized in many ways. Most studies consider it to be a form of cognitive trust toward a particular object, such as a product, company, service, Website, or community. Others view it in a more general form, such as the trust of community members. Based on the complexity and depth of the concept of trust, previous studies developed di<sup>f</sup>erent views to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the core characteristics. First, trust can be considered to be a multi-dimensional construct that has distinct components (e.g., [78,79,81]), yet it can also be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a one-dimensional construct which is formulated from the expectation of someone’s reliable behavior or statements (e.g. [49,50,56,87,89,92],). Second, some take an integrative approach to conceptualize trust as a belief that is based on another’s demonstration of rational (integrity and judgment) and emotional (benevolent) behavior [10,66,74,91,116]. Previous organizational studies have di<sup>f</sup>erentiated between cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective components of trust and highlighted that trust is involved with both rational and emotional components (e.g., [67,68,79,100,101,108]).

Most previous studies conceptualize trust as a higher-order construct comprising integrity, ability, and benevolence [118]. Typically, beliefs of integrity and ability are considered to be cognitive trust, whereas benevolence is an example of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust [118]. Although these three dimensions collectively formulate trust, the emergence of cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, driven by di<sup>f</sup>erent antecedents, has been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed [84], and it has long been argued that the causal relationship between the two is strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the research context. As suggested by Robert et al. [99], the common characteristics of virtual settings may outline the di<sup>fi</sup>culties of building trust due to the lack of well-developed personal relationships for individuals to judge team members’ trustworthiness and due to the involvement of diverse personal backgrounds limiting the ability of individuals to develop similarities [99]. These characteristics may impede individuals in assessing cognitive trust e<sup>f</sup>ectively, thus leading to a failure to further develop a<sup>f</sup>ective trust over time.

Individuals develop a<sup>f</sup>ective trust based on emotional responses from members, as demonstrated by the experienced members in an online community who are normally sourced from social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and personal preference [53]. Because online communities require a higher level of sociability and create stronger feelings of belonging, the affective aspects of trust are in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors toward continuous use intention [23,75,122]. As a result, we argue that without having suf-<sup>fi</sup>cient levels of cognitive trust, members will easily switch to another online community that has better content and resources. Conversely, without having su<sup>fi</sup>cient levels of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, members may continue to use the community if it can still provide useful information, but they will leave quickly once they <sup>fi</sup>nd a new community that is attractive and more reliable. Therefore, as the underlying processes of their in<sup>fl</sup>uences are not identical, both cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust are separately important for the continued intention to participate in a community.

## 2.3. Interpersonal trust in a game-based community

Membership in di<sup>f</sup>erent online communities is dependent on dif ferent business models that virtually constrain members’ behaviors and activities. A game-based community is considered to be an information sharing community that provides a virtual platform where its members can exchange their comments on their favorite games with others [51]. Similar to the border online context, game-based communities allow members to exchange gaming feedback and innovative knowledge, and to provide their own views and experiences that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence other gamers’ intention to purchase products (see Table 2). Trust toward members has frequently been investigated and has proved to be critical in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing knowledge-sharing and intention to stay. However, previous studies have measured the concept of trust di<sup>f</sup>erently and shown inconsistent results, thus hindering our understanding of the role of trust in game-based communities. The complex array of units and levels of analysis of trust and its multifaceted nature require more explanations regarding how interpersonal trust has been applied in this study. The conceptual development of interpersonal trust is outlined and discussed below.

Interpersonal trust is viewed as two di<sup>f</sup>erent components - cognitive trust and afective trust - to specify the relationships among the components of trust and their di<sup>f</sup>erent relationships with antecedents and consequences [108]. Cognitive trust means that a member has a set of rational beliefs that the other community members are competent, benevolent, have integrity, and are reliable in certain situations, which develops from an individual’s “rational expectations that the trustees will have the necessary attributes to be relied upon” [68, p.943]. It is mainly based on others’ performance behavior that is enhanced by the shared understanding of the dilemma and the belief that everyone will cooperate to resolve the dilemma [100]. Individuals with cognitive trust toward a group will have a rationalized expectation that they are involved with members who possess attributes that make them de pendable [68]. If they are aware that members are free-riding, community members will be less willing to contribute in order to avoid being taken advantage of [94]. As the e<sup>f</sup>ort in building an online community is intangible, the fear that community members may behave opportunistically can be reduced by increasing the expectation that members are competent, honest, and predictable [49]. Cognitive trust plays a dominant role in mitigating the possible suspicion among members, as it represents a degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the cooperative intentions of others [79]. With a con<sup>fi</sup>dent perception of the honesty and reliability of the exchange members can pursue, they will have higher intentions to cooperate and participate in the online group [59,60,100].

Members do not form interpersonal trust by relying only on rational beliefs; they also constitute an a<sup>f</sup>ective trust based heavily on intuitions and feelings. In this case, a<sup>f</sup>ective trust means that a member has a set of emotional beliefs that include caring for the other members and concern for the community's welfare. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust is “grounded in reciprocated interpersonal care and concern” [79, p.25], and involves a personal emotional response to be treated in a way that makes him or her feel secure and cared for [108]. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust is rooted in an emotional attachment between a trustor and a trustee [79]. Members with a higher a<sup>f</sup>ective trust toward the community have invested a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time and emotion in their memberships. Rock mann and Northcraft [100, p. 110] highlighted that “a<sup>f</sup>ective-based trust, in particular, should engender a sense of relational reciprocity that should lower the rate of deception.” In the online marketplace, Sun [108] determined that a<sup>f</sup>ective trust provides a warranty for trustors, thus granting them a positive expectation toward their current interaction with trustees. It is expected that members with high a<sup>f</sup>ective trust represent greater intentions to stick with the community because they are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with past relationships and perceive the desired a<sup>f</sup>ect from the usage. Hsiao and Chiou [50] also found that community trust improves a player’s intention to stay in an online game, because such a community is considered reciprocal altruism, and all members have a sense to guard against cheating in reciprocal partnerships.

Social identity consists of self-identity and membership in a social category. We argue that the self-identity of an experienced member is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by his or her in-group evaluation process, which in turn is associated with membership to produce interpersonal trust. Thus, interpersonal trust in this study will focus on the collective level, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a collectively-held trust toward the community, as opposed to an individual person or institution. In the online community context, Chen and Shen [23] a<sup>fi</sup>rm that trust toward an institution is developed through interpersonal trust. This is because trust among a group of members will lead them to believe that mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behavior is governed by the principles developed within the community, and a strong interpersonal trust has been recognized as a vital precursor to achieve institutional trust. In this regard, interpersonal trust is the trust the individual places on the community, based on and generated by being a member of the same game-based community.

Interpersonal trust is reinforced by community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which is generated by self-categorization and social comparison processes based on self-knowledge, experience, and interaction with members to categorize oneself into a desired prototype. Theoretically, interpersonal trust emerges from ingroup interaction over time and does not imply naively trusting others in general (e.g., Fang, Shao and Wen [34]). In this study, interpersonal trust depends on the con<sup>fi</sup>dent expectation that other community members can be relied upon to act with reciprocal altruism. This conceptualization of interpersonal trust does not re<sup>fl</sup>ect an uncertain anticipation of the trustees’ future behavior, and places less emphasis on the extent to which the trustor is willing to be vulnerable to potential risky behavior by the trustees. Interpersonal trust is not directly related to dispositional trust, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting expectancies about the trustworthiness of others in general (e.g., Hallikainen and Laukkanen [42]), yet interpersonal trust is a form of relational trust per taining to the community members as a whole over time.

Another di<sup>f</sup>erent type of relational trust has been expanded to a societal level. Social trust is a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of relational trust in a social capital perspective, which represents an overall generalized trust of people in a society [28,46,93]. Social trust has been conceptualized as either an institutional or cultural foundation, rather than as a relational foundation. Social trust does not require the existence of a membership toward an individual or a particular group because it depends on institutional and cultural constraints (structures, regulations, and shared norms) to assure that people will behave dependably and faithfully [64]. Using social trust to study a group of anonymous newcomers in an online community would be preferred because their relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments are not as great, and uncertainty is likely to be present. Newcomers are more likely to require the presence of social trust in order to know what to expect and how to communicate their expectations to others.

Interpersonal trust and social trust are inherently di<sup>f</sup>erent in terms of their multifaceted nature, units, and levels of analysis. Social trust is a single-faceted trust where one expects that most people in the given society can be trusted in general [28,46,93], whereas the focus of interpersonal trust is that one develops cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust toward the community. The multifaceted nature of interpersonal trust, cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, could be related or could be separable and vary independently of each other [35]. Furthermore, although social trust has its basis in individuals, interpersonal trust requires a trustor who has experience and a history of interaction with a group over time in order to devote his or her trust toward the community. Social trust attempts to loosen this requirement of mutual interaction because it emphasizes cultural features and social restrictions that contribute to social trust [93]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, social trust is commonly measured at a system level, whereas interpersonal trust is measured at a relational level [28]. Trust toward an institution such as a society or a community is quite di<sup>f</sup>erent from assuming that members will spontaneously trust one another.

Finally, although previous studies have de<sup>fi</sup>ned calculative, a<sup>f</sup>ec tive, and normative commitments to explain participative behavior [8], trust and commitment are two fundamental elements in the development of long-term relationships [5,36,83]. Commitment entails sacri-<sup>fi</sup>ce and vulnerability, and members are unlikely to be committed unless trust is already established [36,83]. This theoretical di<sup>f</sup>erence leads to the determinant of commitment and trust, which is worthy of investigation. Previous studies have de<sup>fi</sup>ned calculative, a<sup>f</sup>ective, and normative commitments to explain participative behavior [8]. Calculative commitment is based on the perception of net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that are not easily available in other places. Instead of focusing on the economic aspect of cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t, we highlight the importance of reliability and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the conception of cognitive trust. A<sup>f</sup>ective commitment tends to examine members’ commitment toward the institution rather than being associated with individuals [8]. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust establishes a context conducive to more emotional relationships and leads members to feel fairly treated and supported by others. Thus, a<sup>f</sup>ective commit ment toward a particular community is a consequence of high levels of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust [30]. Chen and Shen [23] also prove that the commit ment is derived from trust, and their empirical results show that interpersonal trust is di<sup>f</sup>erent from commitment in terms of their speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects on individual intentions. Finally. normative commitment is hard to capture in the online community context because the target of ob ligation and the value of social norms vary from group to group. Bateman et al. [8] thus suggest that the a<sup>f</sup>ective aspect is able to explain a part of the variance of normative trust. Overall, the theoretical di<sup>f</sup>erences in trust and commitment as well as the separate e<sup>f</sup>ects of cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust on individual intentions warrant a closer examination.

## 3. Conceptual model and hypotheses development

## 3.1. The relationship between community identification, cognitive trust, and afective trust

Members need a strong sense of identity as a result of emotional bonds in order to feel comfortable in their respective community and to feel interpersonal care and concern for others [54]. Theoretically, identity can motivate members to engage in supportive behavior by ensuring that the general welfare will not be left behind by individual interests [18]. As members de<sup>fi</sup>ne themselves as being cognitively and emotionally connected with the community, they will strengthen their beliefs that others will behave in a positive way and be willing to look after one another [54]. This is because an in-group positive interdependence leads to interpersonal trust, whereas an out-group negative interdependence leads to distrust [15,112].

The development of identity is a cognitive state of self-categorization based on the shared characteristics derived from one’s membership. Where identity is made more explicit, members develop a better understanding in assessing ability and detecting opportunistic behavior. The experienced members have a more shared understanding regarding others’ behavior, along with an available shared knowledge to judge members’ ability. This can produce an accurate judgment and thus has the potential to increase cognitive trust [79].

McAllister [79] argued that a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is formed based upon cognitive trust because team members need to build up rational expectations for peer reliability and dependability and then decide whether they should become emotionally invested in developing a relationship. Obviously, if an individual does not possess a con<sup>fi</sup>dent perception of the reliability and dependability of other members, he or she may not believe they will act in the best interests of the community, and thus fail to establish an emotional connection with them [100]. Sun [108,p. 192] states that “without strongly believing that the trustee will ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the expectations for him or her and behave honestly and competently, as predicted, and in the trustor’s best interest, the trustor is unlikely to feel comfortable and secure about relying on this trustee.” Cognitive trust encompasses the rational judgments about others’ trustworthiness and the probability of opportunistic behavior. It re duces uncertainty and increases individual emotional attachment toward cooperation [7].

We argue that cognitive trust should be established to facilitate affective trust formulation, because before experienced members become comfortable enough to rely on others, they need to be assured that the others will behave honestly and competently to meet their self-categorization. Therefore, the trust of member’s ability will serve as a prerequisite for the experienced ones to develop a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. The relationship between community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, thus, is reduced. We expect that the experienced members with explicit community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation will tend to develop emotional bonds through the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the others’ abilities.

H1. Cognitive trust will mediate a positive relationship between

community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust.

## 3.2. The relationship between cognitive trust, afective trust, and continuous use intention

Previous studies suggested that when an a<sup>f</sup>ective component is established, the cognitive component will become the basis to supplement trust [102]. This is because sharing a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is likely to result in psychological safety and respect for the competence of members [101], such that they have less desire or motivation to verify the extent of the performance and professional credentials of the trustees. They are likely moving from a cognitive gains-and-losses approach to an emotional response based on connections, viewing cognitive trust as a reference base for individuals to con<sup>fi</sup>rm members’ ability in developing a<sup>f</sup>ective trust [102]. The experienced members have a history of interaction with unfamiliar members to develop a<sup>f</sup>ective connections by which to judge whether each member is acting sel<sup>fi</sup>shly by only taking care of him or herself, making sure they have the ability to put more emphasis on a<sup>f</sup>ective components in constituting long-term relation ships. They have more opportunities to evaluate calculated risks regarding others’ ability and integrity, and in turn to utilize this understanding to build secure and reciprocal connections with others.

Members who are frequently involved in interactions within the group would view a<sup>f</sup>ective trust as having more importance to the long-term group relationship than that of cognitive trust [63]. When considering implications of individual behavior on loyalty, having affective trust toward other members is vital to ensure that a lasting relationship can be initiated and maintained [97]. We argue in hypothesis 2 that the experienced members involved in a history of developing social ties are moving from a cognitive gains-and-losses approach to an emotional response based on emotional ties. At this point, cognitive trust serves as a reference point to underpin a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. The experienced members are more likely to be attached by their emotional bonds to the community only if they believe other members’ capabilities are considered important to the community. The relationship between cognitive trust and continuous use intention is thus reduced by considering the mediator of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. As cognitive trust is evolved into a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, the experienced members will no longer need to largely take cognitive trust into account in developing future engagements. ## 3.3. Control variables of interpersonal trust

The formulation of cognitive trust is largely based on the success of past interactions and the extent of similarity. While establishing cognitive trust, past performance becomes a strong input in assessing trustworthiness, such as whether members are competent, benevolent, and reliable [79]. High response quality is expected if the members make an e<sup>f</sup>ort to provide valuable information from their available resources when searching for a solution for the trustor. The quality of the replies to the questions in the community provide a signal of past performance, thus establishing cognitive trust as likely, depending on the quality of the response. As suggested by McAllister [79, p.28], in a working group “peer performance can have a determining impact on personal productivity, and evidence that peers carry out role responsibilities reliably will enhance a manager's assessment of a peer's trustworthiness.” On the other hand, if individuals do not perceive the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of group discussion as being of high quality, they are indeed less willing to agree that other members are reliable and dependable [59,60,73]. Thus, quality of response is considered a control variable for cognitive trust.

Members usually trust suggestions from people who come from similar backgrounds or share the same interests, making the in<sup>fl</sup>uence inferred from enthusiasts particularly important to build cognitive trust. A person tends to establish and maintain relationships with others who have similar interests (i.e., online game partners) or background (i.e., friends and colleagues) [82]. A common characteristic shared by members would facilitate mutual communication and cause members to favor personal reliance on each other as they have su<sup>fi</sup>cient un derstanding of each other’s perspectives [17]. We consider this characteristic in the online community to be the result of enthusiastic re commendations in which the pros and cons of a community are cognitively derived and assessed by a friend with similar interests. For example, the more positive the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from enthusiasts who have great interest in a particular subject, the more likely the individual will establish cognitive trust toward this subject. Thus, enthusiast re commendation is considered a control variable for cognitive trust.

Perceived enjoyment is a process-based a<sup>f</sup>ective construct which refers to the extent to which the activity of using the technology is viewed as enjoyable [57,126]. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne enjoyment in a community as the extent to which enjoyment can be derived from using the commu nity. A<sup>f</sup>ective states such as enjoyment and pleasure have been pro posed as important factors to in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust formation [58]. Previous studies view a<sup>f</sup>ective trust as a representation of relationship quality, and argue that this quality is based on emotional reactions to social relationships with others instead of a cognitive calculation of gains and losses [68]. A positive evaluation of one’s experience using the com munity shapes one’s perception to produce a positive feeling that social interactions are comfortable, thus reinforcing the belief that members have the best interests of the community at heart. Thus, perceived en joyment is considered a control variable for a<sup>f</sup>ective trust.

An increase in a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is also derived from a reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>t system of community members. Members who perceive the community as a source of social bene<sup>fi</sup>t will be more likely to remain with a<sup>f</sup>ection and to reciprocate their perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with an emotional attachment to it [61]. Wu and Tsang [123] found that when the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were realized by trusters, they saw that trustees cared about the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of both parties; they were led to believe that they would ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the promises they made. Members should not mandatorily, but voluntarily assist other members by providing a broad range of advice and guidance [52]. Users can access the community to retrieve distributed knowledge or obtain the desired answer from dis cussion. If they are able to take such reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the community, they will have a sense that the other members care for the community, which makes the users more likely to build an emotional attachment[70]. Rockmann and Northcraft [100] propose that users will constitute negative a<sup>f</sup>ective trust and feel annoyed if they receive a lot of unrelated or useless information in their social network. McAllister [79] explains that a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is grounded in the development of altruistic behavior from the expectation of reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The perceived reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts reduce the suspicion that members will not continue to reciprocate with the community; therefore, the user is more willing to develop emotional connections with members. Thus, perceived reciprocal benefits are considered a control variable for affective trust.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Measures

We adapted the measured items from previous studies and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed them to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the context of an online community. We reviewed and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the related theoretical constructs through an iterative process with <sup>fi</sup>ve online community users who had more than 5 years of member experience, as well as two academic experts. The constructs have been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed through a two-stage approach. First, we conducted a sorting method based on the item-construct classi<sup>fi</sup>cation from a panel of experienced members to secure construct reliability and face validity. Second, we demonstrated construct validity by conducting a pilot test on convenient samples. In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned interpersonal trust, continuous use intention, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as an individual level construct and indicated the level of analysis in this study as such.

Data collection was conducted in one of the most representative online gaming communities. All the respondents were focused on a particular community instead of a generic one. Although some studies approached identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as a group-level construct, we conceptualized community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as an individual perception that involved cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective concerns toward the community. Gundlach et al. [37] asserted that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an individual-level concept as it represents the extent to which individual members perceive a sense of belonging with a particular group. As a result, community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was measured as the extent to which an individual perceives himself or herself as representative of a particular online community [2,76].

The measured items of cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust were developed by McAllister [79] in organizational settings, which were adapted and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the context of an online community. Cognitive trust refers to the rational expectation that the community members are competent, honest, and predictable. The measurement of cognitive trust consisted of <sup>fi</sup>ve items measured on the rational assessment of the community members. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust refers to the emotional assessment that the members reciprocate care and concern. The measurement of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust consisted of <sup>fi</sup>ve items measured on the emotional attachments of the members. Continuous use intention is referred to as the extent to which individuals will use the community in the future [113]. We included two measurement items to capture the full meaning of this. One was to measure individual intention to continue to participate in the future. The other was to measure individual intention to frequently return to it in the future.

There were four control variables for cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust: quality of response, enthusiast recommendation, perceived enjoyment, and perceived reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In terms of the control variables for cognitive trust, quality of response was measured as the extent to which an individual perceived and approved of the quality of response regarding online posts, replies, and answers [107]. Enthusiast recommendation was measured as the extent to which an individual perceived the social recommendation from similar enthusiasts [2,53]. In terms of the control variables for a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, perceived enjoyment was measured as the extent to which an individual perceived their interaction and participation to be enjoyable [57]. Perceived reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were addressed by measuring the degree to which an individual perceived that using the community would allow them to obtain certain shared bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from other members [52,70].

All the measurement items used in this study were subjected to pretest and pilot testing using academic experts and relevant samples. Although the original items were developed by Western countries using items in English Language to capture the meanings of the constructs of interest, we conducted the survey in Eastern countries by using a Chinese-based online gaming community as a research site. Translation issues in the construct development should be taken into account. Translation and back-translation procedures were used to obtain appropriate meanings for the measurement items to ensure reliability and validity. We <sup>fi</sup>rst translated the original items to create a Chinese survey, and then a back-translation of the Chinese survey was conducted by an independent bilingual expert. These procedures enabled us to judge whether the original meaning of the studied items was fully represented and preserved.

In the pretest process, we invited two MIS professionals and fou Ph.D. students to evaluate the measurement items. According to their feedback, we re<sup>fi</sup>ned unclear and wordy sentences to pursue item clarity. We conducted two rounds of Q-sorting to further illustrate re liability and validity [110]. As experienced members in the online gaming community were the targeted population, we recruited a panel of 10 experienced gaming members as judges, and randomly divided them into two separate groups; each independent group consisting of <sup>fi</sup>ve experienced users. The Q-sorting method is characterized by using a panel of independent judges’ assessments to re<sup>fl</sup>ect their subjective impressions of the items and then to classify them into the intended categories [110]. We adopted the overall hit ratio as the evaluated criterion to assess the results of Q-sorting, presented in Table 3. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst round, the overall hit ratio averaged 85%. The misclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed item were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, showing that some judges were confused and unable to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. To identify the cause of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation in round one, we conducted a short interview with the judges to understand their subjective impressions of the items and then modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the items based on their feedback. The second round of Q-sorting was conducted by inviting another group of judges. In this round, the overall hit ratio averaged 95%, revealing a 10% improvement from the previous round, thus indicating reliability and validity were established.

Having gained con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the measured items, we then moved to conduct a pilot test through convenient samples. Thirty Masters’ students who had experience in participating in the online community were engaged in the pilot test process. Based on their suggestions, several items were slightly re<sup>fi</sup>ned to improve understanding of the respective constructs. All measured items are shown in the Appendix B using a seven-point Likert scale ranking from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

## 4.2. Sample and data collection

The emergence of online communities can be recognized as various types including functional, social, and hedonic ones. Communities can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed by their shared characteristics and the purposes of their registered members. DeSanctis, Fayard, Roach and Jiang [31] concluded that information kiosks, associations, and communities of practice are three primary types of online communities. Armstrong and Hagel [3] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four types: transactional, interest-based, fantasy, and relationship communities. Previous studies have outlined di<sup>f</sup>erent types of communities and activities taken up by their members; however, a particular community may fall into more than one de<sup>fi</sup>nitions or may overlap with others in terms of its functionalities and attributes [57]. Hence, the main objective of a community has been acknowl edged as the important schema for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [3].

Di<sup>f</sup>erent types of communities are developed with di<sup>f</sup>erent objectives and characteristics to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll di<sup>f</sup>erent needs. The boundaries of a community will enable the member to realize its objective along with its identity. The structure of the member-community interaction needs to suit its members’ expectations so that each one can participate in group activities and be part of his or her community. A careful review of previous IS literature has shown a few objectives of online communities that can be used for classifying them. Two main types have grown in intensity during recent years: the information-sharing community and the social-networking community [29]. As indicated by the Armstrong and Hagel [3] study, the information-sharing community is related to the interest-based community where members can exchange and search novel events on speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics of interest to them. The target sample of this study was selected from a game-based community. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed Bahamut as a research site because it is the largest game-oriented community in Taiwan. It contains numerous forums concerning online games, video games, and computer games, and it is the one of most popular online communities in the online gaming domain. Bahamut is an information-sharing community where members can exchange their comments on their favorite games and share their gaming information with others.

We developed prerequisites to <sup>fi</sup>lter inexperienced members from our samples. A workshop was hosted by the research team along with the administrators to reach a consensus about the prerequisites. Based on the reviews of user pro<sup>fi</sup>les from previous studies using the same research site as a sample, we developed the prerequisites and discussed with administrators the appropriateness of existing requirements to assess member experience. As a result, respondents to the survey were <sup>fi</sup>ltered according to prerequisites pertaining to (1) the length of time of registration, (2) the frequency of visits, (3) post frequency, and (4) the number of online friends and guilds. We detailed the prerequisites as follows.

First, the respondents should be registered members for at least 24 months, which is the common standard to distinguish inexperienced from experienced members [9,19]. Second, we considered visit frequency as an index to identify the experienced members. We sought respondents who visited the community more than 20 times per week over a 6-month period, with each visit lasting longer than 30 min, meaning that the targeted respondents spent more than 10 h each week in the community. Post frequency was another requirement to ensure that experienced members have a certain degree of interaction through online activities. Targeted respondents were required to have posted more than 300 posts. Lastly, respondents were required to have at least three members as their online friends and must have also joined at least one guild in the community. Respondents quali<sup>fi</sup>ed for this study if they satis<sup>fi</sup>ed all the above prerequisites.

Data collection was conducted in a two-stage survey design. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we released our online survey to the homepage of the Bahamut community, where members could access the hyperlink to respond to the survey. The <sup>fi</sup>rst page in our online survey introduced the purpose of this study and promised that the survey would keep respondents' identities con<sup>fi</sup>dential. The second page required the potential respondents to complete the survey about their use and experience. If they could not satisfy all the prerequisites, they were directed to the end page and rendered unable to continue to answer the rest of the questions. The quali<sup>fi</sup>ed respondents were directed to another page to answer the constructs of interest. They were asked to complete the survey with regard to community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, control variables, and their continued intention to use the community. We recorded each respondent’s IP address, domain name, and personal email to avoid the potential dangers associated with repeat respondents in the online survey. We screened the responses and checked for repeat respondents by <sup>fi</sup>nding those details that were duplicated. The repeat respondent were eliminated to ensure accurate sample representation.

In the second stage, the survey was sent to respondents based on the email they provided during the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage survey. Generally, we emailed the survey to the respondents within 2 weeks after they com pleted the <sup>fi</sup>rst one. In the second stage, the research focus was altered to address the evaluation of interpersonal trust toward members, and the respondents were required to <sup>fi</sup>ll out the survey assessing cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. To encourage response and reduce a nonresponse bias, we provided free prize drawings to participants in the second survey. As we discussed above, we focused on the individual level to study the relationship between community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interpersonal trust, and continuous use intention. Therefore, obtaining data from di<sup>f</sup>erent individuals was impracticable. Podsako<sup>f</sup> et al. [90] suggest that separating the measurement by introducing a time lag between cause and e<sup>f</sup>ect is a potential way to lessen the risk of common method variance (CMV). The two-stage design allowed us to provide di<sup>f</sup>erent themes for respondents, making their perceived independent and mediated variables, or mediated and dependent variables, not directly related. This psychological separation of measurement also enabled us to control the risk of CMV [90].

The initial sample consisted of 732 members from the Bahamut Gamer community. However, 54 respondents did not reply to our questionnaires at the second stage. We decided to omit these incomplete samples in the following analysis, thus leaving 678 usable questionnaires for data analysis. The samples were expected to demonstrate knowledge and experience in the targeted online community. Descriptive details are presented in Table 4. Many of the respondent were male and varied in terms of age, education, and occupation. The homogeneity test was applied to the demographic data between thi study and previous studies which targeted online game communities as a sampling pool [53,55]. The chi-square test showed there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence between our studies and previous studies with a 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence level in terms of gender, age, and education, which suggested that our sample characteristics are representative within the context of the game-based community.

## 5. Data analysis and results

This study used SmartPLS path modeling software to test the proposed hypotheses. SmartPLS is a component-based technique for explaining construct relationships and performing analysis involving re-<sup>fl</sup>ective and formatives constructs [72]. Because the partial least squares (PLS) approach is applicable for testing theory in the initial stage, it is especially suitable for researchers focused on the prediction of dependent variables [39,72]. We determined that it was appropriate to test the hypotheses in our proposed model. PLS path modeling with bootstrap resampling techniques was used to obtain parameter estimates and con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals [39]. Generally, there are two analytical procedures. First, examining internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity is required in the measurement model. Second, estimating the structural path and the explanatory power are required in the structure model. We detailed the results of both these models below.

## 5.3. Common method variance

A time-lag mechanism was employed in the data collection to remedy the contamination of CMV, particularly when we were studying attitude-behavior relationships [90]. CMV is a potential problem in studying behavioral research because we measure our constructs with the systematic method from the same source, which could in<sup>fl</sup>ate the estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cients among di<sup>f</sup>erent constructs. Following Chin et al’s suggestion, we applied the measure latent marker variable (MLMV) approach to correct the estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cients in order to ensure the robustness of the obtained results [26]. In the second stage data col lection, we implemented four MLMV items that assume no theoretical relationship within the current research model. These items were employed at the end of the questionnaire, asking individuals to evaluate their team learning tendency (either a working group or a studying group) regarding learning new ideas in general topics, solving abstract problems in reasoning tests, obtaining collective knowledge from media, or assessing a library/database to eliminate information gaps. These items were drawn from di<sup>f</sup>erent units of analysis in the context of team learning, but with the same format and scale to proxy the ef fects of CMV in our sample data [26].

## 6. Discussions and implications

The application of SIT to explain continuous use intention in an online community has drawn substantial interest in past IS research. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm the theoretical link in SIT. More speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally, cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on the continued intention to use a community service. We also found that cognitive trust will fully mediate the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. Hypothesis 1 focuses on how an experienced member develops a<sup>f</sup>ective trust through a self-categorization process. Furthermore, this a<sup>f</sup>ective trust will fully mediate the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of cognitive trust on continuous use intention. Hypothesis 2 focuses on how an experienced member develops the continued intention to use a community service by appropriating emotional attachment. The current research model advances our understanding concerning this through a fully developed mediation model. The above <sup>fi</sup>ndings examine the theoretical relationship be tween community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuous use intention while also enhancing our understanding of the method by which interpersonal trust explains the role of ingroup favoritism in SIT.

Previous IS studies in the context of an online community have not explicitly considered experienced users’ views to contribute new perspectives on the relationship between cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. Based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the relationship is important to explain and understand the relationship between community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and continuous use intention because cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust have fundamentally di<sup>f</sup>erent roles in ingroup favoritism. A<sup>f</sup>ective trust is created and strengthened when the experienced members are able to con<sup>fi</sup>rm others’ competence based on a previously assessed level of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, thus resulting in faith in the trustworthy intentions of others to engage in long-term relationships. This advances SIT by highlighting how the continued intention to use a community service can be facilitated by interpersonal trust through enhanced community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Despite previous contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning the theoretical link between identity and trust in SIT, this study demonstrates that identity has a distinct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on both cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. There is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant progress in the current IS studies to elucidate the relationship between community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, cognitive trust, and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings establish a new perspective. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation appears to be an important facilitator in cognitive trust development, and its direct e<sup>f</sup>ect on the development of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is diminished by the increasing cognitive trust. Experienced members have assessed the performance of past interactions and participated in the core activities so their community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation will include the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant competencies associated with members. To invest more resources in developing closer emotional ties with the community, experienced members need to de termine the credibility of the others, and thus a baseline level of cognitive trust must be con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Consequently, the fully mediated results imply that although community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation will induce emotional attachment, it is not strong enough to develop a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, especially when experienced members do not possess con<sup>fi</sup>dence in members competence and reliability. This contributes to the past IS research by clarifying the relationship between identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and interpersonal trust.

Trust has been recognized as one of the most prominent factors in explaining continuous use intention. The extant online community studies do not show a clear trend toward theorizing about the di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects of cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust on intention. We conceptualize ingroup favoritism in the SIT in a more explicit way to fully capture how experienced members exhibit their favoritism in a social network. This extends the role of the interpersonal trust lens in the context of an online community. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings bring new insights regarding the distinct e<sup>f</sup>ects within interpersonal trust. Although cognitive trust and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust alone have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on continuous use intention, integrating them in an explanation of continuous use intention reveals a more complete understanding. Compared to cognitive trust, a<sup>f</sup>ective trust is more relational and can lead experienced members to display more favorable actions and reciprocal relationships toward the other members. Thus, a<sup>f</sup>ective trust can better re<sup>fl</sup>ect the long-term exchange relationships relative to cognitive trust. The fully mediated results indicate that as long as community members are grounded in a cognitive appraisal of their predictability and reliability, experienced members will establish a connection as an expression of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust toward other members, who tend to place less emphasis on the cognitive gains and losses inherent in the commitment of a long-term relationship.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reconcile certain contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings from previous studies regarding the e<sup>f</sup>ects of trust and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on individual intentions. First, we established the mediated role of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust between cognitive trust and the continued intention to use. The results suggest that the two components of interpersonal trust do not contradict or exclude one another. Indeed, a cognitive appraisal of the situation is the foundation for developing a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. Therefore, in stead of focusing on the absence of cognitive trust, we highlight the importance of the a<sup>f</sup>ective trust violation as the primary issue in di minishing individual intentions. Second, in the light of previous studie that found insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation on individual intentions, we found that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was associated to the trust of the ingroup members as a whole. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation may have little e<sup>f</sup>ect on the commitment of long-term relationships if members themselves have di<sup>fi</sup>culty developing interpersonal trust rooted in their self-de<sup>fi</sup>ned categories.

Commitment-trust theory has recognized that both trust and commitment are key constructs to develop long-term relationships, and that commitment mediates the link between trust and future intention [5,36,83]. However, while some IS studies indicate that commitment partially mediates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of trust on continued intention [45,103], others declare that commitment is unable to mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of trust because members only perceive a community as a communication platform making it hard to develop commitment [122]. Therefore, the unique explained variance of trust on the continuous use intention should not be ignored. Compared to previous commitment studies, we completely acknowledge that the a<sup>f</sup>ective component plays the most important role in determining use intention [8,62]. In contrast to the previous commitment studies that show that both calculative and affective components in commitment can increase participative intentions, we found that the experienced members who perceive a cognitive trust component will not participate actively but will expand this aspect to help them develop emotional attachment.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide guidance to administrators who are attempting to retain experienced members in an online community. Such members contribute knowledge, shared values, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in pursuit of a common interest and are considered important assets to the said com munity. It is encouraging that they can develop a shared identify within it, but administrators need to ensure that the community is concrete enough to induce interpersonal trust that ful<sup>fi</sup>lls their self-de<sup>fi</sup>ned needs and goals. We suggest that the shared knowledge and the collective behavior perceived by experienced members must be managed in a more reliable and dependable way in order to support them developing emotional attachments. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation itself may not be strong enough to cause an increase in a<sup>f</sup>ective trust unless the quality of social interaction is secured. Administrators need to understand how to maintain and optimize the quality of social interactions. A well-designed mechanism that facilitates cognitive evaluation is important to reinforce the relationship between community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and loy alty. For example, adding more personality to each member to show that the interactions within the community are real and valuable will allow experienced members to feel the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of interaction quality. In other words, searching for information or looking for advice online involves just a few mouse clicks. If the community cannot provide an impression of the people with whom they are dealing, or what this social group looks like, the experienced ones will doubt whether or not they can rely on the other members as dependable allies.

Furthermore, features that point out individual expertise help members to understand others’ capabilities and feel con<sup>fi</sup>dent that others are experts whom they can trust. Alternatively, the continuous use intention is largely derived by the degree of emotional attachment to the community. It is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to leverage reassuring designs (to show members who are behind the collective contributions), thereby assuaging the fear of opportunistic acts of members. The functionality of “social proof” derives from this concept to ease the minds of worried members. Members can receive previous reviews of information, quality ratings, or even endorsements from well-known moderators. By tangibly proving the value of the community and how the collective contributions have helped other members, the experienced ones can minimize their distrust and create attachment to the community, which in turn leads to long-term relationships.

Lastly, the continuous use intention is directly and indirectly in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a<sup>f</sup>ective trust and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which imply that the involvement of long-term relationships is structured by a group of members who care about the common good. For example, a storytelling mechanism is one way to seamlessly connect members with an ideal identity model, because a good member’s experience can be persuasive and more trustworthy than cognitive evaluation, as real examples remain in users’ minds. Additionally, because members are unable to physically connect on an online platform, they are more likely to rely on the shared identity and a<sup>f</sup>ective connection created by experienced members when they become involved. In a managerial way, the community should be able to <sup>fi</sup>nd a shared identity that truly re-<sup>fl</sup>ects what the members look like and emphasizes how this identity groups them together to make them envisage a<sup>f</sup>ective connections.

## 6.1. Future research and limitations

Regardless of the theoretical and practical contributions of this study, there are some limitations which should be acknowledged. First, we did not consider the cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective components of identi <sup>fi</sup>cation, but instead used a higher-level concept to represent its relationship with interpersonal trust. This research construct was structured by enclosing a function of similarities, a shared social value, and a product of collective history. Future research could uncover more insights by considering di<sup>f</sup>erent types of social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation when studying relationships within in-group favoritism.

Second, we collected the data from experienced members in an online gaming community, which inhibited us to generalize the results to all types of online communities and general users, thus limiting the external validity of this study. The way the participants treat the community or interact with other members is largely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by community objectives and characteristics. These could be aspects of contextual moderators between the relationship of community identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation and interpersonal trust. We suggest that future studies take community objectives and characteristics into consideration. The strength of the study improves our ability to generalize our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the population of gamers, comprising a large sample of members in an information-sharing community, Bahamut. However, participants in this study are the members of only one game-based community, and they may not be representative of all populations in online communities. The sample characteristics also set boundary conditions for generalizing our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. There was a larger proportion of males in our sample. Thus, generalizing the results of this study to other populations and contexts should be made with caution.

Newcomers may not be fully aware of the cultural boundary created by ingroup and outgroup di<sup>f</sup>erences which serve to shape community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [19]. They put more attention on calculating utilitarian gains and losses during interactions with experienced members, thereby spending less time developing attachment [19]. We believe that future research could add insights to our research model by comparing the di<sup>f</sup>erences between experienced members and newcomers in di<sup>f</sup>erent types of online communities. Such comparisons could enable us to understand whether the experienced members or the newcomers dif ferentiate in favor of their community by giving a higher level of trust to ingroup members than to outgroup members. A comparative study of newcomers and experienced members could make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant con tribution to the existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Social identity not only induces ingroup favoritism, but also pro duces negativity toward outgroups. More research is needed to investigate the negative bias against outgroups, which can also be ex plained as a facilitator of ingroup favoritism. Last, in addition to cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trust, trust can include one more component – normative trust. This is based on the common values and social norms that create predictability and trustworthiness. Normative trust could be the opposite of cognitive trust if members believe that trust is essentially normative rather than individual and calculative. We must bear in mind that cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective trusts are only two components of interpersonal trust. Other trust components related to the continued intention to use a community service could also be addressed at the theoretical and empirical levels.

## 7. Conclusion

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings at stake here are that experienced members will continuously participate in a community with which they share a collective identity, know the credibility of, and have a sense of attachment. The causality among community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interpersonal trust, and continuous use intention has been established in this study. We empirically examined SIT from the perspective of experienced members in the context of a game-based community, which provides a new understanding to researchers and practitioners. Members who show community identity will commit to a long-term relationship with the community. However, it is not merely the projection of categorical perceptions of a community onto the continuous use intention that determines the creation of this identity; identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is also associated to interpersonal trust. Our results reveal that community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation will enhance the recognition of cognitive trust, which in turn will foster the development of a<sup>f</sup>ective trust. Furthermore, a committed, experienced member is strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a<sup>f</sup>ective trust rather than cognitive trust. Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide insights into the theoretical development of SIT and yield suggestions to practitioners on how to build long-term relationships with their experienced members.
