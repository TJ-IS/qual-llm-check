# Not all digital venture ideas are created equal: Implications for venture creation processes

Frederik von Briel<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Jan Recker<sup>b,a,1</sup>, Per Davidsson<sup>c,d,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> QUT Business School, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Faculty of Management, Economics and Social Sciences, University of Cologne, Cologne, Germany

<sup>c</sup> Australian Centre for Entrepreneurship Research; QUT Business School, Australia

<sup>d</sup> Jönköping International Business School, Sweden

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital entrepreneurship Digital ventures New venture ideas Venture creation process Hardware ventures Software ventures

## A B S T R A C T

Digital ventures are formed around ideas that have digital artifacts at their core. We develop theory that explains how the composition of digital artifacts in<sup>fl</sup>uences venture creation processes. First, we develop propositions that link di<sup>f</sup>erences in the embodiment and coupling of digital artifact components to tensions in venture creation process inputs, behaviors, and outputs. Second, we link compositional di<sup>f</sup>erences in digital artifacts to di<sup>f</sup>erences in venture creation process initiation, duration, and outcome. Our theorizing establishes a foundation for future research on digital artifacts within and beyond entrepreneurship contexts, and for future research on entrepreneurship within and beyond digital artifact contexts.

## 1. Introduction

This paper brings together strengths of the information systems and entrepreneurship <sup>fi</sup>elds to develop theoretical ideas about digital entrepreneurship—the intersection of digital technologies and entrepreneurship (Nambisan, 2017)—in order to provide im portant impetus for future research in both <sup>fi</sup>elds.

Most of the increasing research attention to digital entrepreneurship has focused either on venture creation in (digital) technology-intensive contexts (e.g., Beckman et al., 2012; Bingham et al., 2007) or on particular digital technologies such as 3D printing (e.g., de Jong and de Bruijn, 2013) or crowdfunding (e.g., Mollick, 2014) as enablers of particular aspects of venture creation processes. We take a di<sup>f</sup>erent perspective: we focus on emerging entrepreneurial ventures that have at the core of their market o<sup>f</sup>erings a new digital artifact; that is, a product or service either embodied in information and communication technologies or enabled by them (Lyytinen et al., 2016, p. 49). We call such ventures digital ventures and the ideas around which they are formed digital venture ideas.

Digital ventures are an important phenomenon in the global economy. Four of the world’s <sup>fi</sup>ve most valuable brands—Apple, Google, Microsoft, and Facebook—started as digital ventures (Badenhausen, 2016). While all digital ventures share the ambition of bringing digital artifact o<sup>f</sup>erings into existence, the ideas they pursue are by no means uniform: some digital ventures create digital artifacts that manifest primarily as software with an ephemeral embodiment (e.g., SurveyMonkey and WhatsApp), others more closely resemble traditional notions of hardware—that is, technological objects with a perpetual embodiment (e.g., GoPro and Stratasys)—and still others create digital artifacts that combine salient components of both software and hardware and whose technological identity is not immediately apparent (e.g., Fitbit and Square).

As these examples demonstrate, the composition of digital venture ideas varies. What might not be that apparent is that these variations also in<sup>fl</sup>uence how ventures around these ideas are created. Our thesis is that di<sup>f</sup>erences in digital venture ideas not only change the identity of the digital artifact eventually established as a market o<sup>f</sup>ering, they also carry fundamental, practical implications for the ensuing venture creation process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, building on the layered modular architecture of digital artifacts (Yoo et al., 2010) we make a two-step argument: <sup>fi</sup>rst, on the component level, di<sup>f</sup>erences in two salient characteristics of digital artifact components—their embodiment and their coupling—can lead to tensions in the venture creation process because they entail di<sup>f</sup>erent process inputs, behaviors, and outputs. Second, on the venture level, di<sup>f</sup>erences in the digital artifact’s composition in terms or the characteristics of its components imbue di<sup>f</sup>erences in process initiation, duration, and outcome.

Developing this thesis is an important move in establishing a digital entrepreneurship knowledge tradition on at least two counts. First, digital entrepreneurship is of increasing economic importance but still a nascent scholarly nexus (Nambisan, 2017). Repeated calls have been made to produce (and test) theoretical models that provide an understanding of “the nature of the relationship between IT and entrepreneurism” (Del Giudice and Straub 2011, p. iii). We take one such step. We develop a novel conceptual account of how the characteristics of digital artifacts at the core of emerging digital ventures’ market o<sup>f</sup>erings in<sup>fl</sup>uence the nature of the venture creation process. Proposing such a model is an important step toward establishing a cumulative tradition because it gives fellow scholars impetus, clarity, and speci<sup>fi</sup>city to develop alternative accounts, test our predictions, and enact our theory in other, possibly unforeseen ways.

Second, developing a thesis around digital venture creation is important because variations in entrepreneurial processes—especially those resulting from attributes of the emerging ventures themselves—is an area of entrepreneurship research that is in high demand. but so far underdeveloped (Davidsson, 2015: McMullen and Dimoy. 2013: Zahra and Wright, 2011). Extant en: trepreneurship research has traditionally assumed that di<sup>f</sup>erences in venture creation processes are attributable to variance in the dispositions of entrepreneurs.<sup>2</sup> We complement this type of theorizing assumption by outlining an account of how the characteristics of that on which they act the new (digital) venture idea is also an important source of process variance.

We proceed as follows. First, we review the role of new venture ideas in entrepreneurship and discuss how this concept relates to digital artifacts and venture creation processes. We then introduce our new concepts of digital ventures and digital venture ideas and describe how they build on the layered modular architecture of digital artifacts. Having introduced these concepts, we next develop two sets of propositions: <sup>fi</sup>rst we suggest a new account of venture creation process tensions inherent in di<sup>f</sup>erent compositions of digital artifacts; and, second, we build on these tensions to develop a new account of venture creation process diferences based on di<sup>f</sup>erences in digital artifact compositions. Next, to make our abstract theoretical logic tangible and accessible, we then describe a brief thought experiment that relates our notions to concrete possible empirical scenarios. Lastly, we discuss how scholars in information systems and entrepreneurship can use our theorizing to enrich the understanding of digital artifacts, digital entrepreneurship, and venture creation processes and how future research can build on our theoretical ideas to enact, extend, or refute them.

## 2. Conceptual background

## 2.1. Entrepreneurship, new venture ideas, and the theoretical void addressed in this paper

Contrary to the information systems <sup>fi</sup>eld with its traditional focus on technological artifacts (Nevo et al., 2009), the <sup>fi</sup>eld of entrepreneurship has traditionally focused on entrepreneurial agents (Brandstätter, 2011; Unger et al., 2011). Early entrepreneurship research predominantly addressed the personal characteristics of entrepreneurs and the management of independent small businesses (Gartner. 1988: Hornaday. 1988) and paid little attention to characteristics of the artifacts entrepreneurs create. In their seminal paper, Shane and Venkataraman (2000) tried to give entrepreneurship research a more meaningful role in the broader realm of economic and organizational studies by focusing on an important but otherwise relatively neglected phenomenon: the process by which new economic activities come into being. In an attempt to reduce the exaggerated person-focus they included in their framework that on which they act which they called the opportunity as an equal partner to the individual actor in determining action and outcomes in such processes. In many ways, their reorientation was successful. The notion of entrepreneurship as process of creating new economic activities has become widely embraced and sizeable literatures have developed that focus on entrepreneurial opportunities (Shepherd and Grégoire, 2012; Short et al., 2010) and nascent entrepreneurship; that is, projects that longitudinally follow large samples of venture creation processes in real time (Davidsson et al., 2011; Davidsson and Gordon, 2012; Reynolds and Curtin, 2011).

However, one clear implication of Shane and Venkataraman s (2000) framework is that characteristics of that on which they act (the opportunity) should in<sup>fl</sup>uence the venture creation process. Yet on this topic progress has to date been close to non-existent (Davidsson, 2015). Table 1 provides a compilation of in<sup>fl</sup>uential entrepreneurship theories and frameworks. It illustrates a limited focus on systematic and predictable e<sup>f</sup>ects of “that on which they act.” Some attention has been paid to novelty and what comes with it (e.g., uncertainty) (e.g., Bhave, 1994; Shane, 2003). However, not even for basic classi<sup>fi</sup>cations like product versus service, or business-to-business versus business-to-consumer. has any in-depth theorizing about generic process differences been developed. One reason for this might be that Shane and Venkataraman’s (2000) notion of opportunities has been conceptually challenging and empirically elusive (Davidsson, 2015; Hansen et al., 2011; Kitching and Rouse, 2017). Another reason for this might be that the focus of investigation in entrepreneurship processes is, at least initially, a not-yet-existing entity (Baker and Nelson, 2005; Ramoglou and Tsang, 2016). When process di<sup>f</sup>erences have been proposed or hypothesized, these have instead predominantly been attributed to the entrepreneurial agent who arguably already exists before venture creation activities commence.

Even those frameworks that purport to have opportunities as a main focus discuss the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of opportunity attributes on the process rather casually and vaguely (e.g., Ardichvili et al., 2003). Several other frameworks (e.g., Bruyat, 2001; Dimov, 2007; Sarason et al.. 2006) strongly emphasize venture creation as the outcome of idiosyncratic person–opportunity combinations. thereby refuting the idea that characteristics of “that on which they act” can have generalizable e<sup>f</sup>ects across agents and ventures. Subsequent empirical work follows a similar agent-centered pattern. For example, Bakker and Shepherd (2017), Farmer et al. (2011), and Furr et al. (2012) all focus on agent attributes as process moderators (cf. also the review of Davidsson, 2015)

In this article we create a much-desired integrative perspective of information systems and entrepreneurship (Del Giudice and Straub, 2011; Nambisan, 2017) to explain how characteristics of “that on which they act” in<sup>fl</sup>uence entrepreneurial processes. The traditional reference disciplines of entrepreneurship—psychology, sociology, and economics—provide an abundance of conceptual and methodological tools for describing and assessing human individuals, whereas the same does not hold for the emerging and malleable entity that eventually might manifest as a fully developed and viable business. The information systems discipline. with its conceptual and methodological tools for describing artifacts and assessing their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on organizing, is well-positioned to <sup>fi</sup>ll this void. The integration of both perspectives is a timely challenge to take on because entrepreneurship is gaining increasing attention of information systems researchers (e.g., Ojala, 2016; Spiegel et al., 2016; Street et al., 2017). By moving beyond the prevailing assumptions of extant entrepreneurship theory that either “one process <sup>fi</sup>ts all” or “processes vary by persons” and focusing on “that on which they act”, we lay the foundation for information systems researchers to make an important contribution to research on digital entrepreneurship and entrepreneurship at large.

We build on the notion of new venture ideas as a more workable alternative to “opportunities” (Davidsson, 2015) in our theorizing. New venture ideas are the imagined future ventures that give direction to entrepreneurial agents’ e<sup>f</sup>orts. Whereas the portrayal of opportunities as objective vs. subjective entities varies across and within authors and works, new venture ideas are necessarily and unambiguously subjective. Further, they can be of any quality, whereas the notion of opportunity always comes with a connotation of favorability. Because new venture ideas can be shared by a team and communicated to others, they represent a venture level phenomenon (Davidsson, 2015). In this paper, we focus on variation in one salient characteristic of the new venture idea: the imagined market o<sup>f</sup>ering.

## 2.2. Digital venture ideas, digital artifacts, and their layered modular architecture

We now introduce the key constructs we require for our theorizing. Appendix A summarizes construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and their conceptual departures from related notions where applicable. We start by de<sup>fi</sup>ning digital ventures and digital venture ideas: new venture ideas and resulting ventures are digital if the (imagined) market o<sup>f</sup>ering has a digital artifact at its core (e.g., Greenwood and Gopal, 2017; Huang et al., 2017; von Briel et al., 2018);<sup>3</sup> that is, digital ventures and digital venture ideas, respectively, are based on product or service o<sup>f</sup>erings that are at least partially embodied in information and communication technologies or enabled by them (Lyytinen et al., 2016, p. 49). By contrast, new venture ideas and the ensuing ventures are not necessarily digital just because their creation processes are enabled by digital technologies (von Briel et al., 2018). For example, arts and craft entrepreneurs use digital market places such as Etsy or Dawanda for marketing and sales purposes (Reuber and Fischer, 2011), but their o<sup>f</sup>erings remain traditional, non-digital goods such as textiles, jewelry, or pottery (Kuhn and Galloway, 2015).

This distinction is important because the creation of digital artifacts as market o<sup>f</sup>erings di<sup>f</sup>ers from traditional, non-digital goods. Digital artifacts have been characterized in several ways (e.g., Ekbia, 2009; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Nambisan, 2017), but common to their portrayals is their ability to be re-combinable, editable, and distributable (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Tiwana et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2010)—traits that allow them to evolve their identity over time and generate new forms of agency, both within and across processes (Nambisan et al., 2017). These distinct characteristics are the foundation for scholars’ calls to focus on the “digital” in entrepreneurship and related phenomena, because many believe that these characteristics upend existing theories of organizing (e.g., Nambisan, 2017; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010). Like our colleagues, our focus is also on these three traits.

To describe digital artifacts at the core of digital venture ideas and explain how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence venture creation processes, we start by considering their make-up; that is, their underlying layered modular architecture (Yoo et al., 2010).<sup>4</sup> Digital artifacts are made up of at least one but potentially multiple components, each being dedicated to speci<sup>fi</sup>c functions. Because the layered modular architecture of digital artifacts situates components in relation to one another, we can di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between their coupling and embodiment.

With regards to coupling, digital artifact components that are responsive to and distinct from each other (e.g., separated by module or layer boundaries) are loosely coupled, whereas components that are responsive to but not distinct from each other (e.g., integrated in one module) are tightly coupled (Orton and Weick, 1990). Whether a digital artifact is made up of loosely or tightly coupled components is often a matter of degree, rather than being one or the other (Mikkola, 2006; Yoo et al., 2010). For example, early versions of the Mozilla Firefox web browser were made up of a few large and tightly coupled source code <sup>fi</sup>les, while later versions adopted a design with smaller and less tightly coupled source code <sup>fi</sup>les (MacCormack et al., 2006). Supported by the homogenization of digital data, loose coupling between components, which can be achieved via standardized interfaces and protocols, makes digital artifacts decomposable and their components product-agnostic. Coupling thus determines the capacity of digital artifact components to be re-combinable (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Tiwana et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2010).

With regards to embodiment, digital artifacts can be made up of components with an ephemeral and/or perpetual embodiment (cf. Yoo, 2010). Components with an ephemeral embodiment exist in a logical state, which makes them malleable and fungible. In this state, they provide the semiotic functional logic of digital artifacts (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>rmware, software, and content). Components with a perpetual embodiment exist in a physical state, which makes them rigid and stable in comparison. In this state, they carry out the storage, transmission, processing, and display of digital data (e.g., microprocessor, memory, and wireless connectivity chip). Put simply, ephemeral components exist as series of binary digits, whereas perpetual components exist in physical form, such as logic gates, <sup>fl</sup>ip-<sup>fl</sup>ops, and multiplexers that display, process, and store series of binary digits.

Whether digital artifacts are made up of ephemeral and/or perpetual components and whether these components are loosely or tightly coupled is important because it determines their capacity for editability and distributability. For example, software can exist as an ephemeral electric current and be loosely coupled to a computer's random access memory (RAM) making it malleable and fungible, but it can also exist as a perpetual indentation when it is tightly coupled to the polycarbonate layer of an optical disk, making it rigid and stable. The separation of form from function and content from media that can be achieved via loose coupling of ephemeral and perpetual components in<sup>fl</sup>uences digital artifacts’ capacity for editability, because ephemeral components make digital artifacts malleable and fungible and loosely coupled components make them re-combinable (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). This separation also in<sup>fl</sup>uences digital artifacts capacity for distributability, because it diminishes traditional product boundaries and allows not only for vertical but also horizontal disintegration (Nambisan, 2017; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010).

## 3. Process tensions arising from di<sup>f</sup>erent digital artifact components

In the following, we theorize why the di<sup>f</sup>erences between digital artifact components that form the core of digital venture ideas matter to venture creation processes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we develop the thesis that venture creation processes can face tensions that stem from di<sup>f</sup>erences in the coupling and embodiment of components underlying digital venture ideas (Langley et al., 2013; Smith and Lewis, 2011);<sup>5</sup> that is, we reason that the composition of digital artifacts in terms of their component coupling and embodiment imbue paradoxical implications for organizing, which require reconciliation during venture creation.

Three assumptions provide initial boundaries to our theorizing. First, we focus on conceptual “ideal types” (Avison and Malaurent, 2014) of digital artifact components,<sup>6</sup> portraying them as being either loosely or tightly coupled and having either an ephemeral or perpetual embodiment. Ideal-type reasoning allows us to provide a clearer theoretical exposition of the differences that lead to tensions in venture creation processes. Second, we focus on three key dimensions of process research (cf. Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999; Van De Ven and Engleman, 2004): venture creation process inputs as precursors for, process behavior that shapes patterns of, and process outputs as the consequences of venture creation activities. Third, again to increase clarity of our reasoning, we assume the size and complexity of di<sup>f</sup>erent digital artifact components to be virtually identical.

Table 2 presents six tensions relating to process inputs, behaviors, and outputs that can arise depending on the manifestation of digital artifact components at the core of a new venture idea. We formalize these tensions in the following in the form of six propositions.

## 3.1. Process tensions arising from component embodiment

The <sup>fi</sup>rst set of process tensions that can be inherent in digital venture ideas arises from the material embodiment of their components. For example, web browsers are made up of components with an ephemeral embodiment such as modules for user interfaces and rendering engines, whereas microprocessors are made up of components with a perpetual embodiment such as arithmetic logic units and control units. This fundamental di<sup>f</sup>erence in material component embodiment has important, paradoxical consequences for input diversity, process intensity, and output pliability of venture creation processes (see Fig. 1). Below we explain each process characteristic and how it is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the embodiment of artifact components.

## 3.1.1. Input diversity tensions

Input diversity refers to the degree of heterogeneity of the process inputs that are required to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact component in the market. Input diversity tensions arise because ephemeral components such as software are materialized through rather homogeneous activities, whereas perpetual components such as hardware are materialized through rather heterogeneous activities; that is, whereas ephemeral components are mostly materialized by writing lines of code (cf. Faraj and Sproull, 2000), perpetual components are materialized through a more diverse set of activities ranging from electrical and mechanical engineering to physical prototyping to sourcing and manufacturing (cf. Marion et al., 2015, 2012). This homogeneity–heterogeneity spectrum of activities in<sup>fl</sup>uences the diversity of process inputs, such as knowledge, skills, technologies, and tools (Lyytinen et al., 2016) that are required to realize a digital artifact component. For example, due to the homogeneity of inputs, ephemeral components are often developed by self-contained teams that have everything they need to create and shape artifact versions, leading to low external dependencies (Maruping et al., 2009; Moe et al., 2010; Tripp et al., 2016). By contrast, due to the input heterogeneity, the development of perpetual components usually requires complementary upstream and downstream assets, such as for components, manufacturing, and distribution, leading to high external dependencies (Hallen et al., 2014). In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the material embodiment of digital artifact components can lead to venture creation process tensions because these di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the diversity of process inputs that are required to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact in the market, leading to our <sup>fi</sup>rst proposition:

Proposition 1. Perpetual artifact components require more diverse venture creation process inputs than ephemeral artifact components do.

## 3.1.2. Process intensity tensions

Process intensity refers to the pace and concentration of activities that are undertaken to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact component in the market. Process intensity tensions arise mainly because ephemeral components have in<sup>fi</sup>nite expansibility since they can be (re)produced and distributed rapidly and at marginal to no cost, whereas perpetual components have a restricted expansibility due to supply side restrictions (Faulkner and Runde, 2009, 2011). For example, an ephemeral web browser can be downloaded unlimited times once its source code has been written and uploaded to the internet, whereas each copy of a perpetua microprocessor needs to be physically manufactured and distributed.

Regarding pace,<sup>7</sup> the development of ephemeral components is usually fast-paced with short iterations, rapid changes, and frequent releases (Conboy, 2009), because source code can be tweaked, compiled, and distributed in a matter of seconds. As a result, it is common practice to release premature software versions (e.g., alpha or beta versions) to customers and end-users to obtain early feedback and test acceptance (Kautz, 2011). By contrast, the development of perpetual components is comparably slower because their physical characteristics prevent them from being produced and distributed ad-hoc. Even with virtual prototypes (Bogers and Horst, 2014) and advanced manufacturing techniques, such as 3D printing (Bogers et al., 2016), the creation of perpetual components involves more activities, such as transferring premature prototypes into designs that can actually be manufactured (Yu et al., 2018). Hence, the creation of perpetual components requires time and imposes delays between development iterations and changes. Moreover, it is not easily possible to distribute premature versions to potential customers and end-users for feedback.

Regarding concentration, venture creation activities of ephemeral components are concentrated around development (Kautz, 2011). This is again because additional copies can be automatically produced and distributed ad-hoc and at no cost. As a result, ventures can pursue a continuous deployment strategy for ephemeral artifact components and dedicate their attention to honing existing or developing new components (Rodríguez et al., 2017). The inexhaustible expansibility of ephemeral components together with the possibility to concentrate on their continuous re<sup>fi</sup>nement also lays the foundations for rapid and essentially unlimited scaling potential (cf. Huang et al., 2017); that is, digital ventures with artifacts based on ephemeral components can already begin to scale the distribution and adoption of these components before the ventures have achieved a stable market o<sup>f</sup>ering (Huang et al., 2017) By contrast, venture creation activities of perpetual components are more broadly distributed across development, manufacturing, and distribution (Wu et al., 2008). Once perpetual components have been developed, attention shifts toward manufacturing as manufacturing requires set up time for procuring materials, creating tooling, trial runs, product assembly, quality control, and so on (Marion et al., 2012). Hence, manufacturing can usually not be done ad-hoc. Moreover, scaling involving perpetual components is limited by production capacities and distribution systems which can hinder ventures from ful<sup>fi</sup>lling demand (Jain et al., 1991). In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the material embodiment of digital artifact components can lead to venture creation process tensions because these di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the intensity of processes that need to be performed to o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact in the market. Hence, our second proposition:

Proposition 2. Perpetual artifact components lower the intensity of venture creation processes compared to ephemeral artifact components.

## 3.1.3. Output pliability tensions

Output pliability refers to the capacity for change and adaptation of a digital artifact component that is o<sup>f</sup>ered in the market.

Fig. 2. Process tensions inherent in the coupling of digital artifact components.

Output pliability tensions arise because ephemeral components manifest in an abstract and immaterial state that makes them inherently malleable, whereas perpetual components manifest in a material state that makes them inherently more rigid. For example, a web browser can still be changed via updates after it has been downloaded and installed by customers, while the physical casing and underlying electronics of a microprocessor cannot. As a result, ephemeral artifact components are often purposefully designed as incomplete and continuously adapted even after they have been introduced to the market (Garud et al., 2008). The “release early, release often, and listen to your customer” (Raymond, 2001) mantra that is popular in software development describes this circumstance well.

Digital ventures that create software artifacts based on ephemeral components often follow this mantra and enter the market with a minimum-viable-product (MVP) (Blank, 2013) that has features “just good enough” for it to get used by customers. The ventures then continue to forge their components based on customer feedback (Heirman and Clarysse, 2007). Because of ephemeral components’ high capacity for change and adaptation, digital artifacts that are composed of ephemeral components also have a high propensity for radical transformation (i.e., pivoting) (Arteaga and Hyland, 2013). By contrast, the “do it right the <sup>fi</sup>rst time” (Boothroyd, 1994) mantra from engineering and manufacturing is the dominant logic in the perpetual component context. Changing perpetual artifact components becomes more di<sup>fi</sup>cult closer to mass-production, such as when injection molds have already been ordered, often causing delayed product launches and substantially higher costs (Liker and Morgan, 2006). Once a perpetual artifact component has been mass-produced (or purchased in large quantities), a lock-in situation arises that causes structural inertia (Hannan and Freeman, 1984) because changes to the component are essentially impossible without further production runs. In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the material embodiment of digital artifact components can lead to venture creation process tensions becaus di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the pliability of a digital artifact that is o<sup>f</sup>ered in the market. As such, we propose:

Proposition 3. Perpetual artifact components impede, whereas ephemeral artifact components promote change and adaptation of venture creation process outputs.

## 3.2. Process tensions arising from component coupling

We now turn to three process tensions that arise from the coupling of digital venture idea components: input distributability, process synchronicity, and output generativity of venture creation processes (see Fig. 2). These tensions arise because digital artifacts can be made up of loosely or tightly coupled components. Again, think of a web browser where extensions such as ad blockers or respective <sup>fi</sup>lter lists are loosely coupled, and microprocessors where multiple cores are tightly coupled on one chip

## 3.2.1. Input distributability tensions

Input distributability refers to the dispersion of supply and functionality of components that are essential to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact in the market. Input distributability tensions arise because loosely coupled components can be sourced externally but come with a loss of control, whereas tightly coupled components preserve control but need to be developed internally.<sup>8</sup> For example, consider a microprocessor supplier's decision to stop production of a specific microprocessor and the implications this has for a digital venture that builds its smartwatch based on this externally sourced microprocessor. Because a layered modular architecture diminishes traditional product boundaries, loosely coupled components that are used to create a market o<sup>f</sup>ering can often even remain externally distributed and do not necessarily have to be internalized (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). For example, platforms such as smartphone operating systems provide important components for many app o<sup>f</sup>erings of digital ventures, but remain outside these ventures boundaries. Building on external components comes with a loss of input control because it distributes agency over component supply. functionality, interfaces, and protocols to third parties. For example, external components can evolve and change their functionality, thereby requiring changes of artifacts that build on them or otherwise jeopardizing the artifacts’ functionality (Tiwana et al., 2010). Hence, building on external components increases uncertainty and coordination costs that arise from the external dependencies (Tiwana, 2015). For example, Instagram, Snapchat, and WhatsApp suddenly revoked third-party access, thereby removing the foundation for digital ventures that had built their digital artifacts on top (Bell, 2015; Brandom, 2015; Perez, 2015).

By contrast, internal development of tightly coupled components preserves input control because agency over component supply and functions remains with the emerging venture. To be able to engage in internal development of tightly coupled components, digital ventures need to possess knowledge, labor, and technologies for component creation (Brusoni et al., 2001), which decreases their external dependencies and uncertainty (Parmigiani and Mitchell, 2009). Internal development of tightly coupled components also enables ventures to gain production experience (Argote and Epple, 1990), which reduces their dependencies on external process inputs further over time. Although, while the internal development of tightly coupled components might appear bene<sup>fi</sup>cial from a control perspective, one has to keep in mind that emerging new ventures usually face resource constraints (Baker and Nelson, 2005), which is why building on loosely coupled components from external providers might provide an attractive strategy despite the inherent loss of control. In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the coupling of components that are required to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact in the market can lead to venture creation process tensions because these di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the distributability of venture creation process inputs. Hence, our fourth proposition:

Proposition 4. Tightly coupled artifact components prevent, whereas loosely coupled artifact components promote the distribution of venture creation process inputs.

## 3.2.2. Process synchronicity tensions

Process synchronicity refers to the temporal concurrency of activities that need to be coordinated to create digital artifact components. Process synchronicity tensions arise because artifact components that are loosely coupled have low interdependencies, whereas artifact components that are tightly coupled have high interdependencies. As a result, artifacts with loosely coupled components can be created through asynchronous parallel processes (Ulrich, 1995) that increase overall process <sup>fl</sup>exibility and time-tomarket (Schilling and Hill, 1998). Because loosely coupled components have speci<sup>fi</sup>ed interfaces that separate them from the overarching artifact they can essentially evolve independently. As a result, loose coupling blurs the boundaries between individual development stages (including development vs. commercialization) and relaxes the assumption of discrete process interdependencies that would require synchronized sequential processes.

By contrast, the creation of artifacts with tightly coupled components requires highly sequential processes with intensive coordination between activities (Sanchez and Mahoney, 1996). Tightly coupled components are potentially incomplete and need to be adapted until the development of all interdependent components has been completed, because they lack standardized interfaces and protocols (Sanchez and Mahoney, 1996). Hence, a change of one component “is likely to require extensive compensating changes in the designs of many interrelated components” (Sanchez and Mahoney, 1996). While it might sound counterintuitive for new ventures to create artifacts based on tightly coupled components because of the aforementioned disadvantages, tight coupling allows achieving superior product performance and, in the context of perpetual components, lowers manufacturing costs because components can be optimized for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c use case (Brusoni et al., 2007). Hence, despite the <sup>fl</sup>exibility advantages of asynchronous parallel processes that can be achieved with loosely coupled artifact components, many new ventures develop tightly coupled artifact components (Nambisan, 2002). In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the coupling of components that make up a digital artifact can lead to venture creation process tensions because these di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the synchronicity of processes that need to be performed to create and o<sup>f</sup>er a digital artifact in the market. This leads to our <sup>fi</sup>fth proposition:

Proposition 5. Tightly coupled artifact components increase the need for synchronization in the venture creation process compared to loosely coupled artifact components.

## 3.2.3. Output generativity tensions

Output generativity refers to digital artifact components capacity for unprompted change through third parties (Zittrain, 2006). Whereas input distributability and process synchronicity focus on ventures use of loosely or tightly coupled components to assemble digital artifacts, output generativity focuses on ventures’ creation of digital artifacts that manifest in loosely or tightly coupled components. This is an important distinction because even artifacts that are made up of loosely coupled components can appear tightly coupled to third parties. For example, even if a web browser might have loosely coupled modules for user interfaces and rendering engines that provide developers with <sup>fl</sup>exibility, these will likely appear tightly coupled to end users. If digital artifacts are loosely coupled by providing open interfaces and protocols, third parties can re-combine (Faulkner and Runde, 2009, 2011; von Briel et al., 2018) and potentially edit them (Ekbia, 2009; Kallinikos et al., 2013). Conversely, if digital artifacts are tightly coupled and created without open interfaces and protocols, third parties cannot easily combine them with other components or edit the artifact.<sup>9</sup>

Both loosely and tightly coupled artifact components as outputs of venture creation processes provide advantages and disadvantages. Tightly coupled artifact components encapsulate their functionality, which allows digital ventures to ensure the artifacts functional performance (Erat et al., 2013) and to protect their intellectual property (Baldwin and Henkel, 2015). Moreover, by creating tightly coupled artifact components, digital ventures can shift competition to the systemic performance of their o<sup>f</sup>erings (Fixson and Park, 2008). The development of tightly coupled artifact components is also usually faster and cheaper, which is why many new ventures choose to pursue their digital venture ideas based on this product architecture (Nambisan, 2002). However, as indicated earlier, tightly coupled artifact components sti<sup>fl</sup>e the potential for artifact changes and extension through third parties. Hence, not only control over. but also responsibility for the evolution of digital artifacts remains exclusively with the digital venture

By contrast, it is costly and time intensive to develop standardized interfaces and protocols that make artifact components loosely coupled (Brusoni et al., 2007). However, loosely coupled artifact components increase <sup>fl</sup>exibility and open artifacts up for continuous evolution (von Briel et al., 2018) and unprompted change through third parties (Zittrain, 2006). Thus, loosely coupled artifact components can shift the locus of value creation from the emerging venture to third parties (Parker et al., 2017). One way for digital ventures to achieve loosely coupled artifact components is by developing standardized application programming interfaces (APIs) that empower external developers to create new functionality for their o<sup>f</sup>erings (Boudreau, 2012). For example, many web browsers provide interfaces that allow third parties to develop extensions and in turn provide users with an ever increasing functionality. Of course, loose coupling is an important precondition but not su<sup>fi</sup>cient cause for generativity to develop. Once artifact components are loosely coupled, other factors such as network e<sup>f</sup>ects (Boudreau, 2012), the frequency of artifact change (Kapoor and Agarwal, 2017), or cross-platform compatibility (Adner et al., 2015) might play an important role for generativity to actually unfold. In sum, di<sup>f</sup>erences between the component coupling of a digital artifact that is o<sup>f</sup>ered in the market can lead to venture creation process tensions because these di<sup>f</sup>erences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the generativity of the market o<sup>f</sup>ering. Hence, we propose:

Proposition 6. Tightly coupled artifact components impede, whereas loosely coupled artifact components promote the continuous change and adaptation of venture creation process outputs through third parties.

## 4. Process implications of di<sup>f</sup>erent digital venture idea compositions

So far, our discussion of the tensions that can be inherent in digital venture ideas suggests that venture creation processes di<sup>f</sup>er substantially depending on whether digital artifact components have an ephemeral or perpetual embodiment and are loosely or tightly coupled.

In reality, many digital artifacts combine components of these contrasting ideal types to varying degrees. For example, smartwatches are made up of components with both an ephemeral embodiment (e.g., their operating systems and apps), and a perpetua embodiment (e.g., their microprocessors and wireless connectivity chipsets). This is possible because digital artifacts are re-combinable and can act as components for other digital artifacts (von Briel et al., 2018). Hence, our subsequent theorizing focuses on what Baldwin and Clark (2000) call the “microstructure” of digital artifacts to develop propositions about how di<sup>f</sup>erences in this microstructure lead to di<sup>f</sup>erences in venture creation processes (Langley et al., 2013).<sup>10</sup>

Building on process tensions that can arise from di<sup>f</sup>erences in component embodiment and coupling, in the following we theorize about how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence three key elements of venture creation as a process (cf. Davidsson, 2016; McMullen and Dimov, 2013; Van De Ven and Engleman, 2004): how digital venture creation processes are initiated, what the duration of such processes is, and what the outcome of digital venture creation processes is. ## 4.1. Changing relative ease of process initiation

A higher proportion of ephemeral and loosely coupled components that make up a digital artifact makes venture creation processes easier to initiate, because required process inputs are more homogeneous and their creation can be distributed. For example, the venture creation processes of Dropbox, Facebook, and Vine—all ephemeral software artifacts—were initiated by single founders who built on external components, such as cloud computing platforms, and in turn “only” needed software development skills and a computer.

Homogeneity of process inputs makes it easier for digital ventures to accumulate these inputs and, thus. to initiate the creation of market o<sup>f</sup>erings based on digital artifacts. Homogeneity of process inputs also accelerates the buildup of experience in using these inputs (Venkatesh et al., 2006). In turn. loosely coupled components allow to access and build on process inputs that have been, or are, created by others. For example, reusing software code from other developers is a common approach to build on components that have been created by others (Hae<sup>fl</sup>iger et al., 2008), integrating externally created components, such as Google Maps, to create “mash-ups” is another (Yoo et al., 2012). Hence, loosely coupled components reduce the need to obtain process inputs that would be required to develop these components internally (von Briel et al., 2018). In sum, the homogeneity of process inputs and the potentia to leverage externally created inputs decreases the range of critical resources, such as human capital, physical capital, and socia capital, that are required to initiate the venture creation process (e.g., Davidsson and Honig, 2003; Grossman et al., 2012; Vissa and Chacar, 2009). Therefore we propose:

Proposition 7. The more (a) ephemeral and (b) loosely coupled the artifact components, the easier it will be to initiate the venture creation process.

## 4.2. Varying process duration

A higher proportion of ephemeral and loosely coupled components that make up a digital artifact leads to faster venture creation processes because more activities can take place autonomously and with high intensity. For instance, in our mash-up example, Googl Maps could be an integral part of the digital artifact—but it is created and updated independently of it.

The loose coupling of components allows these components to develop in parallel (Garud et al., 2008), which reduces the cycle times of development iterations (Schilling, 2000), particularly if the development is distributed across di<sup>f</sup>erent external actors as it allows digital ventures to pool their resources with other actors. In turn, because of ephemeral components expansibility, digital ventures can shift the boundary between artifact development and customer involvement to earlier stages of the venture creation process (Kallinikos and Mariátegui, 2011). Earlier customer involvement increases the chances to achieve a product–market <sup>fi</sup>t (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1995) and decreases the overall time-to-market (Feng et al., 2012). In the context of loosely coupled ephemeral components it is even possible to distribute the entire artifact and obtain feedback while work on its individual components is still in progress (Garud et al., 2008). In sum, a higher proportion of ephemeral and loosely coupled artifact components relaxes the assumption of discrete process interdependencies and blurs the boundaries between individual development stages. It thus accelerates venture creation processes and makes it possible to achieve critical milestones and success factors such as having prototypes and launching an initial product earlier (e.g., Bird et al., 1990; Wasserman, 2003; Wu et al., 2008). Hence, we propose:

Proposition 8. The more (a) ephemeral and (b) loosely coupled the artifact components, the shorter the venture creation process duration will be.

## 4.3. Shifting process outcomes

A higher proportion of ephemeral components and loose coupling of a digital artifact leads to more iterative and evolutionary venture creation process outcomes because digital artifacts become more editable and re-combinable; that is, digital artifacts, and thus the market o<sup>f</sup>erings of digital ventures, can still be honed and altered even after they have been created and introduced to the market. For example, a device such as a <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker is essentially a motion-detecting sensor coupled with software that interprets the data created by this sensor. Because of the ephemeral software component, the perpetual motion sensor could evolve into various di<sup>f</sup>erent market o<sup>f</sup>erings, such as an automatic emergency call device for elderly people (e.g., a deviating movement pattern triggers an emergency call) or a game controller for video games (e.g., tilting translates into movement along the X and Y axis), even after it has been introduced to the market.

Ephemeral and loosely coupled components make it possible for digital ventures to continuously adapt their digital artifacts. This possibility provides them with strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility because emerging digital ventures operate under uncertain conditions and in nove environments (Navis and Ozbek, 2015); that is, the evaluation of digital venture ideas is ex-ante rather subjective (Davidsson, 2015) and customer needs often evolve rapidly (Slater et al., 2014). Rindova and Kotha (2001) show that Yahoo! and Excite, both digital ventures with ephemeral software artifacts, needed to pivot their market o<sup>f</sup>erings several times from initially being search engines to becoming content providers (e.g., news, stock quotes, and weather reports) to <sup>fi</sup>nally ending up as service providers (e.g., email, calendar, and polling services). Similarly, Huang et al. (2017) show that swift transformation of digital artifacts is a precondition for the rapid growth of digital ventures because it allows digital ventures to hone their product-market fit. Arguably, venture creation processes can become path dependent and self-reinforcing over time (Mahoney, 2000) as experience with the processes is built, required resources are accumulated, and digital artifact components stabilize; that is, switching costs increase. However, digital ventures can increase digital artifacts’ fundamental propensities for change through the ventures themselves or third parties by adding ephemeral components and loose coupling. These propensities make the boundaries of entrepreneurial outcomes more <sup>fl</sup>uid and open (Nambisan, 2017). thereby unlocking a potentially iterative and evolutionary nature of new venture ideas, In sum ephemeral components and loose coupling of digital artifacts not only reduce lock-ins and allow digital ventures to gradually improve their product–market <sup>fi</sup>t but also make o<sup>f</sup>erings receptive to external change, which is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in indicators such as growth, introduction of new product versions, product upgrades, product modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, and extensions (e.g., Huang et al., 2017; Lomberg et al., 2017; Nambisan, 2002). Thus, we propose that:

Proposition 9. The more (a) ephemeral and (b) loosely coupled the digital artifact composition, the more iterative and evolutionary the nature of the venture creation process outcome will be.

## 5. Illustrating our theory through counterfactual thinking

The nine propositions as the key output of our theorizing are necessarily abstract and conceptual. To make our theorizing more immediately and practically useful for fellow researchers, we now demonstrate how our propositions can be related to empirica matters. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we describe three simple thought experiments based on counterfactual thinking (Baron and York, 2000; Gaglio, 2004); we examine an actual digital venture idea but contemplate varying assumptions for its realization.<sup>11</sup>

Our thought experiments are based on a magazine article about Dropcam (Hernandez, 2013), an actual digital venture that o<sup>f</sup>ered indoor video surveillance solutions. Dropcam’s founders, Greg Du<sup>f</sup>y and Aamir Virani, realized that existing hardware and software for indoor video surveillance were not easy to use and often did not work as expected. As Du<sup>f</sup>y explained (Hernandez, 2013):

“That hardware was really out of date. The software was virtually non-existent. They were just really arcane and required wizardry most people just do not have.“

Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani came up with the idea to develop software that could automatically upload surveillance videos to the internet and an indoor video surveillance camera that could connect with it.

As this brief narrative illustrates, Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani’s digital venture idea contains elements of both ephemeral and perpetua embodiment. However, the development of this idea can be realized in di<sup>f</sup>erent ways. In our thought experiment, we imagined in some detail three scenarios on how this digital venture idea could have been pursued and the process implications that would arise from the three scenarios. For clarity of exposition, we use relative quali<sup>fi</sup>ers to compare the individual process dimensions across the three scenarios.

Table 4 summarizes the theoretical analysis of the three scenarios, suggesting a variety of process di<sup>f</sup>erences in relation to the imagined variance in compositions of digital artifacts that can form the core of the new venture idea. The key point of this exercise is to demonstrate, through the thought experiments, how our theoretical concepts can be readily associated with concrete, speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of a digital venture idea in a much more detailed and nuanced way than what would have previously been broadly cast as an “opportunity” matter for venture creation processes. Moreover, our thought experiments also demonstrate that considering the various arrangement of distinct characteristics of digital artifacts, such as their potential for loose coupling and editability, through the lens of our theoretical account, opens up actionable, di<sup>f</sup>erent paths for entrepreneurs to pursue digital venture ideas.

## 5.1. Scenario #1: Software solution

Let us assume Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani had decided to create a software solution that complements the video surveillance cameras of other vendors and makes them easier to use. If that were the case, the venture creation process to realize their market o<sup>f</sup>ering would be purely based on the creation of ephemeral components. As a result, Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani would have had to create a rather homogeneous software code (P1: low input diversity) and once an MVP version was ready, they could have immediately started to distribute and commercialize the software at no additional costs (P2: high process intensity). Hence, it would have been relative easy to start pursuing the idea (P7: easy process initiation), as the time elapsed before releasing their <sup>fi</sup>rst product (and generating revenue) could have been rather short (P8: short process duration). ## 5.2. Scenario #2: Integrated software and hardware solution

Now let us assume that Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani decided that Scenario #1 would provide too much uncertainty (P4: low internal control), on the one hand because they could neither ensure the supply of video surveillance cameras with open interfaces from third-party vendors, and on the other hand because they could not ensure the user experience and performance of the <sup>fi</sup>nal solution. As a result, let us assume that Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani had decided to create an integrated solution consisting of the video surveillance cameras and the software themselves in order to reduce uncertainty (P4: low distributability but high internal control). If that were the case, the venture creation process to realize their idea would have involved heterogeneous activities because they would have had to not only create the software code but also engage in hardware development and manufacturing (P1: high input diversity). This would likely have increased their capital requirements (P7: di<sup>fi</sup>cult process initiation) and likely slowed down the venture creation process (P8: long process duration). Because the physical video camera would have been a central component of their market o<sup>f</sup>ering (P3: low output pliability), they would also have had to follow the “do it right the <sup>fi</sup>rst time” mantra and wait with their <sup>fi</sup>rst release until the development of all components was completed (P5: high process synchronicity). Subsequently, they would have had to set up manufacturing, organize distribution, and slowly scale the venture (P2: low process intensity). Assuming that Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani had not created open interfaces because they wanted to control the user experience, their market o<sup>f</sup>ering would not have been open for change through third parties (P6: low output generativity; P9: partially <sup>fi</sup>xed process outcomes).

## 5.3. Scenario #3: Software solution coupled with third party hardware

As a result, they would have had to create the software code and source the video surveillance cameras but would not have engaged in hardware development and manufacturing. Compared to Scenario #1 this would likely have increased, and to Scenario #2 decreased, the diversity of required inputs (P1: medium input diversity), di<sup>fi</sup>culty to pursue the idea (P7: medium di<sup>fi</sup>culty to initiate process), and duration of the venture creation process (P8: medium process duration). Moreover, because the physical video surveillance camera already existed and would not have to be developed, they could have pursued a rather <sup>fl</sup>exible venture creation process where the software evolves separately from the hardware (P5: low process synchronicity). Consequently, Du<sup>f</sup>y and Virani could have avoided orchestration of manufacturing, instead just sourcing and distributing the existing video surveillance camera (P2: medium process intensity). ## 6. Discussion

This article respondeds to calls to develop a perspective on digital entrepreneurship (Del Giudice and Straub, 2011; Huang et al., 2017; Nambisan, 2017) and the process nature of entrepreneurship (McMullen and Dimov, 2013; Zahra and Wright, 2011), by exemplifying a way of theorizing how characteristics of a new (digital) venture idea in<sup>fl</sup>uence the process of (digital) venture creation. We suggested that not all digital artifacts necessarily and unequivocally live up to their portrayals as being distinct from non-digital artifacts, and propose that fundamental artifact di<sup>f</sup>erences implicate tensions in, and di<sup>f</sup>erences between, key aspects of venture creation processes. Our theorizing had two parts. First, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that di<sup>f</sup>erences in the embodiment and coupling of digital artifact components can give rise to venture creation process tensions in terms of input diversity and distributability, behavioral intensity and synchronicity, and output pliability and generativity. Second, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that di<sup>f</sup>erent compositions of digital artifacts in terms of their components’ embodiment and coupling in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relative ease of venture creation process initiation, venture creation process duration, and the evolution of venture creation process outcomes.

As the focus of this paper is theory development, the broader implications of our research relate primarily to future research that enacts or evaluates our theory through empirical research. Our aim was primarily to stimulate such research by providing an initial conceptual account tailor-made for empirical testing, because this allows for and hopefully motivates future empirical research to evaluate, re<sup>fi</sup>ne, extend, or refute our theory. Describing operationalization and measurement strategies for our theory in full would require an entire paper; however, we provide testable propositions and two aids about how empirical research procedures could be carried out. First, in Appendix A we provide construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, including their theoretical departure from established notions. Studies could now be conducted to examine construct and discriminant validity, and to evaluate whether our departures are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed, coherent, and distinctive. Second, we also described a set of three thought experiments to illustrate how our abstract propositions can be related to concrete empirical phenomena, such as the development of a digital artifact, the entrepreneurial decisions founders make on their journey, the <sup>fi</sup>nal manifestation of a digital market o<sup>f</sup>ering, or the pivoting that occurs during the process from idea to realization. Our thought experiments can serve as inspiration and provide guidance for the design of lab experiments and other empirical studies.

Beyond these broader implications, our mode of theorizing also has more speci<sup>fi</sup>c implications for future research on digital artifacts within and beyond entrepreneurship contexts, and for future research on entrepreneurship within and beyond digital artifact contexts. We discuss them in turn.

## 6.1. Implications for research on digital artifacts within and beyond entrepreneurship contexts

Digital entrepreneurship research is in its early stages but gaining increasing attention of researchers (Huang et al., 2017; Nambisan, 2017; von Briel et al., 2018). Similar to research on digital innovation and related contexts (e.g., Lyytinen et al., 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010), the underlying assumption is that existing entrepreneurship theories need to be revisited because characteristics of digital artifacts upend traditional ways of organizing. As yet, however, little is known about how exactly digital artifacts in<sup>fl</sup>uence entrepreneurial processes even if they are at the very core of digital ventures market o<sup>f</sup>erings and ideas. To the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to disentangle digital artifact characteristics (Faulkner and Runde, 2009; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010) to develop theory that links their fundamental characteristics to speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of venture creation processes. By doing so, we seek to lay the foundation for future research on the role of digital artifacts within and beyond digital entrepreneurship contexts in four important ways.

First, building on the layered modular architecture (Yoo et al., 2010), we developed formal propositions that link di<sup>f</sup>erences of two salient characteristics of digital artifact components—their component embodiment and coupling—to six types of venture creation process tensions—input diversity and distributability, behavioral intensity and synchronicity, and output pliability and generativity tensions. Formalizing the tacit process implications <sup>fl</sup>owing from the layered modular architecture and di<sup>f</sup>erences thereof has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical and practical value. Future research can apply our theorized dimensions of variability and associated propositions to digital product or service ideas within and beyond the entrepreneurship context to provide insights for research, start-ups, and organizations at large. For example, future research could use con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approaches (Misangyi et al., 2017) and large empirical samples of digital product and service ideas to test whether certain process tensions impede the likelihood that ideas are successfully realized, as per our propositions. Naturally, our propositions lend themselves particularly well to process research and as a foundation for further process theorizing (Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999; Van De Ven and Engleman, 2004). By testing our propositions, we believe that future research might not only be able to re<sup>fi</sup>ne insights pertaining to our theory but also extend it by identifving additional process tensions and digital artifact characteristics

Second, building on our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of propositions, we developed a second set of propositions that explain how di<sup>f</sup>erences of digital artifact compositions in terms of component embodiment and coupling imbue di<sup>f</sup>erent venture creation process initiations, durations, and outcomes. Like our <sup>fi</sup>rst set, our second set of propositions also lends itself particularly well as a foundation for process research. For example, these propositions might provide a fruitful starting point for future research on the little understood, but important, change trajectories of digital artifacts (Kallinikos et al., 2013), as they can potentially help to explain why digital artifact compositions change in the way they do. Approaches such as design structure matrixes can help to empirically compare digital artifact compositions across ventures, to track their evolution over the venture creation process (e.g., MacCormack et al., 2006), and to empirically link digital artifact compositions to venture creation processes and changes thereof. Since we focused on dichotomous ideal types and compositions thereof in our theorizing, future research could also investigate whether taking component and/or artifact size and complexity as additional factors into account would necessitate changes to our propositions, or whether dominant components might exist that give digital artifacts their technological identity and have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on venture creation processes than other components do. For example, what is it that makes a smart watch to what it is? Is it the perpetual embodiment, the ephemeral logic, or are both inextricably linked? These questions might become particularly important in the context of highl complex artifacts that combine large numbers of ephemeral and perpetual components, such as digitized cars, planes, or robots. Hence, our theorizing about digital artifacts and their compositions provide researchers a fertile ground for a wide range of futur research topics on digital entrepreneurship and beyond.

Third, research has shown that humans and digital artifacts can in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other (e.g., Leonardi, 2011), and our second set of propositions lends itself to investigate these interactions between digital artifacts and the entrepreneurs who bring them to fruition. Future research in this vein could use our propositions together with complementary agent-centric entrepreneurship theories such as those listed in Table 1, to provide a more holistic understanding of why ventures develop in a certain way. These theories vary in their emphasis on a planned and linear process versus an iterative and interactive one. Proposition 9 suggests that the suitability of these alternatives is not determined only by person factors or the stage of development, as implied in earlier literature, but also by the artifact that is (to become) the venture’s market o<sup>f</sup>ering. Again, while the propositions were developed with a focus on digital entrepreneurship, they might also provide important impetus for related areas of investigation, such as digital innovation or processes of organizational renewal of incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Fourth, research in nascent areas, such as digital entrepreneurship requires a clear conceptual foundation to be able to develop a cumulative and coherent body of knowledge. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne boundary conditions to delineate digital entrepreneurship from other phenomena of interest and develop one core part of a conceptual foundation that di<sup>f</sup>erentiates digital venture ideas based on the digital artifacts that form their core. Given the variability of digital artifacts and the consequences for venture creation processes, scholars aiming to develop theory about digital entrepreneurship need to pay attention to and describe the type of digital artifact they are focusing on Davison and Martinsons (2016) and Zahra et al. (2014). For example, our theory indicates that the composition of digital artifacts might explain why some digital ventures can scale rapidly while others cannot, why it takes some digital ventures longer to go to market than others, and why some digital ventures struggle to develop their market o<sup>f</sup>erings while others do not. Hence, our mode of theorizing can give direction to researchers on digital entrepreneurship, and help them to focus on and describe the characteristics of digital artifacts and new venture ideas that are meaningful in their focal contexts and for the phenomenon under investigation. Researchers can also use our theory to organize their thinking about digital venture ideas, to compare important di<sup>f</sup>erences and similarities between digital ventures, and overall to stimulate thought experiments on digital entrepreneurship. Of course, our theorizing might also be fruitful in spawning the development of rival accounts and the desire to prove our ideas wrong—but such pursuits of failure are also important elements of research programs (Burton-Jones et al., 2017; Gray and Cooper, 2010), and thus are just as welcome to progress knowledge on digital entrepreneurship.

## 6.2. Implications for research on venture creation processes and the entrepreneurship nexus

Despite Shane and Venkataraman’s (2000) call for researchers to look beyond the entrepreneurial agent and also consider the importance of “that on which they are acting” (their individual–opportunity nexus idea), the entrepreneurship <sup>fi</sup>eld has seen little development in conceptualizing the characteristics of these entities and their e<sup>f</sup>ects. To an even lesser extent have such characteristics been linked to di<sup>f</sup>erences in the venture creation process, despite the increasing acceptance of the importance of viewing venture creation as a process (e.g., McMullen and Dimov, 2013; Zahra and Wright, 2011).

The paucity of theory on how characteristics of the emerging new venture itself a<sup>f</sup>ect the venture creation process is a considerable limitation of the entrepreneurship literature. It is highly unlikely that the e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of a particular type of process is unrelated to what type of venture idea is being pursued. Without theoretical language and empirical insights about such relationships, entrepreneurship teaching, counseling, and policy-making cannot be designed and delivered with high accuracy. For example, Shim and Davidsson (2018) showed that sampling new ventures that are currently in the venture creation process that is, after initiation but before outcome—will lead to over-sampling of cases with long venture creation process duration. This indicates that not paying attention to the emerging venture itself and its potential implications for venture creation processes and their durations can lead to methods issues and decrease the accuracy of insights because it might bias the composition of prevalent cohort samples

Our theorizing illustrated that Davidsson’s (2015) new venture idea concept may provide a more e<sup>f</sup>ective tool than the “opportunity” construct for developing such theory. Based on this premise, we developed theory about one important aspects of venture idea variation, namely the characteristics of the imagined market o<sup>f</sup>ering. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that variations in the material embodiment of and coupling between digital artifact components imbue variations in venture creation processes. Apart from hopefully being directly ap plicable for better understanding and predicting the creation journey of digital ventures, this can serve as a model for future attempts to theorize relationships between other venture idea characteristics on the one hand, and process characteristics on the other.

This type of theoretical development could become a much-needed complement to existing theories and frameworks pertaining to the venture creation process, like those reviewed in Table 1. Notwithstanding other strengths, the rightmost column in that table re<sup>fl</sup>ects that these theories and frameworks have not produced much validated, generalizable and widely accepted knowledge about how characteristics of emerging ventures in<sup>fl</sup>uence the venture creation process and its outcomes (Davidsson, 2015). The conceptual tools and type of theorizing we have exercised in this paper can help rectify this shortcoming. As noted above, some process theories in entrepreneurship, best exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by discovery theory (Delmar and Shane, 2003; e.g., Shane, 2003), emphasize analysis and planning, followed by a relatively orderly and linear process of implementation. Other theories, among which e<sup>f</sup>ectuation theory (Sarasvathy, 2001) is the most-cited, depict a more <sup>fl</sup>uid process of taking (im)provisional steps towards an unfolding goal. Our theorizing suggests that the likelihood of observing either type of process as well as their relative e<sup>fi</sup>cacy for attaining desirable outcomes are contingent on the nature of the artifact to be developed and o<sup>f</sup>ered. In our particular context, we pointed to the embodiment and coupling of digital artifacts as salient characteristics. Similar characteristics are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence venture creation processes beyond the digital context and may pertain to the venture itself (e.g., its business model) and not just the artifact to be o<sup>f</sup>ered. Because our type of theorizing is not linked to a particular extant process model it can be applied to several of them. This would add attention to the venture’s characteristics to the traditional emphasis on agent disposition, thereby creating a basis for a more complete understanding of the antecedents, characteristics, and e<sup>f</sup>ects of new venture creation processes.

## 7. Conclusion

Digital entrepreneurship research is still at a nascent stage. One reason is certainly the observation that relevant technological advances have occurred only recently. Another reason is the dearth of novel and appropriate theorizing as a foundation for digital entrepreneurship research. The absence of strong substantive theory on digital entrepreneurship imposes a dilemma; the rapid rise of technological advances and their increasing entrepreneurial exploitations in practice endanger the timeliness and relevance of any academic pursuit of analysis and prediction. In pursuit of both explanatory and prescriptive theorizing, we have provided what we believe is a promising, integrated way of theorizing that combines questions of digital artifact identity, form, and function with their implications for entrepreneurial processes and outcomes. The digital artifact characteristics and e<sup>f</sup>ects we suggest are merely a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to systematically outline several theoretical issues and challenges that are important to ask and answer. Future studies that pursue the ideas presented here will hopefully not only create an explanation for the phenomena already visible in the market, but also ideally contribute to important policies, support schemes, regulations, and broader knowledge about entrepreneurship in the digital world.
