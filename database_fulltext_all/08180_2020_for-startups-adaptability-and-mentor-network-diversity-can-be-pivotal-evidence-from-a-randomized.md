# FOR STARTUPS, ADAPTABILITY AND MENTOR NETWORK DIVERSITY CAN BE PIVOTAL: EVIDENCE FROM A RANDOMIZED EXPERIMENT ON A MOOC PLATFORM<sup>1</sup>

Charles Eesley

Department of Management Science and Engineering, Stanford University, Stanford, CA 94305 U.S.A. {cee@stanford.edu}

Lynn Wu

Wharton School of Management, University of Pennsylvania,

Philadelphia, PA 19104 U.S.A. {wulynn@wharton.upenn.edu}

Entrepreneurs leading digital ventures are often advised to be adaptable. However, research on how to pursue adaptable strategies and whether such strategies improve short- or long-term digital venture outcomes is sparse. By utilizing the ability to control content presentation and to measure outcomes through a course using a MOOC platform, we can introduce exogenous variation in strategies and mentorship characteristics, and link these attributes to venture outcomes over time. Contrary to expectations, we find that minimizing adaptability by adhering to a strong, persistent vision often results in better short-term outcomes as measured by quality of the pitch in digital startups. It also, however, results in worse long-term outcomes as measured by revenue, funding, and pivoting to a new venture. A more adaptable approach, when combined with a mentor who can facilitate this strategy by providing access to a structurally diverse social network, can offer the best combination of short- and long-run outcomes. The results suggest that guidance on mentor selection—especially selecting for the mentor’s social network attributes—is important over time for reaping the benefits of an adaptable strategy, particularly for digital ventures at their early-stage.

Keywords: Digital venture, adaptable strategy, social networks, MOOCs, pivot

## Introduction

Innovation and entrepreneurship have long been important in the IT industry (Bardhan et al. 2013; Kleis et al. 2012) and this is reflected in the disproportionate allocation of venture investment to digital and IT-related industries.<sup>2</sup> An emerging stream of research reveals the need for strategic adaptability and agility in digital ventures (Chakravarty et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2017; Kaplan and Orlikowski 2013). New versions and upgrades can be released faster in digital products than physical ones, and the time from ideation to product deployment is shortened as bits are easier to change than atoms (Huang et al. 2017). We know that as industry environments become more dynamic, IT competencies are critical for organizational agility and adaptability, which in turn can enhance firm performance (Chakravarty et al. 2013). Despite the growing body of work on digital ventures, it is still unclear how adaptability affects digital-venture performance, especially whether complementary strategies, such as network resources, play a role. Furthermore, adaptability may affect the short- and long-term performance differently and, accordingly, it is important to examine the optimal strategies that balance their tradeoffs. Using a randomized field experiment on a digital entrepreneurship class, we explore these questions to understand the tradeoffs of using an adaptability strategy for improving both short- and long-term performance and whether having a mentor who can provide network diversity to the entrepreneurs plays a role in balancing the tradeoffs. In contrast to prior work that has strongly advocated for a highly adaptable approach to digital ventures (Chakravarty et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2017), our work places important boundaries and much-needed nuance, in terms of different timing and network characteristics, on that previous understanding of adaptive strategies.

It has been hypothesized that the ability to adapt quickly is especially important for digital ventures because their dynamic technological environment requires fast-paced learning and the pursuit of new business opportunities (Chakravarty et al. 2013). Accordingly, a highly adaptable strategy is increasingly advocated, not only by scholars but also by policymakers and practitioners.<sup>3</sup> However, a tension exists between adaptability strategies that enable firms to compete in a dynamic technological environment, and the observation that digital ventures should avoid excess flexibility because it may increase mistakes, undermine confidence in the strategy, and delay time to market (Coghlan 2015). In addition, prior work has made the empirical observation that business planning (an approach that is rarely focused on rapid change and thus can be thought of as the opposite of adaptability) facilitates the development and survival of ventures (Delmar and Shane 2003). Thus, the relative merits of a highly adaptable strategy for early stage digital ventures and whether such strategies should be encouraged are still unclear. The argument for adaptability may also be insufficiently nuanced. For example, is adaptability only beneficial at the earliest stages of venture formation or do its benefits extend to the longer term? In addition, implementing an adaptable strategy may not be sufficient without simultaneous investments in network ties that can help entrepreneurs navigate which directions to pursue among the myriad possibilities and how to successfully adapt to the new direction. The role of mentors, and especially mentors with structurally diverse social networks, could be critical in accessing timely information (Reagans et al. 2004) to successfully help a venture adapt to a new strategic direction.

Advisors can heavily influence strategic choices both in their formulation and execution (Carpenter and Westphal 2001).

In addition, it is well understood that much of the value of mentors is created through access to their network of personal and professional connections who can provide information and other resources critical to a venture’s success (Lerner and Malmendier 2013; Nanda and Sørensen 2010; Wu 2013). Prior work in the context of established firms shows that cohesive social networks (which we also refer to as having lower network diversity) are less likely to adapt, and firms with structurally diverse networks (higher network diversity) have greater flexibility in changing requirements and new assignments (Gargiulo and Benassi 2000). This observation is consistent with research on social networks which shows that access to a structurally diverse social network can yield unique and novel information for improving performance (Burt 1992; Granovetter 1973). In fast-paced digital transformations, receiving information sooner and beating competitors to resources can increase a venture’s opportunities to capture value. By contrast, a network with strong, cohesive ties has the information advantage of sharing tacit, complex knowledge that may require more trust and higher communication bandwidth (Centola and Macy 2007; Coleman 1988; Kraatz 1998). However, advisory ties, regardless of whether they are embedded in a cohesive or a diverse network, should provide guidance and, accordingly, information sharing should happen more readily with advisors (Wang and Noe 2010, p. 123) than other settings where information sharing is not a primary purpose. Having access to unique information is critical for digital ventures that are willing to change strategies. As a result, pursuing an adaptable strategy may require access to timely information from a mentor with network diversity.

To test these ideas, we leverage a unique research environment, a Massive Open Online Course (MOOC), focused specifically on entrepreneurship for digital ventures. We use this setting to experimentally investigate the performance effect of nudging entrepreneurs toward certain venturedevelopment strategies which either have a relatively higher or lower degree of adaptability and whether this effect is moderated by mentorship characteristics (relatively higher versus lower degree of structural diversity in a mentor’s network, or network diversity in short). To generate variation in venture strategy, we randomly assign entrepreneurs in the class to different versions of the same assignments that encourage them to adopt different degrees of adaptability or nudge them to select mentors who have networks that vary in their relative degrees of structural diversity. We measure outcomes both in terms of a startup pitch at the end of the course (a short-term measure) as well as longer-term venture outcomes (through a follow-up questionnaire administered two years after course completion). Capturing long-term performance is particularly important as few field experiments have been large enough in scale or sustained for long enough to generate appreciable differences in firm performance in the long run (Chatterji et al. 2016). This unique setting enables us to generate exogenous variation in strategic choices, observe these choices, and link them to both short- and longterm outcomes, all of which would be difficult or impossible using archival data analysis including data from natural experiments.

This causality problem is particularly acute in understanding digital ventures for two reasons. It is extremely difficult to observe why strategic choices were made, especially ex ante, and it is difficult to assign randomized treatments in realworld settings. Through a field experiment using a MOOC, we can overcome these limitations to some extent and observe detailed outcomes associated with treatments that nudge ventures toward a specific strategic choice. Field experiments are an important research design, especially considering the wide discrepancies observed in economics, political science, and medicine between experimental results and the results of observational studies (Glazerman et al. 2003; Gordon et al. 2019). Thus, we provide a much-needed complement to existing studies that are, by and large, based on archival data, by applying randomized experiment methods which are widely used in e-commerce settings and strategy research.

Using one of the largest samples of entrepreneurs on a MOOC (that we are aware of) that includes more than 900 individuals speaking more than 40 languages, we find that a strategy which minimizes adaptability is superior to a highly adaptable strategy for performance at the end of the class (as measured by the quality of the startup presentation). However, startups with highly adaptable strategies can shrink the gap in the short-term performance by finding a mentor with network diversity. In long-term outcomes—defined as two years following course completion and measured by the amount of funding and revenue and the likelihood of starting another venture—we find a highly adaptable strategy is generally superior to a less adaptable strategy. We also find that ventures performed best when they were nudged toward finding a mentor who possessed network diversity in addition to implementing a highly adaptable strategy. This effect is especially pronounced for pivoting to a new venture in the future. Overall, these results confirm the benefits of adopting a highly adaptable strategy in the long-term, yet provide a cautionary tale on short-term performance. However, in balancing the tradeoffs of using an adaptability strategy, we highlight the importance of having a mentor with network diversity that makes a highly adaptable strategy feasible in supporting superior outcomes in both the short and long terms. In this way, we advance theory by linking social network theory with adaptive strategies and, for the first time (that we are aware), provide important theoretical boundary conditions on the effectiveness of adaptive strategies. This is in stark contrast to recent work which has (almost) universally advocated for the benefits of adaptive strategies for digital ventures.

## Adaptability in Digital Startups

The IS literature has examined adaptability as a key strategic factor in understanding how digital technologies impact business decisions and performance (Grover and Segars 2005; Sambamurthy et al. 2003; Vitale et al. 1986). Recent literature in strategy and entrepreneurship also outlines the degree of adaptability as a key dimension that guides the strategic process. At the low end of the adaptability spectrum, the emphasis is on comprehensive planning to increase the power of prediction and to reduce environmental uncertainty (Segars and Grover 1999; Shrader et al. 1982). At the high end of the spectrum, entrepreneurs should respond to environmental changes and meet needs that are discovered along the way rather than firmly adhering to the firm’s original vision (Lee et al. 2015). Thus, the difference between low- and highadaptability strategies can be thought of as the degree to which a firm responds to new information and environmental signals.

A high-adaptability strategy may be especially important for building digital ventures (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008; Lee et al. 2015; Pavlou and El Sawy 2010),<sup>4</sup> because the business environment in which digital ventures operate necessitates flexibility and quick adaptation. Unlike analog technologies, digital technologies separate content from medium and are thus more easily changed, replicated, and distributed compared to analog ones. Accordingly, new functions can be added to digital products without a complete change to the existing design (Huang et al. 2017). Coupled with the ease of organically collecting user activities in real time, digital startups can use data to quickly evaluate new services, detect errors in the production process, and explore new areas of development (Henfridsson et al. 2014; Kiron et al. 2016). Because of this inherent flexibility, digital companies can face stiff competition and strong industry turbulence. Unlike hardware ventures, infrastructure investments cannot be easily accrued in digital ventures (Brynjolfsson et al. 2008). Thus, the fast pace of innovation and obsolescence suggest the importance of examining the effectiveness of adaptable strategies over time; yet prior work in this area has left unexplored short- versus long-term effects. The lack of understanding of adaptability strategies may be even more pronounced for digital startups, as most of the work on the low versus high degree of adaptation focuses on established firms which have different information and resource endowments than startups. Even for established firms, a metaanalysis of existing work on high- versus low-adaptable strategies suggests a considerable number of contradictory results across individual studies (Brinckmann et al. 2010; Premkumar and King 1994). Collectively, it is important to closely examine how adaptability strategies facilitate firm performance, especially for startups.

One hindrance preventing firms from adapting to a new market is that they often lack the necessary resources, competence, information, and guidance (Tripsas and Gavetti 2000). This effect often shows up because it is easier for firms to develop incremental technologies that appeal to their existing customer base than adapting to develop services that can capture a new market space (Tripsas and Gavetti 2000). Without a deep understanding of customers, markets, and technology needed for adaptation, firms often fail even when they are committed to adapting (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom 2002). As a result, despite having the desire to capture new market opportunities, digital startups often experience an unusually high failure rate (Krishnan and Ulrich 2001).

Some organizational scholars have begun to examine the factors that help some firms adapt better than others (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997; McDonald and Eisenhardt 2019). Simulation results suggest that young firms may need to add more structure, whereas large, established firms need to reduce structure, especially in dynamic environments (Davis et al. 2009). Surprisingly, despite the presence of a large body of literature pointing to the organizational lifecycle (Quinn and Cameron 1983), recent work on adaptability has largely neglected the issue of whether adaptability is more or less beneficial at different times in a venture’s lifecycle. For example, a high-adaptability strategic process is likely to result in lower performance in the short run simply because it delays the team from coming to a consensus and formulating a coherent business model as the idea keeps shifting. Similarly, if the earliest stages of adaptation resemble experimentation, then it may take time before experiments can be run, interpreted, and alternative plans and business models developed (Kerr et al. 2014). Thus, relative to a low-adaptability strategy which focuses on an initial idea and persistence in developing it, we expect that a high-adaptability strategy will be associated with lower performance and slower progress in the short-run. However, over time, these early investments in learning and adapting begin to pay off, resulting in better performance than less adaptable ventures that may have persisted with a subpar initial idea for too long.

H1: A high-adaptability strategic process will negatively affect performance in the short-run and positively affect longer-term performance.

## Social Networks and Digital Entrepreneurship

Social networks are understood to play a substantial role in a venture’s strategy and success. The literature has strongly suggested that the social network of the founder can affect her ability to receive and act on valuable information (Hahn et al. 2008; Ingram and Roberts 2000; Lerner and Malmendier 2013; Nanda and Sørensen 2010; Nicolaou and Birley 2003; Shane and Cable 2002). As a result, a founder’s social networks can affect a variety of choices and outcomes including entry (Stuart and Ding 2006), fundraising (Aggarwal et al. 2012; Burton et al. 2002; Roberts and Sterling 2012), financing, and performance (Dahl and Sorenson 2012; Rider 2009; Roberts and Sterling 2012). Prior work also suggests that the network characteristics that drive performance in routine, execution-oriented tasks differ from the characteristics that benefit innovation-oriented tasks (Moran 2005). This finding hints at the importance of examining not only the link between network diversity and adaptation, but also that the effect may differ over time as the venture shifts from innovation to execution.

It has been documented that having access to a structurally diverse network with bridging ties provides information benefits that are critical to venture success. Firms in geographic clusters rich in bridging ties have been shown to develop greater competitive capabilities due to novel information flows (McEvily and Zaheer 1999). By bridging ties across various groups in social networks, mentors with a structurally diverse network can confer information advantages on the venture by facilitating the flow of information between these otherwise unconnected groups (Burt 1992, 1997; Cross and Cummings 2004; Wu 2013; Wu and Kane 2020). They help ventures reduce susceptibility to social influence and objectively understand the importance and relevance of information (Singh and Phelps 2012). While mentors can be an advisory tie (identifying expertise) or offer a friendship tie (building trust with key stakeholders), both types of ties can introduce strategic resources critical for decision making and performance (Krackhardt 1990; Wu 2013). Having access to key resources in a timely manner is critical in the fast-changing business environment faced by digital ventures. Indeed, prior work has shown that randomized matching to a mentor with prior entrepreneurial experience encouraged recent graduates to join entrepreneurial firms at a higher rate than their peers with mentors who lacked founding experience, suggesting that mentor attributes matter more than simply having a mentor (Eesley and Wang 2017).

## Network Diversity Aids Adaptability in Digital Ventures

The core argument of the structural holes theory (Burt 1992) is that bridging structural holes in a structurally diverse network increases the likelihood of different types of knowledge being shared. Diverse information about technological and market conditions can strongly affect the strategy and direction that a firm needs to adapt in order to compete in a fastchanging business environment (Aggarwal and Singh 2013; Greenwood and Gopal 2015). Having mentors with structurally diverse networks can provide such information including how to decide on what to change and experiment with. Previous work in the entrepreneurship literature shows that diverse ties outside of the founding team help to improve creative outputs (Shalley and Perry Smith 2008). In the fastchanging field of digital ventures, a mentor can expand an entrepreneur’s network diversity to identify and capture new opportunities, and to avoid less fruitful choices. For instance, if a venture is shifting to pursue a newly discovered opportunity that is outside its industry experience, a mentor may provide the necessary industry knowledge.<sup>5</sup> Bridging between disconnected groups allows information to be exchanged in a broader network (Ahuja 2000; Burt 1992, 2000). This can facilitate knowledge sharing and recombinations that are critical for adapting to new product development, cost reduction strategies, and innovation capabilities, which in turn can improve team/firm performance (e.g., Arthur and Huntley 2005; Collins and Smith 2006; Cummings 2004; Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch 2009; Wu et al. 2019).

Thus, the success of implementing a high-adaptability strategy, especially in the short-run, may depend on having advisor ties that can overcome the difficulties of executing the strategy. Here, we build on prior research that shows the performance benefits for corporate R&D teams whose members have access to more structurally diverse social networks (Reagans et al. 2004; Reagans and Zuckerman 2001). Network diversity can offer similar information advantages that are critical for entrepreneurs to discover new opportunities, evaluate whether to act upon those opportunities, and implement the new vision based on the opportunities. Indeed, receiving novel information through social networks has been shown to be important for developing innovations (Ruef 2002), and this information advantage can be critical in implementing an adaptive strategy at an early stage of venture formation. Furthermore, even when the information is available, it is only useful to the entrepreneur who is receptive and willing to act on it, a condition that is less likely to be met when utilizing a low-adaptability strategy, especially if the information does not support the original vision. However, the same mentor characteristics can have different effects based on the strategy used by the entrepreneur, and these effects can vary as we consider long-run and short-run performance. An entrepreneur who is pursuing a less adaptive entrepreneurial strategy is less likely to take advantage of the benefits of having a diverse mentor. Prior descriptive research suggests that entrepreneurs may begin with cohesive, identity-based networks and then evolve toward more diverse networks as their firms grow and resource needs change (Hite and Hesterly 2001). However, the dynamics of the strategic process or adaptability can also shape whether the entrepreneur is able to make the most of the network structure. Entrepreneurs using a low-adaptability strategy may find access to mentors with bridging ties to diverse networks to be less important compared to entrepreneurs using a highadaptability approach. The main difference between a lowadaptability strategy and a high-adaptability strategy is that the threshold for strategic change is higher under the lowadaptability strategy, meaning an entrepreneur using a lowadaptability strategy is more likely to reject a mentor’s information if it significantly alters the original vision. This shortcoming is more likely to appear over time than at the beginning because the benefits of adaptation to support business change need time to play out. Even with aid from a mentor with network diversity, diverse knowledge must be gathered and interpreted, new strategies need to be brainstormed, and experiments must be planned, executed, and interpreted, all of which take time before performance benefits accrue. Thus, due to the time needed for the process to unfold, we expect entrepreneurs using a high adaptability strategy would benefit more (in the long-run) from a mentor with high network diversity, compared to entrepreneurs using a low adaptability strategy.

Furthermore, a bonding tie (in contrast to a bridging tie) in a cohesive network has been shown to help facilitate trust and convey status signals that can help an entrepreneur realize her vision (Coleman 1988; Shane and Cable 2002). A mentor with a bonding tie may help an entrepreneur using a low adaptability strategy more so than a mentor with a bridging tie. Compared to a structurally diverse network, the presence of strong overlapping ties in a more cohesive network can lead to greater trust between an entrepreneur and a mentor (Kautonen et al. 2010). More cohesive networks have also been shown to exhibit a higher degree of homophily (Lutter 2015), which further facilitates building trust and cohesion between an entrepreneur and a mentor. Having a mentor in the same cohesive network enables the entrepreneur to be more open in communicating problems, such as uncertainty over next steps, or anticipated difficulties which may be particularly beneficial when undertaking a low-adaptability approach. Because errors or negative feedback may reflect poorly on the entrepreneur who has devised the plan, the entrepreneur would value a mentor who is a trusted confidant. Thus, we expect that the performance effect of using a lowadaptability strategy may be enhanced by a mentor with bonding ties in a cohesive network as opposed to a mentor with bridging ties in a structurally diverse network. This performance effect may become more pronounced over time. While the benefits of using a high-adaptability strategy would accrue in the long run with the help of a mentor that has a structurally diverse network, the same mentor would provide limited benefits to an entrepreneur utilizing a low-adaptability strategy, resulting in an even greater performance gap between low- and high-adaptability strategies. For these reasons, we expect that a mentor with high network diversity will result in better long-run performance for entrepreneurs using a high adaptability process than for entrepreneurs using a low adaptability process.

H3: A mentor with high network diversity will result in better long-run performance for entrepreneurs using a high adaptability process than for entrepreneurs using a low adaptability process.

## Setting

We use a popular high-tech entrepreneurship course taught on NovoEd.com, a MOOC platform primarily designed for entrepreneurship education, specifically in digital business. The class consists of a series of video lectures alongside seven week-long assignments, which culminate in a final project called the “opportunity analysis project.” The course covers the early stages of strategy formulation including: finding mentors, co-founders and teammates; identifying the market opportunity for the business; creating an initial business model for generating revenue; and evaluating the opportunity based on a qualitative/quantitative market analysis and customer interviews. The final project summarizes the team’s work over the previous seven weeks in written and presentation form and evaluates whether their chosen entrepreneurial opportunity is worth pursuing as a startup. Over 95% of the ventures in the class are digital businesses. They range from social networking to mobile payment applications, from exercise management to healthcare management tools, and from online education to music sharing platforms. Other ventures aim to develop next-generation core technologies such as handling the computational needs of genomics or building the new hardware for handling deep-learning applications. Some ventures created during the class have received venture financing. For example, TommyJams, a digital platform that matches musicians to venues in India, has received funding from Startup Chile and Microsoft Venture Accelerators (Khan 2014).

The class was conducted in the fall of 2013 with tuition-free enrollment for anyone via NovoEd. Awareness of the course was spread through social media and distribution lists of previous online courses offered by NovoEd. More than 15,000 students were enrolled in our class, half of which resided outside of the United States. The students on the platform speak 61 different languages, come from more than 100 different countries, and vary widely in age and education levels.

Mentors were also recruited to sign up for the class using a similar recruiting process. Once registered, each student and mentor created a profile page that included biographical and work experience information. The platform allows for students and mentors to search for one another using keywords and other profile information, and to message each other. The class also provided communication tools for students to exchange ideas, ask questions and communicate with each other through email, chat, and a public forum.<sup>6</sup> Compared to a single university setting that is often used in prior research (Burt and Ronchi 2007; Lerner and Malmendier 2013), MOOCs are superior because they can reach a much wider audience in both advanced and emerging market economies, thus providing a multi-country setting that is often rare in digital venture research. The wide reach also enables the observation of a much larger number of ventures than prior studies, often at an order of magnitude greater in size.<sup>7</sup> Although the setting is a MOOC course, the purpose of the course is to guide students through the process of starting a venture. This type of setting should closely mimic the processes that entrepreneurs use in the field, therefore our findings from the course should be informative.

Similar to e-commerce settings that have been used for many randomized experiments in fields such as marketing, MOOCs provide a rare parallel environment for conducting randomized experiments to advance research in strategy and entrepreneurship. Randomized experiments can provide a more causal interpretation and the MOOCs setting allows these experiments to be easily repeatable and verifiable because the software infrastructure does not need to be built specifically for the experiments. The digital trace captured in MOOCs can also be used to observe the social processes in entrepreneurship and social network formation, an observation that is otherwise extremely difficult to achieve. Using MOOCs to manipulate firm choices on strategy (which is extremely difficult to do), we take a step forward and provide an attractive setting for introducing exogenous variations and observing complex problem-solving behaviors. By customizing methods learned from randomized experiments in e-commerce settings and applying them to advance strategy research, we provide a much needed complement to existing studies that are primarily based on archival data.

Furthermore, unlike using in-residence MBA students or undergraduate students from a single university, a MOOC setting can sample potential entrepreneurs more uniformly from a wide range of institutional and national affiliations. However, we acknowledge that there is a tradeoff between (1) reaching a mass scale of participants as enabled by technologies such as MOOCs and (2) detailed observations about the complexity in the interactions and relationships that are better facilitated through ethnography on a smaller number of participants. Overall, MOOCs allow for the observation of thousands of entrepreneurs and their activities on the platform and enable causal inferences through randomization (which is rare for entrepreneurship studies that often achieve only a fraction of the scale that MOOCs can provide). These characteristics make our study an ideal complement to the existing work on digital ventures and entrepreneurship.

## Experimental Design

We use a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to test our hypotheses. One of the challenges of empirical analysis of digital ventures is that it is difficult to observe specific processes and outcomes of entrepreneurial activity and to do so on a consistent basis across ventures. Moreover, making any type of causal inference is especially challenging because it is difficult to separate the effect of venture strategy from the characteristics of individual entrepreneurs. For instance, successful entrepreneurs may tend to prefer an adaptable strategy even if it has no direct effect on success. By using RCT in MOOCs to encourage some venture strategies over others, we can create a degree of exogenous variation in strategy choice. By focusing on two separate and independent choices in a 2 × 2 design, we can examine how the matching of strategies influences outcomes. This often enables a stronger causal inference than is possible by looking at individual strategies in isolation.

Specifically, we perform our intervention by manipulating the instructions on an assignment (the fourth out of eight) that involves students choosing a venture strategy (low- or highadaptability) and selecting their mentor (who has either a diverse network or no intervention on the mentor’s network characteristics). Students are randomly assigned to one of four groups, representing different combinations of mentor selection and degree of adaptability. The students receive identical assignments but each of the four groups receives a different supplementary paragraph describing the approach they should take (see Table 1 and Appendix A for details).

The first treatment dimension is along the venture development process. The group assigned to the low-adaptability condition is asked to focus on finding a mentor to implement a preconceived vision of their project at an early stage: “You should pitch them on your startup idea. Share with them your vision and see if they agree and share that vision” (Appendix A, low adaptability strategy). In recognition that complete inflexibility at an early stage may not be appropriate, the instructions encourage some limited adaptability but only at an early stage and only if they are unable to attract interest: “If you cannot find a mentor in this way, then it may be a sign that you need to adapt and change your vision or pitch and try again to find a mentor” (Appendix A, low adaptability strategy).

By contrast, students randomly assigned to the highadaptability condition are encouraged in the instructions to find a mentor to support a process of flexible co-development of their initial idea: “you might mention an initial idea. Then, you should ask them what they think and how they would approach that problem …. [and] resist the temptation to ‘pitch them on your idea …. remain open to change … [and] indicate that you are open to shifting the goals of the project or the approach in response to their suggestions” (Appendix A, high adaptability strategy). Students are also asked to be adaptable when working with their chosen mentor, emphasizing that mentors, “commit resources in exchange for a chance to reshape the goals of the project, to influence what future will ultimately result” (Appendix A, high adaptability strategy).<sup>8</sup> The latter instruction was incorporated based on prior research that suggests that adaptable strategies can be especially useful when advisors are willing to provide some assistance in conceiving or implementing the changes in direction (Brown and Eisenhardt 1997; El Sawy and Pavlou 2008; Sambamurthy et al. 2003; Wiltbank et al. 2006).

The second treatment dimension is along the mentor’s social network. Students are motivated to find a mentor who can create a more structurally diverse network for them. We present the benefits of having mentors with network diversity since, through their bridging ties, they are more likely to “introduce new resources … [and] identify and provide critical resources when needed” (Appendix A, diverse mentor strategy). We also explicitly encourage students to exploit weak ties beyond the immediate connections of the mentor: “find out not only whom [the mentor] know[s] but whom their friends know as well” (Appendix A, diverse mentor condition). Past research has shown that mentors with bridging ties in a structurally diverse network can reach into many distinct groups and thus offer a much greater diversity of information and skills compared to mentors with a structurally cohesive network that lacks those ties (Burt 2004; Wu 2013). For students in the non-intervention control groups that were not treated with diverse mentors, they were not prompted to seek mentors with diverse networks. Instead, we only show them the general benefits of having a mentor, but do not point out the particular advantage of having mentors with certain network characteristics. For example, mentors who have experience in a related industry can provide useful insights in helping the entrepreneur to develop the venture. While these students were not encouraged to find a structurally cohesive network, they would be, on average, less likely to seek mentors with high network diversity compared to students who are instructed to find a mentor with high network diversity.

Except for this randomized assignment, all students received the same course content.<sup>9</sup> That is, other than the extra one or two paragraphs describing the type of strategic development process and type of social network benefits a mentor can bring, the assignments are the same for everyone. Mentors also received a brief description of the class and were told that successful mentor relationships in the course involved meeting with the mentees at least three times and being available by email or phone to answer questions.<sup>10</sup> Surveys from an in-person version of the class indicate that students commonly report that their mentors have provided feedback on the startup idea, communicated their industry experiences, and facilitated introductions to strategic partners in their networks. A few weeks after the assignment, we conduct a survey to examine the degree of adaptability the students used and the network properties of their selected mentors. Outcomes from the surveys serve to check that the manipulations had the predicted effects.

We recognize that our manipulation is simplistic. For instance, our treatment cannot control for the nature of the mentor relationship such as creating an advisory or friendship tie, although an advisory tie is more likely to happen given that the role of a mentor is to advise the founder. However, the simplicity of our manipulation in a digital platform allows us to observe how mentor networks can causally affect venture outcomes for a much larger population than earlier studies in the physical world, potentially making our results more generalizable. The tradeoff between having a large number of participants with fewer details and a small number of participants with more details is typical for studying phenomena between digital and physical platforms (Kane et al. 2012). In the field experiments literature, this has become known as a process field experiment rather than a strategy field experiment.

Furthermore, MOOC settings can (partially) offset the traditional tradeoff inherent in randomized field experiments that offer greater internal validity and identification at the expense of generalizability. Our design falls under the category of process field experiments, which use a smaller intervention, closer to the theoretical construct to test a mechanism or process (Chatterji et al. 2016), as opposed to strategy field experiments, which are characterized by a larger (however, often infeasible) intervention, but where it is also difficult to identify the mechanisms. The MOOC setting also provides the replication advantage as the same experiment can be replicated in the same class that is offered many times digitally, in different contexts at a low cost. This can enhance the generalizability in our process field experiment compared to prior field experiments in strategy, which are still rare and limited in scale.

However, we also face new limitations typical for online experiments. Leakage could be an issue if students discover that they were given different versions of the same assignments. This could cause non-compliance if students then choose to deviate from the strategy that they were prompted to follow. However, given this is an online class and social engagements between students in MOOCs are minimal, problems from this type of leakage are less likely (Baek and Shore 2016). To verify this assumption, we examined the public forums used by the class and find no evidence that students were aware of the different versions of the same assignments, suggesting leakage and the associated contamination effects are minimal.

## Data

We have a complete record of 942 students who completed the class out of 15,414 students who enrolled and participated in at least one activity in the class. The completion rate is similar to other NovoEd classes and consistent with other MOOC platforms (Zheng et al. 2015). Despite the high attrition rate, a dataset of 942 subjects is still an order of magnitude greater than a typical entrepreneurship study (e.g. see Stam et al. 2014, Table 1). Although the high attrition rate (typical for a MOOC class) can potentially create some bias, the bias can also be beneficial because students who completed the class are more likely to be serious entrepreneurs and, accordingly, the effect of intervention on this population may be more realistic than on a population that includes students who drop out. After all, the goal of the class is to help serious entrepreneurs develop an idea into a viable venture as opposed to broadly introducing the idea of a startup to many people. Nonetheless, we check the sample differences in our randomized groups before and after the attrition. We also use Heckman selection models to ensure that attrition would not bias our results.

The summary statistics about these students are shown in Table 2. Panel A shows the statistics of all 15,414 students in our sample and the summary statistics for those who completed the class. Of the entire 15,414 students enrolled, 19.7% of the students are native English speakers, the mean age is in the twenties, and most hold a bachelor’s degree. This international profile, coupled with our large sample of ventures, provides a rare opportunity to examine the global applicability of our findings, thus potentially making our findings even more generalizable than prior studies with a much smaller sample size covering a specific demographics such as MBA students in a university. We also showed the summary statistics for the 942 students who completed the class. Although the demographic statistics of the 942 students and the entire pool of 15,414 original enrollees are different, the differences among the four randomized groups are negligible in the samples before and after students who dropout (or “dropouts”), as shown in Panel B of Table 2. This indicates that although dropouts altered the sample averages, they did not systematically bias any particular group.

In Panel C, we show the summary statistics of students who responded to our surveys about long-term outcomes and students who did not. While there are observable differences between students who responded and students who did not respond, there are no systematic differences between the four randomized groups as it relates to respondents and nonrespondents. These results suggest that non-respondents did not bias any particular randomized group.

## Treatment Effect Variables

The main independent variables are the four treatment variables related to venture strategies and mentors’ social networks. To ensure the effect originates from the treatment, we survey the type of strategy students used (high- versus low-adaptability) and the type of mentor they pursued or found (diverse versus non-intervention control). The scale for measuring the mentor’s network diversity is between 0 and 10, with 10 indicating that the mentor is acting as a bridging tie who has contacts that are significantly more structurally diverse than the mentee’s network and are non-overlapping with the mentee’s contacts, and 0 indicating that the mentor is a bonding tie whose network is very similar to the mentee’s network. We then create a binary metric in which a mentor is diverse if the value is greater than 5. To measure the degree of adaptability used in the venture strategy, we ask the student to rate this on a scale of 1 to 9, with lower scores indicating a low-adaptability strategy, 5 indicating a mixed strategy, and higher scores indicating a high-adaptability approach. We then create a binary variable for the high-adaptability strategy if the student scored it above 5. However, because this data is collected 3 weeks after the assignment, the survey may miss students who had not fully adopted the strategies in the prompt. Thus, the results from the survey should be a lower limit on the treatment effect.

## Dependent Variables

We collect detailed records of students’ assignment grades, class engagement activities, and their final project grade on the presentation of their entrepreneurial opportunity and business model. These are short-term outcomes captured at the end of the class. We then measure performance at the twoyear mark by conducting a survey that measures the survival, fund raising, revenue for the ventures created in the class, and whether they have started another new venture. These longterm metrics are used to further assess enterprise outcomes affected by the treatment and how they differ from short-term outcomes.

## Venture Pitch Outcome

The main output of the class consists of a final presentation, or a start-up pitch, and a short, written document that covers the business problem, a prototype of the solution, analysis of the market size, customer interviews, finances, and a business model of the student’s proposed venture. Delmar and Shane (2003) show that entrepreneurs who went through similar steps that our final assignment covers, such as gathering information about the market and competition and developing financial projections, eventually developed a product, filed the paperwork, and purchased equipment to start a firm. Thus, the completeness of these activities that is similar to the final class assignment and has been shown to be associated with survival and other important entrepreneurial outcomes. Similarly, decision heuristics have been shown to accurately predict subsequent outcomes for early-stage ventures (Åstebro and Elhedhli 2006).

Due to having more than 15,000 students, the grading for all class assignments was done by peer review, as it is typically done in MOOCs. Prior work and a meta-analysis show a strong correlation (0.69) between peer grading and instructor grading. This indicates that peer evaluation is a reliable and valid assessment, especially if five or more peer-assigned grades are averaged over one project, as is true in this case (Bouzidi and Jaillet 2009; Falchikov and Goldfinch 2000; Freeman and Parks 2010; Sadler and Good 2006). To prepare students for peer grading, they are given detailed grading criteria from the instructor and then trained on a set of five assignments that the instructor had previously graded from an earlier course. Students then grade five submissions before they see their own scores and feedback. The peer evaluation scores are then averaged to generate the score for each student’s final project.<sup>11</sup> The scale is included in Appendix B.

Peer evaluation scores serve two purposes. First, they are the main criteria for awarding a certification at the end of class. Second, they serve as a proxy for the likelihood of a venture’s success. We acknowledge that a high peer evaluation does not guarantee that the venture will ultimately perform well, but it can serve as a signal for how customers, investors, and employees view the venture. At the early stages of a startup, a key goal is to convince potential cofounders, early employees, users, and investors that the venture is an exciting and potentially viable opportunity. Entrepreneurs who receive a high peer score are likely to have an easier time gathering the initial resources (teammates, customers, investors) than entrepreneurs who are ranked poorly by peers or who failed to complete these initial steps to validate the potential market opportunity. We do not observe whether the individual eventually becomes a successful entrepreneur (which may take a decade or longer). However, completing and performing well on the startup opportunity presentation is meant to convince others that the business opportunity is viable, which gives us some indication about the likelihood of the venture’s future success. Thus, in the first part of the analysis, we use the grade in the final startup presentation to measure the venture’s potential for success. Recognizing grades are not an entirely accurate predictor of venture outcome, we survey the students again two years later to assess whether these effects persist or diminish over time.

## Longer-Term Performance Metrics

To complement the short-term outcomes from the class, we collected a set of long-term performance metrics two years later by using a follow-up survey sent to everyone enrolled in the class. Similar to prior studies on private firms, we use alumni surveys because private firms are not subject to public reporting requirements. Although data is available on public firms or firms that went through IPOs or acquisitions (Hochberg et al. 2007), focusing on these firms is not practical for our purposes because the sample would be biased toward a very small percentage of successful firms and it would take a long time to observe the outcome (typically, it takes 5 to 7 years for a successful startup to file for an IPO).

To collect a representative sample, the norm is to collect performance data via surveys and self-reports (Wiklund and Shepherd 2003). To mitigate the limitations of potential reporting bias, we allow respondents to complete the survey anonymously and ensure that their data from both the surveys and the class is also anonymous. The alumni connection also helps to ensure higher trust and data accuracy. We then look for robustness across multiple measures of performance in the survey. However, if any bias from self-reporting is present, it is expected to be consistent across randomized groups and not systematically biased toward any of our treatment conditions; we verified this assumption using the demographics variables (see Panel C of Table 2).

The survey asks if they are still working on the same startup from the course, and if they are working on a new startup. We then gather data on the product launch, financial performance (revenues and funding measured in the thousands of dollars) and survival of the ventures. We received responses from 554 alumni out of those who were treated in the experiment, representing a 6% response rate (relative to the number who opened the email). Of these responses, 336 contained complete data on all the variables needed for our base regressions. Importantly, the response rates are very similar among the randomized groups, consistent with the expectation that each group should comprise an equal share of the overall sample.

## Results

## Manipulation Checks

Prior to testing our primary hypotheses, we perform a manipulation check to determine whether our intervention generated the desired exogenous variation in adaptability strategy and mentor network diversity. Although the survey was taken 3 weeks after the treatment, which may underestimate the eventual degree of adaptability and mentor network diversity, we find that our nudges largely worked. Figure 1a plots the average adaptability score by whether they are treated with the adaptability conditions. Indeed, the adaptation score is higher for the students who are treated with high-adaptability conditions (high-adaptability-only and diverse-high-adaptability) than students who are treated with low-adaptability conditions (low-adaptability-only and diverse-low-adaptability). The detailed score for each of the four treatment groups is shown in Figure 1b: a similar pattern emerges in which the adaptability scores are higher for both the highadaptability-only and the diverse-high-adaptability groups compared to the other two groups treated with low-adaptability conditions. Similarly, we show the network diversity score by treatments in Figure 2. The students who were treated to find a mentor with a structurally diverse network have a higher mentor diversity score than students who were not treated (Figure 2a). Examining individual groups, we find that the two groups that received the diversity treatment (the diverse-high-adaptability group, and the diverse-low-adaptability group) have a higher network diversity score than the two groups that were not treated with diversity intervention. Regressions in Table 3 provide the point estimates: students who are encouraged to seek a mentor with high network diversity have an increased probability of selecting such a mentor (ß = 0.037, p < 0.1), and students who are encouraged to pursue adaptive strategies are more likely to have a highadaptability score by 5.1% (p < 0.05). While Table 3 uses OLS models, the Probit estimates are similar, as shown in Table D1 in Appendix D.

These results suggest that, while there is considerable variance in strategy, our sample is large enough to identify the differences between the treatment groups. The low R-square values reflect the general heterogeneity in the entrepreneurs as well as the characteristics of the chosen venture. Accordingly, these heterogeneities limit the degree to which one can nudge entrepreneurs toward a particular venture strategy.

We also verify that dropouts do not affect our results using a Heckman selection model where the first stage instrument is prior experience on the MOOC platform. The identifying assumption is that students who have taken multiple classes before taking this class are less likely to drop out independent of their treatment group. Consistent with this conjecture, we find that prior MOOCs experience is a significant predictor of completion. Applying the Heckman model with prior experience as the selection instrument, the results are similar (and perhaps slightly stronger in point estimates), suggesting that the results of the intervention are not affected by survival bias (Tables D2 and D3 in Appendix D). These results are consistent whether we use OLS or probit regression models in the Heckman estimates. Furthermore, the correlations between the errors for the first stage selection equation and the second stage equation (ñ) are very low and statistically insignificant, suggesting that these selection biases are not a serious concern.

We also examine whether the students’ probability of class completion is related to the treatment group they are assigned to. As shown in Table 4, none of the estimates are statistically significant. These results are also shown graphically in Figure 3a which plots the probability of class completion. Together, these results suggest that dropouts do not seem affect our estimates. It is possible that the self-reported results are subject to a response bias such that participants provide answers according to the questioners’ beliefs instead of their own beliefs. However, if this were true, we would not observe the performance impact at the end of the class or in the long-term performance metrics two years later. Furthermore, students are told prior to filling out the survey that their responses do not affect their grades, which should reduce the susceptibility to response bias.

## Short-Term Learning Outcomes

To measure short-term learning outcomes, we analyze the grades received on the startup presentation, which are a plausible leading indicator of future success and have the benefit of measuring performance consistently across a variety of venture types. We first graphed the average score on the final presentation by each treatment group (Figure 3b) and show that the high-adaptability-only group (which had no instruction to find a structurally diverse mentor) has the worst presentation score at the end of the class, but the gap almost entirely disappeared when the students are also nudged to find mentors with network diversity (diverse-high-adaptability group). The regression results in Table 5 provide the point estimates for the effect of each treatment group using the high-adaptability-only group as the baseline group. Overall, students in the low-adaptability-only group, on average, receive a higher final score (about 0.552 points or 2.2% higher) compared to students in the high-adaptability-only group (Column 1 of Table 5). However, the disadvantage of adopting a high-adaptability-only strategy is mostly eliminated when students are also asked to find a mentor with network diversity, as the score for the diverse-high-adaptability group is not statistically different from any of the lowadaptability groups (low-adaptability-only or low-adaptability-diverse). We also show that having a diverse mentor does not seem to affect the performance of students using low-adaptability strategies, as the difference between the lowadaptability-only and the diverse-low-adaptability groups is minimal. Moreover, the addition of demographic controls did not change our results (Table D4).

## Follow-Up Survey Results

As noted earlier, our longer-term outcomes come from a follow-up survey conducted two years following the course completion. Of respondents to the survey, 46.7% were still working on a startup idea based on or related to what they worked on in the course, and of the students who had abandoned their original idea, 49.4% were working on a different venture.<sup>12</sup> The fact that many students were persistent in building their venture is encouraging regarding the benefits of using the MOOC to study ventures. These students also provided a sufficient sample size for outcome analyses.

First, we show the distribution of the outcome variables in Figure 4. Because the sample in the long-term analyses is small, we use the entire sample to estimate the treatment ef fects of long-term outcomes and show that outliers do not play an important role. For ease of examining the results, we plot the average of each of the outcome variables (revenues, funding, and new start up) for each treatment group in Figure 5. It shows that the treatments appear to have a lasting influence. The effects differ between the short-term outcomes where ventures benefit the most from the low adaptability treatment. Contrasted with the short-term outcomes, the longterm outcomes were much worse for the low-adaptability-only group. However, we find that the diverse-high-adaptability group consistently performed well in both long- and shortterm outcomes, suggesting it is the overall optimal strategy. Specifically, both the high-adaptability-only and the diversehigh-adaptability groups generated more revenue and raised more venture funding than did the two low-adaptability groups. Furthermore, the difference between the highadaptability-only and diverse-high-adaptability groups is negligible in revenues and funding. We also find that the diverse-high-adaptability group has the added advantage of having a higher probability than any other group in launching a new start up that is different from one created in the class. However, having a mentor with high network diversity does not seem to affect students in the low-adaptability groups, suggesting there are limitations of pairing such mentors with students utilizing low-adaptability strategies. These results are consistent with the regression results in Table 6 that use the high-adaptability-only group as the baseline comparison.

Next, we use the Heckman selection model to address potential selection biases in Table 7 and show results similar to our OLS estimates. We use a student’s participation, measured in days on the NovoEd site prior to the course, as the predictor for responding to the survey. We note that due to the twoyear difference between the outcome variables and the instrumental variable used in the Heckman selection model, the estimates may be less robust than results from our earlier short-term analysis. However, based on the demographics analysis for students who responded to the survey and those who did, we did not observe any systematic biases toward any one particular randomized group, suggesting that our estimates are likely to be robust. Similar to the short-term outcome analysis, we also included demographic variables in the first stage selection and find that the selection biases are minimal as the Wald test cannot reject that ñ is zero and the Heckman analysis is similar to the OLS results. Including the covariates in the second stage shows very similar results (Table D6). To ensure that outliers are not driving our results, we also use the logged value of the outcome variables and show consistent results (Table D7).

Overall, these results suggest that a high-adaptability strategy creates the worst short-term outcomes but also superior performance in the long run, providing support for H1. However, we find that at the earliest stage of forming a digital startup, entrepreneurs using a high-adaptability strategy would benefit significantly from having a mentor with a structurally diverse network. In fact, having such a mentor can erase the performance gap entirely by the end of the class, providing support for H2. Because the information and resources provided by a diverse mentor can aid an entrepreneur by offering new opportunities, highly adaptable approaches help entrepreneurs to seize the opportunity and effectively use the information. As a result, their startup presentation at the end of the class is superior to the startup presentations of students who use the high adaptability strategy but without a mentor with network diversity. For ventures that use a high-adaptability strategy, the benefits of having a mentor with high network diversity extend beyond the short term, especially in the likelihood of starting a new venture two years later. Furthermore, the evidence does not indicate that having such a mentor affects ventures that use a low-adaptability strategy for both long- and short-term outcomes. Collectively, these results provide support for H3. However, the best strategies for both short- and long-term outcomes are strategies used in the diverse-high-adaptable group. We find that having a mentor with high network diversity can particularly help entrepreneurs using a highadaptability strategy compared to entrepreneurs who only used a high-adaptability strategy without such a mentor. Entrepreneurs in the diverse-high-adaptability group performed well in both the startup presentations at the end of the class as well as in the enterprise outcomes two years later, especially for pivoting to another new venture.

## Limitations and Future Research

Due to the unique setting and the use of this randomized process field experiment, we can better address some (although not all) of the potential problems of entrepreneurship and digital venture research, including the difficulty of comparing strategies and isolating the role of venture strategy independent of the entrepreneur’s ability or other characteristics. Our long-term outcome measures also provide a rare opportunity to clarify the robustness of our findings on entrepreneurship in the long run and how these findings differ from the findings of the short-term outcome measures. Understanding the impact of these strategies through different stages of the venture is becoming more important as highly adaptable strategies are being advocated in both research and in policy.<sup>13</sup> Examining long-term performance in a context of a randomized field experiment is rare in entrepreneurship and strategy research (Chatterji et al. 2016). While we provide evidence on both short- and long-term performance, future research should also examine adaptability and short- versus longer-term outcomes alongside traditional explanatory factors. For instance, it may be that founding team experience reduces the need for adaptability or that functional diversity alters the need for mentors with network diversity (Eesley et al. 2014; Eesley and Roberts 2012).

That said, a primary limitation of our setting is that it is focused on very early-stage venture formation by inexperienced entrepreneurs, two conditions that likely contributed considerable unexplained variance in our performance outcomes as shown by the low R² in our models. Furthermore, the entrepreneurs themselves as well as the characteristics of their digital ventures are considerably heterogeneous. While, our use of the MOOC setting enables us to overcome an inherent limitation of small sample sizes and generalizability in field experiment designs, our results should be interpreted with appropriate caution as future work may seek to replicate our design with other samples and interventions such as one that increases cohesive or low diversity ties as one arm of the study. While our Heckman selection models show that attribution does not significantly affect our inference, it is important to closely examine the implications of attrition in research designs where attribution may play a more significant role. One potential solution is to randomize subjects closer to the end of the class where the majority of the attrition has already occurred. If it is possible to track students over time, surveys could be used to gauge performance instead of class grades, which would eliminate the need for students to complete the class. Another source of heterogeneity lies in the type of mentor tie. Future research could use this research design and context to explore the impact of personal versus professional advisory ties. Our setting is limited to ventures at their earliest stage and thus it is an open question of whether the value of adaptability and a mentor’s network diversity persist as ventures move beyond this very early stage. Our results in this setting are an encouraging sign that sufficient participation in both the short term and long term can generate meaningful results.

## Conclusion and Discussion

There has been increasing interest in the importance of strategic adaptability in digital ventures (Kaplan and Orlikowski 2013). Prior work points to the idea that modifications can be realized much faster in digital products compared with their physical counterparts, and the time from conception to product launch can be significantly shorter thanks to digitization (Huang et al. 2017). With ever-increasing dynamism in industry environments, digital competencies have been shown to increase strategic adaptation and thus, firm performance (Chakravarty et al. 2013). However, despite this growing consensus on the importance of adaptability for digital ventures, it is still unclear whether or when adaptability has downsides for digital ventures. It is especially important to better understand whether certain social network mechanisms, such as the network diversity of mentors may play a role. In addition, how outcomes are influenced dynamically from short- to longer-term venture performance has gone unexplored. It is critical to understand if such strategic adaptability is to be advocated as a universal prescription and whether complementary strategies should be implemented in conjunction with adaptability.

Despite the existence of a robust literature on entrepreneurship and digital strategy, we know little about the strategic processes and their moderating factors that can affect outcomes of digital ventures. For instance, it is unclear how mentor characteristics can support specific early stage strategies. Through our deployment of a randomized field experiment, we identify specific strategies that likely increase shortand long-term success for early-stage digital ventures. Addressing this question is critical as the U.S. economy is going through structural changes and traditional physical goods and services are being disrupted by digital ventures. We find that the best near-term outcomes are achieved by combining a highly-adaptive strategy with a mentor who is capable of supporting adaptability by providing access to novel information and other resources that become accessible through a structurally diverse social network. Our results suggest that the earlier debate which seemingly focuses on high adaptability versus adhering to planning may be less important than a more nuanced focus on the temporal dimension and the advisory networks that support adaptable strategies. In particular, adaptable strategies that are typically encouraged for early-stage digital ventures are effective in the short-run only when combined with the right network ties. Whereas prior work implicitly suggests a highly adaptable approach to digital ventures is universally beneficial (Chakravarty et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2017), our results add nuance, in terms of both timing and network characteristics, on the effectiveness of adaptive strategies.

Our work contributes to the IT strategy literature by addressing the implications of choosing between low- and highadaptability strategies and how social networks can modulate this relationship in digital ventures. We show that the success of using a high-adaptability strategy for creating digital ventures depends, in the short-term, on having a mentor with high network diversity. Our results contribute to the emerging literature on digital entrepreneurship (Aggarwal et al. 2012; Aggarwal and Singh 2013; Burtch et al. 2013; Lin et al. 2013) by using a novel randomized design in a MOOC to document the strategic processes that were previously difficult to observe directly (El Sawy et al. 2010). Furthermore, our work responds to suggestions for the use of more robust research approaches by researchers studying digital innovation and ventures (Chatterji et al. 2016; Tanriverdi et al. 2010).

Our results build on an emerging stream of work on the importance of timing differences in strategy-making and entrepreneurship in digital environments (Kaplan and Orlikowski 2013). In prior work, strategic choices and social networks are often static, and captured in a single snapshot. However, our results show the need for more nuanced and dynamic models of venture strategies, especially in the rapidly changing technology environment faced by digital ventures. In doing so, our work also responds to calls in prior work for research that takes a process perspective and examines network dynamics over time rather than a purely static crosssection (Hoang and Antoncic 2003).

We also contribute to the social network and social media literature that has argued that social networks are resistant to change due to history, personality and past actions (Powell and Grodal 2005). We find that a relatively small educational intervention on social networks and venture strategy has a measurable positive effect on venture outcomes. We find evidence of a more causal relationship between prompting students to find mentors with network diversity and an increase in the short-run performance benefits of a highadaptability strategy. Using an RCT, we advance recent calls to causally identify social network effects (Aral et al. 2013; El Sawy et al. 2010; Wu 2013) through using new media and technologies.

Core notions of the “lean startup” movement which encourages entrepreneurs to pivot and adapt originated from the information systems literature (Osterwalder and Pigneur 2002; Osterwalder et al. 2005). However, it has recently been pushed forward more by strategy literature (McDonald and Eisenhardt 2019). We believe it is important to reclaim and advance its intellectual roots in IS. In this way, IS research can inform the strategic disciplines in understanding the essence of business models and improving their design, especially as digital transformations continue to accelerate (Osterwalder and Pigneur 2013). Historically, decision making and support constitute a large and important part of IS research (Taylor et al. 2010). Thus, our results advance these intellectual roots in IS and also help to inform the strategic disciplines in understanding the creation of business models and new ventures (Osterwalder and Pigneur 2013).

Finally, we also open avenues for using randomized field experiments on MOOCs to understand the process of entrepreneurship and strategy. While randomized field experiments are abundant in e-commerce settings, they have not been extended to the field of strategy (Chatterji et al. 2016). This is especially acute as research has shown that randomized experiments often produce different findings from archival studies (Gordon et al. 2019). Our MOOC setting can overcome some limitations in creating interventions large enough to generate a change and long enough to sustain the change. This is important because these limitations are a key in hampering the use of randomized field experiments in strategy. Our study shows we can use MOOCs as a complement to push the research further. Through offering several versions of class assignments, we show the effectiveness of using small nudges to test mechanisms of how certain venture strategies can causally affect performance. These small nudges can support a theoretical underpinning of the causal relationship of interest by making it possible to vary one factor at a time. As Ludwig et al. (2011) point out, even smallscale interventions on testing mechanisms can inform larger policy or strategic choices. This feature allows researchers to do question-driven research as opposed to being constrained by existing data, an important aspiration for the field. As with all emerging methods, we also provide suggestions and clarify some of the advantages and trade-offs of utilizing MOOCs for field experiments, such as the tradeoff between having more observations and fewer observations but better-quality data per observation. Replications are also more feasible using MOOCs as the classes are offered digitally several times a year, which can potentially provide generalized results across different settings. Collectively, randomized experiments on MOOCs can advance our understanding of entrepreneurship providing the much-needed complement to existing archival studies on strategies and entrepreneurship for digital ventures

## High Adaptability Only

To find a mentor, look for someone with either (1) startup experience in the same industry or a closely related industry to your startup idea, (2) years of industry experience in the same industry or a closely related industry to your startup idea, (3) a potential user/customer or partner for your startup idea, or (4) someone with startup or industry experience more generally.

You should tell them briefly about who your user or customer is and what general problem you want to solve in the industry for the user or customer. You do not necessarily need to propose a solution though you might mention an initial idea. Then, you should ask them what they think and how they would approach that problem. Resist the temptation to “pitch” them on your idea and instead focus on co-creating the project with them. It’s your responsibility to execute, but remain open to change if they are willing to commit time, introductions or resources. Indicate that you are open to shifting the goals of the project or the approach in response to their suggestions. In exchange, you simply ask them if they would commit to helping you build a product/service as a mentor. Mentors provide feedback from time to time, make introductions, or provide other resources. ## Low Adaptability Strategy

To find a mentor, look for someone with either (1) startup experience in the same industry or a closely related industry to your startup idea, (2) years of industry experience in the same industry or a closely related industry to your startup idea, (3) a potential user/customer or partner for your startup idea, or (4) someone with startup or industry experience more generally

You should tell them briefly about who your user or customer is and what general problem you want to solve in the industry for the user or customer. You should pitch them on your startup idea. Share with them your vision and see if they agree and share that vision. Provide them with whatever market data and evidence you have so far that shows that this is a promising venture. If they do not agree with your vision and plans, then you need to keep looking for a mentor who is a good match with your vision of the startup and its market.
