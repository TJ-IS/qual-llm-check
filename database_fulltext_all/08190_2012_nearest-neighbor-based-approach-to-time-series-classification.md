## a b s t r a c t

Many interesting applications involve predictions based on a time-series sequence or a set of time-series sequences which are referred to as time-series classification problems Prior classification analysis research predominately focuses on constructing a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model from training instances that involve nontime-series attributes. Direct application of traditional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques to time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems requires the transformation of time-series attributes into non-time-series ones by applying some statistical operations (e.g., average, sum, variance). However, such statistical-transformation-based approach often results in information loss and, in turn, imperils classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness. In this study, we propose a time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique based on the k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach. Using churn prediction of the mobile telecommunications industry as an evaluation application, our empirical evaluation results show that the proposed kNN-based time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (kNN-TSC) technique achieves better performance (measured by miss and false alarm rates) than the statistical-transformation-based approach does. © 2012 Elsevier B.V. ## 1. Introduction

With the advances of information technology and widespread diffusion of databases systems in organizations, large volumes of data are generated and maintained by organizations. The dramatic expansion of data, as measured by sheer volume of data and number of data repositories, has generated an urgent need for new analysis techniques that can intelligently and automatically transform the processed data into useful patterns or knowledge. Data mining represents an appealing approach to facilitate the required knowledge discovery in databases [8,15]. On the basis of the type of patterns or knowledge that can be discovered in databases, data mining techniques can broadly be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into several categories, including classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis, clustering analysis, dependency analysis (including association rule and sequential pattern analysis), data visualization, and text mining [33]. Due to its predictive nature, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis has been widely adopted in many real-world data mining applications [5,9,20,26].

Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis is a process of automatically constructing a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model that establishes relationships between decision classes and values of input attributes (i.e., decision variables) from a set of preclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instances (i.e., training instances). Such classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model, once induced, can be used to classify unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instances. Widely adopted classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques include decision tree induction (e.g., ID3 [28] and C4.5 [29]), decision rule induction (e.g., CN2 [10,11]), neural networks (e.g., backpropagation neural network [30]), Bayesian classi<sup>fi</sup>er [19], Support Vector Machines (SVM) [31], k-nearest-neighbor classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [12,13]. Most of existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques are designed to deal with classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of non-timeseries data; i.e., data described by non-time-series attributes (e.g., age, salary, education level). However, in many real-world classi<sup>fi</sup>cation applications, input attributes that are time-series in nature are commonly encountered.

A time-series sequence is an ordered list of real numbers, each number representing a value at a time point [2,3]. Examples of time-series sequences include call patterns of telecommunications subscribers, stock prices of companies, sales trends of products, exchange rates, biomedical measurements, etc. Many classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis applications involve input attributes that are time-series sequences. In this case, we are dealing with time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. For example, the call behavior of a telecommunications subscriber is described by a set of time-series call patterns (e.g., measured by daily frequency and minutes of use) and each subscriber can be labeled as a churner or a loyal customer. Conceivably, subscriber churn is not an instantaneous occurrence that leaves no trace. Before an existing subscriber churns (i.e., terminates his or her subscription relationship with the target telecommunications service provider), his/her call patterns may change (e.g., frequency and minutes of use gradually decrease). In other words, call patterns are likely to include warning signals pointing toward churning [32]. Therefore, it is both desirable and essential to develop a time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model for churn prediction on the basis of subscribers' call patterns. The resultant classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model can then be employed to predict whether a telecommunications subscriber will be a churner according to his or her call patterns.

In addition to the described churn prediction of the telecommunications industry, time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation may also be required in such applications as fraud detection and intrusion detection [16,22,27]. For example, on the basis of credit card usage patterns (e.g., daily transaction amount and frequency of use), fraudulent credit card accounts could be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. On the other hand, using network traf<sup>fi</sup>c patterns (measured by, for example, total number of bytes sent or received, number of packages sent or received, and number of Internet Protocol (IP) addresses connected to or from) collected for each IP address by a router, suspicious traf<sup>fi</sup>c patterns in and out of an IP address might be discovered. By monitoring such suspicious traf<sup>fi</sup>c signals, we can detect intrusions or attempts at the router level.

As mentioned, existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques predominately focus on learning a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model from training instances that involve non-time-series attributes. Direct application of existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques to time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems requires the transformation of each time-series attribute into one or more non-time-series attributes by applying some statistical operations (e.g., average, sum, variance) [4,32,37]. We call such method the statistical-transformation-based approach to time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems in this study. However, statistical transformation may result in information loss. For example, assume minutes of use of two telecommunications subscribers in <sup>fi</sup>ve consecutive periods are (55, 45, 35, 25, 15) and (5, 20, 35, 50, 65) minutes, respectively. Although their average minutes of use are identical (i.e., 35 minutes), the two subscribers indeed have very different call patterns, exhibiting opposite trends during these periods. The described information loss may hinder classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness of the statistical-transformation-based approach to time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. The preservation of trends in time-series sequences when inducing a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model for a time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem can reduce such information loss and, hence, is more desirable.

Motivated by the aforementioned applications and the need for improving effectiveness of time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, this study aims to propose and develop a novel time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique. We will empirically evaluate our proposed technique using a statisticaltransformation-based approach as a performance benchmark. The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses our selection of learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. The development of the proposed time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique will be depicted in Section 3. We report our empirical evaluation using churn prediction of the mobile telecommunications industry as the evaluation scenario and discuss important evaluation results in Section 4. We conclude in Section 5 with a summary and discussion of our contributions, together with some future research directions.

## 2. Analysis and selection of learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation

In this section, we formulate two different learning strategies for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation on the basis of our review of the strategies adopted by existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques. We will also analyze their advantages and disadvantages to justify our selection of learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems in this study.

As mentioned, popular classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis techniques include decision tree induction, decision rule induction, neural networks, Bayesian classi<sup>fi</sup>er, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and k-nearestneighbor (kNN) classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Among them, the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>ve adopt the model-based learning strategy, whereas the last one (i.e., kNN) uses the instance-based learning strategy. With the model-based learning strategy, a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique constructs a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model (e.g., a decision tree, a set of decision rules, a trained neural network, Bayesian probability rules, or a SVM model) from a set of training instances before receiving new instances to classify. The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model induced is then used for classifying future unclassi-<sup>fi</sup>ed instances. In contrast, the instance-based learning strategy used by the kNN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique assumes instances that are close together, in some similarity metric, tend to belong to the same decision class [12,13]. Toward this end, given an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instance, training instances that are closer to the target instance are given more weights. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, k neighbors nearest to the target instance are selected. Accordingly, the kNN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique combines the known decision classes of the k nearest neighbors (e.g., using the voting mechanism) and predicts the decision class for the target instance.

## 2.1. Model-based learning strategy for time-series classification

When adopting the model-based learning strategy to a time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem, in order to preserve trends in its time-series sequences during the induction and prediction process, each timeseries attribute V needs to be appropriately transformed into a set of atomic attributes [37]. A plausible approach, extended from the sequential classi<sup>fi</sup>er that constructs a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model for establishing decision classes with sequences of actions [24], is to <sup>fi</sup>rst discover representative time-series sequences or frequently occurring subsequences from the time-series sequences of V across all training instances. The discovered representative sequences or frequently occurring subsequences are then assessed by their capability of differentiating decision classes using such metrics as $x ^ { 2 } .$ -statistic and entropy function. This process, commonly referred to as feature selection, has important implications for improving classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. As a result, the representative sequences or frequently occurring subsequences selected form a representation space (i.e., new input attributes) and are used to represent the time-series attribute V for each training instance. After all time-series attributes are processed accordingly, any existing model-based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique can be adopted to induce a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model for the target time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem.

For example, assume a dichotomous classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem (with decision classes of Y and N) involves only one time-series attribute as its input attribute. ## 2.2. Instance-based learning strategy for time-series classification

To predict the decision class for an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instance described by a set of time-series attributes, the instance-based learning strategy measures the similarity of the target instance and each of the training instances based on a time-series similarity measure. Upon the selection of k neighbors that are nearest to the target instance, a decision combination method that combines the known decision classes of the k nearest neighbors is then applied to derive the overall classi<sup>fi</sup>cation prediction for the target instance. Thus, the use of the instancebased learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation requires two major components: a time-series similarity measure and a decision combination method. In fact, when using the instance-based learning strategy, the only distinction between time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and non-time-series one lies in their similarity measures. The required decision combination method is identical for both time-series and non-time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems.

As discussed, the model-based learning strategy for addressing the time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation requirements involves three complicated tasks, including 1) discovery of representative time-series sequences or frequently occurring subsequences, 2) feature selection (i.e., selecting representative sequences with high discriminating power), and 3) instance representation (i.e., representing each training instance with the set of representative sequences selected previously). Apparently, the instance-based learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is far less complicated than the model-based strategy. Such advantage becomes more considerable when the number of time-series attributes involved in a time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem increases. Thus, in this study, we choose the instance-based (i.e., k-nearest-neighbor classi<sup>fi</sup>cation) over the model-based as the underlying learning strategy for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems.

## 3. Development of kNN-based time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique

As mentioned, the k-nearest-neighbor-based time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation (kNN-TSC) technique involves two major components: a time-series similarity measure and a decision combination method. We will detail the design of each component in the following subsections.

## 3.1. Time-series similarity measure in kNN-TSC

Previous research on similarity measures for time-series sequences can broadly be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three categories: shape search approach [3,34], Discrete Fourier transform (DFT) approach [1,14], and subsequence matching approach [2]. The shape search approach does not compare time-series sequences directly. Rather, it transforms each time-series sequence into a series of symbols, each of which represents a possible transition type. For example, Agrawal et al. [3] de<sup>fi</sup>ne various symbols to represent different types of transitions $( \mathsf { e . g . , \Psi ^ { u } p } ^ { \prime \prime }$ for a slightly increasing transition, $\ " \mathrm { U p } ^ { \prime \prime }$ for a highly increasing transition, “appears” for a transition from a zero value to a non-zero value, “zero” for a transition whose initial and <sup>fi</sup>nal values being zero, etc.). Thus, a normalized sequence $S = ( 0 , 0 , 0 . 0 2 , 0 . 1 7 , 0 . 3 5 , 0 . 9 0 , 0 . 7 5 )$ corresponds to a transition sequence $T = \left( \mathrm { z e r o } \right.$ , appears, up, up, Up, down). Accordingly, a string comparison technique is employed to estimate the similarity between two transition sequences. The shape search approach is capable of ignoring outliers in sequences when assessing their similarities. For example, a transition sequence (zero, appears, Up, Up) is considered to be similar to another sequence (zero, appears, $\mathrm { U p , }$ down, Up) if the fourth transition in the second sequence (i.e., down) is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an outlier and, thus, ignored. However, transforming a time-series sequence into a transition sequence involves a discretization process and may result in information loss. Increasing the variety of transitional symbols will lessen the information loss problem; however, string comparison will become more complex.

Agrawal et al. [1] propose the Discrete Fourier transform (DFT) method for similarity matching of time-series sequences, assuming that all sequences are of the same length [1]. This method transforms each time-series sequence to the frequency domain using the Discrete Fourier transform. Subsequently, the <sup>fi</sup>rst few frequencies of each time-series sequence are used to measure the similarity of two sequences. Although the original DFT method has been extended to allow similarity matching of data sequences with different lengths [14], this approach still incurs several limitations [2]. First, because the DFT approach commonly employs the Euclidean distance function for measuring the similarity of two time-series sequences, it is susceptible to outliers in time-series sequences. Second, the DFT approach does not have the provision for identifying and excluding these outliers as gaps in time-series sequences when measuring their similarities. Third, the amplitude scaling and offset translation problems are not addressed by the DFT approach.

In response to the discussed limitations of the DFT approach, Agrawal et al. [2] propose a time-series similarity model, based on the subsequence matching approach, which allows non-matching gaps, amplitude scaling, and offset translation [2]. In their model, the similarity of two time-series sequences is measured by the fraction of the length of nonoverlapping time-ordered pairs of similar subsequences to the total length of the two sequences. Two subsequences are considered similar if one is enclosed within an envelope of a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed width drawn around the other. By matching subsequences rather than sequences themselves, some portions of sequences that are considered as outliers or gaps can be left out in the matching process, and the matching subsequences need not be aligned along the time axis. In addition, the scale and offset of the two sequences can be adjusted appropriately before determining the subsequences of one sequence that match the subsequences of the other sequence. Due to its capability in addressing outliers, amplitude scaling, and offset translation in time-series sequences, we adopt the subsequence matching method proposed by Agrawal et al. [2] for measuring similarities of time-series sequences in our proposed kNN-TSC technique. In the following, we provide a summary of the subsequence matching method [2].

## 3.2. Decision combination methods in kNN-TSC

Using the similarity measure described previously, the training instances nearest to an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instance (i.e., instance whose decision class is to be predicted) are selected as the nearest neighbors of the target instance. Subsequently, a decision combination method that combines the known decision classes of the k nearest neighbors is needed for deriving the overall prediction for the target instance. Several decision combination methods have been proposed in the literature [36], including voting, weighted voting, and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods. The voting method selects the k nearest neighbors for the unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instance $o _ { p }$ and then assigns the decision class for $o _ { p }$ as the majority decision class of the k nearest neighbors. The weighted voting method assumes that the neighbors that are more similar to $o _ { p }$ carry more weights on their votes. In this vein, the weighted voting method calculates the sum of similarity $\left( \tan O p \right)$ of the k nearest neighbors that belong to each decision class and assigns $o _ { p }$ to the decision class that receives the highest sum of similarity (i.e., weighted votes) from the k nearest neighbors.

In real-world classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis applications, the distribution of decision classes may be asymmetric in training instances. Such asymmetric class-distribution may also be encountered in timeseries classi<sup>fi</sup>cation applications. The aforementioned decision combination methods (i.e., voting and weighted voting) typically used by the kNN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation generally are incapable of dealing with applications with asymmetric class-distribution because none or only very few of the k nearest neighbors will be selected from the minority decision class and, thus, will lead to decision predictions favoring the majority decision class. Yang et al. [36] propose a decision combination method (referred to as the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method in this study) to address the discussed class asymmetry problem. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, instead of selecting k nearest neighbors for an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed instance $o _ { p } ,$ the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method requires a prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed number of nearest neighbors for each decision class. For a dichotomous classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation problem (assume the two decision classes are $C _ { 1 }$ and $C _ { 2 } ) ,$ , this method selects $k _ { 1 }$ nearest neighbors from the training instances pertaining to $C _ { 1 }$ and $k _ { 2 }$ nearest neighbors from those of $C _ { 2 } .$ Accordingly, the average similarity (to $o _ { p } )$ of the $k _ { 1 }$ nearest neighbors and that of the $k _ { 2 }$ nearest neighbors are derived independently for the two decision classes, respectively. The strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method then assigns $o _ { p }$ to the decision class that receives a greater average similarity than the other class does.

In this study, we adopt and implement the voting, weighted voting, and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods as alternative decision combination methods for the proposed kNN-TSC technique. We will empirically compare the effects of the three decision combination methods on kNN-TSC's classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness in the next section.

## 4. Empirical evaluation

In this study, we use churn prediction of the mobile telecommunications industry to empirically evaluate our proposed kNN-TSC technique. In the following, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the application background and the data preparation process, followed by the evaluation design and discussions of some important evaluation results.

## 4.1. Overview of application background and preparation of data sets

Due to deregulation and globalization, telecommunications service market becomes more competitive than ever [17,21]. In this everincreasing competitive marketplace, subscriber churning becomes a challenging issue faced by all mobile telecommunications providers around the world. Broadly, customer churning in general and subscriber churning in speci<sup>fi</sup>c can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two types, including nonvoluntary and voluntary [18]. Non-voluntary churners refer to those subscribers whose services are revoked by the mobile telecommunications provider, because of their abuse or nonpayment of services. Voluntary churn happens when a subscriber makes a conscious decision to terminate his/her service with the provider and can be either incidental or deliberate. Incidental churn occurs when changes in subscribers' circumstances (e.g., a move to a different geographical location where the provider's service is unavailable) prevent them from further requiring the provided service, whereas deliberate churn occurs when a subscriber decides to move to competing mobile telecommunications carriers for better rates/services or for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., receiving the latest cellular phone) of signing up with new carriers. Prior studies on churn prediction and management mainly concentrate on voluntary churns [6,32], mainly because they are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify and have stronger <sup>fi</sup>nancial and competitive implications to mobile telecommunications providers.

The mobile telecommunications company investigated in this study is one of the largest providers in Taiwan. Although its average churn rate was below the industry average in Taiwan, the company still experiences a churn rate of 1.5% to 2% monthly. Thus, it is essential for the mobile telecommunications provider to build a churn prediction model and develop effective churn management and customer retention policies accordingly.

A prior research suggests that contractual data (e.g., length of services, payment type and contract type) and call patterns (aggregated from call records) of subscribers are useful for churn prediction [32]. Because the purpose of our evaluation is to investigate classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness of the proposed kNN-TSC technique, we incorporate as input attributes only subscribers' call patterns, because they are timeseries sequences. Three measures frequently used by the telecommunications industry to describe the call patterns of a subscriber include:

1. Minutes of use (MOU): MOU refers to the total number of minutes of the outgoing calls made by the subscriber over a speci<sup>fi</sup>c period.

2. Frequency of use (FOU): FOU is the total number of outgoing calls made by the subscriber over a speci<sup>fi</sup>c period.

3. Sphere of in<sup>fl</sup>uence (SOI): SOI captures the in<sup>fl</sup>uence power of the subscriber and is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the total number of distinct receivers contacted by the subscriber over a speci<sup>fi</sup>c period.

In this study, call records to be used for deriving call patterns of subscribers were collected from a southern Taiwan branch of the mobile telecommunications provider investigated. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the call records made over four months were obtained for evaluation purposes. We excluded from this evaluation study those subscribers whose mobile services were terminated by the provider during this data collection period because their payments were delinquent (i.e., non-voluntary churners). As a result, the subscriber base consists of around 114,000 subscribers; 4500 of them voluntarily disconnected their mobile services with the provider during this data collection period.

We follow the model proposed by Wei and Chiu [32] to determine “eligible” subscribers and to establish an appropriate data set for churn prediction purpose. As we illustrate in Fig. 4, a prediction period (P) is used to specify a speci<sup>fi</sup>c time period for determining the churn status of subscribers. A subscriber disconnecting his/her mobile service during P is considered a churner. If, at the end of P, a subscriber still has service with the investigated provider, he/she will be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a non-churner. Any subscriber discontinuing his/her cellular phone service before P will be excluded from this evaluation data set for the particular P. A retention period (R) is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed immediately prior to P for customer retention endeavors and, thus, the call records during R will not be used for churn-prediction purpose. Prior to R, an observation period (T) is designated and the call records made in T are employed for generating the time-series call patterns (measured by MOU, FOU and SOI) of subscribers. Any subscriber whose contract started no earlier than the beginning of T is excluded from this evaluation data set. That is, given a data set prepared in the described manner, the call patterns of eligible subscribers in the observation period T are used as time-series input attributes for predicting their churn status in the prediction period P.

Adopting the experimental results suggested by Wei and Chiu [32], we use 7 days as the length for the prediction period, 14 days as the length for the retention period, and 30 days as the length for the observation period, to generate data sets for evaluation. Furthermore, the 30-day observation period is divided into n sub-periods (e.g., 15) of equal duration. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, each subscriber is described by three time-series attributes (i.e., MOU, FOU and SOI), each of which is a sequence of n values of the concerning call pattern measure.

## 4.2. Evaluation procedure and criteria

Given a randomly determined prediction period, instead of using all eligible subscribers, 800 subscribers are sampled randomly and included in a data set. This data size is deemed suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and can result in reliable performance estimates of a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique investigated because the number of time-series attributes involved is not considerable. Moreover, when generating a data set, we intend to control the ratio of churners and non-churners as 1:x (where x≥1) in order to examine the effects of class asymmetry on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness of the proposed kNN-TSC technique. We then randomly and evenly divide each data set into two data subsets, one for training purpose and the other for testing purpose. Thus, two trials of evaluation will be performed on a given data set. Furthermore, to avoid sampling bias, <sup>fi</sup>fteen data sets using randomly selected prediction periods are generated. As a result, a total of 30 trials are conducted, and the overall performance is obtained by averaging the performance attained in each of the 30 trials.

In this study, the performance of a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis tech nique for churn prediction is measured by miss and false alarm rates. The miss rate is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the percentage of that a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique fails to detect the churners, whereas the false alarm rate is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the percentage of the non-churners that are incorrectly predicted as churners by a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique. When we consider churners pertaining to the positive class and non-churners to the negative class, the miss rate essentially is the false negative rate and the false alarm rate corresponds to the false positive rate. Conceivably, a high false alarm rate increases unnecessary cost for retaining loyal subscribers, while a high miss rate increases opportunity cost of not retaining at-risk subscribers. Because the cost of retaining a subscriber is much lower than that of acquiring a new subscriber in the telecommunications industry, reducing opportunity cost is clearly more important than lowering unnecessary retention cost. In this light, a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis technique should aim at achieving the lowest attainable miss rate while maintaining false alarm rate at an acceptable level.

## 4.4. Parameter tuning experiments

Because three time-series attributes (i.e., MOU, FOU, and SOI) are involved in this target time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation application, the similarity between two subscribers is measured by averaging the similarities of the three time-series sequences describing the call patterns of the two subscribers. We follow the evaluation procedure described in Section 4.2 to generate <sup>fi</sup>fteen data sets for parameter tuning purpose and conduct a total of 30 trials for each parameter tuning experiment. For each data set created for parameter tuning purpose, the ratio between churners and non-churners is set as 1:2 and the number of sub-periods (i.e., n) for the 30-day observation period is set as 15.

The use of the proposed kNN-TSC technique requires the proper tuning of the following parameters: w (window size), γ (gap size), ε (envelop width), θ (maximal scaling difference), and number of nearest neighbors (i.e., k for the voting and weighted voting methods, and $k _ { 1 }$ and $k _ { 2 }$ for the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method). To reduce the computational demand for the tuning experiments and avoid over-tuning our proposed kNN-TSC technique, we tune these parameters sequentially rather than exploring all possible value combinations of these parameters. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when we conduct the tuning experiment for the <sup>fi</sup>rst parameter, we set the remaining parameters to their default values. For the second tuning experiment $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ for the second parameter), we adopt the value determined in the <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment for the <sup>fi</sup>rst parameter and employ the default values for the third to the last parameter. We repeat this tuning process sequentially until we carry out all tuning experiments. Table 1 summarizes the parameters of kNN-TSC and their default values employed in our parameter tuning experiments.

## 4.4.1. Effects of window size (w)

We <sup>fi</sup>rst investigate the effects of window size (w), ranging from 3 to 10 at increments of 1, while setting the remaining parameters to their default values (as Table 1 speci<sup>fi</sup>es). As Fig. 5 illustrates, the increase of w generally results in a decrease of miss rate but an increase of false alarm rate across the three decision combination methods examined. As we mentioned previously, churn prediction should aim at minimizing miss rate while maintaining an acceptable false alarm rate. The false alarm rate of the voting method increases rapidly as w expands from 4 to 5 and reaches 100% when w is greater than 5. The miss rate of the voting method exhibits an opposite trend; that is, it deteriorates swiftly as w increases from 4 to 5 and remains at the 0% level afterwards. Therefore, setting w to 4 appears to be appropriate for the voting method of the kNN-TSC technique. For the weighted voting and the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods, an increase of w from 3 to 6 has a marginal effect on its false alarm and miss rates. Any further expansion of w signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases its false alarm rate. In the range between 3 and 6, the weighted voting and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods attain the lowest miss rate when setting w to 4 and $5 ,$ respectively. Accordingly, we adopt 4 and 5 for w for the weighted voting and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods, respectively.

Based on the appropriate window size selected previously for each decision combination method of the kNN-TSC technique, we then examine different gap sizes (γ), ranging from 2 to 8 at increments of 1. ## 4.4.3. Effects of envelope width (ε)

We further investigate the effects of envelop width (ε) on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness of kNN-TSC. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine various envelope widths, ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 at increments of 0.1. As Fig. 6 illustrates, as ε expands from 0.1 to 0.9, the resulting miss rate increases while its respective false alarm rate decreases for all decision combination methods investigated. The increase of ε will increase the number of ε-similar window pairs between two timeseries sequences; thus, increasing the similarity of two time-series sequences. Because in our tuning data sets, the number of non-churners is two times of that of churners, such over-estimated similarity resulting from a larger ε will increase the number of non-churners being selected as nearest neighbors of an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed subscriber and thus will favor the prediction of a non-churner. As a result, the increase of ε results in an increase of miss rate and a decrease of false alarm rate. As we also show in Fig. 6, the miss and false alarm rates become relatively stable when ε is greater than 0.5 for each decision combination method investigated. Considering the tradeoff between miss and false alarm rates, we decide on ε of 0.3, 0.1, and 0.2 for the voting, weighted voting, and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods, respectively.

## 4.4.4. Effects of maximal scaling difference (θ)

Maximal scaling difference (θ) is used as one of the criteria for determining whether two similar windows (or subsequences) can be stitched to form a larger subsequence. Various values for θ, ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 at increments of 0.1, are investigated for each decision combination method. Our tuning results suggest that, as with the effects of $\varepsilon ,$ an increase of θ increases the similarity of two time-series sequences and, hence, will favor the prediction of being a non-churner for an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed subscriber. That is, as θ grows from 0.1 to 0.9, the resulting miss rate increases while its respective false alarm rate decreases for all decision combination methods investigated. Again, trading off between miss and false alarm rates, 0.2, 0.1 and 0.1 are considered appropriate for θ for the voting, weighted voting, and strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average methods, respectively.

## 4.5. Comparative evaluation

Using the parameter values experimentally determined previously, the proposed kNN-TSC technique is evaluated using the statisticaltransformation-based approach (with C4.5 as its underlying induction method) as the performance benchmark. As mentioned, additional <sup>fi</sup>fteen data sets are randomly generated and used for this comparative evaluation. For each data set, the ratio between churners and non-churners is set as 1:2 and the 30-day observation period is divided into different numbers of sub-periods of equal duration. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate two different numbers of sub-periods: 10 and 15.

As Table 2 shows, when the number of sub-periods is 10, the proposed kNN-TSC technique with either the voting or the weighted voting method is comparable to the benchmark technique (i.e., the statistical-transformation-based approach) in miss rate, but achieves much lower false alarm rates than the benchmark technique does. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the miss and false alarm rates achieved by the voting method are 0.06% and 6.16% lower than those of the benchmark technique, respectively. For the weighted voting method, its miss rate is slightly higher (0.79%) than that of the benchmark technique, but its false alarm rate is substantially lower (9.42%) than that of the benchmark technique. When adopting the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method, the kNN-TSC technique records the lowest miss rate among all the techniques investigated. This is because the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method deliberately favors the minority decision class (i.e., churners) and thus can detect more churners than the other two decision combination methods. Due to this tendency, the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method results in the highest false alarm rate among all the techniques examined. In particular, the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method outperforms the benchmark technique by 7.95% in miss rate, but underperforms by 6.62% in false alarm rate.

When the number of sub-periods increases to 15, both the voting and weighted voting methods of the proposed kNN-TSC technique outperform the benchmark technique. Particularly, the miss and false alarm rates of the voting method are substantially lower than those attained by the benchmark technique, recording the performance differentials of 4.39% and 5.46%, respectively. Similarly, the miss and false alarm rates of the weighted voting method are 6.51% and 2.47% lower than those of the benchmark technique. The strati-<sup>fi</sup>ed average method continues to achieve the lowest miss rate (34.83%) among all investigated techniques. Although the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method still results in the highest false alarm rate (31.04%), the gap to the benchmark technique reduces from 6.62% to 2.65%.

In addition, when the number of sub-periods increases, the miss rate achieved by the three decision combination methods of our proposed kNN-TSC technique improves. The increase of sub-periods (i.e., data points) likely improves the accuracy of similarity estimation between two time-series sequences. Because our data sets contain more non-churners than churners, more accurate time-series similarity estimation will help include additional churners from a training data set as the nearest neighbors of an unclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed subscriber who in fact is a churner, and thereby will improve the miss rate of the kNN-TSC technique. Overall, our empirical evaluation results suggest that the proposed kNN-TSC technique generally is superior to the benchmark technique across different numbers of sub-periods investigated.

Overall, our evaluation results highlight the capability of the proposed kNN-TSC technique (with the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method for decision combination) to address the asymmetric class-distribution problem in timeseries classi<sup>fi</sup>cation applications. Our results also suggest that the weighted voting method is appealing for training data sets with a “balanced” class distribution (i.e., 1:1). For training data sets with asymmetric class-distribution, the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method represents the best approach for the proposed kNN-TSC technique.

## 5. Conclusions and future research directions

Existing research dealing with time-series data mainly concentrates on providing the similarity search capability over a set of time-series sequences. However, use of time-series data may go beyond similarity search. Many interesting applications involve predictions based on a time-series sequence or a set of time-series sequences, referred to as time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. In this study, we propose the kNN-based time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (kNN-TSC) technique, on the basis of the nearest neighbor classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach. Using churn prediction of the mobile telecommunications industry as an evaluation application, our empirical results show that the proposed kNN-TSC technique achieves better performance (measured by miss and false alarm rates) than the traditional statistical-transformation-based approach does. Moreover, with the use of the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method for decision combination, the proposed kNN-TSC technique can effectively handle the asymmetric class-distribution problem.

This study thus makes several contributions to data mining (or speci<sup>fi</sup>cally classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis) research and practices. First, we highlight the importance of time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems and identify the limitations of current classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analysis research. In response, we propose a novel approach to address time-series classi-<sup>fi</sup>cation problems and develop the kNN-TSC technique on the basis of the k-nearest-neighbor classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approach. Our comparative evaluation results shed light on the viability and applicability of the proposed kNN-TSC technique and suggest its relative effectiveness compared with the traditional statistical-transformation-based approach that is commonly used in previous research. Second, we formulate two learning strategies for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (namely model-based and instance-based learning strategies for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation). These two learning strategies provide a foundation to future development of time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques. Third, although we use churn prediction for our empirical evaluation purposes, the proposed technique can be applied to support an array of applications, including fraud detection and intrusion detection, in which time-series input attributes are involved.

Our study also offers several managerial implications to our evaluation application (i.e., churn prediction of the mobile telecommunications industry). First, prior research on churn prediction of the telecommunications industry mainly employs demographics, contractual data, and/or call summaries (e.g., average call duration, number of outgoing calls) aggregated from call details as input attributes. Our study highlights the applicability of using time-series call patterns for churn prediction, without involving subscribers' demographics and contractual data as input attributes. Hence, if collecting comprehensive subscriber demographics is too costly, our proposed technique represents a viable churn prediction solution to mobile telecommunications providers. Second, this study demonstrates the utility of time-series call patterns, described by MOU, FOU, and SOI, for churn prediction. Mobile telecommunications providers should devote their efforts to develop additional measures for pro<sup>fi</sup>ling the call patterns of subscribers and incorporate such measures to further improve the effectiveness of churn prediction by our proposed kNN-TSC technique.

Several future research directions should be continued along this line of research. First, although our proposed kNN-TSC technique follows the instance-based learning strategy, the model-based learning strategy represents another interesting approach for time-series classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. It would be interesting and desirable to develop a timeseries classi<sup>fi</sup>cation technique on the basis of the model-based learning strategy and empirically compare its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation effectiveness with that achieved by our kNN-TSC technique. Second, the major limitation of the instance-based learning strategy is its inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, as compared to the model-based learning strategy. In the future, we should adopt or develop more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient kNN algorithms to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the proposed kNN-TSC technique. Third, applying and evaluating the proposed kNN-TSC technique in different domains (e.g., fraud detection in telecommunications and <sup>fi</sup>nancial industries and intrusion detection for network security management) represents an interesting direction for future research. Fourth, in this study, we employ the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method to address the asymmetric class-distribution problem. Prior research has proposed several other approaches for handling asymmetric class-distribution, including oversampling, undersampling, cost sensitive, and ensemble approaches [7,23,25,35]. Future research can consider extending our proposed kNN-TSC technique by adopting various approaches to manage asymmetric class-distribution. Fifth, our empirical evaluation examines the degree of class asymmetry (i.e., the ratio between churners and non-churners) only up to 1:7, which does not represent the real-world ratio in the focal evaluation application. Although our evaluation results demonstrate the capability of the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed average method in addressing the asymmetric class-distribution problem, future research can consider conducting additional evaluations that involve higher degrees of class asymmetry. Sixth and <sup>fi</sup>nally, this study focuses on empirical evaluation that involves only time-series attributes. Additional evaluations that cover both time-series and nontime-series attributes will be desirable.
