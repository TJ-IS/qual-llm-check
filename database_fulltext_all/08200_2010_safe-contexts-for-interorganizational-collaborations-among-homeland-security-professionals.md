Abs tra ct: In many domains of increased turbulence and volatility, interorganizational ad hoc collaborations are common. One such domain is homeland security in which security professionals collaborate virtually with individuals outside of their own organizations in response to a security threat. In such a domain, a safe context is needed to ensure that interactions with collaborators not only help to solve the immediate threat but also avoid the improper use by outside parties of information released during these collaborations. We use the heuristic systematic model of information processing to hypothesize that the relationship between different safe context factors and a security professional’s perceptions of collaboration success will be contingent on differences in geographic proximity of the collaborating parties—differences in proximity that are not related to differences in physical face-to-face contact but to differences in social proximity. Our exploratory empirical investigation finds support for the hypothesized interaction effect: safe contexts that require deeper processing are related to higher levels of perceived success when the parties are geographically proximal (with no differences in face-to-face contact), whereas safe contexts that involve heuristic-based processing are related to success when parties are geographically less proximal. Our findings suggest that the utility of safe context factors is contextualized based on the proximity of interacting parties, that geographical proximity’s social space dimension plays a key role independent of differences in physical face-to-face contact, and that, practically, to be successful, ad hoc collaborators should have access to a range of safe context factors, using them in different combinations depending on the proximity of network members.

Key words a nd phras es : dual process theories, geographic proximity, knowledge protection, personal networks, safe contexts, security professionals.

Hom ela nd s ecurity threa ts a re a gra ve concern toda y, requiring collaboration among parties representing different organizations, sectors, and specialties [22, 23, 47, 69, 84, 85]. The highly contextualized nature of security solutions generally requires that the parties not simply exchange information but that they jointly sense-make about the shared information—what we call collaboration [81]. These collaborations tend to be ad hoc and temporary, focused exclusively on the short-term objective of resolving the security threat [28].

Despite the value of collaboration in helping to solve these security threats, reports continue to surface that security professionals fear that the act of collaborating with other parties will create more problems than it solves because of the possibility that collaborating parties may misuse information shared during the collaboration [22, 24, 28, 41, 44, 56]. Extant research has identified a variety of reasons why collaborating interorganizational partners may inappropriately use shared information [3, 11]. These reasons may be further exacerbated by the fact that most security information is shared via electronic means [72]. Collaborations that are successful, then, are those that not only resolve the security threat but do so in a way that ensures that the information shared is not misused.

While a security professional responding to a security threat such as hacking may simply call a friend at the FBI and ask him or her not to share sensitive information with others as he or she helps to resolve the hacking problem, the use of personal friends is rarely sufficient [23]. Security professionals need to collaborate with a range of parties, including openly competitive parties [22], in order to solve a security problem. They may need to collaborate with specialists in a range of domains, such as explosives, civil infrastructure, law enforcement, drugs, immigration, and network engineering. They may need to collaboratively exchange information with others playing key roles in the security event. For example, during large-venue events, a security professional responsible for the safety of a controversial politician may need to collaboratively exchange information with the event coordinator, security professionals employed by the venue, and the facility manager to determine the least compromising ingress, egress, and seating for the politician. None of these parties are likely to be friends.

As such, when a security threat occurs, security professionals often turn to members of their personal networks that go far beyond their personal friends [28, 32, 56, 77]. The parties in such a network will not be bound by any formal contractual arrangement designed and approved by senior management, as these relationships are bilateral, individual, and extraorganizational [28, 56]. Consequently, mixed motives may be a serious concern. As examples, a federal agent’s primary motive is to investigate a potential crime even if the investigation disrupts a company’s business, and a competitor firm’s primary profit motive may lead the firm’s security professional to use information about a security threat experienced by another firm to improve his or her own firm’s reputation at the expense of the competitor firm [39, 61]. Because security professionals often face a wide variety of threats, personal networks are likely to include individuals with whom the professional has not as yet collaborated and with whom collaborations may not necessarily recur often [28, 82]. Not only do these conditions inhibit the development of interpersonal trust [51], but they engender the possibility of incidents in which information is inappropriately used even within the personal network [56].

The interorganizational literature (e.g., [3, 29, 38, 43, 63, 65, 83]) has demonstrated that organizations resort to formal processes and reporting arrangements as well as informal processes [74] to engender a safe context in which an individual can interact with others while protected from, or minimizing the possibility of, information misuse by collaborators [52]. While previous research has suggested the value of safe context factors for collaboration success when there are substantial concerns about information disclosure, we theorize that such effects are not universally positive and may differentially affect the perceived success of the collaboration depending on factors affecting individuals’ cognitive processing capabilities. Therefore, we ask the research question:

RQ: When a security professional enters into a collaboration with members of a personal network, what is the relationship between safe context factors and perceived success of that collaboration?

Our paper offers a sociocognitive theory to predict that different safe context factors may have different relationships with perceived success of collaborations. In particular, drawing on dual process theories of cognitive processing, we hypothesize that the ability and motivation of an individual to engage in deeper cognitive processing during the ad hoc collaboration (an ability and motivation that becomes salient when the collaborating parties are physically more proximal) moderates the positive effect of safe context factors on perceived collaboration success. Our focus on micro-forces that facilitate collaboration success among security professionals complements the extant information systems (IS) security literature that has focused on the effectiveness of formal policies at the national and firm level [69, 85] as well as the economics of security detection technologies [84] and other countermeasures [47].

Our theoretical explanation may also generalize to other interorganizational collaborations in which sensitive information is shared, particularly via electronic channels (e.g., [73]). The interorganizational relations literature describes numerous incidents of collaboration between and within firms with harmful consequences (e.g., [7, 42, 48, 52, 60, 63]). Our paper suggests a more nuanced view of interorganizational collaborations involving sensitive information than what currently exists in the literature. Next, we develop our theory and hypotheses.

## Safe Contexts

Collaborations in risky environments tend to rely on what some have called a managed dialogue with other parties to ensure that sensitive information, if shared, is properly used [76]. According to Lewicki et al. [52], this managed dialogue involves the parties’ exercising preemption in the way they work together—that is, devoting cognitive or physical resources to monitoring or attending to potential vulnerabilities. Preemptions set up a secure social area for the interactions to take place by constraining, compartmentalizing, or regulating behavior during the interaction.

Building on this preemption notion, we advance three safe context factors for interorganizational collaborations that can be applied to the individual security professional level. These factors help to protect against inappropriate information release and, as such, allow security professionals to collaborate in a way that successfully resolves the threat. The three safe context factors that we identify are as follows: (1) degree to which the security professional interdependently relies on others to solve the security threat, (2) rules promoted by the company about what information can be released to external parties, and (3) selection of different network members with which to collaborate for each new threat event. These factors are described in turn.

## Interdependence

Task interdependence refers to the job requirement to interact with others in a reciprocal manner. Task interdependence has been identified as a safe context factor in interorganizational collaborations involving sensitive information [52, 59, 63]. For example, in a study of competing furniture manufacturers, the manufacturers refused to share sensitive information about suppliers that would have reduced their collective costs until they became convinced of the interdependencies between them [59]. An individual who feels interdependent with others is more likely to seek out common goals that require the participation of both parties. These common goals then help to protect against inappropriate information release because harming one party ends up harming the other party [52]. For example, in one incident, a security professional confronted a situation in which an employee left the company for a competitor firm with sensitive information. The security professional proceeded to resolve this situation by first establishing interdependence with the competing firm that hired the employee by convincing the security professional at the competing firm that they needed each other to solve the problem: he or she knew what the sensitive information looked like, and the competing firm, not wanting to suffer reputation and financial losses due to a lawsuit, knew where the sensitive information might be located and had the resources to remove it. Having established the task interdependence, the two professionals engaged in a collaboration that resulted in the successful removal of the sensitive information without having either company be harmed publicly.

## Rules Restricting Release

A second safe context factor is the use of rules to limit what types of information are released to external parties [3, 52, 63]. When parties follow rules restricting release, this promotes cautious sharing and creates a safe context for successful collaborations [52]. Restrictive rules validate that some information can be shared; this can promote successful collaboration because the collaborative information sharing can be safely focused and restricted to that information. The lack of rules, or using rules that are too liberal, would not yield a strong signal of what can or cannot be shared [16, 27]. Lewicki et al. [52] use the example of the Boeing 777 joint venture with Japan in which the Boeing engineers protected themselves from vulnerability by enforcing rules that allowed Boeing engineers to only release certain kinds of engineering drawings to the Japanese joint venture engineers. This promoted a more targeted and protected interaction process [76]. As another example, during a collaboration to resolve a bomb threat to a bridge, a contractor responsible for building the bridge may have restrictive rules about what to share publicly about how the bridge is built. Although some information about the bridge will clearly need to be shared so that the potential damage to the bridge and surrounding environment can be estimated and interventions taken, the rules help to alert the collaborating parties that any information sharing about the bridge needs to proceed cautiously. The awareness of rules can be enough to prompt their use and deter inappropriate disclosure [13].

## Selection of Different Collaborators

Finally, the use of different partners can create a safe context for collaborating with network members [61]. In the outsourcing literature, the value of having a number of partners to avoid strategic opportunism has been proposed [75] and demonstrated [3]. Using different collaborators for different tasks provides insurance that no one vendor sees enough of the client’s proprietary environment at any one time to understand it fully and exploit the understanding opportunistically [75]. This mechanism extends beyond outsourcing to other domains in which sensitive information disclosure is of concern [28, 52]. Using different collaborators allows the individual to feel protected from questionable behavior by a network member because the individual can more easily shift to alternative collaborators [61]. Also, by using different collaborators for each threat event, there is greater insurance that no other party knows enough about the threat events in which the professional is engaged to fully understand and exploit that knowledge.

This brief review of the literature on safe context factors in interorganizational collaborations offers three safe context factors as examples that help to create an environment in which protections are in place for successful collaborations. However, this literature ignores the individual’s cognitive-processing capabilities. In the situations of rapid, dynamic, and time-pressured interactions that security professionals often face, the individual is easily overwhelmed and cannot deeply cognitively process all the incoming information—whether it relates to the threat event or the possibility of misuse of shared information [23, 77]. Consequently, despite the state of the literature, safe context factors that require more information-processing capacity may not be positively associated with the perceived success of collaborations. By failing to take into account an individual’s cognitive-processing capacity, we argue that this literature has ignored the possibility that these safe context factors do not have equally positive effects on the perceived success of ad hoc interorganizational collaborations. In the next section, we offer a theory that accounts for differences in cognitive-processing capacity.

## Heuristic-Systematic Model of Information Processing

The heuristic-systematic model (HSM) [9, 10] and its variant, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) [67], are a set of long-established sociocognitive theories in the paradigm of dual processing theories. These theories advocate that individuals are often engaged in validity-seeking contexts in which they seek cognitive clarity from information obtained from others as evidence of reality [15, 20]. In the IS literature, cognitive clarity has been conceptualized in a variety of ways, including perceived success of virtual conferences [21], perceived usefulness of e-mails [79], and perceived usefulness of a technology for one’s job performance [6]. Thus, we would expect that cognitive clarity of information obtained during a collaboration will be related to perceptions of the success of the collaboration because the information collaboratively exchanged with the other parties in a successful collaboration is more useful, pertinent, and helpful if not misused.

HSM suggests that people process incoming information using two distinct forms of cognitive processing that can be differentiated by being more or less effortful [14]. Less-effortful ways to process incoming information include “simplifying” strategies, such as assimilating, categorizing, stereotyping, and relying on heuristics and schema [62], for example, “don’t take candy from a stranger.” In contrast, a more effortful way to process incoming information is to carefully and systematically scrutinize information related to a message’s content before deciding on the merits of the message, for example, “talk to the stranger to analyze the situation and then decide if you can take candy from him or her.”

People generally strive to limit the amount of cognitive resources expended [17]. The influence of either more or less effortful processing then is determined by the individual’s motivation and ability. When motivation or ability is high, individuals cognitive clarity will be more influenced by the deeper, more effortful cognitive processing strategy. When motivation or ability is low, individuals’ cognitive clarity will be more influenced by the heuristics they apply [10, 62]. Thus, in accordance with HSM, we suggest that the safe context factors may not be equally positively related to the perceived success of the collaboration, because they may require different levels of cognitive processing effort.

## Reconceptualizing Safe Context as Posing Cognitive

Processing Requirements

Using the language of HSM, we suggest that safe context factors may be reconceptualized as invoking the processing of either less-effortful heuristics or more effortful deeper processing in rendering incoming information useful during collaboration. The relationship between safe context factors and perceived collaboration success would then depend on the match between the individual’s motivation and ability to engage in deeper processing and the depth of processing that the safe context factor invokes.

Among the three safe context factors, we suggest that heuristic-based processing forms the basis for two of the safe context factors—use of different collaborators and rules restricting disclosure. A safe context in which different collaborators are used for different threat events can be applied by the individual as a heuristic. As argued in the interorganizational literature above, the use of different partners provides a protection heuristic by ensuring that each party does not receive too much information. Simply following this heuristic allows the individual to assume that the information being collaboratively shared will not be misused, and thus allows the individual to engage in the collaboration feeling protected, leading to perceptions of greater collaboration success. Similarly, the safe context factor in which rules exist (and are followed) for restricting disclosure of particular types of information can be applied by the individual as a heuristic. As argued in the interorganizational literature above, the use of rules restricting information release provides a protection heuristic by ensuring that each party shares only that information that is allowed to be shared, thus allowing the individual to engage in the collaboration feeling protected, leading to perceptions of greater collaboration success. This suggests, then, that when an individual is not motivated to engage in deeper processing, the use of these heuristics will lead to perceptions of greater collaboration success.

The third safe context factor—task interdependence—is likely to invoke deeper cognitive processing than heuristics. In interdependent relationships, risks, benefits, differences, common concerns, and implications for meeting and protecting the needs of each party are discussed as information is obtained and processed during the threat event [48, 59]—issues that involve deeper processing than the application of heuristics. As explained in the interorganizational literature above, the interdependence of the relationship will invoke discussions about common goals that will facilitate feelings of protection. Provided the individual is motivated and able to engage in this deep processing elicited by task interdependence, the individual should obtain a deeper understanding of the commonality of the goals, leading to a higher perceived collaboration success since the individual will have experienced a collaboration effort under a safe context commensurate with the level of cognitive effort expended.

## Conceptualizing Geographic Proximity as Promoting Deep Processing Capabilities

HSM argues that an individual’s motivation and ability to engage in deep processing is determined by the situation [10, 68]. A key situational factor—especially in electronically enabled communications as is typically experienced in homeland security contexts [72]—is the geographic proximity between the collaborators [36, 37, 45]. Geographically proximal professionals are likely to share more local knowledge about the threat [5, 46, 51] and engender greater shared situational knowledge, leading to more accurate attributions, fewer coordination and interpretation errors, and greater common ground [12, 35, 64]. Even when there is little time to collocate and the communication happens electronically—as might be the case due to the time pressure of a security threat—parties that share a geographic region tend to share local media (and thus the latest updated information), geographic reference points (“the tall tower on 5th Avenue”), and tacit knowledge about the local culture and assumptions (e.g., cab drivers in New York City) [5]. This shared situational knowledge may increase the ability of collaborating parties to engage in deeper cognitive processing [78]. More proximity between parties may affect motivation as well. Proximal parties may be more motivated to engage in deeper processing through sharing a common fate [2, 4] and social space [49, 50] in which they are more likely to be influenced by others in that social space compared to less-proximal parties.

## Hypotheses

Depending on the geographic proximity of the collaborating members, we suggest that collaboration success can be achieved in different ways. When the security professional collaborates with others that are less proximal, the security professional will conserve his or her cognitive resources and be influenced by heuristics instead of deeper processing. The heuristics of restrictive rules or use of different parties will provide assurances that information shared will not be misused, and hence collaboration can proceed and yield useful information that in turn increases collaboration success. When security professionals collaborate with others who are geographically proximal, they are likely to be aware of their shared situational knowledge (e.g., both would be concerned about their personal safety and that of their local constituencies) and, in the course of their collaborations, share information that might be considered sensitive but feel protected because of the task interdependence. We offer the following hypothesis, as summarized in Figure 1:

Hypothesis 1: The effect on perceived collaboration success of safe context factors will be moderated by proximity. More specifically,

(a) When an individual draws from a network of less geographically proximal collaborators (versus more-proximal partners), the use of heuristic-based safe context factors will be positively related to perceived collaboration success.

## Research Design

## Research Context

We tes ted our hypothes es on a sam pl e of profes ionals who solve ad hoc problems concerned with homeland security—either for their home organization (such as a security officer), for clients (such as a security consultant), or for the public (such as the FBI, CIA, law enforcement). We were sponsored by the FBI InfraGard program, which is a public-private high-security information-sharing partnership to fight cyber and physical threats to critical infrastructures [32]. Most InfraGard activities function through regional chapters bound to large metroplex areas [26]. As nonmembers, we were not allowed direct access to members in the program. Therefore, intermediaries in two regions solicited the interest of InfraGard members to complete a survey on how they collaborate with others in response to security threats. Initially, we focused on collaborations occurring solely among InfraGard members but quickly learned through initial exploratory interviews and piloting that the individual members had their own lists of contacts to which they turned when they had a problem. Owing to the sensitive nature of the sample, we had no access to the names, e-mail addresses, or other individually identifying information on survey respondents.

Our initial qualitative research included interviewing the InfraGard intermediary at each chapter and ten other informants not affiliated with InfraGard who were part of our personal networks and who were involved in interorganizational collaborative responses to security threats. The interviews were open-ended, lasted from 30 to 90 minutes each, and focused on stories about collaborations the individuals had engaged in to respond to a security event. An illustrative security event was a bomb threat under a bridge in a busy port. In response to this event, the law enforcement officer we interviewed engaged in a several-hour collaboration with representatives from a variety of national, county, and city agencies (such as the Port Authority, Coast Guard, Sheriff’s Department) to determine the validity of the threat while also collaborating with shipping companies to identify possible ways to reroute ships. Details of the stories were held in the strictest confidence, and the stories were helpful in developing the language and contextualization for our measures.

The intermediaries in each region sent out an e-mail to all members of InfraGard in their regions (500 in one region and 120 in the other region) announcing that a Web survey was being administered by an independent university with the knowledge of FBI InfraGard, and that InfraGard hoped to benefit from the survey results. The e-mail indicated that the purpose of the survey was to learn about ad hoc collaborations they engaged in with others outside their organizations to respond to security events; those having engaged in such collaborations were encouraged to respond. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured. A short report of the results was later delivered to the intermediaries, who forwarded it to all InfraGard members as a token of our appreciation and as feedback to the organization and its membership.

A total of 104 individuals completed the Web-based survey, 63 from one region and 41 from the other. This sample size appeared to us initially to represent only a 12 percent response rate from one region and a 34 percent response rate from the other region. We were initially concerned about the low response rates, so we asked our InfraGard intermediaries to call a random sample of nonrespondents to find out reasons for nonresponse. The calls indicated that the population of 500 from one region and 120 from the other region included a large number of people who would not be collaborating with others in response to security events, and thus the population size for computing response rates was initially inflated. For example, included in the population were individuals who were not security professionals who acted on security information (i.e., they only used the listserver to stay informed), or who were not permitted or expected to collaborate with other security professionals outside their organization. Thus, it is difficult to determine the size of the population from which we drew the sample and thus the response rate.

## Survey Procedure

The survey instructions defined the collaborative experiences we were interested in:

We are interested in learning about the experiences you have had interacting with security professionals in other organizations to better understand or confront a security threat. These security professionals might be part of your informal network or part of a formal security community. Your experiences might have involved brief hallway discussions or informal e-mail exchanges, or formal task force assignments. When a security event occurs or new security information arises, you may have found it helpful in the past to find security professionals from other organizations who know something you don’t know, and involve them in a collaboration to better understand the security information or better confront the security risk. We are not talking about simply hiring security professionals but collaborate with them as partners in a problem-solving process.

We pilot tested the instructions with our 12 informants to ensure that we would be directing the respondents to collaborative experiences rather than simple message transmissions. We then directed the respondents to think about the set of their own personal contacts, defined as their “informal personal network or circle of professionals interested in security generally or interested in a specific aspect of security” from which the partners in the collaboration experiences were drawn. The sensitivities in the population constrained us from asking for specific names of personal contacts to be able to conduct a network analysis.

## Measures

Measures for perceived collaboration success, use of different collaborators, and geographic proximity were developed based on the qualitative interviews; other measures were based on the existing literature. All the measures were on a seven-point scale and are presented in Appendix A.

## Perceived Collaboration Success

As with Ferran and Watts [21], our perceived collaboration success scale measures the usefulness of the information acquired during the collaborations by the degree to which the information has helped the individual in understanding or responding to security threats. We developed seven items based on the qualitative interviews. The scale asked respondents to report how frequently their collaboration experiences with others in their network helped them to produce results (such as issue resolution, case investigation, risk analysis) that were of excellent quality, manage their time effectively, meet important deadlines on time, do a good job of meeting their organization’s needs, focus their attention on the most important security issues, be aware of the latest information on security, and be part of coming up with a solution to the problem. A coefficient alpha of 0.92 with a mean of 4.8 (standard deviation [SD] = 1.1) out of 7 (1 = “almost never” to 7 = “nearly always”) was obtained.

## Geographic Proximity

Because we were focused on identifying shared local context, and both regional areas centered around major metropolitan areas, proximity was measured based on driving time, which in a major metropolitan area is a better indicator of being geographically local than physical miles. We asked respondents to report on the percentage of members of their network that were within driving distance.

## Task Interdependence

Using a measure by Javenpaa and Staples [41], respondents were asked to report on the degree to which cross-organizational interdependence was an important part of their security job responsibilities. The three items asked the respondents to report (with a seven-point disagree/agree response scale) on whether their responsibilities required them to talk to staff from other organizations, share information with staff at other organizations, and use information and solutions from other organizations. A coefficient alpha of 0.93 with a mean of 6.0 (SD = 1.3) was obtained. Because we had limited the sample of respondents to those who had previously engaged in ad hoc collaborations, a high average task interdependence was expected. We checked for nonnormality and obtained a normal kurtosis and a skewness to the left (–1.8). We conducted the analysis using both an untransformed variable and the traditional transformation of log(M – X) for left skewness and obtained similar results. Thus, we kept the variable in its original state.

## Use of Different Collaborators

Respondents were asked to report on the extent to which the same or different people had been involved in their collaborative experiences, from 1 = “almost always the same people” to $7 = ^ { 6 6 }$ “almost always different people.” A mean of 3.84 (SD = 2.2) was obtained. We found very low correlations (–0.01 to 0.05) for the confounds by examining the correlations of the use of different collaborators with personal network size, number of years developing the network, and number of years working in the security domain.

## Use of Restrictive Rules

This variable measured (1) whether there were rules in place by respondents’ organizations to restrict interactions with other organizations and (2) whether the respondent adhered to those rules. Using a measure developed by Norman [63] with a sevenpoint agree/disagree response scale, respondents were asked the extent to which there were rules in place restricting interactions in three information areas—security, technical, and marketing—and restrictions on the channels of communication used. A coefficient alpha of 0.83 was obtained. To measure the adherence of the individual to these rules, we found from our exploratory interviews that a direct question would be too sensitive; therefore, instead we weighted restrictiveness of rules with a scale by Heiman and Nickerson [33] measuring an individual’s degree of concern over whether people in other organizations might acquire too much knowledge, not share necessary knowledge with the individual, aggressively protect their knowledge from the individual, and probe the individual for valuable knowledge lying outside the scope of information-sharing agreements. Response options were $1 = { } ^ { 6 6 } \mathrm { I }$ almost never worry” to $7 = { } ^ { 6 6 } \mathrm { I }$ worry all the time.” A coefficient alpha of 0.85 was obtained for the concern measure. The correlation between the concern and restrictiveness measures was 0.29; the two scales were multiplied to account for not only rules being in place but also adherence to those rules.

## Controls

We controlled for background characteristics of the respondent as well as characteristics of the respondent’s personal network. We assessed the percentage of people in the personal network that the respondent had collaborated with in the past (“Past Collaborations”), the number of years the respondent had spent developing his or her personal network (“Years”), total size of the personal network as the number of people in the network (“Size”), and the degree of expertise specialization within the respondent’s set of personal contacts [8, 53], measured using the three-item subscale of degree of specialized knowledge in a group developed by Lewis [53]. A seven-point agree/disagree option was offered.

## Results

## Description of the Sample

Individuals reported ha ving pers onal networks ranging in size from 4 to 500 with a mean of 103. As described in the introduction, these types of personal networks are likely to involve people the individual has not worked with in the past; across the respondents, on average, only 31–45 percent of those in their personal networks were people that the respondents had worked with in the past to jointly solve a problem. They interacted with members of their network relatively infrequently (on average, “a few times a year”). Respondents varied in their backgrounds, including how long they had been developing their personal networks (from 0.5 to 42 years, with a mean of 10 years), whether they were from the private (61 percent) or public sector (39 percent), and whether they were from profit (50 percent) or nonprofit (50 percent) organizations. We tested for t-test differences between respondents from each of the two regions from which the sample was drawn and found no differences in any of the obtained variables. We asked when the last collaboration experience had occurred, with a mean of 4.5 (“sometime in the last six months”). Finally, on a seven-point scale $( 1 = \mathrm { ^ { * } n e v e r ^ { * , } t o } 7 = \mathrm { ^ { * } d a i l y ^ { * , } } )$ , we confirmed that the respondents were more likely to use e-mail to collaborate (mean = 5.8) than face-to-face (mean = 3.2), indicating that electronic communication was an important means for conducting these collaborative activities.

To assess the degree of common method bias, we applied three tests. ## Hypothesis Testing

To test for moderation effects, we followed suggestions by Aiken and West [1] by mean-centering the safe context factors and geographic proximity and then calculating interaction effects of the continuous variable of proximity with safe context factors. We then regressed perceived collaboration success first on control and main effects and then with the interaction effect in the regression model. Finally, we subdivided proximity at the median to create subsamples and tested for direct effects within each subsample. Correlations with the interaction variables are shown in Table 2; the correlations between the interaction effects and main effects were low enough to rule out error due to multicollinearity.

We performed a moderator regression analysis of collaboration success with the control variables entered as Model 1, the direct effects as Model 2, and the interaction effects entered as Model 3. Table 3 presents the results. There is a significant increment in $R ^ { 2 }$ attributed to the interaction effects, over and above the controls and the direct effects, with a total adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.29. All three interaction effects were significant and in the expected direction. That is, there was a positive beta coefficient for interdependence, indicating that when proximity was higher, the effect of interdependence on perceived collaboration success was positive. In contrast, there were significant negative beta coefficients for use of different collaborators and use of restrictive rules, indicating the opposite from interdependence: when proximity was lower, the effect of the safe context factors on perceived collaboration success was positive. To confirm this result, following Aiken and West [1], we conducted a subsample analysis.

To perform the subsample analysis, we subdivided the sample at the median of 4 on the seven-point scale (the response for 4 was 46–60 percent of people in the individual’s network were geographically local). Because there were 13 respondents at the median, assignment of those at the median to either the more-proximal or lessproximal subgroups led to uneven subsample sizes. Therefore, we performed the subsample analysis both ways (where those at the median were assigned to the moreproximal subgroup and then to the less-proximal subgroup). The results were similar both ways. We show the subsample results with respondents at the median assigned to the more-proximal subgroup. Thus, the subsamples consisted of those indicating that 46 percent or more of the people in their networks were within driving distance (assigned to the more-proximal subgroup) and those respondents indicating less than 46 percent were within driving distance (assigned to the less-proximal group). This yielded 57 respondents in the more-proximal group and 47 respondents in the lessproximal group.

## Alternative Plausible Moderation Effects

We checked for alternative plausible moderation effects of the relationship between safe context factors and perceived collaboration success. One possible alternative explanation was that those who collaborated with more-proximal parties had higher task interdependence and used restrictive rules and different collaborators less than those who collaborated with less-proximal parties. There were no significant t-test differences (see Table 2 for means), indicating that both subgroups used all three safe context factors to the same degree. Thus, it is not that one subgroup uses the safe context factors to a greater extent, but that the use of the safe context factors has different effects. Another possible alternative explanation was that task interdependence had a stronger effect on perceived collaboration success for individuals with more-proximal networks because the parties in more-proximal networks feel psychologically safer to share opinions [18, 25]. We measured psychological safety (see Appendix B) using the scale by Edmondson [18] $( \alpha = 0 . 7 7 )$ . T-tests were not significant, indicating that psychological safety is not greater with one safe context factor than another; all safe context factors contribute equally to psychological safety. Another alternative explanation was that task interdependence had a stronger effect because the more-proximal parties felt a higher degree of trust [34]. We measured trust (see Appendix B) using the scale based on Mayer et al. [58] $( \alpha = 0 . 9 0 )$ and found no significant differences, indicating that safe context factors contribute equally to feelings of trust among personal network members. A final explanation was that more-proximal parties engaged in more spontaneous discourse, which would promote deeper cognitive processing [39, 64]. We used the scale of Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak [39] to measure spontaneous discourse $( \alpha = 0 . 9 4 )$ and found no differences, indicating that spontaneous discourse is likely to be observed regardless of the safe context factor used and, thus, is not an explanation for the differences found in the safe context factors.

## Discussion

This pa per is novel yet com pl em enta ry to the current l itera ture (e.g., [22]) in taking a sociocognitive perspective on security professionals engaging in ad hoc collaborations to solve security threats. The creation of a safe context within which professionals can collaborate without their information being misused requires understanding how personal network factors such as geographic proximity moderate the relationship between the safe context factors and the perceived success of collaboration. In this paper, we have used three safe context factors as examples of protection factors that could be applied. There are likely to be others.

The main finding of the study is that different safe context factors, when used primarily through electronic means, have different relationships with the degree to which the individual perceives that collaborations with network members have been successful. These different relationships are explained by the geographic proximity of the collaborators in the personal network. We found a differential interaction effect of the safe context factors with geographic proximity such that the effect on perceived collaboration success of task interdependence was moderated positively by geographic proximity, whereas the safe context factors of using different collaborators and using restrictive rules were moderated negatively by proximity. Thus, in contrast to the existing interorganizational literature on safe contexts, we did not find a universally positive effect on outcomes. Finally, we suggest that this finding is due to cognitive limitations that homeland security professionals experience during time-pressured stressful security events—a consideration that previous interorganizational literature has ignored.

## Limitations

Our findings are tentative because of several limitations. First, we drew our sample from a high-security homeland security population where we could only have one person’s perspective on the collaboration and geographic proximity in his or her network. Thus, we need to be careful to limit inferences to an individual’s perceptions of collaboration success, not to whether all parties to the collaborations felt they had successfully collaborated. Second, our study does not explicitly measure the implicit (generally unmeasured) cognitive processes by which safe context factors affect perceived collaboration success. We are inferring that the moderation effect of geographic proximity is due to the varying effect on an individual’s implicit motivation and ability to engage in deeper versus heuristic processing. Future research should identify ways to measure explicitly these implicit cognitive variables postulated by the theory. Moreover, although we ruled out several alternative explanations for our findings, future research should continue to explore alternative explanations we did not rule out. For example, task interdependence (as positively moderated by proximity) may be related to perceived collaboration success because more complex and useful information is expected and obtained in a collaboration among interdependent parties, whereas the use of different collaborators (as negatively moderated by proximity) may be related to success with less-proximal collaborators because only basic information is expected. Thus, our application of HSM may provide only one explanation for this differential effect of different safe context factors. A third limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the survey, which prevents statements of causation; thus, it may be that successful collaborations create safe contexts, rather than the inverse, which we have postulated.

Fourth, the small sample size, especially at the level of the subsample analysis, suggests that further research should attempt to replicate these findings on a larger sample.

## Theoretical Implications

The literature on interorganizational relationships (e.g., [3, 52, 74]) and interorganizational systems [31, 40] suggests that the need for a safe context is not limited to homeland security professionals. Collaboration with outsourcers requires protection against strategic risk [3, 11]. China-based electronics manufacturing brokers responding to client orders with interorganizational ecosystems and networks must be able to share the knowledge of the client’s needs without giving away the client contact [30]. Firms are increasingly encouraged to be transparent over the Internet with customers to stimulate co-creation between customers and the firm, and yet protect the firm’s core intellectual assets [80]. Individuals engaged in cross-organizational virtual software development teams may need to avoid sharing certain kinds of knowledge that could put them at a competitive disadvantage [19]. Therefore, our findings may have implications for virtual interorganizational collaborations concerned with information release beyond those of security threat conditions [47].

For this broader context of primarily virtual interorganizational collaboration with sensitive information, our study suggests that the use of safe context factors may not always be related to perceived collaboration success. Previous research has generally suggested the value of the three safe context factors we examined. Our findings suggest a more nuanced view. Our application of HSM provides a theoretical framing for judging the value of different safe contexts by suggesting that motivation and ability to engage in deeper processing may affect the value of different safe contexts. This framing offers a reinterpretation of previous findings. For example, McEvily and Zaheer [59] argued in their study of collocated collaboration among competitive furniture makers that task interdependence is an important safe context factor for interorganizational collaborations. The HSM theoretical framing suggests instead that the value of task interdependence in this context may be due to the participants motivation and ability to engage in deeper processing of the interactions with interdependent partners because they are more geographically proximal. If the authors had studied furniture makers that were less proximal, the less-shared situational knowledge (e.g., different local laws and regional customer needs) and less-shared fate due to situationally specific supplier requirements would suggest that the furniture makers might not have perceived their collaborations to be successful; instead, they might have found heuristics such as reputation rankings [71] of the furniture makers to be more instrumental. Thus, our research suggests that the effects of interorganizational safe contexts are more complex than previously considered.

Moreover, because information technology (IT) plays a significant role in security collaborations [72] as well as in other interorganizational collaborations across heterogeneous environments [46], our findings offer several suggestions for future research on collaborations that are primarily virtual. First, our findings suggest that researchers studying parties engaged in primarily virtual interorganizational collaborations should consider the extent to which these collaborations are affected by individual members concerns about sharing. That is, virtual team success may be a function not simply of a willingness to share, but an ability to ensure protection while sharing. Second, researchers should not assume that all safe context factors will be equally effective for the individuals involved. Thus, advocacy of any factor over another needs to be situationally driven. Third, even in virtual conditions, proximity appears to matter; the effect of shared situational knowledge and shared fate in more- and less-proximal settings should be considered.

Given the importance of safe context factors, future research should examine how IT can be designed to help promote safe context factors. For example, IT could provide collaborators with shared virtual workspaces geared not to each specific ad hoc collaboration but to personal networks from which ad hoc collaborations are drawn. Such an IS could provide counters to indicate who in their network the individual has collaborated with previously to facilitate knowing how to apply the safe context heuristic of collaborating with different parties. The IS could inform the individual where members of the network are geographically located to facilitate switching between heuristic-based and systematic-based cognitive processing strategies. Such an IS could potentially help track information usage by other parties in the collaboration, thus providing new ways of facilitating a safe context. More research is needed to explore ways that IT can help individuals protect themselves during collaborations.

While we examined only the role of geographic proximity in affecting the motivation and ability to more deeply process information, there may be other drivers as well. For example, other factors affecting motivation and ability might include the seriousness of the threat event or the individual’s need for social identity or past negative experiences with opportunism. Dual process theories such as HSM were originally developed using tightly controlled experiments in which the only plausible explanation that could be supported by the results is differential cognitive effort. There have been calls for assessing the nature of heuristic and systematic processing in more “ecologically meaningful settings in which several motives are potentially relevant, as well as the factors that may lead perceivers to engage in one form of motivated processing over another” [10, p. 79]. This study addresses this call and demonstrates the viability of using dual process theories to explain phenomena in more ecologically meaningful contexts. We suggest that interorganizational researchers consider in the future such theories that provide a more nuanced understanding of the effect of safe context factors on collaboration success.

Our premise in this paper has been that in a cross-organizational collaboration on security, each individual in the collaboration is fundamentally responsible for the security of his or her own organization, which gives rise to the mixed-motive situation. Thus, the level of analysis in the current paper was the individual. However, we encourage future research to explore alternative levels of analysis. In some crossorganizational mixed-motive situations, superordinate goals may supersede the motives of the individuals or individual organizations, such as in some joint ventures or some large-scale crises, thus allowing the formation of temporary teams [62]. Generalizing our findings to the team level would suggest that the team would need to be able to create a safe context that would be helpful not just to each individual but to the team’s structure and coordination processes. Future research on how teams are able to create such safe contexts is needed.

Practically, our research suggests that successful ad hoc collaborations are more likely when individuals are not restricted to one safe context factor or another. In many organizations today, individuals collaborating in situations involving opportunistic risks are often severely restricted by their management in how they proceed, such as with rules about what can and cannot be released. Such restrictions, however, have predefined what should be shared. In the ever-evolving world of information security, viral interdependencies, dynamic adaptation to new forces, new threat potentials, and new technologies are increasingly changing the nature and content of information that is needed to successfully resolve security threats. Imposing severe information-sharing restrictions will not lead to successful collaborations in such situations. However, opening up the collaborations with no protections against strategic opportunism is problematic as well. Our research suggests a middle ground: provide collaborating individuals with a set of alternative safe context factors to use and guidance about when each is best used.

In conclusion, we show that safe context factors do not have universally positive relationships with perceived collaboration success in ad hoc collaborations where sensitive information is being exchanged. We hope that this study inspires future research to take a more nuanced view of these relationships—a view that considers individuals’ information-processing capabilities.
