## a b s t r a c t

Although most quantitative <sup>fi</sup>nancial data are analyzed using traditional statistical, arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence or data mining techniques, the abundance of online electronic <sup>fi</sup>nancial news articles has opened up new possibilities for intelligent systems that can extract and organize relevant knowledge automatically in a usable format. Most information extraction systems require a hand-built dictionary of templates and thus need continual modi<sup>fi</sup>cation to accommodate new patterns that are observed in the text. In this research, we propose a novel text-based decision support system (DSS) that (i) extracts event sequences from shallow text patterns, and (ii) predicts the likelihood of the occurrence of events using a classi<sup>fi</sup>er-based inference engine. The prediction relies on two major, but complementary, feature sets: adjacent events and a set of information-theoretic functions. In contrast to other approaches, the proposed text-based DSS gives explanatory hypotheses about its predictions from a coalition of intimations learned from the inference engine, while preserving robustness and without indulging in formalism. We investigate more than 2000 <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports with 28,000 sentences. Experiments show that the prediction accuracy of our model outperforms similar statistical models by 7% for the seen data while signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improving the prediction accuracy for the unseen data. Further comparisons substantiate the experimental <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

There has long been a strong interest in applying computational intelligence to the analysis of <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. Such analysis has traditionally concerned forecasting based on past price data. One area of limited success in <sup>fi</sup>nancial prediction comes from textual data [39]. Textual data contain more information than numeric data because the former not only allow us to predict <sup>fi</sup>nancial trends but also provide us with justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the predictions. For example, a news article on a company containing words and phrases such as “shortfall”, “risk of default”, “resignation” gives reason to expect a fall in the company's stock price, even if the company's reported <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>gures appear sound. The current availability of huge volumes of <sup>fi</sup>nancial electronic text has created a pressing need for better knowledge discovery and the construction of applications for managing the knowledge that is extracted. Most of the existing research into <sup>fi</sup>nancial text mining or knowledge discovery from text (KDT) relies on the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned set of keywords. In this approach, a text is usually scanned for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of event template, such as corporate acquisitions. The main goal is to <sup>fi</sup>ll in the values for sets of handcrafted and prede<sup>fi</sup>ned template slots. Consequently, the construction of event extraction templates is a fairly laborious activity. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to design templates that anticipate all of the possible combinations of events or objects of interest that can be described, as well as to cover trivial redescriptions. Given the weakness of the approach and the demand for high-level representations, that is, not just keywords, to take advantage of linguistic knowledge, there has been considerable interest in the development of an automatic means of learning shallow event patterns from text, without indulging in linguistic formalism.

In this article, we propose a novel inference engine for <sup>fi</sup>nancial text sequence prediction that brings together the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of shallow text processing and classi<sup>fi</sup>er-based inferences to produce effective knowledge discovery. Our approach aims to extract key underlying event sequences from <sup>fi</sup>nancial texts and then hypothesize and assess incoming, even new and unseen, event sequences in the prediction. Unlike similar approaches, an inferential mechanism is developed in the engine that can extract event sequences from a collection of relevant texts, and collate the sequences in such a manner that both explicit and implicit information can be tailored to the needs of users. The task we are addressing and the problem of predicting the <sup>fi</sup>nancial event sequence can be stated as follows. Given a corpus of <sup>fi</sup>nancial documents that demonstrate event sequences, we explain how to extract all of the event sequences from the texts and predict the interesting and unseen relationships between them. The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the research that applies linguistic style information to enhance KDT. Section 3 gives an overview of our system and issues regarding text preprocessing, shallow parsing, textual information generalization, and event sequence extraction. Section 4 describes the design of the inference engine for event sequence prediction. A practical boosting algorithm is introduced into the engine to produce a set of prediction rules. Numerous features that characterize the sequences and their latent inter-event relations are captured in the engine. The system prototype is implemented and we conduct a series of experiments to evaluate and compare the engine with the hidden Markov model. Section 5 provides an overview of our experimental design. We also quantify the outcome and give a detailed analysis of the results in our evaluation. Finally, the conclusions and further research directions are presented in Section 6.

## 2. Related work

Knowledge discovery from text (KDT) is not an isolated activity, as it is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by, and in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences, other decision support activities such as business intelligence, text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and information extraction. Schumaker and Chen [34] employ a predictive machine learning approach for <sup>fi</sup>nancial news article analysis that uses several different textual representations. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that their model containing both article terms and stock price performs best in stock price prediction. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that among the textual representations, proper nouns outperform noun phrases which in turn outperform the de facto standard, the bag of words (BOW). The success of noun phrases is attributed to their having less term noise. Coussement and van den Poel [8] introduce a screening mechanism to improve an e-mail complaint-handling strategy. Using a set of linguistic style information as a new type of textual information, the mechanism can differentiate customer complaint e-mails from non-complaint ones. The differences in the linguistic style between the two types of e-mail are also investigated. It is found that special linguistic features, such as the number of words or articles, presence of time words, or use of different verb tenses, are all indicative of a customer complaint. They also demonstrate that incorporating linguistic style features into a conventional e-mail classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model results in an increase in its predictive performance. Lavrenko et al. [23] devise a language model that can characterize some <sup>fi</sup>nancial trends. Associating news stories with forthcoming trends, the model learns that words such as loss, shortfall, and bankruptcy are most likely to precede a downward trend in the stock price, whereas merger, acquisition, and alliance are likely to be followed by an upward trend. This model produces noticeably bette predictions for all trend types than the popular vector-space model. Similarly, Fuller et al. [15] suggest a language model for deception detection in verbal communication. They incorporate different linguistic clues into their text-based decision support system (DSS) for deception detection tasks. Armed with more than 31 different linguistic clues that involve sentence length, content words, temporal ratio, lexical diversity, and verb and word quantities, their DSS prototype has an accuracy level approaching 74%, while professional lie catchers, such as police of<sup>fi</sup>cers or customs of<sup>fi</sup>cers, usually demonstrate the overall accuracy ranging from 49 to 64% [41]. Their experiments also show that an automated text-based DSS can be built and provide value-added results to decision makers. However, the search for a more parsimonious, but appropriate, set of linguistic clues is both vital and desirable for all of the tasks in KDT [1,21].

Recently, many researchers advocate document warehousing to capture complete business intelligence, in response to the hard fact that textual linguistic clues provide an extra dimension in decision making. Document warehouses, unlike traditional document management systems, include extensive semantic information about documents, cross-document feature relations, and document grouping or clustering to provide more accurate and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient access to text-oriented business intelligence [40]. However, few systems have applied machine learning to the task. One of the earliest systems for the acquisition of extraction patterns is the AutoSlog [31]. AutoSlog is a knowledge acquisition tool that uses a training corpus to generate proposed extraction patterns. It learns extraction patterns in the form of domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c semantic case frames that contain a maximum of one slot. The creation of the semantic frames relies on the existence of a partial parser, small lexicon, and small set of general linguistic rules. Similarly, the PALKA system applies a conceptual hierarchy, which is a set of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned keywords that can be used to trigger each pattern, and a semantic class lexicon [22]. To learn extraction patterns, PALKA looks for sentences that contain case frame slots using semantic class information. The conceptual hierarchy is used to control the generalization or specialization of the target case frame slots. Likewise, WHISK requires the syntactic preprocessing of the text and learns extraction patterns in the form of semantic case frames [38]. The triggers for the patterns in WHISK comprise a detailed speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of linguistic context that includes the subject, verb, or object of any of the surrounding constituents. A set of inductive learning techniques, which successively generalize input examples, are derived to learn extraction patterns and their relatively complicated triggering constraints. Given a preprocessed input, WHISK can also be extended to handle semistructured text.

One of the customary steps in extracting information from texts is to use a knowledge base to support the text inferences. Harabagiu and Moldovan [17] address this issue by using WordNet [27] as a commonsense knowledge base and designing relation-driven inference mechanisms that look for common semantic paths from which to draw conclusions. Hearst [19] proposes a domain-independent method for the automatic discovery of semantic relations in unrestricted text collections by searching for corresponding syntactical patterns. Once the basic relations, such as hyponyms and hypernyms, are triggered in WordNet between the texts, common links are built, and the existing patterns in the text are then shared among the others. One of the main advantages of this method is its low cost and the simplicity of the relations. However, it relies heavily on a perfect ontology in which general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c concepts are all included and the semantic relations are well de<sup>fi</sup>ned. Although much progress has been made in KDT, many research issues still need to be resolved. First, although some of the abovementioned models take advantage of different linguistic clues in their DSS, most KDT tasks are forced to depend on impoverished text models such as BOW, when the decisions that they are making ought to be based on the meanings of those words in context. The lack of linguistic clues inevitably induces heavy noise and causes misinterpretation of meaning that results in failure to extract the knowledge [8]. Second, although existing approaches work well when a large amount of pre-annotated information is made available, it is debatable whether the same methods would work for domains with limited annotation, or for a user who is not experienced enough to tag the information. How does one effectively extract knowledge from a text based on minimum training? Last but certainly not least, most of the methods described above address the constituents to be extracted that appear explicitly in the text, without any attempt to invoke the prior knowledge with which they have been associated. For example, the oil-price-surges pattern is associated with the stock-market-dives pattern in <sup>fi</sup>nancial news articles. The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of these implicit contexts as a cause-consequence pair is important in every KDT system. In this article, we attempt to circumvent the handcrafted and massive annotation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial text by using shallow parsing. We also demonstrate an inference engine that can extract knowledge from a collection of relevant <sup>fi</sup>nancial texts, and collate the knowledge in such a manner that both explicit and implicit information can be tailored to the text-based DSS using a classi<sup>fi</sup>er-based inference engine.

## 3. System overview

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present an overview of our system and the relevant methodology used throughout this study. Our text-based DSS includes four major components: text preprocessing, textual information generalization, event sequence extraction, and a classi<sup>fi</sup>erbased inference engine. The system architecture of the DSS is shown in Fig. 1. For an incoming text, after text preprocessing, as described in Section 3.1, every sentence in the text is analyzed. Textual information is then extracted via a shallow parser and semantic roles are assigned to it in the textual information generalization module. In Section 3.2, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe how the parser works and how textual information is extracted. Every piece of textual information may indicate a possible event, and a series of consecutive events signals an event sequence that is decisive for the sequence prediction. Section 3.3 includes a discussion of how event sequences are extracted from the textual information database, and the inference engine is explained in detail in Section 4.

## 3.1. Text preprocessing

The English language usually contains redundancy, and not every piece of English text delivers useful meaning. Sentences tend to include information of unequal importance. One possible way to eliminate redundancy is to use a preprocessing technique to transform the input text into a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned format. Our preprocessing consists of the sequential application of three major components in a pipeline as depicted in Fig. 1. The components include tokenization, part-of-speech (POS) tagging, and morpho-syntactic analysis. The output of preprocessing is textual information that contains the most important information in the sentence and not the irrelevant information. As shown in Fig. 1, the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of preprocessing is carried out by a tokenizer, which segments the input text into sentences and phrases. While punctuation marks, such as periods, may suggest the end of a sentence, they can also, as shown in the sentence (P1) below, signal the end of an abbreviation (e.g., U.K.) or be used in the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of dates, times, initials of names, or e-mail addresses.

The pound fell against the dollar and the euro. Reports showed the U.K. economy slumped the most in two decades and home sales dropped to the lowest level since at least 1978.

$$
\text { The   New   York   Times }\tag{P1}
$$

Similarly, spaces delimit English words and do not necessarily identify name entity boundaries, as in the case of many named entities such as The New York Times. After tokenization, all of the sentences of the text document are tagged with a POS tagger. POS tagging is the task of assigning to each token its corresponding part of speech, that is, its syntactic word category, such as noun, adjective, verb, or adverb.

Different tagsets and paradigms are applied to the task. In this research, we adopt the Brill tagger, which is a transformation-based approach in the sense that a tag is assigned initially to each word and changed using a set of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned rules [3]. The basic rationale of the tagger is that it <sup>fi</sup>rst computes the error score of each candidate rule and then selects best rules with higher scores. The winning candidates are added to the ruleset until no further rule has a score above a given threshold.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal step in text preprocessing is morpho-syntactic analysis, which is used to reduce the number of irrelevant and rare terms in the sentences. Rare terms are the words with occurrence frequency less than 5 times. Irrelevant terms, in a stoplist, usually refer to the words that are poor index terms. They are either insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant or so common that omitting them does not damage the analysis. For example, the words the, and, that, was. They are sometimes called negative dictionary. Even though the stop words may vary from system to system, in this research, we employ a stoplist of 400 words derived from the Brown corpus of 1,014,000 words drawn from a broad range of literature in English [13]. In addition, we also perform stemming and lemmatization in the analysis. Stemming is a process that reduces derived words to their base form and removes all pre<sup>fi</sup>xes and suf<sup>fi</sup>xes for each token. Our dictionary-based stemmer reduces the words predicts, predicted, predicting, and prediction to their root word, predict. Similarly, lemmatization is the process of reducing the morphological variants of the words to their corresponding base form. These two tasks provide the important morphological features of each word. Moreover, only words with informative POS tags are included, simply because these words contain relevant and important information to support the prediction.

## 3.2. Textual information generalization

After the lexical processing in the preprocessing module, further textual information is extracted in the textual information generalization module. In this module, a set of <sup>fi</sup>nite-state rules is applied to the sentences, which yields a sequence of non-overlapping phrases as output. The main component in this module is a shallow head-driven language parser which is the on-going research work of the <sup>fi</sup>rst author [4–6]. The parser provides a shallow but effective sentence chunking and semantic labeling. It extracts all the phrases from the input sentences, without being bogged down into deep semantic parsing and understanding. Basically, our shallow parsing is a heuristic approach that consists of matching certain regular expressions and rules with the POS tags of the input sentences. Parsing begins with the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of every potential phrasal head of the input sentence, which is guided by phrasal patterns registered in the regular expressions. It mainly distinguishes the possible chunks of the sentences. Chunks are non-overlapping and non-recursive. Non-recursive means that the chunks are not embedded within other chunks. The chunks extracted exhibit islands of words that build up syntactic units, which include noun phrases, verb phrases, prepositional phrases, location/position phrases, date/time phrases, and quanti<sup>fi</sup>ers, among others. These units are then categorized. Noun phrases, for example, are categorized based on proper nouns, such as the names of countries, stocks, currencies, organizations, individuals, or places, and further tagged by a named entity lexicon.

Technically speaking, our shallow parser has a chunker and a phrase recognizer. In the chunker, we have explored a new approach that aims at identifying chunk boundaries. The input of the chunker is a POS tag sequence $< x _ { 0 } . . x _ { n } . . . x _ { m } >$ of a sentence, where the POS tag sequence is the output from the text preprocessing module as shown in Fig. 1. Assume $y _ { n }$ be the point of focus between two consecutive POS tags $x _ { n }$ and $x _ { n + 1 }$ . The basic rationale of the chunker is to classify all focus points as either a chunking point or merging point at the relevant level. A focus point $y _ { n }$ is a merging point $\operatorname { i f } x _ { n }$ and $x _ { n + 1 }$ are siblings of the same parent node in the target parse tree. Otherwise, $y _ { n }$ is a chunking point. While the chunker locates the boundaries of chunks, our phrase recognizer predicts the nonterminal syntactic tag and the semantic classes of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed chunks. In our recognizer, we identify the semantic class of the chunks by their lexical heads. The lexical head is the word in the chunk that is grammatically the most important. The idea of a head for each identi<sup>fi</sup>ed chunk dates back to Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld [2]. For example, the head of a phrase, say, low margin product is the word product. It is central to linguistic formalisms such as head-driven phrase structure grammar [28], and has become extremely popular in computational linguistics with the rise of lexicalized grammars. In our implementation, we follow the footsteps of most current systems that rely on a simple set of hand-written rules, such as a practical one for Penn Treebank grammars given in Collins [7] but developed originally by Magerman [25]. A deterministic lookup table based on the label of the internal node and the labels of the children is used to approximate this linguistic notion. In addition, to develop our chunker and phrase recognizer, we adopt the knowledge encoded in a major treebank, namely the English Penn Treebank [26]. The English Penn Treebank which can be obtained from Linguistic Data Consortium (LDC) has a million words of Wall Street Journal material annotated in Treebank II style. All of them are <sup>fi</sup>nancial-related corpus and were handcorrected at least once, and about half were revised and updated by a different annotator. The Penn Treebank has 48 POS tags and 14 syntactic class (SC) tags. The total numbers of words found in the treebank is 1,074,677, with more than 42,500 sentences. The training accuracy of our chunker and the phrase recognizer are 96.3% and 95.7% respectively. As a result, the parser is found to have 85% F-measure in test data. Its performance is close to the present state-of-the-art of 86–89% F-measure. The extracted chunks, together with their headwords, syntactic and semantic information, are stored in a textual information database (TID) as shown in Fig. 1. The information extracted from the two modules produces a tabulated representation of every sentence in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial text.

In the TID, every sentence is broken down into instance-attribute pairs as shown in the rightmost column of the table. The TID, without any language redundancy, provides a quick reference of the sentence information in the form of instance-attribute pairs, without referring back to the piece of text. In this research, an event is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a non-empty instance-attribute pair as in the right-most column in Table 2. In addition, while sentences are the basic unit in the experiment, they usually display some kind of mutual dependence between neighboring parts of a text, thus enabling its readers to make plausible inferences about the underlying meaning. To capture the dependence, our event sequence is constructed from every pair of consecutive sentences within the same paragraph. Each event sequence which is extracted from two consecutive sentences must have at least four events. For example, the two sentences in (P1) will produce six events in one event sequence. In this research, we have to accept that we have not implemented any mechanism in word sense disambiguation. However, different word senses tend to belong to different conceptual classes and they both usually will not appear in the same context [42]. Since all our texts come from the <sup>fi</sup>nancial domain, we take a crude approximation that the ambiguous words, such as pound, which could be sterling-pound or weight-pound, are resolved under the <sup>fi</sup>nancial-related context.

## 3.3. Event sequence extraction

Causality, temporality, and spatiality are intertwined links among all of the possible events in a text when behavioral episodes unfold in sentences. It is known that readers attempt to tie each event or fact encountered to the prior text. They look for temporal and causal relations, especially, to explain the <sup>fl</sup>ow of sentences. For example, a reader will normally interpret “The chairman resigned. The stock price $f e l l ^ { \prime }$ to mean that the <sup>fi</sup>rst event preceded and caused the second. To model the event sequence underlying a text, we <sup>fi</sup>rst assume $U = \{ u _ { 1 } , u _ { 2 } ,$ $\ldots u _ { m } \}$ to be a set of m distinct instances comprising the alphabet, and $A = \{ a _ { 1 } , a _ { 2 } , . . . a _ { n } \}$ to be a set of n distinct attributes describing the possible states of the instances. An event is a non-empty instance-attribute pair $E _ { i } = ( u _ { i } , a _ { i } )$ , and an event sequence S is an ordered list of events and denoted as $: S = \langle E _ { 1 } \to E _ { 2 } \dots \to E _ { r } \rangle$ . For example, the ordered list of events (UK-pound, fall) → (modi<sup>fi</sup>er, dollar & euro) → (UK-economy, slump) . . → (property, down) demonstrates an event sequence S delineated in (P1). In addition, a sequence S is a subsequence of T, if all elements of S can be found in T in the same order [43,10]. For example, $S = \langle E _ { 1 } \to E _ { 2 } \to$ $E _ { 3 } \mathrm { ~ ) }$ 〉 is a subsequence of $T = \langle Z \to E _ { 1 } \to Y \to E _ { 2 } \to X \to X \to E _ { 3 } \to D \rangle$ 〉. An event subsequence differs from an event substring in that the events in an event substring must be contiguous, whereas the events in an event subsequence need not be. Thus S is an event subsequence, but not an event substring, of T. Frequent event subsequences are particularly useful in two basic aspects in analyzing <sup>fi</sup>nancial news. First, they capture the common intertwined links among the events described in <sup>fi</sup>nancial texts. For example, the frequent subsequence S tells us that at least two events, $E _ { 1 }$ and $E _ { 2 } ,$ , will usually occur prior to event $E _ { 3 } .$ Second, the subsequence can also be used for predicting <sup>fi</sup>nancial trends or behaviors in the market [11].

As shown in Fig. 1, our event sequence extraction is a process of identifying the relevant event sequence in the data set and discarding the irrelevant ones. In this research, we shift our event sequence extraction to the problem of approximate matching of event subsequences speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the user enquiry S with all of the event sequences T stored in the textual information database. Given two event sequences, S and T, a common subsequence $Q = L C S ( S , T )$ is a possible subsequence with events in Q also being events in S and T, and in the same order [9,16]. We compute the LCS as the basis of the computation of an optimal alignment between the two sequences. The process utilizes a technique called dynamic programming in which the production of an alignment between two sequences is based on the computation of the edit distance between them [32]. The edit distance, also called the Levenshtein distance, between two sequences is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the minimum number of edit operations required to transform the sequence from S to T. The edit operations include change, insertion, and deletion. This is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient method for identifying the best matching subsequences, even with some unknown gaps. It is beyond the scope of this paper to explain the algorithm in detail; however, interested readers can refer to the abovementioned studies. Brie<sup>fl</sup>y, for a given input event sequence S, we attempt to extract all of the possible relevant sequences in the database. Each extracted event sequence is in the form of a series of instance-attribute pairs. For example, given two instances, say, “UKpound” and the “property”, the event sequence extraction is to identify all the relevant event subsequences in the TID that may help in predicting the <sup>fi</sup>nancial trend. As a result, the event subsequence shown in Table 2 is retrieved from the extraction module. The attribute of each event in the sequence is not independent but rather relies on the preceding, or subsequent, events in the sequence. For example, the attribute “down” in the instance “property” relies on the attribute of its preceding instance “UK-economy” in the event sequence shown in the sentence (P1). It is interesting to discover: Do all extracted event sequences originating from the input S follow some particular patterns? Do they come from a particular family? If so, then the frequent event sequences of S reveal the strong associations among the events and suggest a clue in the prediction. Next, we clarify how event sequence prediction is modeled as an extrapolation of the underlying interrelated chain of attributes from which the actual events shown in the sequence are generated.

## 4. Classi<sup>fi</sup>er-based inference engine

Machine learning methods often play a key role in sequence prediction because it is extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and costly to manually encode the prescribed prediction models. In this research, we explore a decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er to identify the most likely sequence of attributes that could have emitted the given event sequence. One of the advantages of using the tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er is that sequence prediction generally requires an interpretable model, even though other nonparametric methods, such as neural networks or SVM, may produce more accurate prediction. Without requiring a great deal of time in data preprocessing and careful tuning of the learning parameters, the hierarchical structure of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation trees also makes its construction relatively fast. In the following section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst explain the decision tree induction approach, which is often used in supervised machine learning. We also take advantage of one of its derivatives, the boosting technique, which combines the outputs of many weak classi<sup>fi</sup>ers to produce a powerful coalition. In Section 4.2, we describe in detail our inference engine in which event sequences are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed and predicted. An inference mechanism is devised to handle the intertwined cause-and-effect chains during the prediction. The system prototype is implemented and its evaluation is outlined in Section 5.

## 4.1. Learning using decision tree classifiers

Decision tree learning is one of the most widely used and practical methods for inductive inference. Its purpose is to take training data tagged with a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (for example medical pathology data tagged with a diagnosis) and learn how to classify the data, so as to correctly classify other data of the same kind. Most algorithms that have been developed for learning decision trees are variations on a core algorithm that employs a top-down, greedy search through the space of possible decision trees. This approach is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the ID3 algorithm and its successor C4.5 [29], which forms the primary core of our inference engine. The decision tree learning algorithm in C4.5 is basically a case of supervised learning in that it uses training instances to construct the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The induction follows a divide-and-conquer strategy to split the training instances into two or more partitions based on their features. Each partition subset represents a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation subproblem [24]. The splitting is repeated in each partition to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The entire modeling can be represented in a tree-like structure in which the entire data set is represented as a root node. When a split is made, several child nodes, which correspond to the partitioned data subsets, are formed. If a node is not split further, then it is called a leaf node. A fundamental step of the learning procedure is the selection measure applied to select the appropriate test feature on which to split the data at each node while growing the tree. The most popular selection measure is entropy impurity, which is based on Shannon's information theory [35]. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects how well instances in the training set are separated with respect to the different classes at the nodes. Given a set of training instances, each instance feature is evaluated using a statistical test, called information gain, to determine how well it classi<sup>fi</sup>es the instances. The best explanatory feature that reduces the entropy most is selected at the root node of the decision tree. An offspring of the root node is then created for each possible value of this feature. The entire process is then rippled down or repeated using the training instances associated with each offspring node to select the best features to test at that branch in the tree.

Another important feature of the decision tree method is its ensemble technique, which enhances its predictive power. This technique creates a <sup>fi</sup>nite set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers from random sets of training instances and then uses them together for the prediction. Boosting, a widely used ensemble technique, is an effective method that produces a very accurate prediction rule by combining rough and moderately inaccurate rules of thumb [33,20]. In boosting, an initial base classi<sup>fi</sup>er using a set of training instances having equal weight is constructed. When the prediction of the base classi<sup>fi</sup>er differs from the true outcome, the weight of this poorly predicted instance increases. A new training data set is then selected randomly from the weighted instances. As a result, the learning of the next classi<sup>fi</sup>er pays more attention to the poorly predicted instances. This process continues until a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed number of iterations is reached or a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned termination condition is met. In brief, the main idea of boosting is to combine many simple and moderately inaccurate categorization rules into a single, highly accurate categorization rule. The simple rules are trained sequentially; conceptually, each rule is trained on the examples that were the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to classify by the preceding rules. The <sup>fi</sup>rst practical boosting algorithm, AdaBoost, which was introduced by Freund and Schapire [14], solved many of the practical dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of the earlier boosting algorithms. Table 3 illustrates the main idea of the algorithm; however, interested readers can refer to the literature for more detailed discussions [14,18].

## 4.2. Inference engine of the text-based DSS

Our inference engine is designed to learn a set of prediction rules from the event sequences, which can be used to predict the likelihood of the occurrence of new observations. A set of classi<sup>fi</sup>ers and rules is learned from the training data in the form of n-tuples. For each tuple, we include the properties of the current event and information about its preceding and subsequent events in the training data. The assumption is that the events in the sequence are not independent but rather mutually related. One can model this dependence implicitly by including information about the preceding and subsequent events. This tactic is inspired by methods in natural language processing for tasks such as POS tagging, where the tags of both the preceding and subsequent words are used as features to predict the current POS tag. In this inference engine, for any attribute a of the event E in the event sequence, we consider the following feature set.

(i) Attribute $a _ { i }$ of the current target event $E _ { i } .$

(ii) The attributes of two preceding events, $E _ { i - 1 }$ and $E _ { i - 2 } .$

(iii) The attributes of two subsequent events, $E _ { i + 1 }$ and $E _ { i + 2 } .$

(iv) The relative position of the event $E _ { i }$ found in the event sequence.

(v) Two major information-theoretic functions, mutual information and the likelihood ratio, in quantifying the collocation of the attributes.

To train the classi<sup>fi</sup>er to learn the current target attribute, we provide the attributes of its neighbors in the event sequence S in the learning. The relative position of the event $E _ { i }$ in the sequence S is calculated by dividing the absolute position in the sequence by its length. Two kinds of information-theoretic functions, namely, pointwise mutual information (PMI) and the likelihood ratio (LR), are applied in quantifying the collocation of events. They quantify the collocation disparity between the events around the target event. $$

$$

$$

Both mutual information and the likelihood ratio re<sup>fl</sup>ect the event co-occurrence information as a continuous function rather than discrete values shown in the feature set. The training of the inference engine proceeds as follows. Each attribute in the event sequences produces a training example for the decision tree construction. Given a sequence, the feature set is computed with respect to each target attribute, thereby producing a training n-tuple of the target attribute describing the event. All of the tuples are presented for decision tree learning using the boosting algorithm. As a result, a decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er is learned from the training examples, which becomes the major component in our inference engine. In the prediction phase, this process is repeated as in the training, producing a prediction ntuple for each unknown attribute in the incoming event sequence. The prediction n-tuple is then subject to the verdict of the classi<sup>fi</sup>er. At the same time, as in other decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, each prediction is associated with a certainty factor, which ranges from 0.5 to 1. The factor indicates the con<sup>fi</sup>dence level of the prediction. One obvious hurdle in applying the inference engine in event prediction is the problem of attribute noise. Because the attributes of the adjacent events are unknown, especially for the subsequent events $E _ { i + 1 }$ and $E _ { i + 2 } ,$ mentioned in the feature set, the majority of the features cannot be evaluated. In other words, the classi<sup>fi</sup>er receives a noisy set of features as input because of the attribute dependence on the unknown attributes of the adjacent events. This situation is remedied in two stages in our inference engine. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, the default procedure is to use the baseline attribute, i.e., the attribute most frequently found for the event in the training examples. Although this baseline attribute is clearly far from perfect, it provides a rough estimation, associated with an uncertainty, of all unknown attributes. In the second stage, instead of predicting consecutively all event attributes from the beginning to the end of the sequence, our prediction starts from the attribute with the highest uncertainty in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, in the hope that its adjacent events, which have a higher con<sup>fi</sup>dence level, will shed some light on the current prediction. All of the features with attribute dependence in the tree classi<sup>fi</sup>ers are activated during the prediction. All of the event attributes in the sequence are evaluated, from the highly unresolved to the most promising, in one complete cycle. In this second stage, because of attribute dependence, the adjacent attributes will surely be altered if the current attribute is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed. For the same reason, the current attribute will also be affected by the newly altered adjacent attributes. This intertwined cause-and-effect chain in the prediction is settled by allowing the whole prediction mechanism to repeat iteratively for several cycles until the event sequence becomes steady. An event sequence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as steady if there is no further modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of attributes during the prediction. Similarly, the certainty factor of attributes predicted by the classi<sup>fi</sup>er in the i-th cycle de<sup>fi</sup>nes the precedence order of event attributes in the (i+1)-th prediction cycle. As shown in Table 4, to take into account the effects of its adjacent events, the vote $\nu _ { n }$ of the attribute class n relies on the certainty of its neighbors. In other words, the vote will always be diminished by its neighbors, even though a large certainty factor may be deduced in the classi<sup>fi</sup>er. This is somewhat unreasonable, and the situation will also deteriorate as the certainty factors are propagated along the computational chain before the <sup>fi</sup>nal verdict becomes steady. To maintain the impacts of adjacent events without the deduction shrinking away from the classi<sup>fi</sup>er, a normalized certainty factor (NCF) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned to alleviate the possible negative impacts during the normalization as shown in the mechanism. Without normalization, the certainty factors will get smaller and smaller after each prediction cycle. The NCF at position j of an event sequence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows.

$$
N C F _ {j} = \left(\frac {C F _ {j} - C F _ {\text { min }}}{C F _ {\text { max }} - C F _ {\text { min }}} \times (1 - L B)\right) + L B\tag{4}

## 5.1. Evaluation criteria and design

To evaluate the performance of our predictive model, an 85/15 training/testing strategy is employed. That is, all 20,017 event sequences extracted are randomly partitioned into 17,000 event sequences for training and the rest for subsequent testing. All of the training sequences are used to construct the decision tree for event prediction, and then we use the testing cases to evaluate our textbased DSS. We evaluate system performance in terms of the overall accuracy de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows.

$$
\text { Overall   Accuracy } (O A) = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P C _ {i}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(P C _ {i} + N C _ {i}\right)}\tag{5}
$$

where $P C _ { i }$ represents the number of events correctly predicted (positives) with their attributes in the category $C _ { i } , N C _ { i }$ is the number of events predicted incorrectly (negatives), and n represents the total number of event sequence categories found in all event sequences. In addition, in real-world predictions, we cannot guarantee that the complete information about the event sequences is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the training cases; hence, we address this event prediction problem with the open world assumption. That is, some unknown events are allowed to be found in the testing cases, but there must be none in the training cases. Hence, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne In-Event (IE) as an event that can be found in our training data and Out-of-Event (OOE) as an event that is unknown to the training data.

## 5.2. Results and comparison

Statistical models of sequence prediction, especially Markov chain models, have been extensively studied because of their elegant theoretical framework. However, each state of a Markov chain model corresponds to an observable event, which may restrict the model's application to many problems of interest; hence, the concept of Markov chain models is generally extended to include the case where the observation is a probabilistic function of the state [30,37]. The resulting model is called a hidden Markov model (HMM). The word “hidden” in the name indicates that for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c sequence of events, it is not clear what state the Markov model is in. HMMs are useful for situations where certain sequences can be generated from different important states. In our evaluation, to identify the attributes that best explain the event sequence, we apply the Viterbi algorithm, which ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently computes the most likely event sequence. The algorithm is a dynamic programming algorithm for choosing the state sequence that maximizes the likelihood of the state sequence for the given observation sequence. It is used to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most likely sequence of hidden states that will result in a sequence of observed events, especially in the context of hidden Markov models [12].

As a result, the event collocations, which demonstrate the intertwined links among the events, can be captured in the sequences. In addition, we have moved one step forward in exploring how the extracted event sequences can be used to support the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sequence predictions. Our DSS collates the prior event sequences in such a way that both explicit and implicit knowledge can participate during the predictions. The predictions seek to watch for events that may be missing, but underlying, in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial news. Another important contribution of this research is the design of the classi<sup>fi</sup>er-based inference engine. We have provided a detailed discussion on the inferential mechanism of the inference engine and explained how the engine is applied into the predictive analytics as in other DSS. The engine provides a robust and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient means to handle predictions in <sup>fi</sup>nancial texts, with the support from the extracted event sequences which have a high propensity to provide evidences why the predictions can be made. At the same time, we have evaluated the system and provided a head-to-head comparison with the state of the art hidden markov model. The results demonstrate our engine outperforms the counterpart. While texts usually convey more diverse and “rich” information than purely numerical data, it is wellrecognized it is complicated, if not impossible, to exploit re-occurring textual patterns that can support or perform sequence predictive analytics. The instrumental rules used in sequence prediction are not always obvious. Our approach has provided some hints to extract such rules no matter how hidden they may be in the transactional data, particularly in facing the tremendous collections of textual data. Lastly, while the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are certainly interesting, we have to accept that the experiments conducted may inevitably involve some minimal human intervention, particularly in sense tagging. Indeed, it is intended primarily as a prototype for illustrating the relevant issues in an uncomplicated setting. A massive infusion of linguistic knowledge into the text-based DSS is obligatory when the DSS is being fully deployed. Further investigation needs to be done to extend the work to an unrestricted and fully functional model; however, we have demonstrated and implemented one of the alternatives in revealing the relevant knowledge that can support DSS from texts.
