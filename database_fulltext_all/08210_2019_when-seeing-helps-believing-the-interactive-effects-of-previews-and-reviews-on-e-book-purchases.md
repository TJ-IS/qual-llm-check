# When Seeing Helps Believing: The Interactive Effects of Previews and Reviews on E-Book Purchases

Angela Aerry Choi,<sup>a</sup> Daegon Cho,<sup>b,</sup>\* Dobin Yim,<sup>c</sup> Jae Yun Moon,<sup>d,†</sup> Wonseok Oh<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Online reviews offer consumers the indirect experience of products through others’ consumption evaluations, whereas previews afford them direct experience through product trials. Although conceptual and empirical studies on the business ramifications of online reviews abound, little is known about how online previews moderate the effects of online reviews on sales. To cast light on this issue, the current research investigated the interactive effects of exposure to online previews and reviews on individual purchase decisions. We analyzed a unique two-month panel data set on 270,260 sessions that comprise clickstream data on consumers’ exposure to previews and reviews and data on their subsequent purchase behaviors. On the basis of analyses underlain by a two-stage hierarchical Bayesian framework, we found that online previews positively influence individual purchase decisions. More importantly, significant interactions exist between previews and reviews, as evidenced by the decreasing positive effect of previews with increasing review volume and average review rating. In addition, previews can complement reviews when a high variance in the latter renders purchase decisions difficult. We further examined the sequence effect of exposure to previews and reviews and discovered that exposure to previews following the experience of reviews may exert a considerable positive influence on individual purchase decisions. The results from an additional field experiment and a text-based sentiment analysis reinforced the validity of our main findings by mitigating concerns regarding the endogeneity and the accuracy of the review quality, respectively. The findings provide practical implications with regard to the design of optimal strategies for releasing preview information to digital platforms.

## 1. Introduction

An e-book is a single-purchase, digital experience good whose qualities or characteristics cannot be observed until consumption. In contrast to printed books, which consumers can skim through, e-books do not always allow for browsing before purchase. This constrained access—the information imperfection of e-books—brings forth information asymmetry and generates relatively high uncertainty regarding e-book quality (Kirmani and Rao 2000). In turn, the attendant inability of consumers to ascertain product quality and fit with their tastes may induce inaccurate expectations about a product and may negatively affect sales (Mitchell 1999, Park and Nicolau 2015). These problems can potentially be mitigated by online previews and reviews, which enable consumers to experience digital content directly and indirectly, respectively.

The existing research has focused particularly on how online reviews (e.g., consumer evaluation of a product) shape consumer behaviors (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Duan et al. 2008a, Forman et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010). These online assessments evince primarily overall product quality as established by other consumers (Chen and Xie 2008, Duan et al. 2008b) thereby reducing uncertainty and empowering customers to make informed purchase decisions. By relying on others’ experiences and judgments, consumers can reduce search costs and evaluate the many aspects of products with minimal effort (Chen et al. 2004, Zhu and Zhang 2010).

Despite these economic benefits, however, many consumers who ground their decisions in online reviews frequently end up being dissatisfied with purchase outcomes and regretting their use of online references (Hamilton and Thompson 2007). Unsatisfactory experiences with highly rated products or services may be the results of a number of factors, among which are unreliable and inauthentic consumer reviews created for the purpose of deliberate manipulation, planting, or solicitation (Dellarocas 2006, Mayzlin et al. 2014). Other determinants giving rise to inaccurate rating problems include retailers’ intentional removal of negative and critical reviews, and the fact that product reviews are often based not on the enjoyment derived from consumption, but on consumers’ sense of a product’s popularity and reputation (Goes et al. 2014). High variation and high extremity across reviews eventually exert a negative effect on otherwise credible and helpful peer-generated information resources (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). Finally, many products fail to attract a large number of reviews—an informational deficiency that is particularly apparent in new or less popular products (Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Liu 2006, Zhu and Zhang 2010). These challenges are worth looking into as the accuracy of review valence, volume of reviews, and degree of variation among reviewers’ ratings may play important roles in determining consumers’ reliance on and use of online evaluations.

The extent of online review inaccuracy due to unscrupulous intentions and skewed cognitive perceptions may vary due a range of factors (e.g., product type), but a more important issue for consideration is that online review systems have one fundamental inherent limitation that is difficult to overcome. Online reviews reflect the overall quality of a product or service as evaluated by others (Duan et al. 2008b), but in reality, such aggregated appraisals do not embody each consumer’s private preference (Hamilton and Thompson 2007). That is, online reviews enjoin consumers to “believe” in a product through others experiences in the absence of the opportunity to “see” the caliber of the product for themselves through direct encounters. Research revolving around the effects of online reviews on sales has presented mixed results. Some studies have discovered positive relationships (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008), whereas others have found that online reviews either fail to inform purchase decisions or may actually increase uncertainty (Duan et al. 2008a, Li and Hitt 2008).

To enhance consumers’ direct product experiences and facilitate the validation of product-taste fit prior to purchase, many platform operators have begun to offer online previews (a form of product trial designed to persuade consumers to buy digital content), for example, through the opportunity to listen to the first few minutes of a song or the chance to read the first few pages or chapters of a book. Amazon, for instance, recently launched its “Look Inside” feature, which allows readers to view parts of books free of charge before purchasing them. Another example is Google Preview, a customizable tool offered as part of the search engine company’s Embedded Viewer API service. Similar to “Look Inside,” this tool affords users a firsthand encounter with the books available through the Google platform. These direct experiences through product trials or trailers are becoming increasingly important components of efforts to mold consumers purchase behaviors in digital content markets (Cheng and Liu 2012, Foubert and Gijsbrechts 2016).

The notable difference between online previews and online reviews is a matter of distance (i.e., firsthand knowledge from direct experience versus second hand knowledge from indirect experience). The literature suggests that compared with indirect product experiences, which are perceived as psychologically distant (Hamilton and Thompson 2007), direct encounters pave the way for consumers to more deeply engage in active learning (Hoch and Deighton 1989), generate more credible information (Smith and Swinyard 1982), and build stronger confidence (Smith and Swinyard 1983). The provision of online product previews has become an established scheme for augmenting sales and consumer satisfaction. Nevertheless, few studies have unraveled whether this practice and its interaction with the provision of online reviews are in fact conducive to increased sales.

Foregrounding the effects of online reviews engenders only a partial understanding of the consumer decision-making process over digital content platforms. To address this deficiency, the current research explored the interactive effects of online reviews and online previews on product sales. Drawing on construal level theory (CLT) (Trope and Liberman 2000), we developed hypotheses regarding how consumers perceive and use direct and indirect experiences when making purchase decisions. CLT enables us to theorize how consumers process their own proximal, direct product experience with the distal, indirect experiences of others and thus offers an ideal theoretical framework for grasping the importance of the conjunctive availability of reviews and previews. Specifically, we inquired into the differential effects of online previews, focusing on how these information sources interact with online reviews in influencing purchases. We then devoted our attention to how the effects vary according to the nature of the reviews (e.g., volume, valence, and variance). For example, we looked into whether direct exposure through previews more positively affects sales when indirect experiences through reviews are inconsistent and unreliable. This study extends the literature on CLT by illuminating how consumers of hedonic digital content integrate direct and indirect experiences. Whereas previous works concentrated on a shift in preferences along with different states of mental construals, we disentangle the manner by which consumers combine the high-level construals reflected in online reviews with the lowlevel construals manifested in online previews on the basis of CLT and the information integration framework (Anderson 1971).

We tested our hypotheses regarding the interactive effects of previews and reviews using a two-month panel data set from a leading e-book retailer. The data consist of 270,260 sessions that include clickstream data on consumers’ exposure to previews and reviews as well as data on their subsequent purchase behaviors. We employed a two-stage hierarchical Bayesian framework in our models to control for possible unobserved heterogeneity. The findings suggest that online previews positively influence individual purchase decisions. More importantly, our research reveals that consumers do not simply rely on others’ opinions but leverage a combination of direct and indirect product experiences when purchasing e-books. A significant interaction exists between previews and reviews, as evidenced by the increasing positive effect of previews with decreasing review volume and average review rating.

Previews can also complement reviews when a high variance in others’ experiences renders purchase decisions difficult. We conducted a randomized field experiment to address the potential endogeneity issue that may arise from self-selection biases and carried out a text mining-based sentiment analysis to examine the impact of review attributes. The results of both tests strengthened our main findings. Using the core findings related to the interactive effects, we drew implications for digital content platform operators with respect to the design of optimal strategies for releasing preview information.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Online Reviews and Product Sales

Previous studies in the fields of information systems and marketing have attempted to elucidate how online reviews affect product sales. Goes et al. (2014) found that the volume of online word of mouth and television show ratings are positively related. Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) employed a difference-in-differences approach to establish how the online book ratings of two virtual bookstores (Amazon.com and BN.com) affect consumer purchase behaviors. Furthermore, Forman et al. (2008) noted that reviewer identity disclosure substantially increases product sales.

Some studies, however, have challenged the positive relationship between online reviews and product sales. Duan et al. (2008b) reported that online reviews exert no significant effect on movie sales, and Chen and Xie (2008) revealed that only sufficiently informative online reviews affect book sales. More recent research has teased out the factors that account for the differential effects of online reviews. Zhu and Zhang (2010), for example, explored how product and consumer characteristics moderate the effects of online reviews on product sales, with the authors discovering that online reviews are more influential for less popular games and for players with more extensive Internet experience. Another study identified a complementary effect of online reviews on advertising and promotional marketing (Lu et al. 2013). These studies assumed that indirect experiences through online reviews affect consumer behaviors; however, no research has cast light on how the quality judgments manifested in online reviews are integrated with individuals’ subjective tastes when purchase decisions are made.

## 2.2. Product Trials and Freemiums

Online previews (e.g., free sample chapters of e-books) are a form of product trial designed to allow immediate encounters with products. Previous research has explored the possible effects of direct product experiences from trials on consumer demand and expectations. Goering (1985), for instance, declared that the effect of consumer learning from product trials rests on the substance of the information collected by consumers and on consumer expectations. In a similar study, Smith (1993) examined the manner by which consumers respond to advertisements and free trials. The results of the two studies indicated that both types of marketing schemes can induce favorable perceptions of brand performance and can drive purchase decisions. Bawa and Shoemaker (2004) probed into three effects of product trials on sales: (1) an acceleration effect, wherein consumers engage in repeat purchases of a sampled brand earlier than they otherwise would have; (2) a cannibalization effect, in which the number of paid trial purchases of a brand decreases; and (3) an expansion effect, in which purchases by consumers who do not consider buying a brand without a free sample increase. The findings indicated that product trials exert positive long-term effects on sales but that such effects can vary extensively.

Given the considerable uncertainty that characterizes digital content products, offering free product trials is a widely practiced strategy in the online retail industry. Software freemiums, through which firms offer complimentary light editions or time-locked trial versions of products, are increasingly of interest in product trial research. Studies have shown that the high sales ranks and review ratings for free mobile applications increase the sales of paid versions (Liu et al. 2014) Expanding this line of inquiry, Wagner et al. (2013) examined whether limiting the number of features available through freemiums is the best approach to converting free trial users into paying consumers and found that maximizing a premium fit enhances the probability of conversion. This finding is supported by previous studies that suggest that the optimal scheme for offering free product trials is to develop programs that guarantee the conversion of free trial patrons into paying customers. In summary, the current literature on direct experiences and product trials suggests that online previews have both cannibalizing and promotional effects and that retailers should therefore focus on optimal prepurchase information strategies.

## 2.3. Direct Versus Indirect Product Experiences

Consumers can experience products either directly or indirectly, depending on their level and scope of interaction with them (Mooy and Robben 2002). Indirect experiences consist of product descriptions, advertisements, or online reviews, given that these channels prevent consumption or full interaction with products. Although online reviews also enable consumers to evaluate product quality, they essentially constitute a secondary (indirect) experience derived from the first-hand experiences of others. By contrast, direct experiences comprise product trials, which enable consumers to enjoy hands-on encounters and complete interactions with the commodities (Hamilton and Thompson 2007). Online previews, for example, provide consumers with hands-on encounters with products, after which they evaluate the product quality and preference fit.

Previous studies have compared direct and indirect product experiences in terms of the information value they present. Direct experiences advance consumer engagement in active learning rather than passive learning as direct avenues permit exhaustive interactions with a product (Hoch and Deighton 1989) and provide consumers with more credible information than that derived from indirect experiences. Product trials also foster higher message acceptance (Smith and Swinyard 1982) and greater belief confidence (Smith and Swinyard 1988) than does exposure to advertisements. Hands-on experience, such as reading previews, allows consumers to interact with the products. Such experiences thus permit meticulous evaluation of the products, especially as they relate to the consumer’s tastes and preferences.

In the next section, we develop our hypotheses regarding the interactive impact of the direct experience of e-book contents (through previews) and the indirect experience of e-book contents (through other consumers’ reviews) on consumer purchase.

## 3. Interactive Effect of Previews and Reviews

The direct experience acquired from previews and the indirect experience offered by online reviews reduce information asymmetry, thereby minimizing the perceived risk regarding e-book purchases. An important consideration, however, is that the different evaluative modes offered by previews and reviews may act on consumer purchase intentions in distinctive ways. We propose that the impact of previews on consumer purchase decisions vis-a-vis reviews depends on the level\` of mental construal that previews and reviews trigger regarding the evaluation of the e-book being considered. We draw on CLT (Trope and Liberman 2000) and more general theories regarding psychological distance (Liberman et al. 2007, Trope and Liberman 2010) to propose the interactive impact of previews and reviews on consumer purchase decisions.

## 3.1. Construal Level Theory

CLT (Trope and Liberman 2010) maintains that people can directly experience only the “here and now” with “psychologically distant” things that cannot be seen, observed, or assessed first hand (e.g., the past and future, other places and people, and alternatives to reality), which are relegated to indirect encounters. This framework proposes that individuals are inclined to process more distant objects, events, and people in abstract terms (high-level construal), but tend to represent proximal target events or objectives concretely (low-level construal)

High-level construals involve relatively notional and general mental representations, whereas lowlevel construals entail contextual and subordinate details regarding objects and events. The concept of a construal can be illustrated in the context of product experience. For instance, indirect product experiences based on a reading of product descriptions or reviews represent psychologically distant events that require consumers to form high-level mental abstractions (Liberman et al. 2007). By contrast, direct experiences through product trials enable experiential contact, thereby stimulating concrete, low-level mental construals (Hamilton and Thompson 2007).

Psychological distance can be temporal, spatial, or social, and can stem from the hypothetical rather than the real nature of events under consideration. Temporal distance, for example, drives people to activate high-level, abstract mental construals when they evalu ate distant future events (e.g., an occurrence happening a year from now), but it prompts individuals to employ low-level, concrete construals when they are assessing proximal events (e.g., an event happening tomorrow) (Trope and Liberman 2010). Such psychological distance can also affect consumers’ evaluative thought processes and purchase preferences. People’s preference evaluations may shift when they judge the quality of distant alternatives because of the construallevel change that is triggered by increased remoteness. Correspondingly, preference reversals may occur when consumers assess distant future events, as opposed to near future phenomena (Fiedler 2007). Consumer predilections that are formed on the basis of indirect experiences are likely to differ from those cultivated through direct experiences (Thompson et al. 2005).

Processing product information that is not derived first hand from actual consumption necessitates abstraction (Lieberman 2007) and activates abstract mental construal. Consider a tablet as an example. Processing tablet-related information that is not derived from actual consumption necessitates abstraction (Lieberman 2007) and causes consumers to think of the device as a means of maintaining relationships (i.e., through constant communication). Conversely, interpreting information on the tablet and formulating a corresponding response based on hands-on experience require concretization (Hamilton and Thompson 2007) and induces customers to focus on sophisticated features, such as excellent color rendering onscreen. These phenomena are explained by CLT in terms of the differences in the construal levels that arise depending on the perceived psychological distance (Trope and Liberman 2010). The theory also elucidates how the different construal levels precipitated by direct and indirect product experiences influence consumers information processing and judgment.

Another important effect of differences in construal levels on preference evaluation revolves around the varying degrees to which people focus on outcome desirability versus feasibility in assessing the alternatives. Desirability refers to the “value of an action’s end state” (Liberman and Trope 2008, p. 1204), which translates to a high-level construal feature. Feasibility refers to the “ease or difficulty of reaching the end state,” which corresponds to a low-level construal feature (Liberman and Trope 2008, p. 1204). When planning for succeeding summer vacation options, for instance, consumers not only look at the attributes that lead to coveted outcomes (e.g., cultural attractions, the beauty of beaches) but also consider those that produce viable payoffs (e.g., money and time constraints, language convenience) (Fiedler 2007).

## 3.2. Informational Distances

To examine the integrative relationships between direct experience (previews) and indirect experience (reviews) from a CLT perspective, we used a new dimension of psychological distance, namely, informational distance (Fiedler 2007), which conceptually represents differences in the amount of knowledge or relevant information available to a consumer regarding products. Such distance is also defined as “experiential distance, that is, whether the available information, whatever its amount, is based on first-hand information (e.g., a consumer’s own prior experience) or secondhand or third-hand information” (Fiedler 2007, p. 102). It is “a monotonic function of sample size, or the numerosity of available facts, experiences, details, and knowledge units” (Fiedler 2007, p. 102).

As reflected in these explanations, one of the core elements that constitute informational distance is the availability of direct experience from which individuals can precisely inspect the product quality and “fit” with their preferences. Although online reviews may function as important sources of information about product quality, these resources represent only a collection of particulars obtained through indirect experiences that are often imprecise, abstract, and biased (Dellarocas 2006, Mayzlin et al. 2014) and cannot serve as substitutes for individual direct ex periences. We expect that consumers depend highly on direct experiences (e.g., book previews), particularly when information produced by indirect encounters (e.g., book reviews) is abstract and uncertain (e.g., high variance among reviewers). Given that people rarely discount the trustworthiness of their direct engagement with products or services, these experiences are powerful resources for mental recognition and decision making (Smith and Swinyard 1983).

## 3.3. Information Integration of e-Book Previews and Reviews

Information integration theory (e.g., Anderson and Jacobson 1965, Anderson 1971) is concerned primarily with how individuals combine different pieces of information—an act that subsequently changes their attitudes and preferences. This theory encompasses an averaging model, which indicates that decision makers adjust information weight, defined as the “psychological importance” attached to each piece of information in accordance with its credibility or reliability (Anderson 1971, p. 172). Information integration theory has been widely applied in efforts to understand consumer behaviors and casts light on how people integrate others’ information with their own opinions or preferences when they evaluate products. This theory has contributed to ascertaining people’s tendency to discount the importance and value of information supplied by others when it is inconsistent with their own perspectives (Anderson 1971).

In a similar vein, Chang (2010) demonstrated that when information uncertainty is high, consumers make decisions on the basis of two primary factors: their own preferences and others’ choices. The authors showed that these factors influence each other as consumers adjust the decision weights attached to each resource, depending on the informational credibility and individual predispositions. Information integration theory and CLT collectively suggest that individuals consolidate direct and indirect experiences when they make judgments and decisions, particularly in the presence of information uncertainty. This combinatory tendency is likely to occur when consumers evaluate experiential goods (e.g., e-books) whose quality is unknown prior to use.

In the context of the hedonic experiential consumption of e-books, a high-quality consumption experience is the desired end state of a consumer. The means by which this state is achieved is the reading of a book that is not only of high quality and extensively endorsed by others but also a good fit with a consumer’s own tastes and preferences. Before such reading commences, consumers acquire information about a given book by turning to reviews, which constitute indirect experiences. Correspondingly, such commentaries are likely to trigger high levels of construal regarding the information accessed through them. For instance, people interpret positive-valence reviews as indicative of the objectively assessed quality of an e-book, and hence, tend to zero in on such quality as translating into a potentially enjoyable reading experience. When faced with negative-valence reviews, however, consumers are likely to focus on lower-level construal and seek direct experience to minimize the informational distance (Fiedler 2007). Negative reviews inherently foster risk perceptions that encourage consumers to search for more concrete, low-level detailed information (Adaval 2001). This is likely the case with hedonic products. Sen and Lerman (2007, p. 79) stated that “consumers likely anticipate a positive mood when reading reviews for hedonic products (because they are looking forward to choosing a product that will make them feel good).”

Consequently, it is possible that consumers will discount the negativity in a hedonic product review when it is disjointed from their current and anticipated moods (Sen and Lerman 2007). This propensity for discounting results in increased reliance (i.e., increased weight) on preview information as consumers form purchase decisions with respect to hedonic content. Previous research has also shown that experiential contact with a product can alter the extent to which consumers access mental construals and can serve as a resource that sufficiently produces changes in consumers product preferences (Hamilton and Thompson 2007). When consumers access preview information amid negative reviews, therefore, low-level construals are triggered, and attention is refocused toward direct experience. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. The higher the negativity of the review valence, the stronger the relationship between previews and consumer purchase decisions

The number of reviews reflects the volume of discussion regarding corresponding products. As this number increases, informational distance decreases, thereby enabling consumer exposure to product information. The more reviews, the more trustworthy it appears to be given the greater amount of product information and knowledge made available by these reviews (Zhu and Zhang 2010). This phenomenon diminishes the effectiveness with which previews influence product purchases because quality information is already available through the high volume of consumer reviews. The eventual outcome may reduce consumer reliance on online previews.

CLT also offers an additional explanation for the interactive effects of previews and review volume. A high volume of indirect product experiences gained through reviews augments the stimulation of high construal levels stemming from psychologically distant mediated encounters. At a high level of con strual, the focus rests on the desirability of an outcome (i.e., the purchase of a high-quality e-book). Amid a large volume of reviews, then, consumers tend to disregard a low level of construal (i.e., whether the e-book content is a good fit with their personal preferences and tastes). On the contrary, low review volumes increase the abstractness of the mental construa because they weaken the credibility of information regarding a product in consideration. Consequently, consumers who assess products on the basis of a few reviews potentially gloss over the less credible information presented in these evaluations and base their decisions more heavily on direct experience. In line with this observation, we submit Hypothesis 2.

Hypothesis 2. The smaller the review volume is, the stronger the relationship is between previews and consumer purchase decisions.

We argue that reviews are indirect product experiences, and are hence, psychologically distant en counters. This distance triggers consumer evaluations that are grounded in quality dimensions—an occurrence that encourages the selection of products on the basis of the assessed overall quality under a large volume of reviews and positive commentaries. However, when there is high variance across the reviews, which signals inferior credibility of the provided information, consumers adjust their beliefs and consider that an object’s evaluation is likely driven by direct rather than indirect experience. This proclivity emerges because people more genuinely trust their own experiences over those of others (Smith 1993), thereby prompting them to refocus on their personal preferences and triggering low-level construals. Under such conditions, consumers tend to value previews as a source of direct experiential information. In line with this argument, we forward the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. The greater the review variation is, the stronger the association is between previews and consumer purchase decisions.

## 4. Data Description 4.1. Empirical Setting

The data set used in this work was obtained from a leading e-book retailer in Korea—EbookCo (pseudonym), which distributes e-books and digital versions of print books. As with Amazon.com, EbookCo offers a wide selection of titles under multiple genres, including fiction and romance, through its online store, where consumers can browse and buy through desktop browsers and mobile applications. The online store displays a list of books with images of their cover pages and titles. Users interested in a book can click on either the title or the cover image to obtain further information, including the author’s name, product price, additional descriptions, and other related details. The user experience of perusing and purchasing books in EbookCo’s online store is similar to that in Amazon’s bookstore.

The default product page of an e-book offered by EbookCo presents supplementary information intended to facilitate purchase decisions. This includes summary and/or introductory descriptions of the book, aggregated statistics associated with the online reviews of the product, such as the average review rating and the number of reviews accumulated, and the book’s publication date, publisher, and authors. A complimentary preview (i.e., the first 5% of a book) may also be available, allowing consumers to sample a book’s content and partially assess its quality. This direct experience is accessible through a click of the “preview” button, which launches a proprietary electronic viewer from which a portion of the book can be read. The structure and format of the EbookCo previews are analogous to those offered on Amazon “Look Inside” and Google Preview. Similar to a preview, a review is accessible via a click on the “review” button, which directs consumers to detailed evaluations. Along with the detailed reviews come all other consumer-generated evaluations and related individual ratings, thus enabling consumers to pore over all reviews and obtain information on the review variance. Note that knowledge on the number of reviews and the average review rating for a book can influence the purchase decisions of consumers who do not actively access the full content of online consumer reviews (Forman et al. 2008, Lu et al. 2013), whereas information on the review variance and the full texts of reviews affect the purchase decisions of consumers who actively click on the associated buttons.

## 4.2. Data

Our data set consists of the detailed records of individual consumers’ clickstream or browsing data for product pages, data on previews and reviews, and transactional data on book purchases from EbookCo’s server logs. The data span the period from June 24 to

August 22, 2015. Consumer data on the product page views were obtained from the company’s server log files, which keep track of the cookies for each consumer, page request time, and an e-book’s unique identification number for each product page requested. These data enabled us to identify the nonpurchase sessions of consumers. Because a consumer may view the same product page several times, we aggregated the page-view data at the book level for each consumer. More specifically, we recorded a consumer’s product page view for each book only once and did not take into account the number of visits to a given product page.

After landing on a product page, a consumer can obtain information through online previews and reviews as well as other product-related details displayed on the page. We collected the clickstream data on consumers’ viewing of “preview” and “review” tabs, along with their identification codes, click times, and e-book identification codes. From these clickstream data, we determined exposure to previews (i.e., preview content) and reviews (i.e., review content), and subsequent purchase decisions. Deriving such information involved matching transaction (purchase) timestamps with the periods during which the online “preview” and “review” tabs were clicked We then treated our observations as follows. A consumer (i) was regarded as having been exposed to previews and reviews only when that individual accessed both before a given purchase time. Each click of the “preview” and “review” tabs by the consumer was recorded separately, thereby enabling us to compare the timestamps of exposure for each consumer in each procedure and to ascertain the sequence of individual exposure to the previews and reviews of a book.<sup>2</sup>

In our empirical context, the qualitative features (e.g., texts) of the online reviews are revealed only to consumers who click on the “review” tab. More specifically, the average review rating and the number of reviews for a book are displayed along with the descriptions of the book on the default introduction page; however, detailed review texts appear only when consumers access these evaluations via a dedicated tab, such as “view all reviews.” This review structure can be found in many popular retail sites (e.g., Expedia .com and Hotels.com). We matched the clickstream data on previews and reviews with the data on the consumers’ product page views and transactions on the basis of the consumer and unique e-book identification codes.

We collected additional information on the online review volume, valence, and variance for e-books during each session. The volume of reviews was measured as the number of reviews that represent discussions devoted to an e-book and its popularity.

## 5. Model Estimation

This section details our empirical model. We applied a two-stage hierarchical Bayesian framework in our model to more effectively control for unobserved heterogeneity (Chintagunta et al. 1991) and accurately estimate the joint effects of online previews and reviews on purchase decisions. In particular, we focused on how online previews and reviews operate in concert to promote consumers’ purchase decisions.

## 5.1. The Interaction Between Online Previews and Online Reviews

We formulated a random utility choice framework (e.g., McFadden 1974) to characterize our model. The dependent variable is an observed consumer’s binary response of whether to purchase or not to purchase. For a given e-book $j ,$ the latent utility $( u _ { i j } )$ of the purchase for consumer i is written as

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (p u r c h a s e = 1 | X) = \frac {\exp (u _ {i j})}{1 + \exp (u _ {i j})},
$$

where $u _ { i j } > 0$ : purchas $\mathbf { \Phi } _ { i j } = 1$ and

$$
u _ {i j} \leq 0: p u r c h a s e _ {i j} = 0.
$$

We posited a logit model to understand a consumer’s choice based on the utility that individual attaches to the e-book purchase. Such utility depends on the information that appears on a book’s description page; this information includes the online previews, reviews, and other book-related features $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , prices and publication dates). As a consumer obtains information, uncertainty about the e-book’s quality diminishes, and its purchase likelihood may increase. The latent utility that a consumer (i) attaches to an e-book (j) can thus be specified as the following:

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {i j} = \beta_ {i 0} + \beta_ {i 1} \text {Preview} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 2} \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 3} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 4} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 5} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 6} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 7} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 8} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 9} \text {VarReview} _ {i j} \\ \quad \times \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 1 0} \text {VarReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 1 1} \text {Preview} _ {i j} \times \text {WordCntPreview} _ {j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 1 2} \text {Preview} _ {i j} \times \text {WordCntPreview} _ {j} ^ {2} + \theta_ {1} \text {FixedPrice} _ {j} \\ \quad + \theta_ {2} \text {PaperBook} _ {j} + \theta_ {3} \text {DaysOnMarket} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \theta_ {4} \text {PopularAuthor} _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array}
$$

Our interest in using Equation (1) is to estimate the interaction effects between the online previews and reviews. The latent utility of an e-book purchase is a function of a consumer’s exposure to previews $( P r e v i e w _ { i j } )$ and reviews $( R e v i e w _ { i j } )$ , review characteristics $( R a t i n g R e v i e w _ { i j } ,$ NumReview<sub>ij</sub>, and VarReview<sub>ij</sub>), and several book characteristics, such as preview size,<sup>5</sup> list price, days on market since the publication date, author popularity, and whether a print version exists. We also incorporated random coefficients and random intercepts into our model to account for unobserved consumer heterogeneity. $P r e v i e w _ { i j }$ and $R e v i e w _ { i j }$ allowed us to measure the exposure effect of online previews and reviews on the propensity to purchase a product. To operationalize the effects of interaction between online previews and online reviews (i.e., Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3), we employed the number of reviews, average review rating, and review variance as the review variables.

Note that the average review rating (RatingReview<sub>ij</sub>) and the number of reviews (NumReview ) for a book are displayed along with the descriptions of the book in the default introduction page, whereas the variance of the review ratings appears only when consumers click on the “review” tab for additional information. Accordingly, examining the interaction between the variance of reviews and previews requires the supplemental condition that consumers access the detailed review page.

Given this product page setting, review variance (VarReview<sub>ij</sub>) cannot influence consumers’ purchase likelihood if they do not click on the detailed review content. We thus used an indicator variable (Review<sub>ij)</sub> in the specification of the review variance to restrict the influence of this variable to consumers who are exposed to detailed review content. We specified the interaction terms between exposure to preview $( P r e v i e w _ { i j } )$ and review variance $( V a \ ' r R e v i e w _ { i j } )$ in a similar manner. Given that the effects of the preview size apply to consumers who access online previews, we delineated a preview size term (WordCntPreview ) that comes with an indicator variable (Preview to capture the effects of online previews on consumers who are exposed to such resources.

Hierarchical Bayesian methods allow us to capture the individual-specific effects on the effects of exposure to online previews, exposure to online reviews, interactions between online previews and reviews, and unobserved heterogeneity covariance (refer to $\mathrm { A p \mathrm { - } }$ pendix A in the e-companion for the technical details of the Bayesian method). In other words, individual heterogeneity can be further examined by specifying consumer-specific random coefficients, which would represent the varying effects of the consumer characteristics on exposure to online previews, exposure to online consumer reviews, and interactions between online previews and reviews. Correspondingly, we employed consumer age and gender as observable individual-specific parameters and controlled for unobservable consumer heterogeneity by including random coefficients $\left( \lambda _ { i n } \right)$ in the analysis. These coefficients follow a continuous normal distribution:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \beta_ {i n} = \overline {{\beta_ {n}}} + \theta_ {1 n} A g e _ {i n} + \theta_ {2 n} G e n d e r _ {i n} \\ + \lambda_ {i n} \text {and} \quad \lambda_ {i n} \sim i i d. N (0, V _ {\beta}). \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

This model enabled us to account for a flexible random component specification with both observable (age and gender) and unobservable $( \lambda _ { i \mathrm { n } } )$ individualspecific heterogeneity. We identified individual-specific parameters $( \beta _ { i n } )$ to be drawn from a continuous normal distribution, where $\overline { { { \beta } _ { n } } }$ is the intercept of individual-specific heterogeneity, $\lambda _ { i n }$ represents the unobserved components, and $V _ { \beta }$ is a variance-covariance matrix, which allows the error term in each individualspecific parameter to be correlated in the following manner:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \lambda_ {i 0} \\ \ldots \\ \lambda_ {i n} \end{array} \right] = M V N \Bigg (\left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ \ldots \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \quad \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} v _ {0} ^ {2} & \dots & v _ {0, n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ v _ {n, 0} & \dots & v _ {n} ^ {2} \end{array} \right] \Bigg).
$$

## 5.2. Addressing Selection Bias

One may argue that exposure to online previews is correlated with some unobserved factors that may influence individuals’ purchase decisions. For example, consumers’ exposure to previews may reflect their strong interest in corresponding books, thus potentially giving rise to self-selection issues in our analyses and leading to inconsistent estimations. A common approach to dealing with such an endogeneity problem is to create an instrument variable that is positively correlated with endogenous regressors but does not influence the unobserved factors that may indirectly influence the dependent variable (Wooldridge 2010). However, because both our dependent and endogenous variables are binary, traditional linear regression may be an inappropriate approach to estimating our parameters (Wooldridge 2010). Instead, we use a two-stage residual inclusion method, employing the predicted probability of $P r e v i e w _ { i j }$ and $R e v i e w _ { i j }$ as regressors in Equation (1). Two-stage residual inclusion has been widely used to mitigate concerns regarding the estimations of endogenous parameters (Che et al. 2012, Danaher et al. 2015). Danaher et al. (2015), for instance, adopted this approach to address endogeneity in coupon allocations in response to a coupon promotion, and Terza et al. (2008) showed that incorporating residual probability into a nonlinear demand model substantially reduces endogeneity in parameter estimates.

We obtained the predicted probabilities of individuals to be exposed to online previews $\stackrel { \bullet } { ( } \hat { p } _ { i j } ^ { p r e v i e w } = \mathrm { P r } ( P r e v i e w _ { i j } = 1 ) )$ and online reviews $( \hat { p } _ { i j } ^ { r e v i e w } { = } \operatorname* { P r } ( R e v i e w _ { i j } = ~ 1 ) )$ in the first-stage of the auxiliary regression. We next computed the probability residuals of Preview and Review by differentiating the predicted probabilities from the endogenous variables, which are given by $P r e v i e w _ { i j } - \hat { p } _ { i j } ^ { p r e v i e w } ( R e s _ { - } p )$ and $R e v i e w _ { i j } - \hat { p } _ { i j } ^ { r e v i e w } \left( R e s _ { - } r \right) .$ respectively. We then integrated these probability residuals into Equation (1) to allow for endogeneity, generating the second-stage equation as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {i j} = \beta_ {i 0} + \beta_ {i 1} \text {Preview} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 2} \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 3} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 4} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 5} \text {RatingReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 6} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 7} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 8} \text {NumReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 9} \text {VarReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 1 0} \text {VarReview} _ {i j} \times \text {Review} _ {i j} \times \text {Preview} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 1 1} \text {Preview} _ {i j} \times \text {WordCntPreview} _ {j} \\ \quad + \beta_ {i 1 2} \text {Preview} _ {i j} \times \text {WordCntPreview} _ {j} ^ {2} \\ \quad + \theta_ {1} \text {FixedPrice} _ {j} + \theta_ {2} \text {PaperBook} _ {j} \\ \quad + \theta_ {3} \text {DaysOnMarket} _ {i j} + \theta_ {4} \text {PopularAuthor} _ {j} \\ \quad + \delta_ {1} [ \text {Preview} _ {i j} - \hat {\rho} _ {i j} ^ {\text {preview}} ] \\ \quad + \delta_ {2} [ \text {Review} _ {i j} - \hat {\rho} _ {i j} ^ {\text {review}} ] + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

Equation (3) can then be rewritten as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} u _ {i j} = & \beta_ {i 0} + (\beta_ {i 1} + \delta_ {1}) P r e v i e w _ {i j} - \delta_ {1} \hat {p} _ {i j} ^ {p r e v i e w} \\ & + (\beta_ {i 2} + \delta_ {2}) R e v i e w _ {i j} - \delta_ {2} \hat {p} _ {i j} ^ {r e v i e w} + \beta_ {i 3} R a t i n g R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 4} R a t i n g R e v i e w _ {i j} \times P r e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 5} R a t i n g R e v i e w _ {i j} \times R e v i e w _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 6} N u m R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 7} N u m R e v i e w _ {i j} \times P r e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 8} N u m R e v i e w _ {i j} \times R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 9} V a r R e v i e w _ {i j} \times R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 1 0} V a r R e v i e w _ {i j} \times R e v i e w _ {i j} \times P r e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 1 1} P r e v i e w _ {i j} \times W o r d C n t P r e v i e w _ {j} \\ & + \beta_ {i 1 2} P r e v i e w _ {i j} \times W o r d C n t P r e v i e w _ {j} ^ {2} \\ & + \theta_ {1} F i x e d P r i c e _ {j} + \theta_ {2} P a p e r B o o k _ {j} \\ & + \theta_ {3} D a y s O n M a r k e t _ {i j} + \theta_ {4} P o p u l a r A u t h o r _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Equation (4) provides insight into how residual inclusion can be employed to resolve the potential endogeneity of Preview and Review , wherein $( \beta _ { 1 } + { \bar { \delta } } _ { 1 } )$ and $\left( { \beta _ { 2 } + \delta _ { 2 } } \right)$ capture the effects of exposure to online previews and reviews, which are a combination of exogenous and endogenous effects. In other words, $\delta _ { 1 }$ and $\delta _ { 2 }$ are substituted for the unobserved confounders of Preview and Review , respectively. These terms represent how na¨ıve estimation may engender overstated effects from these endogenous variables. It is worth mentioning that residual inclusion is similar to the two-stage least squared model (2SLS), which addresses endogeneity by using instrumental variables (IVs). However, the former is more convenient because the observed endogenous variables

Preview<sub>ij</sub> and Review<sub>ij</sub> are maintained in the model, and these variables can be directly estimated whil controlling for endogeneity.

## 5.3. Testing for Order Effects

For a more comprehensive grasp of the dynamics between previews and reviews in relation to purchase decisions, we considered the sequence of exposure to online previews and reviews as a means of addressing endogeneity concerns. Let us suppose that a potential buyer is more motivated to sample a book if the individual has access to the mixed opinions of reviewers. With this scenario in mind, we investigated how the order of exposure to online previews and online reviews affect e-book purchases. We specified latent utility as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & u _ {i j} = \beta_ {i 0} + \beta_ {i 1} R e v F i r s t _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 2} R a t i n g R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {i 3} R e v F i r s t _ {i j} \times R a t i n g R e v i e w _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 4} N u m R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {i 5} R e v F i r s t _ {i j} \times N u m R e v i e w _ {i j} + \beta_ {i 6} V a r R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {i 7} R e v F i r s t _ {i j} \times V a r R e v i e w _ {i j} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {i 8} W o r d C n t P r e v i e w _ {j} + \beta_ {i 9} W o r d C n t P r e v i e w _ {j} ^ {2} \\ & \qquad + \theta_ {1} F i x e d P r i c e _ {j} + \theta_ {2} P a p e r B o o k _ {j} \\ & \qquad + \theta_ {3} D a y s O n M a r k e t _ {i j} + \theta_ {4} P o p u l a r A u t h o r _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

We employ RevFirst to represent the order effects of online previews and reviews (1 if consumers are exposed to online reviews prior to exposure to online previews, and 0 otherwise). We interacted RevFirst<sub>ij</sub> with the review variables (volume, valence, and variance) to uncover the differential effects of online reviews on the order effects. As in the previous analyses, we defined individual-specific coefficients $\beta _ { i n }$ as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \beta_ {i n} = \overline {{\beta_ {n}}} + \theta_ {1 n} A g e _ {i n} + \theta_ {2 n} G e n d e r _ {i n} \\ + \lambda_ {i n} \text {and} \quad \lambda_ {i n} \sim i i d. N (0, V _ {\beta}). \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

We entered age and gender as observable individual characteristics (Z) into the model and allowed for unobservable individual-specific heterogeneity by including $\lambda _ { i \mathrm { n } }$ in the test of order effects. We identified individual-specific parameters $( \beta _ { 0 } )$ to be drawn from a continuous normal distribution, where $\beta _ { 0 }$ is the intercept of individual-specific heterogeneity, $\lambda _ { i n }$ is the unobserved components, and $V _ { \beta }$ is a variancecovariance matrix that allows the error term in each individual-specific parameter to be correlated in the following manner:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \lambda_ {i 0} \\ \ldots \\ \lambda_ {i n} \end{array} \right] = M V N \bigg (\left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ \ldots \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \quad \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} v _ {0} ^ {2} & \dots & v _ {0, n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ v _ {n, 0} & \dots & v _ {n} ^ {2} \end{array} \right] \bigg).
$$

## 6. Results

## 6.1. Estimation of the Interaction Between Online Previews and Online Reviews

The initial step of estimating the interaction between online previews and online reviews involves the construction of residuals (Res p and Res r). To operationalize the residual inclusion approach (Section 5.2), we first obtained the predicted probabilities of exposure to online previews and reviews by using logistic regression. We employed some of the variables used in Equation (1), given that these variables may influence an individual’s choice to assess online previews and reviews. An important task is to ensure that the exogenous variables disregarded in Equation (1) are employed in the model identification (Wooldridge 2010). Thus, we incorporated the historical patterns of individuals’ exposure to online previews and reviews as instrumental variables in our choice model, with the patterns denoted as HistViewPreview and $H i s t V i e w R e v i e w _ { i } ,$ respectively. We obtained information on the total number of product page views, the degree of exposure to online previews, and the extent of exposure to online reviews for each consumer from May 23 to June 24, 2015. The degree of exposure to online previews and reviews was normalized on the basis of the number of product page views for each consumer. In accordance with the normalization, then, HistViewPreview and HistViewReview reflect the percentages of product page views related to each consumer’s exposure to previews and reviews prior to June 24, 2015. To determine the validity of the instrumental variables, we conducted a Sargan test for overidentifying restrictions, which is designed to validate the orthogonality conditions (Baum et al. 2003). The test revealed that the null hypothesis is supported when both the instrumental variables are uncorrelated with the error term in the main equation. The test results for HistViewPreview $( p = 0 . 2 8 0 )$ and HistViewReview $( p \ : = \ : 0 . 1 0 9 )$ indicated insufficient cause for rejection of the null hypothesis, thus reinforcing the validity of our instrumental variables. The estimations are listed in Appendix B in the e-companion.

Using the residuals (Res p and Res r), we estimated the interaction effects between online previews and reviews. We ran the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation for 10,000 draws and discarded the first 5,000 as burn-in. The mean of the rejection rate for the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is 0.70, which lies within the acceptable range of rejection rates (0.6–0.9) (Hastings 1970). More specifically, the information accessed at a later time will exert a stronger impact than the information derived at an earlier period. 6.3.1. Field Experiment. Consistency between the sample of the books used in the field experiment and those employed in our main analyses was guaranteed by selecting nonserial romance and fantasy e-books with fixed prices close to the average fixed price in our main data set (i.e., US\$3.60). ## 7. Implications

This study investigated the effects of previews and those of its interaction with reviews on e-book pur chases. It also explored the conditions under which this relationship varies, such as the characteristics of products and the heterogeneity among consumers. To the best of our knowledge, our research is among the first to identify the role of previews in e-book purchase settings and to offer empirical evidence on how consumers integrate direct product experiences (previews) with indirect encounters (reviews). In do ing so, the current work enhances our understanding of research on online reviews (Duan et al. 2009, Tucker and Zhang 2011). The online evaluations shared by other consumers generally provide valuable information that mitigates uncertainty regarding a book’s quality. In comparison, online previews provide information that is relevant to consumers’ personal preferences with respect to a book through firsthand encounters with the product. Through previews, e-book readers can grasp an author’s literary style and personality, the rhythm of the language with which the narrative is presented, and the development of the characters. The comprehension of such details is instrumental in efforts to attract and satisfy consumers. Special attention was devoted to an examination of how digital content consumers integrate their preferences through direct experiences with the product quality evaluations of other consumers.

Our findings present important implications for scholarship on online reviews and the prepurchase information-seeking behaviors of consumers. Previous studies have conveniently presumed that consumers make purchase decisions primarily on the basis of indirect experiences obtained through others’ evaluations. By contrast, our research showed that many consumers do not simply rely on the judgments of others, but rather leverage a combination of direct and indirect product experiences in determining whether an item is worth purchasing. As reflected by the empirical regularities observed in our study, e-book consumers count more heavily on previews than reviews when purchasing e-books, albeit the interactive effects of previews and reviews on sales are contingent on review characteristics. For example, heavier reliance on previews is motivated by minimally informative book evaluations by others, as with the cases characterized by a low volume of reviews or low average review ratings received. Previews complement reviews when a high variance in the latter creates difficulty for consumers in evaluating the book quality. These findings suggest that the value of online previews in purchasing decisions depends on the reliability of online reviews. We found no significant order effect from exposure to online reviews and previews, but the sequential effects of exposure to these two prepurchase information sources become salient when the review characteristics and individual heterogeneity are taken into consideration.

We expanded the literature on online reviews by unraveling how online reviews and previews interact and jointly influence consumers’ purchase behaviors. Theoretically, we drew on the CLT of psychological distance (Trope and Liberman 2010) and also the information integration framework (e.g., Anderson 1971) to understand the underlying mechanisms by which consumers integrate proximal direct experiential information from previews and distal indirect experiential information from other consumers’ reviews. On the basis of these theoretical underpinnings, we are able to develop a richer understanding of how previews and reviews interactively influence consumer purchase decisions, as evident in the patterns summarized earlier. When there are many reviews and the overall rating is positive with low variability across reviews, the psychological distance results in high-level construals, leading consumers to focus on their own direct experiences. The converse is true when there is a low volume, negative reviews, and/or high variability across reviews. The current study thus expands our theoretical understanding of the role regarding the simultaneous provision of different information cues on consumer purchase decisions.

The business community can also benefit from the actionable insights emerging from our results. First, the identification of the contingent effect of online previews on e-book purchasing decisions presents an opportunity for e-book sellers to take advantage of the online preview feature to enhance profits. Books that have not been featured or recommended, or have no garnered enough reviews are more likely to benefit from previews. Second, most books go through several stages, as do online reviews. For books that are at the introduction stage, or for books that receive few consumer reviews or have low ratings, publishers and retailers can capitalize on online preview structures (e.g., Kindle Instant Previews) to market e-books and increase traffic on their sites. Specifically, such structures can be embedded or shared as a link vi text or other popular apps, similar to the mechanism underlying photo and video sharing. Third, our re search sheds light on the current landscape of the electronic marketplace. Although consumers can purchase from an ever-increasing selection of books in th electronic market, a disproportionate number of the overwhelming volume of books receive online reviews (Goes et al. 2014). This implies that the majority of such publications simply will not be evaluated by consumers. This issue is exacerbated by the emer gence of self-publishing services, such as CreateSpace or Lulu, which allow both professional and nonprofessional writers to self-publish and thus increase market competition. Our elucidation of what vendors must contend with in today’s market is a source of practical strategies for leveraging previews to in crease sales. Finally, our findings call for e-book retailers and developers to identify each consumer’s “literacy taste” and recommend previews that fit with such individual preferences.

On a broader level, our findings have implications for the publishing and retail industries, which are undergoing a transformation. Given the successful entry of electronic retailers such as Amazon into the publishing industry, publishers and authors are compelled to change the way that they produce books. Some of the avenues through which Amazon has driven such change are its tracking of past sales, genres, and specific interests to better evaluate authors and their compensation structure and its monitoring of promotion decisions. This means that authors are now being asked to take more risks and assume accountability for the success of their books. Given our findings, authors may be asked to develop more appealing previews to spur book sales—a requirement that is particularly salient for emerging authors.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper provided insights into the interactive effects between previews and reviews in relation to the purchase of digital content. Although studies on online reviews have increased in volume and complexity, little effort has been extended to examining the economic effects of previews on the sales generated from digital products. Online previews serve as an economically significant sales driver, given that they enable consumers to evaluate e-books on the basis of taste or preference. The interaction of these information sources with online reviews has given rise to the joint influence on purchase decisions. We illustrated the differential effects of online previews relative to those of online reviews with the necessary condition for complements determined by whether and what users write with respect to books.

Several study limitations are worth discussing. First, our exclusive use of a specific e-book category (i.e., fiction and romance) may limit the generalizability of our findings. Given that the distribution of online review ratings and valence may vary substantially across other book categories, the interactive effects of online previews may not follow suit. Because unobservable heterogeneity may exist among diverse book categories, future studies can extend our findings by comparing the value of online previews for hedonic (e.g., fiction) and utility-based (e.g., textbooks) book categories and by controlling for category-related heterogeneity. Second, attention was directed toward a quantitative dimension of online previews, yet the quality dimension of the product samples may also determine the value of online previews and their interaction with online reviews. Most online book previews offer a few pages from a book, usually from the first chapter. Similar to how movie trailers are rendered, online previews should offer readers a more enticing sampling of books (i.e., provide a sneak peek of the key moments or narratives) to improve the quality and inspire excitement. An interesting natural extension of this study, therefore, would be to investigate the effectiveness of various online preview structures and formats. Third, several studies have pointed out the endogeneity issues surrounding online reviews (Li and Hitt 2008). The potential bias reflected in product evaluations may have influenced our results. More salient aspects of online previews may be unearthed by considering the endogeneity issues in online reviews. Finally, future studies can delve into how the unique characteristics of individuals and reader application platforms (e.g., PC-based versus mobile) moderate the interaction between online previews and reviews. For example, the ratings and valence of e-books may differ substantially between PC-based and mobile channels. All of these personal and environmental factors affect the value of online previews and their interdependence with online reviews. Accordingly, researchers should reflect on these salient characteristics of users and the channels through which e-books are read.

Digital content markets, including those for e-books, comics and webtoons, movies, music, and games are experiencing a dramatic transformation as they rapidly expand due to the growth of the smartphone market and the expansion of content development. Additionally, consumer preferences are dynamically changing. In this volatile market environment, the growth and survival of digital content retailers depend on a clear understanding of how consumers use online reviews and previews to their advantage and integrate the subjective judgments that they form through previews with others opinions in their decision-making processes. Online reviews have served as critical resources for driving traffic and increasing sales but have also suffered from many market imperfections, such as fake reviews, grade inflation, and deliberate manipulation. For digital content purchases, consumers do not simply rely on others’ experiences but determine whether a product corresponds with their preferences. Striking the right balance between direct and indirect product experience is therefore becoming an important strategic challenge.
