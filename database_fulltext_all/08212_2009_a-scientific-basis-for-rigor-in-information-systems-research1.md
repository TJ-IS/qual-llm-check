## Abstract

Qualitative research is just as able as quantitative research to follow certain fundamental principles of logic in general and scientific reasoning in particular. Two such principles are the logic of modus ponens and the logic of modus tollens. In this essay, we frame different research approaches—positivist research, interpretive research, action research, and design research—in the forms of modus ponens and modus tollens. Three issues emerge from this framing and call into question how research is now conducted in the discipline of information systems. They are the issue of a common scientific basis, the issue of the fallacy of affirming the consequent, and the issue of summative validity. Both rigor and relevance in information systems research may be better achieved by attending to the three issues.

Keywords: Theory testing, theory building, research methods, methodology, epistemology, positivist research, interpretive research, action research, design research, quantitative research, qualitative research, rigor, relevance

## Introduction

For many years, the academic discipline of information systems has considered qualitative research to be no less valid than quantitative research (Markus 1997). The road to acceptance traveled by qualitative research offers a lesson worth revisiting. The lesson is that qualitative research is just as able as quantitative research to follow certain fundamental principles of logic in general and scientific reasoning in particular. In this essay, formal logic or simply logic will refer to the ways by which a researcher relates abstract symbols and propositions to one another. Science, which incorporates logic, will additionally refer to the ways by which a researcher relates the symbols and propositions to empirical referents.

As a proponent of qualitative research, this essay's first author has explained how qualitative research is able to follow these principles, where he has offered explanations in the form of narrative (Lee 1989, 1991, 1999; Lee and Baskerville 2003). Still, because he originally derived his ideas about scientific reasoning from some fundamentals of formal logic, he has always believed that he could more effectively convey his ideas if he were to express them in the notation of formal logic. He has done this (see Appendices A and B). This essay will examine the consequences that these ideas, expressed in formal notation, have for empirical inquiry in information systems research. As is often the case when framing ideas in mathematics or other formal notation, the notation has had the effect of sharpening the ideas and leading to new, unanticipated issues. In fact, the ramifications for quantitative and positivist research may even be more significant than the ramifications for qualitative and interpretive research.

As used in this essay, positivism refers to a genre of social-science research that, among other things, regards the natural sciences as the model for the social sciences to live up to. In subscribing to the “natural science model,” positivism puts forth elements often associated with the natural sciences. These include independent and dependent variables, mathematical propositions, quantitative data, inferential statistics, and experimental controls. Positivism requires the social sciences to incorporate these elements if they are to become as “scientific” as the natural sciences. This description of the term positivism is consistent with its usage among information systems researchers including Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), Ngwenyama and Lee (1997), Trauth and Jessup (2000), Dubé and Paré (2003), and Weber (2004). Interpretive research, as used in this essay, is associated with ethnography, hermeneutics, and some forms of case research. It acknowledges that the understanding held by the human subjects in the researcher’s field of study (i.e., the subjective understanding) is part and parcel of the overall subject matter that the researcher is studying, and therefore requires observation and data collection no less than any other part of the objectively existing subject matter being studied. In interpreting what the local setting and its context mean from the “natives’ point of view,” the researcher develops an interpretive understanding of the subjective understanding. Fundamentals of interpretive research are provided by Lee (1991), Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), and Walsham (1995).

We are raising three methodological issues in this research essay: a common scientific basis, the fallacy of affirming the consequent, and summative validity. These issues can be controversial and call into question how information systems research is now practiced. To support our argument, we will use a framework that we build from some elementary aspects of formal logic. We call it the MPMT framework, where MPMT refers to a specific way of using modus ponens and modus tollens.

The primary contribution of this essay is to demonstrate that the MPMT framework provides a scientific basis for the rigor of research, where the bulk of our examination focuses on rigor in positivist research and interpretive research. A corollary to this examination will be that the MPMT framework can also provide a scientific basis for the rigor of research which focuses on relevance, such as action research and design research. (We use the term design research as a synonym for design science.)

## The MPMT Framework

Modus ponens and modus tollens are well established and accepted forms of syllogistic reasoning. The deductive logic of the syllogism is a fundamental of formal logic and is covered in textbooks used in introductory philosophy and logic courses. As such, modus ponens and modus tollens provide a sound framework with which to identify the ramifications that formal logic can have for the reasoning used in scientific research.

Syllogistic reasoning is perhaps best known in the following form: “All humans are mortal,” “Socrates is a human,” and “therefore, Socrates is mortal.” In this example, the statements are instances of the syllogism’s major premise, minor premise, and conclusion, respectively. As straightforward as this reasoning may seem, the straightforwardness is deceptive. A general familiarity with syllogistic reasoning does not substitute for a detailed understanding of the major premise, minor premise, and conclusion as they are used in the empirical testing of a scientific theory.

Theories can take diverse forms (Gregor 2006). Not all theories (including positivist theories) do, can, should, or must fit the MPMT framework, including the framework's mathematical notation (introduced below). However, in the process of building a theory, a researcher can make the choice of shaping it to fit the MPMT framework, whereupon the researcher could use the framework as a basis on which to establish the theory's rigor. We will provide examples of how this is accomplished.

Because positivism is familiar to a majority of information systems researchers, it is useful as a starting point for illustrating the logic of the MPMT framework. The following illustration will focus on positivist behavioral research in information systems that involves statistical inference and multivariate analysis. Published positivist behavioral research in information systems often provides a boxes-and-arrows (or ellipses-and-arrows) diagram to depict a theory's variables and the relationships among them. Each box represents a variable; each arrow, a relationship between variables. The direction of an arrow indicates any direction of causality theorized between two variables. The equivalent mathematical depiction takes the general form, “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, ..., X_{in})$ for i = 1, 2, ..., m.” In this general form, “n” is the number of independent variables and “m” is the number of equations or dependent variables. The context for the mathematical depiction includes verbally expressed assumptions, conditions, and definitions, as well as the empirical referents and causal relationships that the theory purports to explain.

Not all positivist theories need be expressible in the form of boxes-and-arrows diagrams and operationalized in the form of mathematical equations. However, those positivist theories which can be expressed and operationalized in this fashion include many, if not most, of the behavioral theories that undergo statistical analysis in information systems research. For example, these diagrams appear in studies by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw (1989), Ang and Straub (1998), and Zhu and Kraemer (2005). The general form “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, \ldots, X_{in})$ for i = 1, 2, ..., m” covers linear, nonlinear (including polynomial), and interactive (moderating) relationships between variables and can be expressed in matrix notation (Mendenhall and Sincich 2003, pp. 721-753). Representing a theory’s variables and relationships in the form of equations has the benefit of allowing the use of powerful, multivariate statistical estimation procedures, such as multivariate regression and structural equation modeling.

Modus ponens is the form of the syllogism in which the major premise takes the form “if p is true, then q is true”; the minor premise, “p is true”; and the conclusion, “therefore q is true” (the first column in Figure 1). An example of modus ponens is

major premise: If “all humans are mortal” is true, then “Socrates is mortal” is true. $^{4}$

minor premise: “All humans are mortal” is true.
conclusion: Therefore, “Socrates is mortal” is true.

Modus tollens (the second column in Figure 1) takes a different form: the major premise is the same as in modus ponens, but the minor premise “q is not true” and the conclusion “therefore p is not true” are different. An example of modus tollens is

major premise: If “all humans are mortal” is true, then “Socrates is mortal” is true.

minor premise: “Socrates is mortal” is not true.
conclusion: Therefore, “all humans are mortal” is not true.

The statement “if p is true, then q is true” is used in the empirical inquiry of science. The general or universal proposition, p, denotes the theory. A singular proposition, q, denotes facts or data that the researcher expects, if the theory is true, to observe in the particular experiment, organization, laboratory, population or other setting where the theory is being tested. Tables 3 and 4 illustrate this in the case of positivist behavioral information systems research involving theories that can be mathematically operationalized. ## Notes for Table 2:

1. We use an upper-case letter to designate a variable and a lower-case italicized letter to designate an actual numerical value taken by the variable. The exceptions are $p$ and $q$ , which we use as statement variables in modus ponens and modus tollens.

2. In positivist research that seeks to perform multivariate statistical testing, the theory can be depicted in a boxes-and-arrows diagram, which can be mathematically operationalized in the form, “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, \ldots, X_{in})$ for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, m$ ,” where n is the number of independent variables and m is the number of dependent variables or equations. The simplified case, “ $Y = f(X_{1}, X_{2}, \ldots, X_{n})$ ,” is sufficient for illustrative purposes.

a. “Y” is the dependent variable. “ $X_{i}$ ” is the $I^{th}$ independent variable. “f” is the function that relates the variables to one another. “ $x_{i}$ ” is a numerical value taken by the variable $X_{i}$ .

“ $f(x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n})$ ” is the numerical value computed for $y_{predicted}$ . It results from plugging the number $x_{i}$ into every independent variable $X_{i}$ . The numerical value that is then calculated and predicted for Y, $y_{predicted}$ , is defined as equal to $f(x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n})$ . In contrast, $y_{new}$ is the numerical value observed for Y (see point c, below) in an empirical test of the theory.

The notation “ $Y = f(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n)$ ” stands for how the variables are related to one another, for which some examples (where $n = 3$ ) are “ $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \beta_3X_3$ ,” “ $Y = (\beta_1X_1/\beta_3X_3)^2 + \beta_2X_2$ ” and “ $Y = (\beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2)(\beta_1X_1 - \beta_3X_3)$ .” In these examples, $\beta_i$ refers to the true but unknown numerical value for the coefficient of independent variable $X_i$ . When statistical inference is used, $\beta_i$ is typically estimated with a sample-based number, to which this essay refers as $b_i$ .

b. The initial conditions are facts describing an instance of the phenomenon that the theory is offered to explain. In a statistical, laboratory, field, or natural experiment, the initial conditions describe the phenomenon without or before the application of the experimental treatment.

c. The prediction is an assertion by the theory about something that should be observed about the phenomenon with or after the application of the experimental treatment. Specifically, the prediction is that the dependent variable's calculated and predicted numerical value, $y_{predicted}$ , should equal the dependent variable's observed numerical value, $y_{new}$ . For this reason, the term expected subsequent conditions would actually make better sense than the term prediction, as well as nicely complement the term initial conditions.

d. The terms initial conditions and prediction suggest a temporal sequence or longitudinal analysis, but the concepts behind these terms also appear in cross-sectional research. Consider statistical experiments. They may properly involve only data that are all collected at one point in time. In such an experiment, the initial conditions would refer to values taken by the variables in the situation without the application of the “statistical treatment,” and the prediction would be the value of the dependent variable in the situation with the statistical treatment.

e. One may use the term observational consequence in place of prediction for two reasons. First, this term refers to what one expects to observe in the given setting as a consequence of the theory's being true. Second, this term neither specifies nor requires any time element. As such, observational consequence is more general than and subsumes prediction. It is even sufficiently general to be used in descriptions of the logic of the syllogism in interpretive research (see point h in the notes for Tables 5 and 6).

The incorporation of modus tollens as a feature of empirical scientific reasoning, although often credited to Popper (1968a, 1968b), predates him (Kolakowski 1968) and appears in the thinking of diverse scholars. In the philosophy of pragmatism, whose founders are often considered to be William James, Charles S. Peirce, and John Dewey, modus tollens can be found in pragmatism's emphasis on the consequences (q) that follow from beliefs (p) about truth, rightness, or value (Rescher 2005, p. 747). In qualitative organizational studies, modus tollens can be found in Argyris and Schön's (1974) concepts of testability and disconfirmability. These concepts refer to whether or not a person's theory of action (p) is able to be corrected through the empirical testing of its consequences or predictions (q) in this person's everyday experience. Argyris (1999) also uses testability and disconfirmability in his formulation of action science. In his textbook on logic, Copi (1986) explicitly identifies modus tollens as a distinguishing feature of scientific reasoning. Finally, Popper inserts modus tollens into his notion of falsifiability (1968b, pp. 54-56), where he puts forth "falsifiability as a criterion of demarcation" (p. 20) with which "to distinguish between the empirical sciences on the one hand, and mathematics and logic as well as ‘metaphysical’ systems on the other” (p. 11).

We will use the MPMT framework to identify and examine three issues in information systems research. This framework appears not only in positivist research, just discussed, but also in interpretive research, where modus ponens and modus tollens take the forms detailed in Tables 5 and 6. These tables mention hermeneutic interpretation, which originated as the study of the interpretation of text, but extends to interpretivism in general where “text” is replaced by “text analogue” (see the notes for Tables 5 and 6). The MPMT framework may readily incorporate the hermeneutic circle, which Klein and Myers (1999) describe as fundamental and foundational in interpretive field studies, as an instance of the logic of modus tollens (note e in the notes for Tables 5 and 6). Finally, the MPMT framework also appears in action research and design research, which we examine toward the end of this essay. The conclusion “therefore, the reader’s interpretation $I_{k}$ of the text is not a valid hermeneutic interpretation,” if reached, would call for $I_{k}$ to be replaced by an improved or completely new interpretation. This interpretation, $I_{k+1}$ , would then be assessed as to whether it is a valid hermeneutic interpretation of the text, just as $I_{k}$ was assessed. The process of crafting an interpretation in this iterative manner, involving the logic of modus tollens, is precisely the logic of the hermeneutic circle, which Klein and Myers (1999) characterize as the overarching principle of interpretive research.

f. Interpretive research may involve the interpretation of not only text, but also text analogues. For example, if a researcher's interpretation of the body of shared rules for allowable behavior in a particular organization is valid, then the researcher's observation of any action by an organizational member will be consistent with this interpretation. Here, the text analogues are "the body of shared rules for allowable behavior" in this organization and "any action by an organizational member."

g. A major point following from the MPMT framework is that even when an interpretation is assessed to be a valid interpretation, this assessment does not allow the reader to describe the interpretation as the "final" interpretation, the "definitive" interpretation, or the "correct" interpretation. There always remains the possibility for a different interpretation to be formulated so as also to be a valid interpretation of the same text or text analogue. Interpretive researchers regard as acceptable the coexistence of more than one interpretation of the same text or text analogue. Interpretive researchers acknowledge the reality of multiple realities. Another reason for not describing any interpretation as final or definitive is that there is always the possibility for an inconsistency in the interpretation to arise when reading an additional passage (e.g., the next "paragraph" or next "chapter") of the text.

h. Positivist research and interpretive research have a difference in terminology with regard to q as it appears Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. The positivist term for q, prediction, is rarely or never used in interpretive research. Fortunately, the philosophy of science already has a term, observational consequence (explained above in the notes for Table 2), which is sufficiently general to subsume prediction and also to be appropriate for interpretive research. For instance, the prediction or observational consequence that follows from the positivist theory f=ma in a setting where the mass, m, is 10 and the acceleration, a, is 4 is that, if the theory is true, the force will be 40. As for interpretive research, consider the theory that e-mail can support rich communication only if the e-mail users share a socially constructed reality; the observational consequence of this theory for an actual organization where the e-mail users do not share a socially constructed reality is that, if the theory is true, the e-mail communication will not be rich. Also worth noting is that, in both the positivist and interpretive examples, the theory's observational consequence for the given setting would still need to be compared to an observation of what actually happens in that setting.
i. Scholars have already noted the parallels between positivist theory development and interpretive theory development. Sarker and Lee (2006, p. 134) refer to Smith (1993) who uses the positivist term falsification in describing the development of a hermeneutic interpretation. Sarker and Lee also refer to Ricoeur (1991, pp. 159-160) who eschews the term verification and instead applies the concept, associated with positivist theory, that the truth of a theory may never be verified: "As concerns the procedures of validation with which we test our guesses, I agree with Hirsch that they are a lot closer to a logic of probability than to a logic of empirical verification. To show that an interpretation is more probable in the light of what is known...is something other than showing that a conclusion is true" Also, in "Hermeneutics and the Hypothetico-Deductive Method," Føllesdal (1994) states (emphasis in the original, p. 233): "the hermeneutic method is the hypothetico-deductive method applied to meaningful material (texts, works of art, actions, etc.)."

## The First Issue: A Common Scientific Basis

The first of three issues to emerge from the MPMT framework is that positivist research and interpretive research can build on a common scientific basis, namely, the application of modus tollens for the purpose of empirically testing a theory. Consider that the first column of Table 4 (for positivist research) and the first column of Table 6 (for interpretive research) share the same formal-logic argument, namely, modus tollens. These tables demonstrate that the propositions of modus tollens may be applied to either positivist or interpretive research. Therefore, positivist research and interpretive research can share modus tollens as a common scientific basis.

A common scientific basis is significant for two reasons. First, sharing a common scientific basis suggests that these two forms of research are not opposed and irreconcilable, but compatible (see Klein 2005; Lee 1991; Walsham 1995; Weber 2004). On this common scientific basis, positivist research can build its theories with the help of, for example, mathematics and experimental design, and interpretive research can build its theories with the help of, for example, hermeneutics and the phenomenological reduction. Second, recognition of a common scientific basis can set the stage for greater collaboration and mutual understanding between positivist researchers and interpretive researchers in the information systems discipline. While different information systems researchers continue to use different methods, their recognition of building on a common scientific basis would contribute to the creation of a cumulative body of information systems knowledge.

It must be emphasized that modus tollens is not science itself, but rather provides a basis on which a structure of science can be built. Positivist research, interpretive research, action research, and design research can, and do, build upon this basis in their respective ways.

## The Second Issue: The Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent

Another issue emerging from the MPMT framework is that researchers in information systems sometimes commit the “fallacy of affirming the consequent” in their reasoning. Familiarity with certain fundamental principles of logic would allow this erroneous reasoning to be identified and avoided.

Consider the three forms of reasoning in Figure 1. The third form of reasoning (in the last column, “if p is true, then q is true,” “q is true,” “therefore, p is true”) is neither modus ponens nor modus tollens. The flaw in such reasoning can be seen in this example: “If X is a human, then X is mortal,” “Socrates is mortal,” “therefore Socrates is a human.” This reasoning is flawed because “Socrates” could designate a living being other than a human. This form of reasoning is what formal logic calls the fallacy of affirming the consequent. It is appropriately called a fallacy because its conclusion does not follow from its major and minor premises. An ironic and counterintuitive lesson following from this is that any evidence that agrees with a theory’s prediction, where the theory is p and the prediction is q, may neither justify nor even contribute to the conclusion that the theory is true, no matter how much such evidence is produced. At best, it would allow the possibility that the theory is true to remain open.

The logic used in scientific reasoning, therefore, may not affirm or accept a theory as true. Fortunately, there is another option open to the logic of scientific reasoning. It is the option to deny or reject a theory as true. The logic of showing a theory to be wrong is the logic of modus tollens: “if the theory p is true, then its prediction q is true”; “the prediction q is not true”; “therefore, the theory p is false.” In the second column of Figure 1, this appears as “ $p \supset q$ ,” “ $\sim q$ ,” “ $\therefore \sim p$ .” The minor premise’s being $\sim q$ (i.e., the prediction is not true) is logically sufficient to justify the conclusion that the theory p is false, hence calling for the theory to be improved or replaced.

The fallacy of affirming the consequent appears prominently in information systems research in its commentaries on the generalizability of case studies. As Lee and Baskerville (2003, p. 223) provide numerous examples, the belief that the study of a single case is undesirable or deficient is widespread among information systems researchers, even among case researchers themselves. However, requiring case study research to involve multiple sites or multiple cases for the sake of substantiating a theory is mistaken because this requirement presumes the (incorrect) logic in Figure 2. In Figure 2, the symbol “•” means “and” and $q_{i}$ is a prediction made by theory p in case study i. The major premise states: “if theory p is true, then its prediction $q_{1}$ in case study 1 will be true, and its prediction $q_{2}$ in case study 2 will be true, and its prediction $q_{3}$ in case study 3 will be true…and its prediction $q_{n}$ in case study n will be true.” The minor premise states that all n predictions turn out to be true. The conclusion, in this illustration, is that the theory p is therefore true. This reasoning is incorrect because it commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent, which it commits not just once, but n times.

There is another way to acknowledge that no theory can ever be proved true: a successful result in empirical testing (for example, turning up evidence that is consistent with a theory's prediction or observational consequences, especially if contradictory evidence was purposely being sought out) only allows the theory to claim the provisional status of "good enough for now" or "has passed muster so far." A theory's status as valid is always provisional because there always remains the possibility for contradictory evidence to materialize in a future empirical test in a new setting. Argyris and Schön, like Popper (all cited earlier), reserve the terms corroborated and confirmed to describe a theory which has achieved this provisional status.

Modus tollens provides a stronger basis for supporting scientific research when it is reinforced by explicit knowledge of, and vigilance against, the fallacy of affirming the consequent. Sound reasoning requires that the fallacy of affirming the consequent not be committed in any research that is to be considered rigorous.

## The Third Issue: Summative Validity

Having expressed the logic of empirical inquiry in science in formal notation (Appendices A and B), including mathematical notation (Tables 3 and 4), we searched for published examples of it. For statistical behavioral research in information systems, which constitutes a large portion of positivist information systems research, we sought out examples of modus tollens' major premise, minor premise, and conclusion as presented in Table 4. We encountered a surprise: We could find no such examples. And for interpretive research, we could find, arguably at best, just a few examples (e.g., Klein and Myers 1999; Trauth and Jessup 2000) of modus tollens as presented in Table 6. The lack of examples has led us to distinguish two types of validity, formative and summative, where the use of modus tollens is required in order to establish the latter. As we will explain, we hold the view that information systems research has done much to establish the formative validity of its theories, but has rarely applied the logic of modus tollens to establish their summative validity.

## Differentiating Formative Validity and Summative Validity

We define formative validity as an attribute of the process by which a theory is formed or built (we will use the two terms, "to form" and "to build," synonymously). We define summative validity as an attribute of the sum result or product of the process, namely, the theory. A theory achieves formative validity by following one or another accepted procedure in the process of its being formed. A theory, once formed, achieves summative validity by surviving an empirical test that uses the logic of modus tollens. Theory testing, we will show, involves comparing the theory's observational consequences with the observed evidence. Theory testing can lead to either of two outcomes: if the evidence is consistent with the theory, then the theory has summative validity; if not, then the theory lacks summative validity.

For a theory to have formative validity in grounded-theory research, the theory's variables or constructs must emerge from, or be “grounded” in, the data rather than be taken entirely from a previously published theory and imposed on the current set of data. For a theory to have formative validity in statistical research, the process of building it must involve, among other things, data obtained through random or representative, rather than biased, sampling. Thus a theory that is formulated from data describing a population's demographics, where the data were collected through biased sampling, would lack formative validity. Both of these examples focus on the quality of the theory-forming process, rather than on the quality of the theory itself as the sum result or product of the process. In contrast, for a theory to have summative validity, the theory must survive an empirical test involving modus tollens, regardless of how it was built, where survival is indicated by the observation of evidence consistent with what the theory predicts. Even if the theory was built in an accepted way so as to achieve formative validity, the theory could still fail an empirical test and thereby lack summative validity.

Two additional examples of summative validity and formative validity can help to clarify how they are different. As a systems developer, one may properly follow all the steps in an accepted systems development methodology when building a new information system (hence achieving formative validity), but still end up with an information system that is an adoption failure (indicating the lack of summative validity). And in the field of education, from which we borrow the terms formative and summative, a formative assessment examines the teaching–learning process while a summative assessment examines the result of that process, namely, the extent to which the student has learned (Bloom et al. 1971; Chang and Petersen 2006).

Of the two types, we consider summative validity to be more important. First, surviving empirical testing is necessary for any theory to be considered rigorous. Second, even though a theory that has formative validity is arguably more likely also to have summative validity because it was built systematically, summative validity does not necessarily follow. Consider our earlier examples of a qualitative researcher who correctly employs the coding procedures of grounded-theory research and a quantitative researcher who conducts sampling that is random rather than biased. In each case, where the researcher continues to form the theory in a way that is accepted and considered valid, one might rightfully argue that the resulting theory is more likely to turn out to be accurate and, therefore, also to have summative validity. However, the resulting product, once formed, remains open for testing. In other words, formative validity is not a sufficient condition for summative validity. Furthermore, there is also the consideration that even if a process of forming a theory relies on intuition and serendipity rather than accepted procedures (so that the theory would lack formative validity), this process can nonetheless result in a theory with which the evidence is consistent in subsequent testing.

A description of how statistical behavioral research in information systems has overlooked, but can still achieve, summative validity requires an extensive examination of certain aspects of statistical reasoning, which we cover in the next section. The extensiveness of the examination is justified by the dominating role that statistical behavioral research has had in the information systems discipline. Our examination pertaining to interpretive research follows in the section after the next.

## Where Positivist Research Needs to Establish Summative Validity

The MPMT framework sheds light on situations where positivist research in information systems needs to complete, or “follow through” to, the task of establishing summative validity. The focus here will be on positivist, behavioral information systems research that employs sampling-based, multivariate, hypothesis-testing methods. This type of research has traditionally engaged in (among other things) testing coefficients for significance, that is, determining the statistical significance of sample-based estimates of the coefficients of independent variables in a multivariate analysis. But the traditional testing of coefficients for significance, we will show, is distinct from the subsequent testing of the overall theory for summative validity.

Our description of how statistical behavioral research has not, but can, follow through to the point of establishing summative validity will cover four items: (1) aspects of current statistical practice, (2) the additional statistical analysis required for summative validity, (3) illustrations with three examples of positivist studies, and (4) replication and hold-back samples.

Aspects of Current Statistical Practice: Consider, to illustrate the testing for summative validity that still needs to take place, a theory that is mathematically operationalized as “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}X_{n}$ .” (We can alternatively use the general form “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, \ldots, X_{in})$ for i = 1, 2, ..., m,” but the simpler form will suffice for purposes of illustration.) The logic of modus tollens, as expressed mathematically in Table 4, will lead us to scrutinize something familiar which we routinely do, but do not question: the statistical hypothesis testing that concerns a coefficient $\beta_{i}$ (where the null hypothesis is $\beta_{i} = 0$ ). The scrutiny is needed to bring out the difference between testing coefficients for significance and testing the overall theory for summative validity.

For an example to use in our scrutiny, we turn to the technology acceptance model (Davis et al. 1989; also see Table 1), which provides a familiar example of “ $\mathbf{Y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\mathbf{X}_1 + \beta_2\mathbf{X}_2 + \ldots + \beta_n\mathbf{X}_n$ ” where Y refers to a person’s “behavioral intention to use” a given technology, $\mathbf{X}_1$ refers to this person’s “attitude toward using” the technology, and $\mathbf{X}_2$ refers to the same person's "perceived usefulness" of the technology. If we substitute BI, A, and U for Y, $\mathrm{X}_1$ , and $\mathrm{X}_2$ respectively, we would then have the relation "BI = $\beta_0 + \beta_1\mathrm{A} + \beta_2\mathrm{U}$ ," which is one of the three familiar equations used in operationalizing the technology acceptance model.

The way in which statistical analysis typically unfolds includes the following two tasks. In one, the researcher takes a sample from the population where the theory is being examined and then uses the sample to calculate a number $b_{i}$ to use as an estimate of the true (but unknown) value of the coefficient $\beta_{i}$ . In the other task, the researcher conducts a test of the null hypothesis, “ $\beta_{i}=0$ ” (which is the hypothesis that no relationship exists between independent variable $X_{i}$ and dependent variable Y) by determining if $b_{i}$ is statistically significant (or, equivalently stated, deciding if $\beta_{i}$ , the unknown value of which $b_{i}$ is only a sample-based approximation, is indeed different from zero). Figure 3 provides some details of statistical reasoning used for the purpose of testing the hypothesis “ $\beta_{i}=0$ .” Finding that $b_{i}$ is statistically significant would lead to the decision to reject the null hypothesis “ $\beta_{i}=0$ .” The importance of such a decision is that it would allow the sample-based estimate $b_{i}$ to be put forward as evidence consistent with the relationship theorized to exist between the independent variable $X_{i}$ and the dependent variable Y. A process that identifies relationships by testing coefficients for significance would be a process that contributes to building a theory.

In such a theory-forming effort, formative validity is achieved by using traditional methods of statistical inference (such as testing the null hypothesis, “ $\beta_{i} = 0$ ,” as just described) to identify relationships among variables. Still, the theory’s summative validity would need to be established. This is because the task of using sample data to calculate each $b_{i}$ and the task of testing each $b_{i}$ for statistical significance both presume that the variables are related to each other in the way that the theory specifies in “ $\mathrm{Y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\mathrm{X}_1 + \beta_2\mathrm{X}_2 + \ldots + \beta_n\mathrm{X}_n$ ”. However, the truth of the theory is exactly what is in question in the first place. As we will show, the empirical testing of a null hypothesis regarding a coefficient $\beta_{i}$ is not synonymous with the empirical testing of the overall theory. We may properly describe the task of using a sample to estimate each coefficient and the task of determining each such estimate’s statistical significance as preceding, but not substituting for, an empirical test of the overall theory to establish its summative validity. In its current practice, statistical behavioral information systems research has engaged in (among other things) testing coefficients for significance, but has not followed through to the point of testing the overall theory. A description of such a test follows.

The Additional Statistical Analysis Required for Summative Validity: Consider a theory mathematically operationalized in the form of an equation with just two independent variables, “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2}$ .” When instantiated in a given population, this equation may take the form, “ $Y_{predicted} = 1.5X_{1} + 0.3X_{2}$ ” where this could be the result of taking a random sample to estimate each coefficient $\beta_{i}$ . The following description of theory testing pertains to the general form, “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, ..., X_{in})$ for i = 1, 2, ..., m,” but “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2}$ ” will suffice for purposes of illustration. In this context, synonyms for “to estimate” include “to measure” and “to calibrate.” And if we were to continue with the technology acceptance model illustration, “ $Y_{predicted} = 1.5X_{1} + 0.3X_{2}$ ” would become “ $BI_{predicted} = 1.5A + 0.3U$ .”

Where the sample-based estimates $b_{1}=1.5$ and $b_{2}=0.3$ of the coefficients $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ are determined to be statistically significant, an empirical test of the overall theory could be conducted by calculating what “ $Y_{predicted}=1.5X_{1}+0.3X_{2}$ ” provides as the dependent variable’s predicted value, $y_{predicted}$ , and then comparing the predicted value to the observed value, $y_{new}$ . This would require observing an additional, out-of-sample data point, one that is apart from and in addition to the data points constituting the sample from which each $b_{i}$ was computed. The new data point would need to be taken from the same population so that the same coefficient estimates ( $b_{1}=1.5$ and $b_{2}=0.3$ ) can be used to calculate the prediction, $y_{predicted}$ . In the earlier illustration where Y, $X_{1}$ , and $X_{2}$ refer to the variables BI, A, and U in the technology acceptance model, the new data point would consist of the respective numerical values observed for an additional research subject’s behavioral intention to use the technology, the same research subject’s attitude toward using the technology, and the subject’s perceived usefulness for the technology. Suppose, for the sake of illustration, that there are no other dependent variables than Y (or BI) and that the values observed for this out-of-sample research subject are $y_{new}=1.9$ , $x_{1,new}=1.2$ , and $x_{2,new}=1.0$ .

For this new data point, the numerical values $x_{1,new} = 1.2$ and $x_{2,new} = 1.0$ would be plugged into the calibrated relation “ $Y_{predicted} = 1.5X_{1} + 0.3X_{2}$ ,” resulting in $y_{predicted} = 2.1$ as the numerical value predicted for the dependent variable. (In Tables 3 and 4, $y_{predicted}$ is calculated from $f_{k}(x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{n})$ ). The predicted value of the dependent variable ( $y_{predicted} = 2.1$ ) would then be compared to its observed value ( $y_{new} = 1.9$ ).

Obviously, 2.1 and 1.9 are different from each other, but this difference would not automatically allow the researcher to conclude that the prediction has failed. The reason is that the researcher would still need to account for the fact that “ $y_{predicted}$ = 2.1," in being calculated from the relation "Y $_{predicted}$ = 1.5X $_{1}$ + 0.3X $_{2}$ ," is affected by the error associated with the coefficients, 1.5 and 0.3, which are only estimates based on a sample. The researcher can account for this by establishing an allowed margin of error (taking the form of an interval) around the value of y $_{predicted}$ , within which the value of y $_{new}$ could fall and still allow the researcher to have sufficient confidence to make the decision that the value of y $_{new}$ is evidence consistent with the theory. The range of values in the allowed margin of error is a prediction interval. The proposition "y $_{predicted}$ = y $_{new}$ " is then rejected only if y $_{new}$ falls outside the prediction interval around y $_{predicted}$ . Standard statistics textbooks provide formulas for constructing prediction intervals for dependent variables (for example, for the bivariate case, formulas are offered by Neter et al. (1988, p. 626) and by Wonnacott and Wonnacott (1984, p. 349)). The concept of a prediction interval stands in contrast to the concept of a confidence interval; the former refers to a dependent variable's value, y $_{predicted}$ , around which an interval is constructed, whereas the latter refers to an interval that is constructed around an estimate b $_{i}$ of an independent variable's coefficient.

In the other possibility, the theory is not true, so that the disparity between $y_{new}$ and $y_{predicted}$ is simply the result of the theory's being wrong (i.e., the independent and dependent variables are not, after all, related to one another as indicated in the theory's mathematical operationalization, "Y = $\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2}$ "). We use the binomial distribution to determine the required number of failed predictions (see the row above “ $\alpha = 0.050$ ” in the table in Appendix C). If 100 out-of-sample points are taken, then the needed number of failed predictions is 10 or more (or the needed number of successful predictions is 90 or fewer) to justify making a decision, at the 95 percent confidence level, to reject the theory as true.

“Rejecting a theory,” it should be noted, can call for replacing the entire theory with a completely new theory or a rival theory, but “rejecting a theory” can also mean something much less drastic. It can call for rejecting just the current formulation of the theory, which can involve making some adjustments in it (for example, retaining most or all of the independent variables but changing the relationships between them, adding a new independent variable, introducing a moderating relationship, etc.).

A word of caution is in order regarding the outcome in which 9 out of 10, or 99 out of 100, data points fall inside their respective 95 percent prediction intervals. A larger number of successful predictions, while constituting more evidence consistent with the theory, would not be evidence that somehow better proves (than a smaller number of successful predictions) that the theory is true; to conclude otherwise would be to commit the fallacy of affirming the consequent. There is still a benefit, of course, associated with a larger number of out-of-sample data points that fall inside their respective prediction intervals. A larger number of successful predictions would increase the researcher's confidence (a subjective human sentiment) in his or her decision not to reject the theory but, we must emphasize, would not somehow translate into greater validity (an attribute of the theory). We are using the term confidence here with the same meaning it has in the term confidence interval.

Statistical techniques that use out-of-sample data points are nothing new. They are known as cross validation techniques, which include K-fold, leave-one-out, jackknife, and delete-d (Good 1999, pp. 180-181). In cross validation, a researcher sets aside part of the original sample for later use as out-of-sample data points, and uses the remaining data points as the sample for making estimates of population parameters (such as the coefficients $\beta_{i}$ and thereby fitting the multivariate model to the data. For each one of the out-of-sample data points, the researcher can calculate the dependent variable's predicted value ( $y_{predicted}$ ), compare it to the dependent variable's observed value ( $y_{new}$ ), identify the difference between them ( $y_{predicted}-y_{new}$ ) as an error, and then calculate a summary measure of all of the errors across all of the out-of-sample data points (such as the mean squared error).

What we offer that is new is the recognition that the use of a prediction interval (or the equivalent procedure of using a p-value) to test a theory's prediction $y_{predicted}$ with an out-of-sample data point is tantamount to an experiment that can help to establish the theory's summative validity. It is the mathematical notation in which the logic of modus tollens is expressed in Table 4 that leads us to see that this manner of using of a prediction interval with an out-of-sample data point constitutes an experiment. Based on the results of such experimentation (which would involve multiple experiments testing multiple predictions), the researcher may or may not justify the decision to reject the theory. This manner of testing a theory in order to establish its summative validity is further discussed below and in Appendix C.

Illustrations with Three Examples of Positivist Research: Table 1 provides three examples of positivist research conducted by well-known researchers and published in prominent journals: Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw (1989) in Management Science, Ang and Straub (1998) in MIS Quarterly, and Zhu and Kraemer (2005) in Information Systems Research. We are not randomly sampling these research studies for the purpose of somehow generalizing from them. Rather, we are specifically identifying them on the basis of their being conducted by well-known scholars, appearing in major journals, and spanning a long period of time for the purpose of suggesting the magnitude of the implications that the issue of summative validity has for statistical behavioral research in information systems.

Each of the three studies examines a theory that its authors express in the form of a boxes-and-arrows diagram. In Table 1, we re-express each theory by mathematically operationalizing it in the form, “ $Y_{i} = f(X_{i1}, X_{i2}, ..., X_{in})$ for i = 1, 2, ..., m.” The authors of each study carry out, among other things, hypothesis testing associated with each sample-based estimate $b_{i}$ of the coefficient $\beta_{i}$ . In fitting their equations to the data, they demonstrate the formative validity of their respective theories. However, in none of the studies do the authors use any out-of-sample data points; thus, there is no instance in any of the studies in which the authors compare a predicted value of the dependent variable to its observed value. These authors have tested coefficients for significance, but they have not tested their overall theories for summative validity. In this regard, these studies are typical of statistical behavioral research in information systems.

The MPMT framework can be readily applied in an effort to establish each theory's summative validity. Consider Davis et al.'s technology acceptance model and its mathematical operationalization in the form of the relation, “ $\mathrm{BI} = \beta_{1}\mathrm{A} + \beta_{2}\mathrm{EOU}$ ” (see Table 1). (For the sake of illustration, we will suppose that the technology acceptance model involves no other relations.) The population that Davis et al. use for the purpose of statistical inference consists of all MBA students at the University of Michigan at the time of the study. In calibrating or fitting “ $\mathrm{BI} = \beta_{1}\mathrm{A} + \beta_{2}\mathrm{EOU}$ ” to this population, they take a sample (of MBA students) from it, resulting in “ $\mathrm{BI}_{\text {predicted}} = 0.27\mathrm{A} + 0.48\mathrm{EOU}$ ” where the two coefficients are statistically significant at the 0.01 and 0.001 levels, respectively (p. 992). Then, if Davis et al. were to apply the MPMT framework, they would proceed to consider an out-of-sample MBA student, for whom the numerical values of BI, A, and EOU would be observed (we designate them as $bi_{\text {new}}, a_{\text {new}}$ , and $eou_{\text {new}}$ ). Suppose that, for this new research subject, the dependent variable BI's observed value $bi_{\text {new}}$ were to fall outside the $100(1 - \alpha)\%$ prediction interval around the dependent variable's predicted value $bi_{\text {predicted}}$ , thereby allowing the researcher to justify making a decision, at the $100(1 - \alpha)\%$ level of confidence, that the prediction fails. And suppose that, where $\alpha = .050$ , the researcher tests a total of 100 such predictions, 10 of which fail. Then according to the table in Appendix C, the researcher could justify making the decision, at the $95\%$ level of confidence, to reject the theory (here, the technology acceptance model) that made the 100 predictions, where the theory includes not only its mathematical operationalization, but also its verbally expressed assumptions, conditions, definitions, and other elements. However, if 4, 5, or 6 predictions fail (instead of 10), the evidence could be considered “in the ball park,” or consistent with the theory's being true, thereby establishing the theory's summative validity.

In Table 1, the proposition provided by Ang and Straub and the five propositions offered by Zhu and Kraemer would need to be similarly tested if the summative validity of their theories is to be established.

Past positivist research in information systems, in the form of statistical behavioral research, provides a wealth of theories whose formative validity has been established. Information systems scholars who have engaged in statistical behavioral research deserve recognition for having met the Herculean challenge of providing, to the information systems discipline, the needed theories in the first place. Should these studies have also established the summative validity of the theories they put forward? There is no methodological reason that a theory whose coefficients have been tested for significance must, in the same study, also be tested for summative validity. Its summative validity can be established by the same or a different researcher in a new, separate study. Research that offers new theories and establishes their formative validity will continue to be no less important and to make no less of a contribution than past research which has done this, such as the research by Ang and Straub, by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw, and by Zhu and Kraemer.

Replication and Hold-Back Samples: There are two additional concerns, associated with statistical behavioral research, which require attention. The first concern is: May the task of observing new, out-of-sample data points for the purpose of testing a theory be considered nothing more than a replication? The answer depends on what is meant by “replication.”

Suppose a researcher uses sample data to compute each $b_{i}$ in “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}X_{n}$ ” and determines each $b_{i}$ to be statistically significant. Then, in a second study, the researcher uses sample data from the same population to recompute each $b_{i}$ and redetermines each $b_{i}$ to be statistically significant. The second study may be properly considered a replication of computing the coefficients in “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}X_{n}$ ” and a replication of determining their statistical significance, but this can occur without the overall theory’s ever having been tested. If no out-of-sample data points are used in either or both studies, then there is no replication of any test of the theory. A study that does test the overall theory, which would call for using one or more out-of-sample data points from the same population with which the coefficients were estimated, would be proceeding beyond the tasks of (re)estimating the coefficients and (re)establishing their statistical significance.

Suppose that another researcher also uses sample data from a given population to compute each $b_{i}$ in “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}X_{n}$ ” and determines each $b_{i}$ to be statistically significant. Then, this researcher uses sample data from a different population to recompute each $b_{i}$ and redetermines each $b_{i}$ to be statistically significant. These results from sampling the latter population may be properly considered a replication of establishing the significance of the coefficients in “ $Y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}X_{1} + \beta_{2}X_{2} + \ldots + \beta_{n}X_{n}$ .” But again, redetermining each $b_{i}$ to be statistically significant can occur without any empirical test of the overall theory ever having been conducted. If no out-of-sample data points are used in the second study (or, for that matter, the first study), then there is no replication of any test of the theory. Figure 3 provides a detailed comparison of the differences between testing coefficients for significance and testing an overall theory.

To clarify this discussion, consider the study by Venkatesh et al. (2003) in which they examine eight theories (including the theory of reasoned action, the technology acceptance model, and the theory of planned behavior) in two different populations. $^{4}$ For each of the eight theories, Venkatesh et al. performed, in one population, the task of estimating the theories' coefficients and the task of testing them for significance and then, in a different population, replicated the two tasks. They do not, however, make use of any out-of-sample data points, which are required in testing theories for their summative validity. Venkatesh et al. also examine a ninth theory (the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology, or UTAUT) in a new population (different from the first two), $^{5}$ but once again, they make use of no out-of-sample data points. We emphasize, as mentioned earlier, that a study which tests coefficients for significance and establishes a theory's formative validity (as Venkatesh et al. have done for UTAUT) need not also establish the theory's summative validity. The latter can be established in a future study that may be conducted by Venkatesh et al. or other scholars.

The second concern, which is related to the first, is that a researcher who uses a hold-back sample to replicate the estimation of the coefficients would not be testing the theory, but would just be accomplishing a reestimation of the coefficients. Still, each data point in the hold-back sample would have the potential to be used as an out-of-sample data point in an empirical test of the overall theory, where the researcher uses prediction intervals (or the corresponding procedure of testing with p-values) to compare the observed value of the dependent variable to its predicted value. In this manner, the number of such data points in the hold-back sample can be the number of different experiments that the theory can be subjected to, where each such empirical test could be properly considered a replication of every other empirical test.

Statistical behavioral research is not alone in still needing to establish the summative validity of its theories. In this regard, it sits in the good company of interpretive research.

## The Need to Establish Summative Validity in Interpretive Research

As Tables 5 and 6 indicate, the MPMT framework indicates that interpretive research, no less than positivist research, also needs to establish the summative validity of its theories in order for it to be considered rigorous. Interpretive research, however, has rarely completed, or followed through to, the task of establishing summative validity. A telling artifact is the MIS Quarterly special issue on intensive research (it consisted of six articles published across three issues: March 1999, March 2000, and September 2000). A requirement for a paper to be published in the special issue was for the paper to specify and apply a set of criteria with which one could substantiate the quality of the paper's qualitative research. The first five studies in Table 8 involved interpretive research.

Table 8 shows the different validity criteria offered by the six papers. In this essay, we are categorizing almost all of the criteria as falling under the heading of formative validity. For the sake of argument, suppose that some of the criteria that we have classified under formative validity were to be reclassified under the heading of summative validity. Even so, none of the papers differentiates summative validity from formative validity. Therefore, none of them recognizes or treats the former as a necessary condition for a theory to achieve in order to be considered scientific.

If we apply the metaphor of baking-versus-tasting a cake, we regard most of the criteria in Table 8 as more akin to the ingredients that go into baking a cake (where formative validity would be concerned with whether the right ingredients are used) than to the quality of the resulting cake (where summative validity would be concerned with testing whether the resulting cake tastes good). In Table 8, examples of ingredients include: “the principle of contextualization,” “particularizing everyday life,” “self revealing writing,” “providing rich detail about the organization,” “selection of appropriate field setting and methods,” and “how do we capture the relevant knowledge?” These examples refer to research activities involved in forming a theory, not those involved in assessing the validity of a theory after it has been formed. Such an assessment, to determine if the evidence is consistent with the theory’s observational consequences, would involve empirical testing using the logic of modus tollens.

Interpretive research, like positivist research, already has ways to establish the summative validity of a theory which are already available and ready for researchers to apply. For example, Sanday (1983, p. 230) offers the following:

If, after having completed [the development of a theory by doing an] ethnography, the observer can communicate the rules for proper and predictable conduct as judged by the people studied, he or she has produced a successful product. The ethnographer is like the linguist who has recorded a foreign language so that others can learn the rules for producing intelligible speech in that language. As Frake (quoted in Wolcott, 1975: 121) says, the adequacy of ethnography is to be evaluated “by the ability of a stranger to the culture (who may be the ethnographer) to use the ethnography’s statements as instructions for appropriately anticipating the scenes of the society.”

In the major premise of modus tollens, “if p is true, then q is true,” p could be “the researcher’s ethnographically developed theory of the culture and social structure of the people in organization O” and q could be “after the researcher shares her ethnography, including the rules for proper and predictable conduct in organization O, to a stranger S, the stranger S will subsequently be successful in anticipating, and not be surprised by, the people’s behaviors in the upcoming scenes C and D in organization O.”

Schutz (1962) also offers a way to assess the summative validity of an interpretive theory (p. 64):

Each term in a [theory] of human action must be constructed in such a way that a human act performed within the real world by an individual actor as indicated by the [theory] would be understandable to the actor himself as well as to his fellow-men in terms of the common-sense interpretation of everyday life.

Here, p could be “the researcher’s theory of human action T” and q could be “the human acts Q and R, performed by the vice president for operations at Acme Corporation as indicated by the theory of human action T, will be understandable to the vice president herself as well as to her colleagues in terms of their own common-sense interpretation of everyday work life at Acme Corporation." The ways of using p and q for the purpose of theory testing as suggested by Sanday and by Schutz are instances of the general form of the logics appearing in Tables 5 and 6.

Past qualitative and interpretive research in information systems, like past quantitative and positivist research, provides a wealth of theories with formative validity and should, therefore, be credited for having moved the information systems discipline forward by accomplishing the major task of providing needed theories in the first place. Just as for quantitative and positivist research, qualitative and interpretive research that offers new theories and establishes their formative validity will continue to be no less important and to make no less of a contribution than past research which has done this, such as the research appearing in the MIS Quarterly special issue on qualitative research.

## Additional Implications of the Modus Ponens, Modus Tollens Framework

We discuss three additional major implications of the MPMT framework.

## The Need to Test Old Theories for Summative Validity

For the most part, we information systems researchers have tested theories for formative validity, but not summative validity. We need to test our theories through their observational consequences (which include predictions) rather than just build them and fit them to the empirical settings where we formulate them. By adding the use of prediction intervals to existing cross-validation techniques, statistical information systems researchers can establish the summative validity of theories that have already been published without collecting any new data. The basic idea is to set aside part of the original sample as out-of-sample data points. One way to do this is to take a subsample from the original sample used in the published study, to use the subsample to estimate the multivariate model, and then to use each one of the remaining data points in a different experiment testing the theory. Another way is to use the original sample, but to leave out just one data point, where the remaining data points are used to estimate the model and the left-out data point is used in an experiment to test the theory; furthermore, each data point takes a turn as the left-out data point, thereby allowing the researcher to conduct as many experiments as there are data points in the sample. Appendix C illustrates the use of the latter cross validation procedure (known as “leave one out”) combined with prediction intervals. Furthermore, just as statistical behavioral theories can be reexamined with the original data, the same can be done with qualitative and interpretive theories if the data had been systematically archived (for instance, using what Yin (1994) calls a “case study database”).

## Rigor in Relevant Research

The MPMT framework can provide a scientific basis not only for theory whose main objective is explanation, but also for theory whose main objective is action. The importance of action research and design research follows from the fact that the discipline of information systems is not a basic or pure science. It is a field of professional study with constituents in government, business, and other organizations to whom it is obligated to offer research with relevance to real-world practice. Just as the logics of modus ponens and of modus tollens are blind to whether their propositions happen to be used in positivist research or interpretive research, they are blind to whether their propositions happen to be used in basic/pure research or relevant/applied research.

The forms of positivist and interpretive research we addressed above are primarily interested in theory whose main objective is to observe and explain phenomena. In contrast, action research (Baskerville and Myers 2004) and design research (Hevner et al. 2004) are concerned not only with observations and explanations of phenomenon, but also with actions that intervene in and alter these phenomena. Action research and design research that use positivist and interpretive methods may avail themselves of the use of the MPMT framework as described previously to provide a scientific basis for their theories. In the following, we offer a general description and two illustrations of how the MPMT framework can provide a scientific basis for theories of action.

For action research, what would p and q stand for in the three statements of modus tollens (“if p is true, then q is true,” “q is not true,” “therefore, p is not true”)? As shown in Table 7, p can stand for the proposition, “a theory of action T for solving a certain type of organizational problem is effective” and q can stand for the proposition, “a particular action A prescribed by the theory of action T for the organizational problem’s instantiation P in organization O will solve the problem P.” And in design research, also mentioned in Table 7, p could be “a design theory T for solving a certain type of organizational problem is effective” and q could be “a particular artifact A prescribed by the design theory T for the organizational problem’s instantiation P in organization O will solve the problem

P.” (Lee [2007] has examined the parallel between action research and design science.)

Providing us with material for an example of the logic of modus tollens in action research are Kohli and Kettinger (2004). For one instance of the major premise “if p is true, then q is true” in their action research, the theory of action p is (pp. 371-372)

Proposition A: Greater information transparency through the use of a performance monitoring information system (providing valid measures of behaviors and outcomes) will lead to greater goal congruence between the principal (hospital) and the agents (physicians)

and the observational consequence q is

the physicians [in Kohli and Kettinger's field site will] begin to use the data [from the clinical DSS] to examine their practice and ultimately adopt quality improvement and/or cost cutting clinical procedures.

In applying the logic of modus tollens, Kohli and Kettinger's action research satisfies a necessary condition for rigor. Also following from the logic of modus tollens is that an observation (or even many observations) of a successful action (i.e., where the minor premise is “q is true”) may never prove the theory of action to be correct (i.e., may never lead to the conclusion “p is true”), but the observation of an unsuccessful action (i.e., where the minor premise is “not q”) can be logically sufficient to disprove the theory.

Hevner et al. (2004) interpret, as an example of design research, the work of Markus et al. (2002), who examine the goal of achieving success in “the development of information systems to support emergent knowledge processes (EKPs)” (Hevner et al., p. 95). Markus et al. create an artifact, TOP Modeler (an implemented software system), with which to achieve this goal. “The TOP Modeler supports a development process incorporating the six principles for developing systems to support EKPs” (Hevner et al., p. 96), where we frame these six principles as constituting p in the major premise of modus tollens, “if p is true, then q is true.” Markus et al. refer to them as “EKP Design Theory Principles” (p. 188), which are “Design for Customer Engagement by Seeking Out Naïve Users” (p. 188), “Design for Knowledge Translation Through Radical Iteration with Functional Prototypes” (p. 193), “Design for Offline Action” (p. 196), “Integrate Expert Knowledge with Local Knowledge Sharing” (p. 196), “Design for Implicit Guidance Through a Dialectical Development Process” (p. 199), and “Componentize Everything, Including the Knowledge-base" (p. 202). If $p$ is true (that is, if the proposition, that the six design principles are valid, is true), then $q$ is true (that is, the proposition, that the TOP Modeler, a particular artifact implementing these design principles, will be successful in achieving the given goal, is true). In applying the logic of modus tollens, Markus et al.'s research satisfies a necessary condition for rigor. And as in the case of Kohli and Kettinger, one may never declare the proposition $p$ (here, that the six design principles are valid) to be true, no matter how many artifacts produce results consistent with the design principles. At best, whenever the proposition survives an empirical test, one may declare it to be valid, where one also needs to make explicit that the status of being valid is always tentative and can never be conclusively established.

The preceding discussion shows that relevant research, such as action research and design research, are as suitable for the application of certain principles of logic in general, and scientific reasoning in particular, as are quantitative, positivist research and qualitative, interpretive research. The rigor of science is, therefore, achievable in relevant research not necessarily by adopting the procedures of sampling-based multivariate hypothesis testing or other quantitative methods, but by adopting the MPMT framework. Relevant research, such as action research and design research, certainly may exercise the option of adopting the procedures of sampling-based multivariate hypothesis testing. But these procedures are never, by themselves, necessary or sufficient for action research, design research, or any other form of inquiry to achieve rigor.

## New Directions Needed in Methodological Research

This line of reasoning leads to the third and last implication of the MPMT framework. There is a need to initiate a new direction in the ongoing development of research methods in the information systems discipline. Currently, in positivist information systems research, there is an emphasis on developing increasingly rigorous methodological techniques that address formative validity (that is, increasingly rigorous ways of measuring $\beta_{i}$ and testing for the statistical significance of its estimated value, $b_{i}$ ). However, there is no less of a need to develop rigorous techniques for empirically testing an overall theory so as to establish its summative validity. And as Table 8 suggests, interpretive information systems research has also concentrated on the development of research methods that address formative validity and, therefore, also needs to turn some attention to the task of developing research methods for addressing summative validity.

## Conclusion

The basic argument that we have been making is actually an old one. There is nothing new about modus tollens or formal logic in general, but a call for the conscientious application of such logic in empirical inquiry is new. Our call for returning to the basics of reasoning is altogether compatible with current streams of positivist, interpretive, action, and design research. The MPMT framework and the notions of formative validity and summative validity affirm what these streams of research have already accomplished, as well as point to what remains to be done.

We may conclude that the information systems discipline, in having built up a large store of theories that have formative validity, has plentiful material with which to build up an equally large store of theories that will have, in addition, summative validity and perhaps even relevance. How would we recognize the situation in which the discipline of information systems has come to accept the requisite role of modus tollens in establishing summative validity? The signs may include the following: (1) information systems researchers who now separate themselves based on the approaches they take (including positivist research, interpretive research, action research, and design research) will instead see themselves as members of the same team who, albeit playing different positions on the team, are pursuing the common goal of building a cumulative body of knowledge in the information systems discipline, which they can do by building on a common scientific basis; (2) information systems researchers will no longer be committing the fallacy of affirming the consequent, particularly in their commentaries on case studies; and (3) the different research streams in the information systems discipline will be giving due recognition to summative validity, whether established by the same researcher in the same study that demonstrates the theory's formative validity, or by the same or a different researcher in a completely separate study. And studies that establish the formative validity of theories (such as the studies by Ang and Straub, Davis et al., Venkatesh et al., and Zhu and Kraemer) will continue to be no less important than they are now.

One may always choose to define a scientific basis so that it includes additional elements, such as mathematical propositions, numerical data, and statistical analysis. Still, regardless of whatever additional elements one might wish to include, no credible scientific research may ignore the fundamentals of formal logic. The discipline of information systems needs to consider returning to the basics, in particular, to the logic of modus ponens and the logic of modus tollens. And in doing so, the information systems discipline will fare better in its efforts to achieve research that is both rigorous and relevant.

## Appendix C

## Testing a Theory with Prediction Intervals and Out-of-Sample Data Points

To test a theory in its mathematically operationalized form, such as the relation “BI = $\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}A + \beta_{2}U$ ,” a researcher may proceed by combining the use of prediction intervals with the “leave one out” cross-validation technique, where the left-out data point plays the role of the out-of-sample data point and where each data point takes a turn at playing this role. The result would be as many predictions of BI as there are data points. If the theory is true and if a researcher is doing the testing with 95 percent prediction intervals, then the researcher would expect, on average, 95 percent of the predicted values of BI to be successful (i.e., to fall inside their respective 95 percent prediction intervals). However, how much would the percentage of successful predictions need to fall short of 95 before the researcher loses confidence in the theory and therefore decides to reject it as true?

Consider the case where the total number of predictions tested is 100 and the number of successful predictions only manages to reach 90. Does 90 fall sufficiently short of the expected 95 for the researcher to make the decision to reject the theory? A task to be performed in order to address this question is to calculate the probability that the number of successful predictions will not manage to exceed 90 in the situation where the theory is true and 100 predictions are tested. This would also be the probability of making a decision error if one's decision rule is to reject the theory if the number of successful predictions will not manage to exceed 90.

To calculate this probability, the researcher relies on the binomial distribution, where (1) the total number of trials, n, is 100 (where each prediction constitutes a trial), (2) the number of successful trials, s, can be 0, or 1, or 2, ..., 90, and (3) the probability that any given trial is successful is 95 percent. To calculate the probability, in the situation where the theory is true and 100 predictions are tested, that the number of successful predictions will not manage to exceed 90, the researcher would apply the binomial distribution 91 times, each time with a different value of s, where s ranges from 0 to 90. Then the researcher would add up the 91 different probabilities. The sixth row of Table C1 indicates that the 91 different probabilities add up to 0.028.

The number 0.028 means that, if the theory is true, (1) there is only a 2.8 percent probability that the number of successful predictions will not manage to exceed 90 and (2) the researcher will therefore face only a 2.8 percent probability of being incorrect in following the decision rule to reject the theory in the event that 90 or fewer successful predictions out of 100 are observed. Suppose the researcher had chosen, prior to any testing, 0.050 or 5.0 percent as the largest probability that she would find tolerable for being incorrect when making the decision to reject the theory. Because 2.8 percent falls within the tolerable 5.0 percent, the researcher would be compelled to make the decision to reject the theory. In this case, the evidence would indicate that the theory fails empirical testing and therefore lacks summative validity.

Suppose that the experimental result is 91 successful predictions instead of 90. The seventh row of the same table indicates that the researcher would face a 0.063 or 6.3 percent probability of being incorrect in making the decision to reject the theory based on observing 91 or fewer successful predictions out of 100. Because 6.3 percent exceeds the 5.0 percent that the researcher is willing to tolerate, the researcher would not make the decision to reject the theory. In this case, the decision would be that the theory survives empirical testing and thereby achieves summative validity.

In general, the 5.0 percent that the researcher in our illustration finds tolerable is called the “critical value, $\alpha$ ,” also known as the significance level or level of statistical significance. It is related to what is called the confidence level, whose value is $(1-\alpha)$ . The experimental result of 6.3 percent, corresponding to the observed 91 successful predictions, is an instance of what is called the “p-value” or “p.” The decision rule for rejecting the theory can be expressed as, “reject the theory if p < $\alpha$ .”

A different researcher might hold the opinion that a higher number of successful predictions should be reached—higher than 91 out of 100—to establish the theory’s summative validity. Certainly, a higher bar may be set. Just as the common use of $\alpha = 0.050$ as the size of the rejection region in conventional hypothesis testing is a matter of subjective choice, the number of successful predictions to be deemed necessary to establish a theory’s summative validity is also a matter of subjective choice. However, with regard to the matter of rejecting a theory, even a proponent of the theory would concede that the experimental outcome of a p-value less than 5.0 percent would constitute credible evidence for a rejection decision. Should a value of $\alpha$ greater than 5.0 percent (in our example, this would correspond to 91 or more successful predictions) be established as the threshold for a rejection decision? This would depend on the subjective choice of a value for a, where such a value should be acceptable both to the researcher and to the audience for his or her research. Changing $\alpha$ from 0.050 to a different value $\alpha_{new}$ , however, would also call for changing the 95 percent prediction intervals to $100(1-\alpha_{new})\%$ prediction intervals.

As mentioned in the essay, “rejecting a theory” can call for replacing the entire theory with a completely new or rival theory, but rejecting a theory can also mean something much less drastic: it can call for rejecting just the current formulation of the theory, which could involve making some adjustments in it (e.g., retaining most or all of the independent variables but changing the relationships between them, adding a new independent variable, introducing a moderating relationship, etc.).

We emphasize that summative validity never indicates the theory to be true. It only indicates that there is sufficient evidence consistent with theory for the researcher to be confident in deciding not to reject it, but to continue using and testing it.

For purposes other than those in this study, the second author collected data from a project financed by the Saudi Arabian government to assess factors that affect the acceptance and use of computers (as a technology) by knowledge workers in Saudi Arabia. The participating organizations represented various banking, merchandising, manufacturing, and petroleum industries. The survey solicited responses from professional knowledge workers in these organizations engaged in the use of desk top computers for the purpose of their work. Through this procedure, a total of 1,190 survey responses were collected. The survey collected data on three of the technology acceptance model's constructs: perceived ease of use (EOU), perceived usefulness (U), and attitude (A). We now use the data to illustrate how to combine the use of prediction intervals with the “leave one out” cross-validation technique. Each illustration involves testing predictions with 95 percent prediction intervals. To make the decision to reject or not reject the theory, each illustration uses $\alpha = 0.050$ .

Suppose we use the technology acceptance model to play the role of the theory being tested. Suppose further that, for the sake of illustration, this theory is mathematically operationalized as just one relation, “ $\mathrm{BI} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\mathrm{A} + \beta_2\mathrm{U}$ .” Combining the use of prediction intervals with the “leave one out” cross-validation technique, we let each and every one of the 1,190 sample points take a turn at playing the role of the out-of-sample data point. We thus conduct 1,190 experiments, each one of which tests the theory mathematically operationalized as “ $\mathrm{BI} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\mathrm{A} + \beta_2\mathrm{U}$ .” It turns out that, in the 1,190 experiments, 1,121 of the predictions fall inside their 95 percent prediction intervals. For the 1,190 experiments, the number of predictions expected to fall inside their 95 percent prediction intervals is 1,131 (i.e., 95 percent of 1,190). Does reaching a level of only 1,121 successful predictions (which is 94 percent of all 1,190 predictions) instead of the expected number of 1,131 (which is 95 percent of all 1,190 predictions) provide sufficient justification to make the decision to reject the theory? Using the binomial distribution (where $n = 1,190$ predictions, $s = 0,1,2,\dots,1,121$ successful predictions, and the probability that a prediction is successful is 0.95), we can calculate the probability of reaching, at most, 1,121 successful predictions when the relation “ $\mathrm{BI} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\mathrm{A} + \beta_2\mathrm{U}$ ” is true. This probability is $p = 0.117$ or 11.7 percent, which exceeds the 5 percent that the researcher is willing to tolerate. Therefore our experimental result of reaching only 1,121 successful predictions or 94 percent does not provide sufficient justification for making a decision to reject the theory as true. Instead, the evidence is considered to be consistent with the theory. In this way, we are successfully establishing the summative validity of the theory. The evidence allows the researcher not to reject the theory, but to continue using and testing it.

What if the sample size had been much smaller than 1,190? We repeat this exercise with a sample of only 250. (We randomly select 250 from the original 1,190 and regard the rest as if they had never been collected.) The result is 230 predictions which fall inside their 95 percent prediction intervals, for a 92 percent success rate. Using the binomial distribution (where n = 250 predictions, s = 0, 1, 2, ..., 230 successful predictions, and the probability that a prediction is successful is 0.95), we can calculate the p-value associated with reaching, at most, 230 successful predictions out of a total of 250 predictions. The p-value turns out to be p = 0.027, which is sufficiently small, when the desired significance level $\alpha$ is 0.050, to justify the decision to reject the theory as true. In this case, the evidence indicates that the theory fails empirical testing and therefore lacks summative validity.

Next, what if the sample size were 500? The result is 469 predictions which fall inside their 95 percent prediction intervals, for a 92 percent success rate. Using the binomial distribution (n = 500 predictions, s = 0, 1, 2, ..., 469 successful predictions, and the probability that a prediction is successful is 0.95), we calculate that the p-value which corresponds to 469 successful predictions to be 0.131. Because the probability of being incorrect in making the decision to reject the theory, 13.1 percent, exceeds the tolerable level of 5 percent, the evidence allows the researcher not to reject the theory, but to continue using and testing it.

In the final repetition of this exercise, we use a sample size of 750, of which 708 (94 percent) involve predicted values of BI that fall inside their 95 percent prediction intervals. Using the binomial distribution, we determine that the p-value for this result is 0.247, which is greater than the desired level of significance of $\alpha = 0.050$ . This means that the evidence allows the researcher to make the decision, at the 5 percent significance level, not to reject the theory, but to continue using and testing it.

The preceding illustrations show that samples of different sizes can lead to different decisions. The illustration with the smallest sample size, 250, leads to the decision to reject the theory, but the remaining three illustrations, whose sample sizes are 500, 750, and 1,190, all lead to the opposite decision. In statistical inference, larger sample sizes are preferred. A satisfactory sample size would depend on several factors, including the number of different variables in the model.

Finally, we emphasize that the decision to reject a theory through the use of prediction intervals and out-of-sample data points would pose an excellent and intriguing opportunity for qualitative researchers. Consider the first example where 100 predicted values of BI are tested and only 90 of the predictions are successful. Interviews with the 10 people for whom the theory predicted incorrectly, if they could be identified, could suggest the need for a new variable, a modification of one or more of the relationships between the theory's variables, the addition of another equation, and so forth. A case study of the organizational setting, with or without identifying the 10 individuals, could likewise reveal factors that caused the theory to predict inaccurately. The case study may be positivist or interpretive. Action research and design research would also be appropriate. Even a case study of a completely different organization, if motivated by the search for reasons that the theory had failed, could be helpful. Interestingly, our suggestion shows how quantitative research can serve as a useful “front end” to qualitative research; this is the reverse of how the relationship between them is usually portrayed.
