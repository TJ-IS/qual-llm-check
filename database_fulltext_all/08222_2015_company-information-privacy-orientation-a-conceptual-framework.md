# Company information privacy orientation: a conceptual framework

Kathleen E. Greenaway,\* Yolande E. Chan<sup>†</sup> & Robert E. Crossler<sup>‡</sup>

\*Reid Here & Associates Wellington, Ontario, Canada, email: kgreenaway@reidhere.com, <sup>†</sup>Management Information Systems, Queen’s University, Kingston, Ontario, Canada, and <sup>‡</sup>Management and Information Systems, Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS USA

Contemporary organisations struggle to develop effective responses to the complex challenge of deploying sophisticated information technology systems in an era characterised increasingly by customer demands for privacy. In this paper, we develop a conceptual framework of Company Information Privacy Orientation that attempts to reconcile the differences between the organisation’s information management objectives and its ethical and legal obligations to address customers’ privacy. Control theory and justice theory are utilised to build an organisation-level framework that is composed of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ethical obligation to its customers, its customer information management strategy and its assessment of the risks to its business created by legal demands to provide customer information privacy. The four different types of company privacy orientation pro<sup>fi</sup>les that emerge from this conceptual framework are then discussed, along with implications for future research.

Keywords: organisational level privacy, privacy orientation, information management, procedural justice, privacy laws, privacy ethics, privacy conceptual framework, control agency theory

## INTRODUCTION

Contemporary organisations struggle to develop effective responses to the complex challenge of deploying sophisticated information technology systems, such as Customer Relationship Management (CRM) and data analytics, in an era characterised increasingly by customer demands for privacy. As the digital climate continues to change, organisations face a balancing act between providing appropriate privacy protections in the midst of changing regulations while embracing technological innovations (Ernst & Young, 2013). Some organisations take a reactionary approach to privacy and focus on what privacy safeguards can prevent, while others look beyond privacy safeguards as a protection mechanism and are empowering customers with an ability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence how their information is used (Price Waterhouse Coopers, 2014).

Regardless of the approach, it is evident that the privacy orientation that organisations take towards their customers affects how they operate their businesses.

The privacy orientation that organisations take towards their customers is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by legal regulations. One example of legal regulations in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the approach an organisation takes towards providing privacy protection to individuals is the European ‘right to be forgotten’. The European Court of Justice mandated that Europeans had the right to ask Google to stop provid ing links to web content that portrays them negatively (Tung, 2014). This court order was necessary, as Google wanted to preserve all data in its original form as an archive rather than provide individuals with control over the removal of their own personal information (Tung, 2014). Google implemented the regulation in such a manner as to restrict its compliance in two ways. Firstly, it left as much information in search results as possible including <sup>fl</sup>agging search results that have had some information removed (Halliday, 2014). Second, Google removed only the search results from those searches conducted and displayed in the European Union (Brian, 2014). This case demonstrates that although there are regulations in place to provide cus tomers with a certain level of privacy, some organisations will <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to minimally comply with regulations that do not align with their business strategy.

Despite the impact that organisational level decisions can have on the privacy of individuals, as illustrated earlier, the information systems literature lacks research that explores information privacy in this context (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). Belanger and Crossler (2011, p. 1029) state that ‘[r]esearchers should explore whether organisations have different stakes in information privacy than the consumers they serve.’ In order to accomplish such a task, it is necessary to understand both the information privacy considerations of organisations and consumers. How different organisations reconcile their information management objectives with their ethical and legal obligations to address customers’ privacy concerns forms a framework we call Company Information Privacy Orientation (CIPO). We capture these inherent tensions by de<sup>fi</sup>ning CIPO as an organisation’s approach to protecting and using its customers’ personal information, where customers are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as individual consumers.

Building on Fair Information Principles (FIP)<sup>1</sup>, we characterise CIPO as the extent to which a <sup>fi</sup>rm<sup>2</sup> delivers consistent, transparent privacy protection to its customers while achieving other important organisational objectives. FIP are ‘procedures that provide individuals with contro over the disclosure and subsequent use of their personal information’ (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999, pp. 107). Providing these privacy protections, while at the same time achieving their organisational objectives, creates an ‘organisational’ privacy calculus similar to the privacy calculus that individuals engage in. At an individual level, people balance the privacy they are will ing to give up for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t that they are going to receive. Organisationally, this equates to the privacy protection they are giving the customers balanced with the objectives or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the organisation is trying to achieve.

Researchers have typically de<sup>fi</sup>ned the privacy challenge to <sup>fi</sup>rms as a problem of either ethics (Mason, 1986), information management (Culnan, 1993) or law (Culnan et al., 1994). These three dimensions, which also relate to FIP, help de<sup>fi</sup>ne the organisationa privacy challenge addressed by the CIPO framework. As such, this framework is composed of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s perceived ethical obligation to its customers, its customer information management strategy and its assessment of the legal risks to its business imposed by external demands to provide information privacy to customers. These three dimensions are distinguished by the extent to which control (Lind & Tyler, 1988) and justice (Greenberg, 1987) provisions are manifested throughout organisational privacy policies (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999) and actual behaviours.

Drawing primarily on the literature on information systems, marketing and business ethics, we contribute to the underdeveloped literature on information privacy as an organisational phenomenon in two ways. Firstly, we offer a middle-level explanatory theory (Gregor, 2006) of how ethical obligations, information management strategies and legal risk assessments combine to create four CIPOs. Secondly, we contribute a multi-level theory (Rousseau, 1985) that integrates corporate policy (ethical obligation), operational actions (information managemen strategy) and top management views (legal risk assessment).

This article is organised into four sections. First, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review the organisational level privacy literature to position our contribution. Then we discuss the theoretical foundations of the CIPO framework. Next, we introduce the CIPO conceptual framework and discuss how the the oretical foundations in<sup>fl</sup>uence a company’s orientation. Finally, we discuss the research and managerial implications of the framework as well as the potential contributions that research using CIPO might make towards improving our understanding of how organisations manage the privacy tensions that arise within their e-business ventures.

## BACKGROUND LITERATURE

Research that explores the privacy considerations of consumers, at the individual level of anal ysis, has a rich tradition (for a thorough review, see Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Smith et al., 2011). While research at the individual level has shown that consumers generally espouse a high level of concern for their personal privacy, few studies have examined responses to cus: tomer privacy concerns at the organisational level of analysis. With the wide-scale expansior of commercial activity into electronic and mobile commerce, the privacy stakes have been raised, with different jurisdictions adopting a variety of approaches to regulate the collection, use and reuse of customer information (Bennett & Raab, 2008). However, very few organisational-level studies have shed light on this growing phenomenon (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). Culnan and Williams (2009) addressed this ‘dearth of prior organisational-level privacy research’ (p. 673) by investigating privacy breaches at ChoicePoint and TJX and providing rec ommendations for how organisations can deal with the ethical, or moral, dilemma that dealing with customer’s privacy can include. Despite this fertile ground, there continues to be a paucity of research on how enterprises negotiate the challenges presented by technology, law and the competitive necessity to collect, use and reuse customer information.

Researchers have explored the extent to which organisations do or do not adopt basic pri vacy practices in online environments, such as the posting of privacy policies and the enactment of FIP (Culnan, 2000; Miyazaki & Fernandez, 2000) and the interpretation of these policies (Schwaig et al., 2005). Some attention has been paid to <sup>fi</sup>rms’ legal privacy concerns that stem from transborder data <sup>fl</sup>ows (Ives & Jarvenpaa, 1991) or the information resource as a source of liability (Bordoloi et al., 1996; Straub & Collins, 1990). Smith (2001) advised US managers on how to avoid making mistakes with the European Privacy Directive. Attention has also been paid to privacy practices within the employment context (George, 1996; Oz et al., 1999; Stone & Stone, 1990; Webster, 1998).

Based on the previous work performed in this domain, FIP have been shown to affect the ethics, information management and legal strategy of organisations. First, privacy meets Mason’s (1995, p. 55) test of what constitutes an ethical problem, that is, ‘whenever one party in pursuit of its goals engages in behaviour that materially affects the ability of another party to pursue its goals.’ Customer information privacy is an ethical issue because it involves the pursuit of organisational interests however de<sup>fi</sup>ned (such as pro<sup>fi</sup>tability or market share) at the potential expense of customers who provide personal information with or without an understanding of the possible positive or negative consequences of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s use of that information. Ethical approaches to privacy in the IS literature include, for example, Smith and Hasnas (1999) who reviewed contemporary privacy issues through the lens of three different ethical theories – stockholder, stakeholder and social contract theory (SCT). Similarly, Culnan and Williams (2009) used privacy breaches at ChoicePoint and TJX to demonstrate that customer information privacy is an ethical issue.

The recent example of Google, discussed earlier, can be considered from an ethical perspective in which the company’s mission is to ‘organise the world’s information and make it universally accessible and useful’ (Google, 2015a). To make this useful to customers, they transparently gather information provided by customers through various free Google services. They then customise what information they present to customers based on the collected data (Google, 2015b). This approach requires Google to archive information about millions of users of their system. However, such an all-inclusive approach to data collection has resulted in the European Court of Justice mandating that Europeans have the right to ask Google to stop providing links to web content that portrays them negatively, or to quit fully utilising a holistic set of information. Google’s solution has been to minimise the effect of this regulation to only remove the search engine results from searches conducted and displayed in the European Union (Brian, 2014). While this may be viewed as a reaction to a legal decision, it is also illustrative of an ethical issue. Consumers expressed a desire to limit information about themselves appearing in search results, but Google only removed these results from a small subset of their search queries. This can be interpreted as Google pursuing its goals over the preferences of its users.

Second, privacy is an information management issue. Information has been described as valuable to an enterprise as ‘other assets such as people, plant and capital’, and it has been recommended that it should be managed with as much care (Lewis et al., 1995, p. 200). The management of customer information has undeniable privacy consequences particularly when considered as part of a company’s e-business endeavours that ‘capture, integrate and distribute data gained at the organisation’s website throughout the enterprise’ (Pan & Lee, 2003). This results in a fundamental privacy trade-off between the promise of customised goods and services and the concern for the amount of detailed information being collected to enable such customisation (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Sutanto et al., 2013; Winer, 2001). For example, Walmart balances their needs to gather and analyse customer information with the customers desire to protect their privacy by giving them the opportunity to opt out of contests and customised marketing (Walmart, 2015). In this way, Walmart is demonstrating an ethica approach in their information management strategy by respecting their customers’ information privacy requests.

Third, privacy is a legal issue that provokes varying responses by <sup>fi</sup>rms. Research into the response of American <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to privacy legislation identi<sup>fi</sup>ed ‘a cycle of organisational responses’ of ‘drift, threat and reaction’ (Smith, 1993). Examinations of seminal privacy events (e.g. Charters, 2002; Culnan & Smith, 1995; Culnan et al., 1994; Culnan & Williams, 2009; DeMarco, 2006; Trope, 2012; Watson, 2013) demonstrate that <sup>fi</sup>rms react differently to legal privacy challenges. Again drawing on the Google example, it was necessary for the European Court of Justice to require Google to provide Europeans with the ‘right to be forgotten’ in accordance with the 1995 Data Protection Directive (Tung, 2014). Google did not believe this law applied to them and fought against the removal of individual search results from search queries they returned. Google may view the legal battles they have fought over privacy as part of the cost of doing business.<sup>3</sup> Another example is that of Uber, a company facing possible regulatory issues in Canada as a result of not properly following the country’s privacy policies as they do not align with Uber’s business model (Geist, 2014). Further, decisions in response to complaints about privacy practices to the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the UK Information Commissioner’s Of<sup>fi</sup>ce and Canada’s Federal Privacy Commissioner show that there is variance in how companies comply with privacy regulations (e.g. Canada, 2013; FTC, 2011; FTC, 2012; FTC, 2013; UK, 2013).

Cavoukian and Hamilton (2002) contend that treating the organisational privacy challenge as uniquely an ethical, or information management or legal issue is an oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>cation of what is a complex business problem comprising all three dimensions. Mason (1986) also argued that ethics and information strategy were interrelated within privacy decisions, maintaining that information ethics dealt speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with how business collected, used and managed information. As illustrated earlier, both Google and Uber face con<sup>fl</sup>icting decisions related to the overlap between various aspects of this challenge. For the sake of simplifying the discussion, the next section of this article will discuss each of these aspects individually – ethics, information management and legal strategy – and then examine how they interact and result in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s CIPO. We bring this together and discuss how these dimensions interact to create an organisation’s CIPO.

In summary, there is a well-established body of research that indicates that customers are concerned about their information privacy. Less well developed is the research stream that ex amines integrated organisational ethical, information management and legal responses to these concerns, especially given the capabilities of information technologies to harvest and utilise large amounts of customer information. In the next section, we outline the theoretical foundation that underpins four different CIPOs.

## THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

Control theory and justice theory, viewed through the lens of FIP, underpin our conceptua framework, as outlined below.

## Control

Control over the collection, use and reuse of personal information is an important aspect of the ‘privacy calculus’ that customers engage in when evaluating <sup>fi</sup>rms from a privacy perspective (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Xu et al.’s (2012) study of privacy assurances within the context of location-based services used control agency theory to distinguish between personal control and proxy control. These concepts locate control either with the indi vidual (‘the self acts as the control agent to protect privacy’) or the organisation (‘powerfu others … act as the control agents to protect privacy’) (Xu et al., 2012, p. 1346). In particular, they found that offering more personal control affected context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c concerns for information privacy. In our conceptual framework, we utilise these classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of control as a way to differentiate how and the extent to which organisations offer their customers the ability to make choices about how their information is collected, used and reused. This is a fundamental aspect of the application of FIP.

## Justice

Culnan and Bies (2003) applied the justice literature to the consideration of the willingness of consumers to provide personal information. The concept of justice is rooted in ideas of fairness (p.328). They argue that three types of justice – distributive, procedural and interactional – provide different explanations for consumer behaviour. In our conceptual framework, we apply procedural justice as it is most closely linked to FIP (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999). In particular, Culnan and Armstrong suggest that procedural justice, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by FIP, can be utilised by companies to address the privacy concerns of consumers as their transactional data and per sonal information are being combined for the purpose of personalised targeted marketing.

Procedural justice involves people perceiving that they have been treated fairly in a process, regardless of its outcome (Greenberg, 1987; Lind & Tyler, 1988). Procedural justice empha sises the extent to which organisations offer transparency to their customers. This is consisten with Culnan and Armstrong’s (1999) study of the relationships among privacy concerns, proce dural fairness and trust. Procedural fairness (Lind & Tyler, 1988) equates to procedural justice. It balances the ‘competing forces of privacy and information use’ (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999, p. 108). The study demonstrated that organisations that explicitly applied procedural fairness through the communication and application of FIP were more likely to retain customers and, as a result, gain an advantage over competitors. The presence of procedural justice, with its concerns for fairness, transparency and accountability for actions, provides customers with tan gible processes that translate into a greater willingness to transact business (Culnan & Bies, 2003). These processes are consistent with FIP.

## Ethical obligation

The <sup>fi</sup>rst component of the CIPO framework is a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of its obligations to its customers. FIP have provided guidance to organisations as to how they can act responsibly in handling customers’ information; however, there is no consensus for implementing FIP (Culnan & Williams, 2009). We draw upon three ethical theories identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Smith and Hasnas (1999) to underpin this CIPO component. What results from this are poles of a continuum anchored by <sup>fi</sup>rms’ different philosophies about whose interests are to be served, those of the producers or those of the consumers, and how control and procedural justice are utilised to meet these interests.

Smith and Hasnas (1999) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed stockholder, stakeholder and SCT theories. Stockholder theory fundamentally privileges the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s stockholders over all other parties. Stakeholder theory seeks to balance multiple interests. SCT argues for an increase in the welfare of all participants. The distinctions among the three ethical theories are powerful and, importantly to researchers, observable. The authors illustrate the distinctions by examining the case of Blockbuster’s proposal in the 1990s to sell its customers’ video rental information. Operating with the assumption that the proposed sale is legal, Smith and Hasnas argued that the three theories delivered different verdicts on the appropriateness of the action. Stockholder theory would oblige the sale of the data as long as it would be pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Stakeholder theory would require an assessment of costs to customers versus the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to stockholders. In this example, the ethical course would be to adopt whatever approach minimises customer costs and maximises <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Lastly, SCT would likely not permit the sale of the information. SCT requires that actions promote both social welfare and justice. Smith and Hasnas argue that while justice considerations would likely not be violated by the sale, customer welfare would be. SCT ‘instructs managers to pursue pro<sup>fi</sup>ts solely in ways that tend to enhance the material welfare… the sale seems to provide no material benefit to Blockbuster's customers' (p. 119).

The concept of information privacy involves two parties: the customer, who possesses information about and of value to himself or herself, and the organisation, which wants access to the information for its own purposes. How the organisation characterises its obligations to the customer shapes its perspective on what it is prepared to do or not do to acquire and use the valued information. In other words, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s perspective on its ethical obligations re<sup>fl</sup>ects its intentions to exploit or assist its customers, through control and procedural justice. We now present the relationship between control and justice and the three ethical theories discussed by Smith and Hasnas (1999).

## Stockholder obligation

Firms in which decisions are driven from the stance of a stockholder obligation make the tensions between firms and customers explicit. There is an acknowledgement that there are differ ences between organisational and customer interests. However, organisational interests are paramount, while customers are seen to be ultimately responsible for protecting themselves in the marketplace. At the extreme, these <sup>fi</sup>rms do not acknowledge any positive obligation to their customers and do not provide customers with information privacy protection. In this instance, organisations assume and exploit an ‘information asymmetry’ (Caudill & Murphy, 2000): customers are not necessarily aware that their information is being collected and used. More likely, organisations assume that customers are both aware that they need to protect themselves and are in a position to do so. As well, customers are perceived as having some negative power in that they can withdraw from the marketplace. However, organisations continue to exploit information asymmetries, justifying their behaviours by invoking the nostrum of ‘buyer beware’ and, as a result, provide minimal information privacy protection. These <sup>fi</sup>rms justify their actions using stockholde theory. Making decisions from perspective leaves customers on their own to protect their persona information. The customer exploitation and customer self-protection stances represent so-called customer relationships in which <sup>fi</sup>rms assume power and use it to place their own interests ahead of those of their customers. This results in organisations providing customers with little to no control or justice mechanisms for dealing with their information.

## Stakeholder obligation

Firms in which decisions are driven from the stance of a stakeholder obligation recognise that a variety of interested parties, including customers, employees and stockholders, have a stake in how business is conducted. This approach characterises the commercial relationship as one of shared interest and obligation. Trade-offs do not favour one group over another but are played out in a model of mutual adjustment. Organisations believe that they and their customers bear a joint responsibility for marketplace behaviour. This perspective can be differentiated from the previous perspective in two important ways. Firstly, it speci<sup>fi</sup>cally acknowledges that there is a power balance between <sup>fi</sup>rms and their customers and that <sup>fi</sup>rms should ensure that their cus tomers are able to participate in the marketplace and not simply be exploited by it (Caudill & Murphy, 2000; Hoffman et al., 1999). Secondly, it argues for customers’ ability to exercise agency. Customers are not merely targets for exploitation but carriers and creators of valuable information. Customers have more positive power because they can negotiate, at least to some extent, the terms of access to and the use of their information.

Firms that acknowledge a mutual interest and obligation can be explained as having a ‘stakeholder’ ethical perspective. They will provide information privacy protections that offer customers some limited control over the collection and use of their information. For example, customers would have access to privacy policy information that would provide generic information that met the letter of the FIP (or relevant statute, depending on jurisdiction). However, <sup>fi</sup>rms following a ‘stakeholder’ ethical perspective would make efforts to ensure that the interests of stockholders were not undermined by privacy actions. This would be accomplished through a consideration of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for each party whereby actions would be taken that minimise customer costs while maximising <sup>fi</sup>rm bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Smith & Hasnas, 1999).

Research suggests that codes of conduct are particularly effective for instilling ethical sensibilities throughout organisations (McDonald, 2000; Oz et al., 1999). However, just as the Generally Accepted Accounting Principles are open to interpretation, so too are privacy princi ples. Different <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to engage in different behaviours even when regulated. Firms with a ‘stakeholder’ ethical perspective would likely meet the higher standard of a code in order to maintain their membership in an industry group (McCarthy, 2009). For example, the Federa

Trade Commission updated its online behavioural advertising principles to include a set of pri vacy related guidelines that bring better education and transparency to behavioural targeted marketing on the internet (FTC, 2009). This set of guidelines is utilised by a coalition of advertising industry trade groups to provide guidance to their member organisations on proper privacy practices. Firms that are part of these trade groups exemplify the ‘stakeholder’ ethical perspective: They balance both their own interests and those of their customers by sharing control over information and expanding justice considerations to incorporate a broader range of opportunities for customers to decide how their information is used.

## Social contract obligation

A <sup>fi</sup>rm in which decisions are driven from the stance of a social contract obligation believes that promoting customer welfare is paramount because it will eventually increase the organisation’s well-being. Consequently, the <sup>fi</sup>rm makes short term concessions to its customers’ interests in order to achieve longer-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for all parties. In other words, these organisations explic itly work to promote consumer well-being even if this means they eschew immediate pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm that is driven by the social contract perspective passes the twin tests of SCT. Firstly, it permits customers to exert positive power (make personal information disclosure choices with full information). The <sup>fi</sup>rms’ privacy policies provide detailed explanations of speci<sup>fi</sup>c (as opposed to generic) privacy and information practices and also provide customers with extensive opportunities for control over their information and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant avenues for accessing justice procedures. Secondly, this perspective improves customer welfare by anticipating and soliciting customer information privacy preferences in the design and development of customer-facing activities. Firms that adopt this ethical stance attempt to improve their customers’ well-being by providing them with tools to control their personal information and will not knowingly engage in privacy-violating behaviour. In sum, they satisfy both the social welfare and justice require ments of SCT.

## Information management strategy

The second component of the CIPO framework is a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s objectives for gathering and using customer information. As mentioned earlier. Mason (1986) argued that information ethics deals speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with how businesses collect, use and manage information. A company’s strategy fo managing information is closely related to this and includes how vulnerable an individual is after giving up control by providing their personal information. This collection of information can likewise lead to unforeseen sharing of the information in which procedural mechanisms for dealing with this information may be lacking or impossible to provide (Culnan & Williams, 2009). Brohman, Watson, Piccoli and Parasuranam’s (2003) speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of CRM orientation using information processing theory (Tushman & Nadler, 1978) underpins our understanding of how <sup>fi</sup>rms strategically manage their customer information resource. It is the customer’s personal information that has value and gives rise to the privacy tensions that must be managed by orga nisations (Milberg et al., 2000). In particular, as Web technologies have grown to include the collection and analysis of personal information through big data initiatives, large amounts of individual information have provided value to the organisation (Chau & Xu, 2012; Park et al.,

2012). This dimension addresses the fundamental organisational question ‘What do we want to do with the information we gather?’ (Davenport & Prusak, 1998). This question is particularly relevant when considering the collection, use, dissemination, retention and reuse of custome information.

Tushman and Nadler (1978) theorised that at their core organisations are informationprocessing entities. It is through their use of information that <sup>fi</sup>rms cope with the uncertainty created by the relative complexity of their internal and external environments. The greater the uncertainty, the more information needed and, by extension, the more complex the necessary organisational systems for processing the information. Brohman et al. (2003) adapted Tushman and Nadler’s theory as a means to distinguish among information processing strategies in a CRM context. They identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four strategies: transaction, data, inference and advice. These strategies are distinguished by the completeness of the customer information being gathered and retained, the time horizon over which the customer information is used and the purpose for which the information is gathered. We employ Brohman et al.’s (2003) four strategies to discuss the information management strategy dimension of the CIPO framework as follows.

## Transaction-focused

Transaction-focused <sup>fi</sup>rms do not engage with customer information for purposes other than to complete immediate transactions or to respond to customer inquiries (Brohman et al., 2003). They do not accumulate or deploy customer information beyond these limited purposes. In this situation, it is unlikely that the <sup>fi</sup>rm would offer information privacy protections for two reasons. Firstly, there may appear to be no necessity to do so; in effect, customers have no relationship with the <sup>fi</sup>rm other than to pay for goods or services acquired. Secondly, the <sup>fi</sup>rm would not appear to have an interest in developing any relationship for which accumulated customer information might be useful. The <sup>fi</sup>rm would see little merit in implementing organisational policies that do not offer immediate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, might impede transaction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and incur unrecoverable costs. As a result, these <sup>fi</sup>rms are unlikely to purchase customer information from outside organisations but might sell their customers’ information as a revenue source. The perceived value of customer information to these <sup>fi</sup>rms is limited. Likewise, their approach to information privacy would be evidenced by low control and justice provisions.

## Data-focused

Data-focused <sup>fi</sup>rms concentrate on using customer information to increase pro<sup>fi</sup>tability (Brohman et al., 2003). Information about customers’ preferences and historical activities with the <sup>fi</sup>rm is used to distinguish and cultivate pro<sup>fi</sup>table customer segments and to shed unpro<sup>fi</sup>table ones. These <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to implement the minimal information privacy practices required to keep their pro<sup>fi</sup>table customers without increasing costs. For example, these <sup>fi</sup>rms often have a link to a privacy policy, but the policy is very basic and does not necessarily cover all FIP. We expect an emphasis on the need to gather information to increase customer convenience, address customer preferences and ensure customer information security. At the same time, we expect these <sup>fi</sup>rms to both pay for information about current customers and future prospects and to sell information about customers. Firms that deploy data-focused information management strategies are perhaps less interested in their customers than in the value of their customers’ information. This would include organisations that utilise big data technologies to collect and analyse data from web interactions, but who adhere to the minimal privacy protections set by legal authorities (Brill, 2013). Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms following a data-focused information strategy are likely to offer minimal information privacy protections such as control and procedural justice, but more than transaction-focused <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## Inference-focused

Inference-focused <sup>fi</sup>rms strive to collect and deploy customer information in order to improve their understanding of customers so that they can increase selling opportunities (Brohman et al., 2003). These <sup>fi</sup>rms collect and retain data in order to understand past customer behaviour and current preferences so that comprehensive pro<sup>fi</sup>les can be constructed. These pro<sup>fi</sup>les are both present and future-oriented in that they do not require that customers be immediately pro<sup>fi</sup>table to be included in targeted marketing efforts. The pro<sup>fi</sup>les are built to facilitate the iden ti<sup>fi</sup>cation of ‘cross-sell and up-sell opportunities’ for current and future customers (Brohman et al., 2003, p. 48). This information management strategy depends on customers providing increased amounts of detailed information to drive the ‘inference’ engines of the marketing systems. Firms that operate with the desire to better understand their customers and have invested large sums to automate their information management processes are likely to under stand that customers also have privacy preferences. As a result, these <sup>fi</sup>rms take care to clearly articulate privacy policies that address both the spirit and letter of the FIP. Particular attention is paid to explaining the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the customer of providing information (e.g. to tailor products to their speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs) as well as the possibility of ‘missed opportunities’ when information is not provided (e.g. not receiving information about sales). While these <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to acquire information about customers from various sources (to round out pro<sup>fi</sup>les of existing customers and to identify potential new customers), they are less likely to sell customer information because it is of competitive value to them. From a data analytics perspective, <sup>fi</sup>rms in this category utilise the information they collect to advance the goals of their <sup>fi</sup>rm, but are unlikely to sell this information to others for the sake of making a pro<sup>fi</sup>t. In sum, <sup>fi</sup>rms following an inferencefocused information management strategy are likely to provide information privacy protection that offers customers some control over the collection and use of their information and mechanisms for recourse due to missing or inaccurate information.

## Advice-focused

Advice-focused <sup>fi</sup>rms provide individualised services to customers as a means to build longterm customer relationships (Brohman et al.. 2003). While historic customer information is necessary for developing a complete pro<sup>fi</sup>le, the <sup>fi</sup>rms are more interested in gathering futureoriented information such as aspirations, goals, constraints and other idiosyncratic personal information. For <sup>fi</sup>rms that adopt this strategy, customers, while clearly still the source of immediate pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and information, are recognised as the basis of their ongoing prosperity. As a result, we expect that these <sup>fi</sup>rms do more than merely attempt to understand privacy preferences. The types of information that they need to gather to assist their customers over the long term may be extremely sensitive, highly personal and deeply felt. These <sup>fi</sup>rms may need to educate their customers about privacy issues as a means to gain their trust and create the conditions for adequate disclosure for effective discussions about future plans. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rms have to adopt a range of privacy policies that might not deliver immediate value. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt policies of never selling or otherwise sharing customer information with outside organisations, of providing periodic customer review of their pro<sup>fi</sup>les to ensure accuracy and of not disclosing information to other units of the <sup>fi</sup>rm for marketing purposes without their customers’ express consent based on an understanding of the desirability of complete information to develop comprehensive advice. These policies might necessitate changes to customer management processes, more training of personnel and careful attention paid to justifying the need for the different kinds of information being collected.

Firms may also utilise new technological approaches to give customers more granular level control over their information. They can do this by utilising a service similar to the one proposed by the Reclaim Your Name initiative. This initiative allows customers to tailor the company’s data handling, notice and choice tools based on the individual’s perceived sensitivity of information (Brill 2013). Another example where this approach may be utilised is in the healthcare industry. In order for adequate medical treatment plans to be put in place, it is necessary for the patient to share information with a medical treatment team. This information may be initially shared with one doctor, but because of trust in the medical team, this personal health information is shared with nurses and other doctors who have a vested interest in providing the best medical care for the patient. Without a long-term focus on the value of the patient relationship and how sensitive information is handled, medical practitioners may <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves without any patients.

It is unlikely that an advice-focused information management strategy could be implemented without attending to all four FIP and in a manner that recognises customer relationships as being more than merely the economic value of the customers’ information. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rms following an advice-focused information management strategy likely exhibit the most stringen privacy practices and provide their customers with enhanced control along with avenues for exercising justice.

## Legal risk assessment

The third component of the CIPO framework is a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s view of the impact that information privacy laws have on its ability to carry out its business. EIP provide the foundation for privacy laws in the USA as well as other jurisdictions (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999). The legal environment dictates the level of privacy protection, including control and mechanisms for procedural justice, to which <sup>fi</sup>rms are legally obligated to adhere. This can mean a high level of mandatory privacy protecting behaviour (for example, as required by the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, the European Data Protection Directive or Canada’s Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act). We employ cognitive categorisation theory (Rosch, 1978) to theoretically distinguish top management perceptions of the need to incorporate customer information privacy into <sup>fi</sup>rm operations as either threats (perceived negatively) or op portunities (perceived positively) (Greenaway & Chan, 2013; Jackson & Dutton, 1988). The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s legal risk assessment re<sup>fl</sup>ects a particular orientation based on the extent to which privacy laws are viewed as a constraint or opportunity for the business. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant aspect of this dimension stems from the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s view of who owns the information in question – the customer or the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We theorise four risk assessment categories as described in the following section.

## Privacy laws as neither threat nor opportunity

Some <sup>fi</sup>rms perceive neither threats nor opportunities from privacy laws. These <sup>fi</sup>rms do not ac knowledge a growing social and regulatory reality – privacy matters to customers, and there are expectations that organisations must meet to be considered legitimate in the marketplace (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Sheehan & Hoy, 2000; Smith et al., 1996). These <sup>fi</sup>rms do not assess privacy laws as either a threat or opportunity and are not likely to provide customers with any mechanisms of control or procedural justice over their information.

In jurisdictions with privacy legislation, organisations may be non-compliant because of igno rance of those legal requirements, an inability to meet them for technical or <sup>fi</sup>nancial reasons or wilful disregard, especially in regions where enforcement of privacy regulations is perceived to be low (Conrath, 2005). In jurisdictions where there is no or little legal requirement fo organisational privacy actions, <sup>fi</sup>rms may not address customer privacy concerns because of an ignorance of these concerns, a lack of appreciation for customer desires for greater privacy or deliberate privacy-violating behaviours. Accordingly, a commissioner from the FTC recently indicated that some companies are willingly violating the law and ignoring privacy rights in orde to collect large amounts of personal data. These data are then being used to capitalise on the growing market for big data analytics (Brill, 2013).

## Privacy laws as threat

Some <sup>fi</sup>rms perceive privacy laws as a threat and assess privacy legislation as a constraint on organisational freedom of activity. These <sup>fi</sup>rms may view themselves as the owners of customer information mainly because they collect, store and manipulate the data. Customer information is simply a raw material that requires <sup>fi</sup>rm resources to be transformed into something of value. Changes to organisational routines and processes to accommodate privacy considerations may be viewed as simply being the minimum effort required to secure the raw material.

This assessment is distinguished from the previous category in two ways. Firstly, the <sup>fi</sup>rms acknowledge that there are some minimum constraints on organisational activities. Firms are not free to engage in activities without regard for the law. Secondly, as a result of acknowledging the constraints, <sup>fi</sup>rms will likely curb information privacy-violating activities to avoid notice by outside agencies that could cause the <sup>fi</sup>rm to lose access to their raw material. However, this approach does not recognise customer ownership of data. Neither does it acknowledge that damage to customers’ interests resulting from poor privacy practices is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for customers to undo and cannot be addressed simply by switching suppliers. Firms that assess privacy laws as a threat operate to minimise this additional cost of producing their goods and services by provid ing customers with minimal control and procedural justice mechanisms.

## Privacy laws as both threat and opportunity

Some <sup>fi</sup>rms perceive privacy laws as both a threat and an opportunity and re<sup>fl</sup>ect a greater awareness of the implications of privacy on the ability to conduct business effectively. These <sup>fi</sup>rms recognise that privacy laws constrain the range of activities in which they can engage, thus, they present a threat to their operations. At the same time, they view privacy laws as necessary vehicles for the facilitation of commercial activity within society as a whole, and hence, laws are also opportunities.

At the core of this dual assessment is the acknowledgement that customers possess useful information that is valuable to organisations and that there is a cost to acquiring that informa tion. This has two important information management implications. Firstly, customers should be able to exercise some control over how the information is collected, used and reused. Secondly, customers should also derive some bene<sup>fi</sup>t from disclosing this information. In essence, privacy laws attempt to establish that the value of the information should be mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial – the gathering organisation can use the information to provide products and services that better respond to customer preferences and, it is hoped, sell more products and services. At the same time, customers should be receiving better value by gaining access to goods and services that better address their wants and needs.

Firms that view customer information privacy both as an important enabler of commercial activity and as a brake on commercial exploitation of the value of the information will likely provide information privacy protection that offers customers some limited control over the collection and use of their information. As well, justice provisions will likely be provided that address customers’ chief concerns about their treatment in the process.

## Privacy laws as opportunity

Some <sup>fi</sup>rms view privacy laws primarily as an opportunity and view the enactment of privacy laws as the platform for positive competitive opportunity. Research has shown that some <sup>fi</sup>rms have seen customer and governmental environmental concerns as opportunities to be pursued proactively versus concerns to be dealt with (Sharma & Vredenburg, 1998). Similarly, some <sup>fi</sup>rms that develop privacy policies that exceed regulations or that interpret laws to privilege their customers’ interests will use this stance to distinguish themselves from their competitors. This approach is adopted by <sup>fi</sup>rms that view customers as the owners of their personal data and seek competitive advantage by assisting customers in preserving the value of their information. These <sup>fi</sup>rms view information privacy as a key enabler of a network of complex relationships over time and thus will likely engage in privacy-promoting behaviours that differentiate them from their competitors.

We present four orientations distinguished by the extent to which customers are offered control and justice processes to manage their personal information. ## Four Company Information Privacy Orientations

Recall that we previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned CIPO as an organisation’s approach to protecting and using its customers’ personally identi<sup>fi</sup>able information. We theorise four Company Information Pri vacy Orientations. These four orientations re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent to which <sup>fi</sup>rms’ privacy policies af fect their customers’ abilities to exercise control and procedural mechanisms over the collection and use of their personal information.

The four Company Information Privacy Orientations described in the following section provide the organisational equivalent of a ‘privacy calculus’ by differentiating the extent to which control and justice in<sup>fl</sup>uence a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s privacy behaviours. We label these orientations as Privacy Ignorers, Minimisers, Balancers and Differentiators.

## Privacy ignorers

Firms oriented as Privacy Ignorers provide little to no control or procedural justice for their customers regarding the control of information collected by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and they rely on individuals taking personal control of their information by choosing when to provide it. For example, they may not display a privacy policy on a website, may not provide detailed information to cus tomers about the organisation's use of personal information when reguested or may misrepre: sent whether and how they gather and use customer information. In these cases, the <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to seize control of customer information, not provide customers with opportunities to express their preferences regarding its use and not provide avenues for recourse (i.e. for procedural justice). For example, they may require that potential customers provide personal information before they can access information on their website. A Privacy Ignorer orientation can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the lack of evident privacy behaviours. Indeed, Privacy Ignorers may consider the legal consequences for their privacy misadventures, such as <sup>fi</sup>nes, as the cost o doing business.

Uber is an example of a <sup>fi</sup>rm that can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a Privacy Ignorer. Ultimately, they offer very little control or procedural iustice to their consumers. If consumers want to control their personal information, their only alternative is to not use Uber’s services. Ethically, Uber demonstrated that they put the organisation’s interest over the consumers’ when they published a blog post where they showed ‘Rides of Glory’ or people who went to a house that was not their own on a Friday or Saturday night and did not leave until 4 to 6 hours later (Geist, 2014). Further, they used the information they had collected to follow a journalist whose critical article they did not agree with (McGarry, 2014). They appear to have a data-focused information management strategy. While they have a privacy policy, they do not appear to always follow it, and they utilise the customer information that they possess to maximise pro<sup>fi</sup>ts (Geist, 2014). Uber has been banned in a number of countries and is facing increased legal regulation in Canada and the USA because they demonstrate a lack of acknowledgement of privacy and other laws by arguing that these laws do not apply to them (Anglen, 2015; Geist, 2014). While Uber’s information management strategy suggests they might be moving towards being a Privacy Minimiser, their ethical and legal decisions demonstrate their orientation as a Privacy Ignorer. However, this approach is working for Uber. In the midst of the news related to their privacy approach, their investors provided them with a \$1.2 billion investment (Griswold, 2014).

## Privacy minimisers

Firms oriented as Privacy Minimisers engage in only as many privacy behaviours as necessary to avoid legal action. Responding to the minimum requirements of government regulation is considered to be a key driver of an organisation’s privacy approach (Milberg et al., 2000; Milne, 2000). They tend to provide the minimal amount of control of information to customers that is required and little to no mechanisms of justice for the expression of customer preferences or for recourse. These <sup>fi</sup>rms can be characterised as being interested in the strict letter of privacy laws rather than in the spirit of offering customers privacy protection. The orientation of Privacy Minimisers is distinguished by their characterisation of privacy as a problem to be minimised rather than as an opportunity to serve customers and, potentially, to distinguish the organisation.

Google represents a company that appears to have a Privacy Minimiser orientation. As discussed earlier, Google’s mission is to collect as much information as possible and make it accessible and useful. In doing so, they provide users with few control or justice mechanisms. However, in Europe, it has been ruled that Google needs to provide consumers with the ‘right to be forgotten.’ Google worked to meet the letter of the law, as opposed to the spirit of the law, and this has provoked further legal proceedings (Tung, 2014). In the USA, Google was <sup>fi</sup>ned \$22.5 million for misrepresenting privacy assurances to users of the Safari browser (Toohey, 2013). Google appears to follow FIP primarily when instructed to do so by the courts. However, recent legal battles could force Google to change their approach to consumer privacy to move closer to being a Privacy Balancer. Google’s <sup>fi</sup>ghting of the European laws and their approach to information management have not hindered their ability to be successful as a company. In fact, it could be argued that this is all part of their ‘organisational’ privacy calculus as they are meeting their organisational objectives as evidenced by consistent revenue growth over the last several years (Winkler & Barr, 2014).

## Privacy balancers

Firms oriented as Privacy Balancers adhere to industry or professionally based privacy codes, including those codes that exceed legal requirements. In this manner, they embrace both the letter and the spirit of privacy laws. In so doing, they provide customers with limited control as well as the ability to express preferences and avenues for recourse related to the handling of information. The orientation of Privacy Balancers is demonstrated by providing customers with a salient means of specifying their information-related preferences and interacting with the company in the use of the information.

Walmart is an example of a company that demonstrates a Privacy Balancer orientation. For example, they collect information from customers from a variety of sources but do not sell o rent the information. Rather, they use the information collected through automation on their website to, among other things, ‘evaluate use of the(ir) website, and to support website analytics and marketing campaigns’. Customers can choose whether or not to participate in contests or to receive notices of new products or special marketing campaigns by exercising choice through various opt-in and opt-out avenues (Walmart, 2015). Further, they anonymise data about the use of mobile devices that is collected by third parties for analytical purposes. They also use Secures Sockets Layer (SSL) encryption for transmitting personal data.

However, as Walmart expands into new sectors such as banking, they appear to be moving closer to being a Privacy Minimiser. Walmart has been trying to enter into the banking industry since 1998. Walmart’s efforts to open their own bank have been stopped by regulations. Instead, they have entered into a partnership with Green Dot Bank who will control the banking deposits (Max<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2014). Walmart’s orientation will be determined in how they change their privacy approach in entering into this new partnership. If this partnership ultimately includes the sharing of customer information with this external partner, then protection provided to Walmart’s customers will have changed. This decision will likely be made purposefully as Walmart addresses their organisational privacy calculus of balancing the privacy they afford their customers with their organisational objectives. Recently, Walmart has experienced increased same store sales<sup>4</sup> only for one of the last eight quarters (McGrath, 2014). Such lack of growth may cause them to reconsider their ‘organisational’ privacy calculus as they seek methods to achieve the organisational objective of increased same store sales.

## Privacy differentiators

Firms oriented as Privacy Differentiators are most likely to offer signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhanced privacy protection as part of their business strategy in order to set themselves apart from their peer group (Greenaway & Chan, 2005). Indeed, privacy has been heralded as a competitive differentiator by, among others, Forrester Research in their 2015 predictions for data security and privacy (Shey, 2014). In today’s highly connected environment, many of these strategies likely include staying at the leading edge of providing security protections for customers’ data, acting as a proxy for controlling customers’ information as they desire by providing customers with enhanced control and justice. This might include utilising two factor authentication to ac cess accounts (Bose & Leung, 2013) and encrypting all internal internet traf<sup>fi</sup>c to prevent hackers from stealing personal information in transit (Panzarino, 2013). These <sup>fi</sup>rms purposefully aim to stay ahead of the curve to protect their customers’ privacy. Privacy Differentiators proactively seek competitive advantage not through exploitation but by offering their customers signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhanced control and justice.

Apple is increasingly operating as a privacy differentiator. They used to offer limited opportunity for customers to control their privacy. However, recent operating system upgrades have placed privacy front and centre by ‘building in’ privacy capabilities (Mogull, 2014). In easily un derstood language, Apple provides explanations for what privacy options are available and how to engage them (Apple, 2015a). For example, Apple requires explicit consent for customers to share information with application (app) developers. They provide the ability to limit ad tracking and reduce personalisation of mobile ads. They provide information on how to disable cookies in their Safari web browser and on mobile devices as well as how to turn off location services to prevent tracking. Further, they use advanced encryption for online services such as the Apple online store, and the iTunes store (Apple, 2015c). Encryption is also in use in applications such as iMessage and FaceTime. Customers can exercise control over whether or not iMessages and SMS messages are backed up to the iCloud (and FaceTime calls are never stored). There are speci<sup>fi</sup>c prohibitions on the use and sale of data collected using the Health app (and send ing and storing information is likewise encrypted). As a <sup>fi</sup>nal example, Apple is pioneering replacing persistent identi<sup>fi</sup>ers by randomising MAC addresses on mobile devices that are passively searching for WiFi connections (Apple, 2015b).

## Summarising the Company Information Privacy Orientation conceptual framework

Differentiating among the privacy orientations is a function of the extent to which <sup>fi</sup>rms offer their customers privacy control and procedural justice. Appendix A illustrates the four CIPOs with ex amples of the application of the four FIPs (notice, choice, access and security).

We have presented four distinct CIPOs that represent different perspectives on offering cus tomers control and procedural justice within organisational privacy policies and practices. As discussed earlier, these factors are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ethical obligations, information management strategies and legal risk assessments. How these three components are enacted in<sup>fl</sup>uence the type of control and procedural justice offered to customers.

## DISCUSSION

## Research implications

The CIPO framework is theoretically derived to explain differences in organisational ap proaches to information privacy offered to customers. As a middle-range explanatory theory (Gregor’s 2006 Type II theory), its utility is in the novelty of approach; that is, by bringing to gether three previously unlinked streams of research, the framework provides a fuller perspec tive on organisational activities in the privacy research domain. In this way, the different orientations provide a basis for insight within a <sup>fi</sup>rm and comparison across <sup>fi</sup>rms. While our intention was not to pursue a framework speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for predicting (such as would be the outcome of a Type III theory), the CIPO framework could be empirically validated. The approach we have taken to theorise the ‘hows’ and ‘whys’ of information privacy differences among <sup>fi</sup>rms lends itself to further investigation. Chief among the possible research questions is the relative impor tance of individual dimensions of CIPO as well as the interactions among the dimensions.

Three additional research questions are raised by the CIPO framework. First is the issue of why different <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt different orientations. It has been argued previously that Institutional Theory and the Resource-Based View of the Firm offer distinctive lenses through which CIPO can be explained (Greenaway & Chan, 2005). A second question involves the extent to which CIPO is an explicit versus emergent organisational phenomenon. Do <sup>fi</sup>rms choose their privacy orientation upfront or does it emerge over time as the result of many different decisions made in different areas of the <sup>fi</sup>rm? Third, is CIPO static or does it change either as a result of accumulated decisions or deliberate behavioural changes? Our examination of Uber, Google, Walmart and Apple suggests that the latter may be the case.

## Process-based Company Information Privacy Orientation research

Existing privacy research about organisational policies has tended to focus on the reading of policy notices posted on websites and using this information to determine if companies are compliant with FIP (Culnan, 1999; Milne & Culnan, 2002; Miyazaki & Fernandez, 2000; Ryker et al., 2002). We believe that this work can be extended through more <sup>fi</sup>eldwork to gain a deeper understanding of organisational actions. Gregor (2006, p. 624) suggests that Type II theorising can be accomplished using such methods as case studies, interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies, historical studies and ethnographies. To do otherwise means researchers risk labelling <sup>fi</sup>rms inaccurately simply because no policy or limited information is posted on a website. Working inside <sup>fi</sup>rms would provide insights lacking in current research.

The CIPO framework could also be used in conjunction with a qualitative study that mines data from websites that catalogue privacy breaches from places such as http://www. privacyrights.org/. It might be expected that companies that have a Privacy Ignorer or Privacy Minimiser orientation would be more likely to have experienced customer-focused privacy breaches. Such an approach would demonstrate a correlation between these two factors, but it would require a longitudinal study to determine whether the CIPO caused the privacy breaches or if because of the breaches the company adjusted their CIPO.

## Company Information Privacy Orientation in a nomological net

The CiPO framework could also be utilised to address organisational level research suggested by Bélanger and Crossler (2011) as both a dependent and independent variable. As an independent variable, CIPO could be used to ‘explore whether organisations have different stakes in information privacy than the consumers they serve (p. 1029)’ and associated outcomes. This could be accomplished by adapting the CIPO framework to a Type III theory and using it to classify organisations and compare organisations’ orientations to individual-level privacy concern constructs that are well established in the literature (e.g. Malhotra et al., 2004; Smith et al., 1996; Stewart & Segars, 2002). In order to accomplish this, it would be necessary to measure the amount of control and justice that organisations provide to their customers. For in stance, this could be accomplished by examining existing organisational resources such as privacy policies and training documents that outline each company’s orientation towards handling customer information. Once the amount of control and justice provided was quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed, this could be used to determine each organisation’s CIPO. Then individual customers of each company for which CIPO had been calculated could be surveyed about their privacy concerns, their willingness to interact with the company in question and their views regarding how well the company currently handles their personal information. This would provide insight into whether a company’s CIPO in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceptions consumers have of them and how that may cause a consumer to continue interacting with the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Further, CIPO could serve as a dependent variable in exploring ‘what causes different privacy attitudes and concerns [orientations] within companies and between companies’ (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011, p. 1029). Because CIPO is determined by control and justice, existing research could be utilised to test antecedents to control and justice at an organisational level, which would in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s CIPO. For example, research at the individual level shows tha as individuals experience privacy invasions, their view of procedural justice decreases (Korsgaard et al., 1995). At the organisational level, as most privacy invasions affect a large number of customers, it would be expected that experiencing a privacy invasion would decrease perceptions of justice and likewise change the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s CIPO. Also at the individual level of analysis, research has shown that anonymity, secrecy and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality affect perceived in formation control (Dinev et al., 2013). At the organisational level, it would also be interesting to see the effect that changes in the amount of anonymity, secrecy and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality have on the control offered to individuals by <sup>fi</sup>rms and how this likewise changes their orientation.

## Practice implications

Different <sup>fi</sup>rms will exhibit different CIPOs as a result of their organisational privacy calculus. For example, a Privacy Differentiator orientation is not for everyone. Not only does this orientation demand the deployment of more organisational resources than other postures, other orientations may not be what their customers want or need. Further, it may be possible for an organi sation to achieve their organisational objectives by not offering as much control or procedural justice to the customer. However, Uber provides the example of a company that is experiencing customer, media and legal backlashes in reaction to the lack of privacy offered by the organisation (McGarry, 2014; Schwartz, 2014). In this instance, the company may identify that their privacy orientation is having an adverse impact on the achievement of organisational objectives and they may in future provide more control and procedural iustice to their customers in an effort to better meet their objectives, thus changing their CIPO.

The paradox of a Privacy Differentiator Orientation is that customers need to be educated and willing to involve themselves in their information privacy protection. Not all customers are likely to be so inclined, and therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms have to determine what makes the most sense for their organisations over time as they gain experience with customers and privacy. However, we echo Culnan and Armstrong (1999) in cautioning that the fundamental privacy issue is not whether customers exercise their privacy options but if <sup>fi</sup>rms offer them. Considering tha 89% of consumers avoid doing business online with companies who are perceived to not pro tect their privacy (Price Waterhouse Coopers, 2014), companies may want to consider altering their privacy behaviours, effectively shifting their CIPO. However, we expect that there is an equilibrium point for companies based on which CIPO provides them with the best ability to meet their company objectives, thus maximising their organisational privacy calculus.

Our theoretical development of the three dimensions and four orientations provides <sup>fi</sup>rms with initial tools to assess their privacy positioning, at least in terms of the privacy principles to which they aspire. Given the complexity of developing privacy programmes, thinking through the impli cations of their CIPO will help <sup>fi</sup>rms consider the coherence of their approach and to diagnose gaps or imbalances. For example, is an emphasis on mitigating legal risk being pursued at the expense of sound information management or considerations of customer preferences? The orientations also offer managers the ability to compare their <sup>fi</sup>rm’s privacy orientation with those of their competitors and peers. A CIPO should be shaped with the knowledge of both custome preferences and competitive realities. As technology evolves to make it easier and easier to collect information from customers (Ernst & Young, 2013), an organisation’s understanding of what shapes their CIPO can help them to balance the privacy-protection mechanisms they offer their customers with their organisational goals. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, managers can make privacy decisions that <sup>fi</sup>t their organisation by examining how they view their obligations to customers, what they want to achieve with the information they gather about customers and the extent to which top management permits the harnessing of constraints to achieve competitive differentiation. By following this approach, organisations can be aided in their effort to align their customer information privacy programme with their organisational priorities (Greenaway & Chan, 2013). Further, it helps to bring senior decision-makers into the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s privacy conversation.

## Limitations

We acknowledge that the distinctions among the categories may not be wholly discrete. Our explanations of <sup>fi</sup>rm behaviours represent a continuum, and <sup>fi</sup>rms’ actual behaviours may blur the boundaries. The objective of our efforts is not predictive but to ‘explain primarily how and why some phenomena occur’ and, as such, ‘could well be labelled a theory for understanding (Gregor, 2006, p. 624). In our discussion, we have tried to demonstrate how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s orientation is positioned on this continuum as a result of the control and justice mechanisms they offer their customers, We acknowledge also that our discussion of control, justice and ethics has not beer argued using philosophy but instead by using theory and practical examples.

## CONCLUSION

It has been argued that ‘information privacy-related issues are evidently of immediate concern to society and, accordingly, they should be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the research conducted by IS academics’ (Davison et al., 2003, p. 342). Concern for privacy protection continues to grow with the expansion of electronic (Wang, 2008) and mobile commerce (Kim & Han, 2009). While there is a growing body of research into consumer privacy preferences, there has been a lack of corresponding research into organisational responses. Given that customer information acquisition is at the heart of computer-based customer relationship management approaches (Pan & Lee, 2003), this is surprising. Businesses that collect, store and manipulate personal in formation need to determine in whose interests they are operating, how they will use the information and the extent to which they should move beyond statutory minimums to provide broader privacy protection to their customers. The competing values and trade-offs driving organisational decision-making that determine their CIPO may be tacit and implicit. Our conceptual framework helps make these trade-offs explicit and actionable. It serves as a guide to related future research and the development of measures to assess privacy behaviours. At the same time, our work is intended to facilitate improved privacy management through an examination of choices available to organisations. Policymakers may also <sup>fi</sup>nd this a useful framework for evaluating organisational privacy behaviours. The CIPO framework can assist organisations to understand their particular privacy posture. It challenges <sup>fi</sup>rms to articulate their ethical obligations, customer information management strategies and their legal risk assessments. Identifying their CIPO will assist <sup>fi</sup>rms to better align their actual privacy programmes with their top organisational concerns (Chan, 2003; Greenaway & Chan, 2013). Researchers, managers and policymakers alike need a better understanding of how and why organisations differ in their treatment of customer information privacy. We hope our conceptualisation of CIPO as a complex business phenomenon provides a helpful foundation for future theoretical and empirical work.
