# ENGAGING PHYSICIANS WITH INTRODUCTORY INCENTIVES: REFERENCES TO ONLINE AND OFFLINE INCOME $^{1}$

Xiaofei Zhang
School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology,

Karen Xie
School of Business, University of Connecticut,
Stamford, CT, U.S.A. {karen.xie@uconn.edu}

Bin Gu
Questrom School of Business, Boston University,
Boston, MA, U.S.A. {bgu@bu.edu}

Xitong Guo
Business School, Sichuan University, Chengdu, China
School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology,
Harbin, CHINA {xitongguo@hit.edu.cn}

Incentives make or break user contributions. While providing introductory incentives to attract new users has become increasingly popular among online communities, their impact on user contributions remains largely unknown. Utilizing a policy change that doubled the incentives paid to physicians in a leading online health community, we examined the impacts of both the initiation and the termination of such introductory incentives on physician contributions (in terms of patient consultations) and how the impacts varied according to the physician's online and offline income. We found that despite an increase in physician contributions during the policy window, the introductory incentives unintentionally decreased physician contributions after the policy window ended. Additionally, physicians tended to anchor their contributions using their online rather than offline income as a reference point, suggesting that mental accounting was at play. Our findings provide a cautionary perspective on the unintended consequences of using introductory incentives and reveal the associated mechanisms of mental accounting when users make contributions (or not) to online communities. These findings provide important implications for incentive design and user engagement in online communities.

Keywords: Online health communities, user contribution, introductory incentives, mental accounting, income reference

## Introduction

Online communities are internet-based platforms that allow different parties to exchange information and resources (Gu et al., 2007). Online health communities (OHCs), in particular, have become increasingly crucial for patient care (Agarwal et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2020). By enabling fee-based medical consultations, OHCs help patients overcome time and space barriers to obtain much-needed healthcare and medical knowledge online without the need for an in-person office visit (Goh et al., 2016). Acting as two-sided markets, OHCs charge patients a health consultation fee and offer physicians part of the fee to reply to patient inquiries (Jiang et al., 2021). Therefore, in many OHCs, physicians are driven by incentives to make for-reward contributions. During COVID-19, there was a surge in the OHC user base, which ballooned to 8-10 times larger than before on a daily basis (Yan, 2021).

While OHCs have become increasingly important and popular, their challenge centers on physicians' for-reward contributions (Wang et al., 2022b). In contrast to patients, who are often eager to participate in health consultations, physicians must balance participation in OHCs with their busy regular jobs. In leading OHCs such as haodf.com and dxy.cn, more than half of the physicians remain inactive and do not make any contributions after enrollment (Ma, 2018). Without active contributions from users (physicians in our case), online communities will eventually die out (Becker et al., 2010). To expand the user base and encourage user contributions, many OHCs (e.g., Teladoc Health, Chunyu Doctor, dxy.cn, and haodf.com) provide short-term introductory incentives to physicians joining the OHC (Liu et al., 2021; Wu & Po, 2016). These introductory incentives are often in the form of extra monetary incentives (in addition to the regular health consultation fees offered by the OHC) within a certain period (i.e., during a policy window), with the aim of encouraging new physicians' contributions. If the user engagement paradigm is increasingly shifting to introductory incentives, understanding their impact on physician contributions is critical research contributing to the practical agenda of OHCs (Becker et al., 2010; Hsu et al., 2022).

In this paper, we seek to understand the impact of introductory incentives aimed at motivating the contributions of physicians who are new to the OHC. The literature documents mixed findings on using incentives to scale users and stimulate user contributions. While the positive effect of incentives has been generally established (Burtch et al., 2022; Sun & Zhu, 2013), some studies have found that it fails to hold (Garnefeld et al., 2012; Sun et al., 2017). Becker et al. (2010) specifically pointed out that even though incentives in online communities can increase the user base, they do not ensure user engagement or subsequent contributions. According to motivation theory, rational individuals are driven by extrinsic motivations to obtain separable outcomes, such as money or praise, for their behavior (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Therefore, our conjecture is that additional monetary incentives will induce more user contributions. Nevertheless, according to mental accounting theory, when incentives diminish, they can potentially induce a disproportionately negative impact (Abeler & Marklein, 2017; Thaler, 1985). This is because users may value losses (losing the extra monetary incentive after the policy window ends) in a segregated loss account that is distinct from their gain account and may place more weight on the loss account versus the gain account. To test our conjecture and add evidence to the literature, we ask our first research question: How do introductory incentives influence the contributions of physicians new to an OHC during and after the introductory policy window?

We further explore the mechanism behind the impact of introductory incentives when physicians contribute to OHCs. Physicians provide health consultations in OHCs as gig work. Because they generally earn decent offline income from affiliated health institutions, the relative value they assign to the introductory incentives may affect their contributions, speaking to the degree to which such incentives can engage physicians (Torchiana et al., 2013). Offline income, as one type of established gain, has been shown to influence individuals' sensitivity to ongoing incentives (Thaler & Johnson, 1990) and subsequent decisions (Suhonen & Saastamoinen, 2018). Meanwhile, individuals' incentive sensitivity may also be dependent on their existing online income from such online communities as another type of established gain (Suhonen & Saastamoinen, 2018). Yet the prior literature on user incentives has focused primarily on the direct effect of online incentives (Garnefeld et al., 2012) without taking offline or online income into account. Drawing on the theory of mental accounting (Abeler & Marklein, 2017; Thaler, 1985), we further explore the role of offline versus online income in influencing the effectiveness of introductory incentives in this context. Thus, our second research question is: How are the impacts of introductory incentives contingent on physicians' online versus offline income?

We collected data from a leading OHC in China that provides fee-based telehealth services for patients wishing to consult with certified physicians online. Physicians receive monetary compensation in exchange for replying to patients' online inquiries. On April 1, 2016, the OHC unexpectedly introduced a policy that doubled the compensation for physicians joining the OHC (i.e., the introductory incentives). Under this policy, any new physician enrolling in the OHC would receive double the normal compensation for their first reply to a new patient in the following calendar month (after the month in which they enrolled). After the policy window ended, the incentives returned to normal. The policy provided an empirical opportunity for us to observe the change in physicians' contributions during and after the policy window. Our identification centered on a difference-in-differences (DID) approach using a coarsened exact matched sample. Our treatment group consisted of physicians who enrolled after the policy was introduced (i.e., affected physicians), while our control group comprised physicians who enrolled before the policy was introduced but were similar to the affected physicians in terms of offline working features, enrollment time, and contributions in their enrollment month.

Our findings are threefold. First, we found that introductory incentives increased contributions from newly enrolled physicians during the policy window. Specifically, there was an 8.48% surge in the number of consulted patient inquiries from affected physicians compared to unaffected physicians. By demonstrating the effectiveness of introductory incentives in driving physician contributions, this finding confirms motivation theory, which suggests that additional monetary incentives and the higher expected utility would motivate physicians to contribute more.

Second, and unexpectedly, we found that the contributions of affected physicians dropped dramatically after the policy window ended, to a level of about 10.6% less than that of unaffected physicians. This finding adds a caveat to the introductory incentive, suggesting that physicians tend to place the perceived loss (losing extra compensation by returning to normal incentives) in a segregated mental account and place more weight on this account. In line with the loss-aversion conjecture per mental accounting theory (Thaler, 1985), such sensitivity potentially leads to a disproportionately negative effect associated with the removal of extra monetary incentives, causing the process to backfire.

Having unveiled the dual impacts of introductory incentives on physician contributions during and after the policy window, some natural questions arise: Why do physicians make contributions (or not) to OHCs? Does physicians' offline income impact the extent to which online incentives from the OHC drive physicians' contributions? These questions speak to the inherent motivation of physicians to contribute to OHCs. We found that the impact of introductory incentives on physician contributions is strengthened among physicians with higher amounts of online income from the OHC (relative to other physicians) but is weakened among physicians with higher amounts of income offline (relative to other physicians). However, interestingly, we found that the impact of offline income becomes insignificant when online income is also at play. The results point to two independent mental accounts: Our findings suggest that when physicians contribute to OHCs, they are clearly motivated more by their online income (including extra incentives) than by their offline income (regular income) as a reference point, creating incentive design opportunities for OHCs to better motivate physicians.

Our study makes important contributions to the literature. We first advance the understanding of the dual impacts (not only the initiation but also the termination) of introductory incentives on physician contributions in OHCs. Taking the entire life cycle of an incentive policy into account, our findings provide important insights, particularly regarding the unintended consequences of terminating temporary incentives on user contributions. Second, by comparing the change in user behavior under different levels of monetary incentives (double vs. regular), we explain the change in user behavior from a unique mental accounting perspective. Third, our study explores both online and offline income as reference points and examines their effects on user contributions, revealing unique insights on income source dependence that can inform incentive design. Finally, our findings speak to the caveat of using introductory incentives to target new users, which are critical to the sustainable growth of any online community, and provide useful suggestions on new user engagement. Our paper points to important directions for future research, which could consider the critical role of monetary incentives on user contributions, how users use incomes types (online vs. offline) as reference points, users' strategic reactions to incentive policy changes (e.g., initiation and termination), and incentive design in general online communities.

Our findings also provide practical implications for incentive design in online communities. First, although introductory incentives boost short-term engagement among new users, we found that terminating such incentives can backfire. A more careful incentive design, guided by an estimation of platform costs and the return on user engagement is critical. Second, we found that user responses to online incentives are not dependent on offline income. Online communities should thus take measures to guide users to separate online incentives from offline income to reinforce the mental accounting of online incentives in a segregated income account. Lastly, to mitigate the negative after-policy effect of introductory incentives and the steep drop in user contributions upon policy termination, platforms should adopt multiple approaches to alleviate loss aversion among users, such as using a gradual incentive reduction approach, awarding introductory incentives based on cumulative contributions during the policy window, and managing user expectations.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We begin with a discussion of the related literature, followed by formal hypothesis development. Then, we describe the methodological details of our analyses, followed by the model specifications and associated results. Finally, we conclude the paper and present supporting discussions and results from a plethora of additional tests in the Appendix.

## Related Literature

## Online Health Communities

The emergence of OHCs has enabled virtual connections between patients and healthcare providers, facilitating new venues for patients to access healthcare and medical resources online (Agarwal et al., 2010; Huang et al., 2021). Rather than going through the hassle of searching for a physician, driving to their office, parking, and sitting in a waiting room when they are sick, patients using OHCs can select a physician and request health consultations (by paying a fee) from the comfort of their own home (Goh et al., 2016). Virtual consultations can also be easier to fit into both patients' and physicians' busy schedules and can prevent the spread of disease by keeping sick patients at home. Due to the many advantages of OHCs, some governments (e.g., China) allow OHCs to sell prescription drugs and even allow medical insurance plans to cover online health consultations. $^{2}$

Physicians are at the core of OHCs. Motivating physicians—especially those who are new to an OHC—to contribute by answering patient inquiries and providing health consultations is vital for OHCs. Prior research shows that online communities can greatly benefit from qualified new users (Becker et al., 2010; Ren et al., 2012). New users can bring new perspectives and knowledge to the communities (Burtch et al., 2022; Ransbotham & Kane, 2011). Without an influx of new users to replace departing users and continuously contribute content, any online community will eventually die out (Becker et al., 2010). However, retaining new users can be challenging because they often start as lurkers, making no contributions (Kraut et al., 2012), and may exit the community after enrollment (Ren et al., 2012). To address this challenge, online communities implement various approaches to build new users' identities (Ma & Agarwal, 2007), recognize new users' contributions (Ren et al., 2012), and help new users learn how to make contributions to the community (Anderson & Simester, 2004). Among those approaches, one increasingly popular one (and also the focus of our study) is introductory incentives, through which online communities offer extra monetary compensation to new users to incentivize their contributions. By empirically investigating the impact of introductory incentives on physicians, we provide a valuable understanding of physician engagement in OHCs.

## Monetary Incentives

Motivation theory suggests that human behavior is driven by both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Intrinsic motivation refers to the inner pleasure and inherent satisfaction of engaging in a particular behavior (Vallerand, 1997), while extrinsic motivation involves obtaining separable outcomes, such as money or praise (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In information systems, various types of extrinsic motivation have been studied for user contribution, such as status motivation (Roberts et al., 2006), network position (Zhang & Wang, 2012), image motivation (Qiu & Kumar, 2017), and peer recognition (Burtch et al., 2022). Among these diverse forms, monetary incentives stand out as a classic and well-recognized type of extrinsic motivation (Qiao et al., 2020). In online communities, monetary incentives are widely used to motivate user contributions; accordingly, the literature is increasingly studying the role of monetary incentives in engaging users (Yu et al., 2022). We summarize related literature in Table 1 using a $2 \times 2$ matrix in which the strategy of monetary incentives (launch vs. decrease) is discussed in voluntary/prosocial and for-reward contexts. We examined both the initiation and the termination of additional monetary incentives in the context of a temporal change in for-reward communities. Our study, situated in the lower-right quadrant, addresses the gaps in the literature from multiple perspectives.

The literature in the upper-left quadrant of Table 1 focuses on introducing monetary incentives in a voluntary/prosocial context. In voluntary/prosocial communities (such as users voluntarily generating reviews or answering questions), users usually contribute content based on their intrinsic motivation without the need to be monetarily rewarded. Some studies in this quadrant found that the launch of monetary incentives increased users' contribution quantity (Burtch et al., 2018; Sun & Zhu, 2013; Yu et al., 2022), while others reported insignificant (Bock et al., 2005; He et al., 2023; Lou et al., 2013) or even negative effects (Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2017) on contribution quantity. In particular, the unexpected effect of monetary incentives in reducing user contributions is primarily attributed to intrinsic motivation being crowded out after introducing monetary incentives in prosocial/voluntary contexts (Deci et al., 1999; Frey & Jegen, 2001). This crowding-out may be due to the fact that the launch of incentives changes the perceived nature of contributions (Frey & Jegen, 2001) from helping others to obtaining rewards. Our study differs from this stream of research by focusing on incentives in a for-reward context, where the perceived nature of contributions likely would not change much with increasing/decreasing incentives, as users receive incentives for each contribution. Adding to this stream of research, we examined the effects of introducing additional monetary incentives in a for-reward community.

As can be seen in the lower-left quadrant of Table 1, only a few studies on monetary incentives have been conducted in for-reward contexts (e.g., paid knowledge and content sharing). Collectively, these studies show consistent positive effects of monetary incentives on user contributions. For example, Wang et al. (2022a) found a positive spillover effect of an incentive-driven feature on user contributions in a knowledge-sharing community (i.e., Zhihu Live). While the context of this stream of literature is close to ours, we examined not only the initiation of introductory incentives but also their termination and how this temporal change affected user contributions.

Some research has focused on the elimination of monetary incentives in voluntary/prosocial contexts (the upper-right quadrant of Table 1). For example, Goswami and Urminsky (2017) found that the expected or unexpected removal of temporary incentives decreased user engagement in the crowdsourcing community; also, this decrease did not vary according to whether individuals knew about the removal in advance. Our study differs from Goswami and Urminsky (2017) in its research questions, theoretical lens, and empirical design and analyses. First, our study examines the effect of both the initiation and the termination of incentives on user contributions, providing a more complete understanding of the varying impact under different levels of incentives. Note that we examined the removal of extra monetary incentives (with normal incentives still in place), which differs from the complete elimination of incentives and the consequent user reactions that have been studied in the literature. Second, we provide a theoretical lens through the mental accounting perspective to shed light on the long-term influence of incentive changes and the effect of online and offline income as reference points, which is different from the goal balancing and effort justification perspectives used in the literature. Lastly, our empirical design draws upon a quasi-experiment with data directly collected from physicians who are new to an online community rather than from recruited subjects in a controlled lab environment, as featured in the literature. These areas not only differentiate our study from the existing literature but also provide important extensions to Goswami and Urminsky (2017).

## Online and Offline Income as Reference Points

Extensive studies have attempted to explain the impact of incentives on user behaviors in specific scenarios involving, for example, social influences (Burtch et al., 2018; Liu & Feng,

2021; Sun et al., 2017), social approval (He et al., 2023), types of incentives (Yu et al., 2022), and types of contributing tasks (Qiao et al., 2021). For instance, He et al. (2023) found that the effects of incentives are different under different levels of social approval. As users may respond to incentives differently due to their individual heterogeneities, studies have explored individual contingencies, such as user reputation (Wang et al., 2022a), in relation to the effectiveness of incentives. We shed light on an underresearched contingency—individuals' online and offline income—examining how this contingency would affect the impact of introductory incentives.

We mainly draw on mental accounting theory, which suggests that people create a set of mental accounts to organize, evaluate, and keep track of financial activities and have different subjective framings for different accounts (Thaler, 1999). According to this theory, people mentally allocate money from different sources or for different uses into different mental accounts and do not treat money as fungible (Abeler & Marklein, 2017; Thaler, 1985). Further, when evaluating money from different accounts, people set different reference points for their decisions based on the context (Heath et al., 1995; Thaler, 1999) and thus have different framings for decisions (Heath et al., 1995). This theory has mainly been used to explain human monetary decision-making, such as consumption, spending, investment, and income/incentives (Silva et al., 2023).

Grouping money into different categories or accounts is the basic assumption of mental accounting. In terms of income or incentives, extensive literature has shown that different accounts can be created according to the source or type of money—for example, gain vs. loss (Thaler, 1985), current income vs. future income (Shefrin & Thaler, 1988), price changes (Heath et al., 1995), windfall vs. regular income (Milkman & Beshears, 2009), repayment vs. regular income (Xu et al., 2021), and reward points vs. money (Stourm et al., 2015). Even though the popularity of using online incentives to engage users is increasing in the gig economy era, this theory has seldom been used to explore users' responses to such incentives. Consequently, there is a notable gap in understanding how users mentally process online incentives and establish reference points for them. Therefore, this study draws on mental accounting theory as the overarching framework to explain the impact of introductory incentives.

First, we use mental accounting theory to explain physicians' responses to a decrease in incentives in an OHC. According to this theory, when a previously high level of incentive is decreased, individuals will evaluate the current incentive according to two segregated accounts, i.e., a gain account reflecting the previously large incentive and a loss account reflecting a decrease in the incentive (Thaler, 1985). This segregation then reduces their value evaluation of the current incentive because the loss function is steeper than the gain function according to the value function (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Thaler, 1999). As shown in Table 1, few studies in the extant literature have explored the impact of a decreased incentive. Using mental accounting to explain its impact can provide new insights into the role of temporal incentive changes and introductory incentives.

Further, in a broader purview, we rely on mental accounting theory to explore how physicians frame the introductory incentive relative to their established online vs. offline income. The phenomenon of prior gains influencing subsequent incentive-seeking behavior has been confirmed in different offline contexts from a mental accounting perspective (Ratnadiwakara & Yerramilli, 2022; Suhonen & Saastamoinen, 2018) but is underresearched in the online context. Also, little is known about how responding to online incentives is influenced by offline income among professional users who have both online and offline income sources. We aim to fill the gaps by incorporating insights from the mental accounting literature (Abeler & Marklein, 2017; Thaler, 1985) into our investigation of how physicians' established online and offline income impacts the effectiveness of introductory incentives. In our context, we conjecture that physicians will consider their income as one single income account or two distinct accounts—i.e., their online earnings (generated through prior contributions to the OHC) and their offline income (derived from regular earnings)—when deciding how to contribute to the OHC. Utilizing the introductory incentive policy, we verify this conjecture by exploring whether their response to introductory incentives used online income or offline income as a reference point. By incorporating the principles of mental accounting, our study contributes to the existing literature by shedding light on the strategic design and operationalization of online incentives for professional gig workers in OHCs, taking both their online and offline income sources into account.

## Hypothesis Development

Informed by the existing literature, we propose hypotheses to explore the impact of introductory incentives on physician contributions in an OHC during and after an introductory policy window (i.e., the temporal change), examining how the impact varies with respect to the physicians' offline and online income.

## Impacts of Introductory Incentives

In our context, introductory incentives are extra monetary incentives offered by an OHC during an introductory policy window. Monetary incentives are widely used to encourage individuals to complete certain tasks (Jenkins et al., 1998). Physicians, like all individuals, can be influenced by these external factors in their decisions to contribute. In line with motivation theory, monetary incentives play an important role, as they extrinsically motivate physicians to contribute (Deci & Ryan, 2002). Under an introductory incentive, physicians will receive increased monetary incentives for each contribution, which will improve their financial well-being and the utility of their contributions during this period (Schoemaker, 1982). Thus, greater financial well-being and a higher expected utility would further improve users' extrinsic motivation, driving them to contribute even more to maximize the incentive (Deci & Ryan, 2002; He et al., 2023). Even though, at the beginning, additional incentives can potentially undermine intrinsic motivation (Frey & Jegen, 2001), the increase in extrinsic motivation is likely larger than the decrease in intrinsic motivation, which is consistent with the law of behavior stipulating that greater incentives will induce more effort (Gneezy et al., 2011). Prior research lends support for this, showing that incentives based on the number of contributions are a primary motivator of contributions because users will focus on increasing their contributions to receive greater incentives (Jenkins et al., 1998; Qiao et al., 2021). Thus, we propose that introductory incentives will increase physicians' contributions during the policy window.

## H1: Introductory incentives have a positive impact on physician contributions during the policy window.

After experiencing an incentive change, users tend to develop a new contingency between their performance and reward (Wright, 1991). In our context, after reaching the end of the policy window, the extra monetary incentives are removed, and the incentives return to the regular level. Removing the extra monetary incentives disrupts the balance because users receive a decreased incentive for each contribution. Even though the incentives are from a single source, e.g., the OHC, a change in incentives can induce mutable mental accounts depending on how users frame the income (Epley et al., 2006). Per mental accounting theory, when encountering a change of income (or loss), individuals tend to use two segregated accounts rather than one integrated account to process the change (Thaler, 1985). Accordingly, when experiencing a decrease in incentives after the policy window ends, users may evaluate the current online incentives in two segregated accounts, i.e., a gain account reflecting the previous high level of incentives (including regular incentives and extra incentives), and a loss account reflecting the decrease in incentives (Thaler, 1985). As individuals generally suffer from loss aversion, the perceived loss from the decreased incentives may be greater than the relative gain received from the extra incentives (Heath et al., 1995; Thaler, 1999). Such a perception may reduce the extrinsic motivation of the affected physicians to a level that is even lower than that of those who were not affected by the policy. As a result, we conjecture that removing the extra monetary incentives at the end of the introductory window will decrease user contributions.

H2: Introductory incentives have a negative impact on physician contributions after the policy window ends.

## Income Reference Points

OHCs provide professional users such as physicians with an extra source of compensation in addition to their offline earnings. These two sources of income can create two income reference points—an online income reference point and offline income reference point (hereafter, online reference point and offline reference point—that influence how physicians respond to introductory incentives (Banerjee et al., 2019; Thaler, 1985), Physicians may allocate their online and offline income into two segregated accounts. It is unclear whether physicians will evaluate the introductory incentives based solely on the online account or on both accounts.

According to mental accounting theory (Thaler, 1999), if the online incentives are processed in a segregated account, individuals will develop a reference point in this account and evaluate the incentives in this account independently (Banerjee et al., 2019). When evaluating whether to contribute online under the introductory incentive, physicians may use their prior online income from the OHC as an important reference point. Furthermore, for physicians who have earned more compensation online, the expectation and appreciation of this source of compensation can cause them to assign a greater value to this compensation source than its absolute value (Robinson & Berridge, 2008), leading them to contribute more to obtain more compensation from this source (Berridge, 2007). That is, physicians who have received more online income may be more responsive to introductory incentives (i.e., online compensation) and contribute more to the OHC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3a: Online income strengthens the positive effect of introductory incentives on physician contributions during the policy window.

In the same vein, the online reference point is likely to be in play when the introductory incentives are terminated. That is, physicians who have earned more income from the OHC may be more sensitive to the loss account resulting from the termination of the introductory incentive from the online source (Banerjee et al., 2019). As a result, terminating the introductory incentive may exhibit a stronger negative impact on physicians who have received higher online earnings from the OHC. Thus, we hypothesize,

H3b: Online income strengthens the negative effect of terminating introductory incentives on physician contributions after the policy window ends.

Regarding offline income, if physicians evaluate introductory incentives against both their online and offline income, they may also develop a reference point based on their offline income, which is typically higher. Prior literature suggests that users with a high reference point based on their higher offline income will likely exhibit a lower value perception of an introductory incentive because it represents only an incremental addition to their higher offline income (Thaler, 1999). This lower value perception results in a lower increase in extrinsic motivation for users to contribute under introductory incentives (Deci & Ryan, 2002). In other words, physicians with higher offline incomes will likely be less inclined to respond to introductory incentives and will consequently contribute less to the OHC. Nevertheless, we propose that even when a physician's online reference point is accounted for, the offline reference point will still impact physicians' contributions to an OHC. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4a: Even when accounting for a physician's online reference point, offline income weakens the positive effect of introductory incentives on the physician's contributions during the policy window.

In the same vein, when online incentives are reduced after the policy window ends, the loss aversion toward the removal of the additional incentive may also be less salient among physicians receiving greater offline compensation. The reference point based on their higher offline income may also make physicians more immune to the psychological impact of the reduced online incentive, as any reduction in online incentives may not significantly alter their overall financial well-being. Thus, terminating the introductory incentive may have less influence on physicians' motivation to contribute to an OHC. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H4b: Even when accounting for a physician's online reference point, offline income weakens the negative effect of terminating introductory incentives on the physician's contributions after the policy window ends.

## Methodology

## Research Context

Our study was conducted in a leading OHC in China. Since launching in 2006, this OHC has attracted about 260,000 physicians from accredited hospitals and medical institutions throughout the country and has provided healthcare services to more than 79 million patients as of July 2022. The healthcare services provided in the OHC include text and phone consultations with physicians, offline appointments, and patient education.

Patients consult physicians by posting health or medical inquiries (in text) on a physician's community page. Figure 1 shows an example of a physician's community page. Each physician has a profile page that displays their basic information (the hospital where the physician works, professional experience, physician rank, etc.), contributions made to the OHC (in terms of inquiries answered and consultation provided), and interactions with patients, such as thank-you letters and virtual gifts (virtual flowers bought online, etc.) sent by patients.

Whereas patients can easily access health information and physician consultations in the OHC, physicians can earn extra income by answering patient inquiries. For the first three questions asked by a patient, physician consultation fees are paid by the OHC (2 RMB if the physician replies to the patient's question within 12 hours, 1 RMB within 24 hours, and 0.5 RMB after 24 hours). If the physician does not reply to a patient's question, the OHC will reallocate the questions to other physicians with similar healthcare expertise three days after the question is posted. After three questions are answered, subsequent physician consultation fees are paid by patients (on average, 42.8 RMB for each question, with physicians receiving $70\%$ during our study period). Patients can also send thank-you letters (free to patients) and virtual gifts (paid by patients) to physicians to express appreciation.

The OHC provides an ideal context for studying the impact of introductory incentives on user contributions. First, it employs straightforward monetary incentives (i.e., the platform pays physicians for the first three patient questions and the patient pays for subsequent questions) to drive physician contributions and is thus different from many other online communities, which primarily use non-monetary mechanisms (Goes et al., 2016). Second, the OHC is solely incentive driven and cannot mandate contributions from physicians. Such a partnership with physicians gives them a high level of autonomy to determine how much or how often they would like to contribute. Lastly, the OHC periodically but unexpectedly experiments with incentive policies (such as the introductory incentive that doubled the normal physician consultant fee for the first three patient questions answered), allowing us to observe the temporal change in user contributions in a quasi-experimental setting. We discuss this unique incentive policy in more detail in the next section.

## The Introductory Incentive Policy

The OHC announced a policy stipulating that those physicians who joined the community after April 1, 2016, would receive twice the normal amount (i.e., 4 RMB) for their answer to every new patient's first question in the following calendar month (i.e., the treatment month). After the treatment month, the pay returned to normal (e.g., 2 RMB). We refer to this policy as an introductory incentive because the incentive was only provided in the calendar month following the physician's enrollment date. The initiation and termination of the policy allowed us to examine the impacts of introductory incentives during and after the policy window, providing a quasi-experimental setting based on the temporal change.

We used physicians who enrolled in the OHC between April 1, 2016, and June 30, 2016, as the treated group, while physicians who otherwise mirrored the treated group but enrolled between April 1, 2015, and June 30, 2015 (one year earlier) served as our control group. $^{3}$ Figure 2 illustrates the temporal change in introductory incentives. For the treated group, the incentive remained normal in the enrollment month, doubled in the treatment month (i.e., the calendar month after enrollment), and returned to normal after the treatment month. For the control group, the incentive level remained at the normal level without any change.

## Data and Measures

We collected daily data about physicians and their contributions to the OHC from April to December 2015 (eight months) and from April to December 2016 (eight months). The data initially consisted of 9,286 physicians. For each physician, we observed their contributions for six months (the enrollment month and five months afterward), their income (online and offline), and their characteristics. Table 2 presents the variable definitions and summary statistics. Our variables center on five categories: physician contributions, policy dummies, physician income (online and offline), physician characteristics, and OHC characteristics.

## Empirical Models

Our empirical strategy is a standard difference-in-differences design (Abadie & Imbens, 2006), capitalizing on the temporal policy change in introductory incentives. We further used a coarsened exact matching (CEM) approach with k2k restrictions $^{4}$ to construct comparable physicians across the treated and control groups (Abadie & Imbens, 2006; Iacus et al., 2012). Our CEM matching process yielded a total of 1,668 physicians—834 enrolled from April to June 2016 (the treated group) and 834 enrolled from April to June 2015 (the control group). For physicians in the control group, we set their assumed pre-treatment and treatment periods to be the same as the treatment group, i.e., the enrollment month as the pretreatment period and the first calendar month after enrollment as the treatment period. The two groups were similar in terms of personal characteristics (i.e., gender, physician rank, hospital level, department), enrollment time (i.e., enrollment day and month), and contributions made to the OHC in the pre-treatment period (i.e., contribution experience). We discuss the comparability between the two groups in more detail in Appendix B.

Using the CEM sample, the main specification of our empirical model is two DID regressions. Our unit of analysis is the physician-day. We modelled the direct impacts of policy initiation and policy termination separately. The equations are as follows. For robustness checks, we also estimated the impacts of policy initiation and policy termination in separate models in Appendix E.

where $Y_{it}$ is $Replies_{it}$ , the number of first patient questions replied to by a physician. As discussed earlier, the introductory incentives offered by the OHC only applied to the first question from a new patient answered by a newly enrolled physician. Although a physician could reply to multiple questions asked by a patient, we focused on the physician's reply to the first patient question only to estimate the policy impact. $Treated_{i}$ indicates whether a physician was affected by the introductory incentive policy, taking a value of 1 for the treated group (physicians enrolled in 2016) and 0 for the control group (physicians enrolled in 2015). $Double_{it}$ and $AfterDouble_{it}$ are two dummies indicating whether the policy was in effect and whether the policy had concluded, respectively. Note that for the control group, these two dummies were always zero. Regarding physician income, $OnlineIncome_{i}$ is the cumulative amount of monetary incentives the physician received from the OHC up to the treatment month, and $OfflineIncome_{i}$ is the annual physician salary according to a nationwide survey (see Appendix A).

In the set of control variables $(Z_{it})$ , we first included several characteristics about the physicians $(l.CumThank_{it}$ and $l.CumFreeQuota_{it})$ as proxies for intrinsic motivation. Since intrinsic motivation reflected from previous contributions can influence their further behavior (Davis et al., 1992), we took the lag of these two controls. We also controlled for their length of tenure on the OHC $(SinceOpen_{it})$ and the total number of patients in the community $(TotalPatient_{t})$ . We performed a log transformation of $TotalPatient_{t}$ to correct its skewed distribution (Gelman & Hill, 2006). Our observations were at the physician-day level, but physicians may face external shocks or factors that are common to all at a given period. We also incorporated the month-fixed effect following the practice of Gutiérrez et al. (2020), Lee et al. (2017), Vana et al. (2018), and Yue et al. (2019) and physician-fixed effects. The residual term, $\varepsilon_{it}$ , captures idiosyncratic shocks.

## Results and Findings

This section presents empirical evidence on the impact of temporal changes in introductory incentives on physician contributions. In the following section, we first examine how the initiation and termination of the introductory incentive affected physician contributions. Next, we investigate how such impacts vary under two income reference points. Then, we rule out alternative explanations and present a series of robustness checks.

## Impacts of Introductory Incentives

Our main analysis explores the direct impacts of introductory incentives on the contributions of physicians who were enrolled in the OHC during and after the policy window. Table 3 exhibits the results. Following the conjecture in the Impacts of Introductory Incentives section (and Hypotheses 1 and 2), we used two models to exhibit the results, with Model (1) reflecting the initiation of the policy and Model (2) reflecting the termination of the policy.

Model (1) shows that the coefficient for $Treated \times Double$ is positive and significant (0.044, p < 0.001). This result indicates that introductory incentives drove treated physicians to contribute more after the implementation of the introductory incentive. On average, physician contributions increased by about 0.044 per day during the policy window. Given that the mean physician contributions were 0.519 (see Table 2), the increase in contributions was about 0.044/0.519 = 8.48% per day. This finding supports H1, that introductory incentives improve physician contributions during the policy window.

Model (2) shows that the termination of introductory incentives negatively influenced physician contributions (-0.055, p < 0.001). On average, physician contributions decreased by 0.055 per day during the four months after the policy window ended (about 0.055/0.519 = 10.6%). This result indicates that treated physicians decreased their contributions more than those who had not benefited from the introductory incentive policy (i.e., control physicians) when the policy was terminated. Therefore, H2, which conjectures that introductory incentives have a negative impact on physician contribution after the policy is terminated is supported. We further analyzed the change of the impact for four months after the policy terminated; the coefficients for the first month (-0.033, p < 0.001), the second month (-0.073, p < 0.001), and the third month (-0.049, p < 0.01) were all negative and significant, with the fourth month (-0.025, p > 0.100) being insignificant. That is, the negative after-policy impact only lasted for a limited time. We report more details of these results in Appendix C.

## Two Income Reference Points

Having confirmed the impact of the temporal change in introductory incentives on physician contributions, a natural question arises: How would such an impact vary according to a physician's online and offline income as reference points? Would the physician continue to value the income received from the OHC (i.e., online income) and make continuous contributions despite earning adequate offline income? We explored the moderating effects of the two income sources as follows: We first tested the online reference point conjecture with the moderating role of online income and then tested the offline reference point conjecture. Table 4 presents the results.

Models (1) and (2) report the moderating effects of online income. The coefficient of $Treated \times Double \times OnlineIncome$ is positive and significant (0.097, p < 0.001), and the coefficient of $Treated \times AfterDouble \times OnlineIncome$ is negative and significant (-0.168, p < 0.001). These results indicate that online income strengthens both the positive impact during the policy window and the negative impact after the policy, supporting H3a and H3b.

Before formally testing H4a and H4b, we verified the offline reference point without accounting for the online reference point in Models (3) and (4). The effect of $Treated \times Double \times OfflineIncome$ is negative (-0.009, $p < 0.01$ ), indicating that as physicians' offline income increases, the impact of introductory incentives on their contributions is weaker. Similarly, the effect of $Treated \times AfterDouble \times OfflineIncome$ is positive and significant (0.013, $p < 0.001$ ), showing that without accounting for the online reference point, when offline income increases, the negative effect of terminating the introductory incentive is also mitigated.

We further explored whether the offline reference point would still hold when the online reference point is accounted for by further incorporating the moderating role of online income. Models (5) and (6) incorporate the moderating effects of both online and offline income in the temporal change of the introductory incentive into Equations (1) and (2) using two pairs of moderation terms. In Model (5), we see that the effect of $Treated \times Double \times OfflineIncome$ becomes insignificant (-0.004, p > 0.10) after including the moderating effect of online income (i.e., $Treated \times Double \times Online-Income$ ). An F-test shows that their coefficients are significantly different (p < 0.001). In Model (6), the effect of $Treated \times After-Double \times OfflineIncome$ also becomes insignificant (0.005, p > 0.10) with the inclusion of $Treated \times After-Double \times OnlineIncome$ , and their coefficients are also significantly different in the F-test (p < 0.001). That is, the moderating effect of the online income is so salient that it overshadows the offline reference point. Therefore, H4a and H4b are not supported. The results indicate that online income is the main reference point for physicians responding to introductory incentives; thus, when the online reference point is accounted for, the offline reference point becomes insignificant.

Intrinsic motivation: Our policy of introductory incentives centers on monetary compensation to physicians and how the extrinsic motivation (or lack thereof) of earning double compensation drives their contributions to the OHC. One might argue that the intrinsic motivation of physicians drove the results of increased contribution during the policy window. Concerns could also be raised about intrinsic motivation being crowded out after the policy window ends, an alternative explanation of the backfire effect of monetary incentives, resulting in reduced physician contributions. However, our concerns about the confounding effect of intrinsic motivation are minimal. First, previous research has primarily found the impact of monetary incentives in voluntary or prosocial contexts (Frey & Jegen, 2001; Gneezy et al., 2011), whereas our research context is incentive-driven and for-reward and is thus more immune to the confounding effect of intrinsic motivation. Second, both our treated and control groups received regular monetary incentives in the enrollment month, with the only difference being that the treated group received double incentives during the treatment month. If intrinsic motivation had impacted physicians in making contributions, it would have affected both groups.

Nevertheless, we generated a proxy of the intrinsic motivation of physicians: $FreeQuotas_{it}$ , where patients paid for further consultations after the first three questions, but physicians could offer “free quotas” that allowed them to ask more free questions. It was measured as the ratio of the number of free quotas physician i provided to the number of i’s new patients on day t. Since intrinsic motivation refers to performing an activity for its inherent satisfaction rather than some separable consequence (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Vallerand, 1997), the physicians’ willingness to answer extra patient questions for free speaks to their intrinsic motivation. We used $FreeQuotas_{it}$ as the dependent variable and replicated Equations (1) and (2) by controlling for the number of new patients replied to on the same day, i.e., $Replies_{it}$ . Table 5 exhibits the results. We found the initiation (0.001, p > 0.10) and termination (0.001, p > 0.10) of introductory incentives did not change (or crowd out) physicians’ intrinsic motivation. Additionally, in all of the regression models throughout the paper, we controlled for $l.CumFreeQuota_{it}$ (the lagged cumulative number of free quotas provided by physician i on day t). We did not find that intrinsic motivation, if present, would change or remove the impact of introductory incentives.

Contribution quality: Our study centers on contributions (in quantity). Prior research suggests that incentives can increase quantity while decreasing quality (Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018). If incentives reduce quality, their impact on contribution quantity may be due to the fact that some physicians seek to obtain introductory incentives by providing low-quality contributions. Thus, one might question whether contribution quantity compromises contribution quality, thus confounding the impact of introductory incentives. We generated a proxy for perceived contribution quality using the number of virtual gifts from patients that physician i received on day t ( $Gifts_{it}$ ). $^{5}$ This measure speaks to patients' perceived quality of physician consultations, which is an arguably valid quality indicator of the quality of physician contributions.

We investigated the impact of introductory incentives on contribution quality using Equations (1) and (2), using $Gifts_{it}$ (i.e., the measure of contribution quality) as the dependent variable while controlling for contribution quantity (i.e., $Replies_{it}$ ). Our findings, presented in Table 6, show that physicians' contribution quality remained the same both during the policy window in Model (1) (0.001, $p > 0.10$ ) and after the policy window ended in Model (2) (-0.001, $p > 0.10$ ). That is, physicians' contribution quality did not appear to be compromised as the contribution quantity increased.

Patient selection: Under the introductory incentive, treated physicians received double payment from the OHC for their first reply to each new patient. One might argue that introductory incentives can induce physicians to purposely select new patients whose health inquiries are easier to answer while ignoring those with more complex inquiries. By doing so, treated physicians could potentially answer more inquiries (and earn more incentives). To address this concern, we generated a proxy for inquiry complexity using the average number of questions a patient asked in their inquiries received by physician i's on day t, i.e., InquiryComplexity $_{it}$ . Our conjecture is that the more patient questions asked over the course of an inquiry, the more challenging the inquiry is and the more expertise the physician needs. Table 7 exhibits the regressions by replicating Equations (1) and (2) using $InquiryComplexity_{it}$ as the dependent variable and controlling for contribution quantity (i.e., $Replies_{it}$ ). We found no evidence of patient selection given the insignificant change in inquiry complexity addressed by physicians during and after the introductory incentive policy.

Patient herding: Some are concerned that physicians who reply to more patients would naturally attract more patients due to patient herding, leading them to contribute more during the policy window. To address this concern, we focused on physician contributions during the enrollment month only, without any intervention in the treatment policy (introductory incentives). ## Robustness Checks

We checked the robustness of the results through a series of tests. The robustness checks yielded results consistent with our main analyses.

Relative time model: One assumption of a DID design is that the affected and unaffected groups have relatively parallel trends before the event—the introductory incentive policy in our case. We relied on a relative time model to check whether parallel pre-policy trends existed between the affected and unaffected physicians. Drawing upon the procedures of Jung et al. (2019), our leads-lags model is specified as

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Summary of Findings

This paper examines the impact of introductory incentives on newly enrolled physicians in OHCs. Our results indicate that introductory incentives impose distinct effects on physician contributions during and after the policy window. Specifically, we first demonstrated that the policy boosted physician contributions during the policy window, aligning with previous research that suggests extrinsic motivation results in more contributions (Gneezy et al., 2011).

We also observed a disproportionately negative impact of the incentive termination on physician contributions after the policy terminates, and this negative impact lasted for a certain period (three months in our case) after an up-and-down trend. The observed up-trend can be attributed to the schedule of payments to physicians. Specifically, physicians receive their incentives monthly, which implies that they only begin to receive incentives for their contributions in the policy window in the first month after the introductory policy terminates. We propose that they thus feel the loss personally for the first time in the second month, which can largely undermine their extrinsic motivation. The reason for the down and dismiss trend might be due to the gradual closure of the loss account. This could occur as physicians become accustomed to reduced incentives over time, thereby internalizing the loss (Imas, 2016) or as they adjust to the lower level of incentives and no longer perceive it as a loss (Higgins & Liberman, 2018). This finding is consistent with Goswami and Urminsky's (2017) findings that the temporal negative effect of incentive removals disappears after a certain time. Therefore, it may be wise to keep in mind the possibility that providing short-term introductory incentives can backfire on user contributions, which is consistent with the predictions of the mental accounting theory that segregating losses from gains induces greater loss aversion (Thaler, 1985).

Our findings further suggest that physicians account for their online and offline income when contributing to the OHC. The impact of introductory incentives is found to be contingent on both the online income obtained from the online community and the offline income obtained from the physicians' regular earnings. Yet when online income is considered, the offline income ceases to act as a reference point and is no longer influential on physician contributions. Our results indicate that physicians evaluate online incentives in an independent “mental account” (Thaler, 1985) that is separate from regular offline income. Consequently, as physicians experience online incentives, they may ignore their offline income and open a new mental account for online income, motivating them to make continuous contributions to the OHC. Therefore, the moderating effect of offline income becomes insignificant when the moderating effect of online income is included. This finding aligns with prior mental accounting literature that the reference point within a specific account holds greater salience for decisions related to that account (Baucells & Hwang, 2017; Evers et al., 2022). In particular, our results show that the impact on contributions is stronger among physicians with a higher level of online income.

## Managerial Implications

Our results provide important implications for OHCs in terms of incentive design for user engagement. Indeed, we found that introductory incentives for physicians who are new to the platform appear to be effective in the short term. However, as physicians start increasing their contributions and maintain a steady flow of participation, it may become increasingly costly for the platform to continue paying double incentives. It may be inevitable that the platform will phase out the introductory incentives and return to normal incentives due to natural cost concerns (which is the case in our study). We point out the caveat of contributions dropping after the policy window ends. Thus, such policies may address the temporary needs of online community managers if the goal is to temporarily boost user contributions for platform strategic promotion campaigns in which a surge in user engagement is desired. In the long run, however, a more careful incentive design that is guided by a careful estimation of the platform costs and return on user engagement will be critical.

Regarding the implications of incentive program design and implementation, we found that physician responses to online incentives are not dependent on offline income. To motivate professional users with adequate offline salaries (physicians in our case), managers should take measures to motivate them to separate online incentives from offline income as a way to reinforce the mental accounting of online incentives as a segregated account of income. For example, a good strategy could be using platform-issued digital wallets to disburse monetary incentives instead of relying on direct deposit to bank accounts (as routine salaries do). Additionally, we found that introductory incentives have heterogeneous effects among different user groups. For instance, the policy had a stronger effect on contributions among users with a higher level of online income. This heterogeneity presents opportunities for managers to strategically target the user groups taking advantage of such incentives. For example, if the major goal is to improve user contributions, targeting users who have previously obtained more online income may be an effective course.

We also propose several approaches to alleviate the negative after-policy effect from the mental accounting perspective. First, instead of a complete cut-off the double incentives, we recommend that platforms take a gradual and stepwise approach to smooth out the deep drop in incentives (and associated physician contributions) until they eventually return to normal, e.g., 20% down in the first month, 30% down in the second month, etc. Such a gradual approach may provide some time for physicians to adjust their expectations and smooth out the negative effect of the sudden removal of introductory incentives. Second, platform owners/managers can offer introductory incentives based on the overall cumulative contributions over a certain period rather than per contribution. This adjusted incentive scheme could reduce the loss aversion of physicians and maintain a relatively more stable downward trend of contribution, if any, when the introductory incentives are eliminated. Lastly, we believe in the importance of expectations management and advocate for a more effective strategy in user communication. For example, platform owners and managers could communicate the personal branding and social welfare of physician contributions beyond the monetary incentives. Additionally, non-monetary incentives, such as user badges and platform recognition, could be announced to users when the policy terminates in lieu of the introductory monetary incentives. Such communications could effectively be used to counteract the negative after-policy impact.

## Theoretical Implications

Our findings contribute several novel theoretical insights to the existing literature. First, existing literature has primarily studied the impacts of the beginning or ongoing incentives (e.g., Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Qiao & Rui, 2023; Wang et al., 2024) rather than the impact of terminating incentives. We fill this void by investigating the entire life cycle of a for-reward double-incentive program, with a focus on the dual impacts (both at the beginning and at the termination of the incentives) and the temporal changes over time. Our findings provide a novel and comprehensive understanding of for-reward incentives. In particular, the sharp drop in physician contributions revealed in our findings informs researchers of the unintended consequences on user contributions of terminating introductory incentives and provides unique insights into incentive design and operation.

Second, the type of for-reward incentive we focused on provides a doubled incentive amount, which is different from existing literature that has primarily studied temporary incentives (Goswami & Urminsky, 2017) or incentives without varying levels (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Qiao & Rui, 2023). We investigated how users react to the double incentives at the beginning of the program and then to the return to regular incentives at the end. Comparing the change in user behavior in response to different levels of monetary incentives (double vs. regular) in a for-reward context helps mitigate concerns about the crowding out of intrinsic motivation when users experience for-reward incentives, which is typical in existing literature (e.g., Cholakova & Clarysse, 2015). We not only provide novel insights on the impacts of varying incentives but also explain the change of user behavior in response to different levels of incentives from a unique mental accounting perspective.

Third, prior for-reward literature has primarily focused on online monetary incentives (e.g., Wang et al., 2022a; Wang et al., 2024) without relating them to other offline income sources. We bridge this gap by exploring both online and offline income and examining their effects as reference points on user contributions through the lens of mental accounting, testing whether physician responses to online community incentives are potentially influenced by their offline salaries. We found that the two sources of income are evaluated, respectively, as two segregated mental accounts, and that physicians tend to anchor their contributions with reference to their online income generated from the OHC rather than their offline salaries. Such findings fill the gap in the literature by providing insights on income source dependence and provide important implications on incentive design with respect to users' other income reference points.

Finally, by investigating introductory incentives that primarily target new users, our study adds to the literature on user engagement through introductory programs. While most studies focus on incentivizing existing users' contributions (Burtch et al., 2018; He et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2022), we provide a different perspective on new users. Additionally, the incentives previously studied in the literature are uniform for all users and not specific to new users. We underline the importance of new users, who are central to the sustainable growth of any online community, and identify a caveat to user engagement, especially that of new users: Be mindful of the potential backfire effect of introductory incentive termination and the effect of online income as a reference point. Our findings not only bridge the gap in understanding the effects of introductory incentives on new user engagement but also provide useful suggestions for incentive design targeting new users.

## Concluding Remarks

User engagement is crucial for online communities; without active contributions from users, any online community will eventually wither. Hence, many online communities are implementing short-term introductory incentives to motivate users, which makes it vital to understand the consequences of such incentives. Drawing upon theoretical perspectives on motivation theory and mental accounting, we utilize the empirical opportunity of an incentive policy change in a major OHC to investigate the impact of introductory incentives on physician contributions. Our results indicate that the impact of introductory incentives is not singular, and online communities would be wise to consider the distinct effects of such incentives during and after the policy to make an informed decision. The results revealed in this study suggest that online incentives are weighed independently and separately from offline income. Therefore, when adopting a new type of online incentive program, managers should also cautiously consider the income reference points among users. Overall, this study provides an integrated theoretical framework for studying the effectiveness of introductory incentives. In doing so, our study provides a new perspective to explain the mixed findings in the online incentive literature and also contributes new knowledge about how online incentives are valued, relative to regular salaries among professional users, a topic that is becoming increasingly relevant in the era of the gig economy. It is our hope that our findings will inspire future research in this growing research area.

In closing, we acknowledge that our study has some limitations, which can provide opportunities for future research. First, when exploring physicians' contributing behavior in OHCs, we did not account for their offline workload or schedule. In reality, their offline activities will also likely influence their online behavior. Exploring such influences could offer more insights into physician behavior in online contexts. Second, when implementing the policy, physicians were aware of the initiation and termination of the introductory incentives. Such awareness may have induced strategic behaviors when contributing to the OHC—for example, physicians may have placed a higher value on the introductory incentives and thus purposefully contributed more during the policy. We recognize that the effect of policy awareness is captured within the overall effect of the policy. Understanding the effect of policy awareness is useful to accurately interpret the policy effect. However, policy awareness could not be recorded using our observed data. Future studies could draw on a qualitative approach, such as interviews or surveys, to explore the strategic behaviors deployed in response to incentive policies. Finally, we studied the impact of introductory incentives in the context of OHCs and focused on physicians who were new to the OHC and were serving both online and offline patients, which may limit the generalizability of our results. Future studies could verify our results in contexts that involve new non-physician users, users working online only, and users having different online and offline tasks.

## Measuring Offline and Online Income

## Offline Income

An online health community, i.e., dxy.com, conducted a nationwide survey among more than 30,000 physicians about their annual salary in March and April 2016, which is near our study period. The survey shows that the sampled physicians' average annual salary was about 80,000 RMB. It further breaks down the annual salary by hospital department, hospital level, city level, gender, and professional rank. Table A1 presents the offline salary specific to these characteristics of the 9,286 physicians in our full sample.

To calculate the offline salary of physicians, we used a three-step approach. First, we begin by assigning the department-level average salary from the survey to each physician in that department. Second, the department-level average salary is further updated according to the hospital level, city level, gender, and professional rank. For example, according to the survey, the annual salary of physicians in the pediatric department is 77,000 RMB. Physicians in this department are assigned an initial income of 77,000 RMB. In the second step, male physicians' income in this department was updated to 81,000 RMB from the initial income of 77,000 RMB, according to the survey. Similarly, the salary of physicians working at Level-1 hospitals was reduced by 6,000 RMB from the initial income of 77,000 RMB. Thereafter, their salary was further updated based on the level of the city and professional rank from the survey. Third, to be consistent with the measure of online income, we adapted the annual salary to the monthly level.

## Online Income

To measure online income, we collected data on the total online incentives physicians earned in their enrollment month, which were mainly from four sources. First, as we explained in the research context section, each patient could ask three free questions through textual consultations, and the platform paid physicians in the control group 2 RMB, 1 RMB, or 0.5 RMB for each patient-question and paid the treated physicians in the treated group 4 RMB, 2 RMB, or 1 RMB. Second, after exhausting the free questions, patients needed to make a payment to ask more questions (on average 42.8 RMB for three questions), 70% of which was distributed to physicians. Third, patients could make phone consultations with physicians by paying a higher fee, e.g., 80 RMB for 10 minutes. Lastly, in the course of the textual consultations, patients could send virtual gifts with monetary value to the physicians (about 16 RMB on average).
