# Interactive and diagnostic uses of management control systems in IS projects: Antecedents and their impact on performance

## A B S T R A C T

We attempted to determine how formal management control systems (MCS) are used by project managers in IS development (ISD) contexts. This involved investigating the antecedents of two types of project MCS use (interactive and diagnostic), and their direct and moderated impact on project performance. PLS analysis of data collected in a survey of 93 projects indicated that project managers level of discretion positively affected their level of interactive use of project MCS but did not influence their diagnostic use. Our findings also showed that interactive use of MCS enhanced performance when task uncertainty (task novelty and complexity) of an ISD was high, but worsened it when task uncertainty was low. Finally, diagnostic use of MCS apparently increased project performance when an ISD task uncertainty was low, but did not reduce it when task uncertainty was high. Overall, these results were stable across different size projects.

## 1. Introduction

Projects are often seen as social settings where there is tension between the need to use a sufficient level of control and the necessity to remain flexible in order to manage factors that contribute to uncertainty. This is particularly true in IS development (ISD) projects where changes in user requirements and/or in technology characteristics frequently occur and usually require alteration of the initial project plans and estimates. According to [15], proper management of an ISD project requires clear task boundaries and well understood individual responsibilities, and the specification of expected outcomes and behaviors. However, Ref. [4] argued that putting too much emphasis on controlling behavior may have deleterious effects by making project team members focus on completing pre-specified tasks and objectives while overlooking the much needed understanding of, and adaptation to uncertainty. Thus, this tension is a major task that project managers often need to undertake, but little empirical research exists about how they do so.

Management control literature suggests that managers can satisfy their need to closely monitor project activities by using management control systems (MCS) in a diagnostic manner, while creating flexibility and change management by using other MCS interactively. Project MCS are defined as formalized procedures and systems that use information to maintain or alter a project activity [8], and they incorporate planning systems, reporting systems, and monitoring procedures for the project activities.

The diagnostic use of MCS closely corresponds to the traditional monitoring role attributed to formal control mechanisms and aims to ensure that a project’s predetermined objectives are met and that corrective action is taken whenever a gap between planned and actual results is detected. On the other hand, interactive use of project MCS refers to using the information provided by a given MCS as a basis for frequent formal discussions between the project’s participants. The main objective of this type of MCS use is to identify sources of uncertainty, to challenge initial plans if necessary, and to encourage the proposal of new ideas which may in turn result in revisions to the project’s initial estimates. Hence, when used interactively, MCS allow managers to deploy control information as a tool for managing uncertainty and enhancing a project’s flexibility.

Thus, the objective of our paper was to address the following three research questions:

\- What factors influence the levels of diagnostic and interactive use of project MCS by ISD project managers?

\- What impact does diagnostic and interactive use of MCS have on project performance?

\- Is the impact of diagnostic and interactive use of MCS on project performance moderated by the novelty and complexity of an ISD task?

## 2. Background

The study of management control in ISD projects has been extensively investigated. Overall, past research has found that both formal and informal controls can be used in ISD projects, but that uncertainty and complexity of a project lead to greater use of informal controls and less reliance on formal controls [11].

These findings are based on the assumption that formal controls are rigid and encourage specified behavior to achieve predetermined objectives, leaving little room for a team to adjust to unforeseen changes in a project. Past research has also assumed that informal controls are flexible and provide project members freedom in determining how to develop a quality system.

However, past research also suggests that the rigidity and flexibility of formal control systems result from the way they are used (diagnostically or interactively) rather than from their formal nature. Accordingly, interactive use of formal MCS tends to make them flexible, whereas their diagnostic use leaves little room for adjusting to changes in the project.

We focused on the use of project follow-up reports which provide the project manager with feedback information on the progress of a given project. The project manager can use some of this information in a diagnostic way, while using other data interactively [6].

The objective of interactive use of MCS is to detect, early in the development process, any uncertainty factors. Unexpected resistance from future users of a system is an example of a problem that may need to be faced during the production of system software. If this can be detected early in the process, the project manager can ask top management to increase their support of the new system, by clearly communicating its advantages to the users, or by finding a project champion. However, if there are high levels of user resistance, then it might be useful for the project manager to revise the project’s original schedules.

It should be mentioned however that allowing changes in ISD projects without a formal change management system increases the probability of rework and system design changes which may result in schedule and budget overruns, and lead to the development of a system that does not satisfy user requirements. Researchers have therefore proposed change management guidelines for ISD projects in order to avoid unnecessary rework. The interactive use of MCS is a formal change control system via which a project manager can influence and orient the types of changes that will be adopted and exclude those to be avoided, depending on several factors such as the project’s scope and the potential impact of these changes, as well as the importance of the project from top management’s perspective. As such, interactive use has been found to be well adapted to contexts involving uncertainty [7].

Under diagnostic use of MCS, problems are detected and treated after they occur. This type of use is therefore thought to be appropriate only when uncertainty is relatively low.

The main objective of our effort was to understand the factors that may influence project managers’ choice of how they use project MCS and the impact of this on project performance.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

As depicted in Fig. 1, our research model hypothesized that project managers’ discretion on project decisions influences the way they use an MCS. The model also hypothesized that there could be both direct and moderated effects of interactive and diagnostic uses of MCS on project performance. More specifically, the novelty and complexity of the ISD task were expected to moderate the relationship between the type of MCS use and project performance.

## 3.1. Managerial discretion and interactive use of MCS

Managerial discretion involves the degree of freedom that a project manager has in determining the actions allowed in ISD and how to implement them. A manager who is granted more autonomy will feel responsible about the results, and thus require more detailed and updated control information from team members. To influence their subordinates’ behavior, managers need to understand the cause-and-effect relationships between their workers’ actions and project performance. As interactive use of MCS allows managers to obtain up-to-date information from different stakeholders [9], found that in firms which promote managerial empowerment, top managers were inclined to use their MCS interactively.

Thus, project managers who have high levels of autonomy will be likely to seek more information from team members in order to understand project activities [10], and control the changes made to the project plans and methodologies by using project MCS interactively. Hence, we hypothesized:

H1. ISD project managers’ decisional discretion positively influ ences their interactive use of project MCS.

## 3.2. Managerial discretion and diagnostic use of MCS

The use of MCS to monitor project schedules, costs and quality objectives is common practice in ISD project contexts, and is prevalent in ISD projects regardless of the project manager’s level of discretion. Hence, no relationship is hypothesized between ISD project managers’ decisional discretion and their diagnostic use of project MCS.

## 3.3. Main effects on project performance

Project managers who have more discretion in their work are more likely to adapt to changing project environments and take greater advantage of new opportunities as they emerge [12]. Further, managerial discretion has been found to positively impact group motivation and project effectiveness. Thus, project managers’ level of discretion is likely to provide them with the independence they need to engage in actions they deem necessary for achieving better project performance. Thus:

H2. ISD project managers’ decisional discretion is positively associated with project performance.

Diagnostic use of MCS allows project managers to follow their project’s cost, time, and quality. They therefore must establish clear performance standards at the start. Thus diagnostic use of MCS costs less than interactive use since it involves discussions of feedback information only when exceptions occur.

Also, the centralization and pre-specification of a project’s required outcomes were found to positively influence ISD project success. At the organizational level [17], found that diagnostic use of performance measurement systems was positively associated with overall organizational performance. Hence:

H3. Diagnostic use of project MCS by project managers is positively associated with project performance.

Interactive use of MCS keeps managers better informed about ongoing project activities. The frequent personal nature of interactive MCS use is also likely to focus the attention of project participants on key issues and success factors which should further positively affect project outcomes [16]. However, the frequent discussions and intensive information-seeking is likely to negatively affect the project performance and could lead to information overload. Indeed, at the organizational level [2], were unable to observe a significant direct relationship between the level of interactive use of MCS by top managers and organizational performance. Hence, no significant direct relationship was hypothesized between the level of interactive use of MCS and ISD project performance.

3.4. Moderated effects of interactive and diagnostic use of MCS on performance

Even though the use of MCS for monitoring seems to be institutionalized in ISD projects, it can be effectively used only when it is possible to specify performance criteria and to measure project outputs against these standards. In ISD projects, Ref. [14] found that the interaction between outcome controls and project uncertainty (i.e., frequency of changes in user requirements) negatively affected project performance.

The novelty and complexity of the ISD task for the members of a project team are other potential sources of uncertainty [1]. Task complexity relates to the need to adapt to change in the cause– effect relationships during the execution of a task.<sup>2</sup>

Ditillo [5] found that, in projects where new technology was used and the development process was complex, outcome and behavior controls were less effective, and reliance on team members’ competencies and expertise was more important. Hence, we expected that any positive impact of MCS diagnostic use on project performance was likely to be stronger in projects where task novelty and complexity were low. Thus:

H4a. Diagnostic use of project MCS has a larger positive impact on project performance when ISD task novelty is low (negative interaction term).

H4b. Diagnostic use of project MCS has a larger positive impact on project performance when ISD task complexity is low (negative interaction term).

In contrast to the diagnostic use of MCS, their interactive use assumes that only tentative objectives can be set at the beginning of a project, and that feedback about ongoing activities must be collected and discussed to challenge them and determine how best new information can be integrated during the project. Consistent with this, using MCS interactively has been found to improve performance in situations of high uncertainty.

Unlike diagnostic use, which is not expected to hinder performance in high uncertainty contexts, interactive use of MCS is likely to reduce project performance if deployed under conditions of low uncertainty due to the high costs it entails. Hence:

H5a. Interactive use of project MCS has a positive impact on project performance when ISD task novelty is high and a negative impact when ISD task novelty is low (positive interaction term).

H5b. Interactive use of project MCS has a positive impact on project performance when ISD task complexity is high and a negative impact when ISD task complexity is low (positive interaction term).

## 3.5. Control variable: project size

As it is likely that project’s size can affect performance, it was added as a control variable.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

ISD project managers were considered to be the best informed participants in the development process and were therefore selected as our study’s key respondents.

The Directory of Top Computer Executives in Canada was used as our population; members from this list were asked for contact information about all ISD project managers in their organization. The first author then personally met with the identified project managers and inquired about other potential participants. This snowball sampling approach yielded a group of 116 project managers, 36 of whom declined to participate due to busy schedules, resulting in a sample of 80 project managers.

We hand-delivered our study questionnaire and a business reply envelope to each respondent and received 48 completed questionnaires.

The questionnaire asked the respondents to answer each question for two projects that he or she had managed (one successful, and one less successful project). Forty-five project managers completed the questionnaires for two projects each (three managers responded for only one project), resulting in a final sample of 93 ISD projects. Descriptive statistics of the respondents, projects and organizations are shown in Table 1.

As can be seen in Table 1, the participants were experienced project managers, and the projects varied widely in terms of their cost, total duration and size, with the 25 participating organizations operating in several sectors.

The questionnaire was completed by one respondent in 14 organizations, and between 2 and 7 project managers in 11 organizations. The 14 organizations with one respondent were not significantly different from the ones that had several respondents in terms of the size of their IS department (number of IS personnel), thus organizational bias was not likely to be a concern.

Paired t-tests were performed to compare the projects’ characteristics between successful and unsuccessful projects. The results are shown in Table 2.

## 4.2. Construct measures

Appendix 1 shows the scales used to measure the study constructs, the item loadings/weights and their psychometric properties. With the exception of the two reflective items measuring ‘‘interactive use’’ that were newly developed, all remaining scales were adapted from existing measures.

## 4.2.1. Managerial discretion

A 4-item reflective scale was used to assess managerial discretion. The participants were asked to indicate how much discretion they had in determining the methods for analyzing user requirements, designing and testing the system, and defining the steps to be completed in the development process (1 = no discretion to 7 much discretion).

## 4.2.2. Task novelty

ISD task novelty was measured via three reflective items which asked the respondents to indicate the extent to which project activities and system functionalities were new to the team, and whether steps to carry out the project activities were easy to identify (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

## 4.2.3. Task complexity

To measure ISD task complexity, three reflective items were used; these asked the respondents to indicate the extent to which project team members encountered problems that were difficult to resolve, if the project was complex, and if the technical complexity of the completed system was high (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

## 4.2.4. MCS diagnostic use

The extent to which project managers diagnostically used project follow-up reports was assessed via four reflective items that asked the respondents to indicate how much they used project follow-up reports to review the project’s performance indicators, follow the project’s progress toward its objectives, track the project’s results and compare the results with project plans (1 = not at all to 7 = very much).

## 4.2.5. MCS interactive use

At the strategic level, Ref. [3] conceptualized interactive use of MCS as a formative construct composed of five different properties.

Table 3

For our study, the following three properties were considered relevant<sup>3</sup>:

(1) Frequent formal face-to-face meetings among project participants. This was assessed with one item which asked the respondents to state the extent to which they discussed project follow-up reports in face-to-face meetings compared to other ways, such as phone calls and email (as a percentage 0–100%, coded from answers to 1–7).

(2) Non-invasive and facilitating intervention by project managers in discussions of feedback information. By engaging in regular discussions, project managers try to focus the team’s attention, guide their decisions, and integrate project tasks. This was measured with five items which asked project managers how much they used follow-up reports to enable team members to focus on common issues and the project’s success factors, to foster the development of a common vocabulary and a common vision, and to integrate the project’s activities (1 = not at all to 7 = very much).

(3) Regular and frequent discussion among project participants of the information provided in follow-up reports. This was measured by one item which asked respondents to indicate the extent to which they discussed follow-up reports with team members even when there were no gaps between the results and the plans (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

The seven items measuring the three properties of interactive use of project follow-up reports were used as formative indicators of this construct. Using the recommendation that reflective items be used to validate a construct’s formative scale, two reflective items were developed for an overall assessment of the level of interactive use of follow-up reports by project managers. These items asked the respondents to indicate the extent to which they engaged team members in frequent discussions on project follow-up reports during formal meetings, and took necessary measures to ensure that the entire team paid attention to the reports (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

## 4.2.6. Project performance

Past research has defined ISD project performance as the efficiency and effectiveness with which the project has been completed. We measured project performance via four reflective items that assessed the extent to which the project and the development process were considered successful and fulfilled their objectives (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

## 4.2.7. Project size

Project size was measured via three indicators which asked the respondents the number of people on the project team, and the approximate cost and duration of the project.

## 5.1. The measurement model

The psychometric properties of the reflective scales were assessed in terms of their reliability and validity. Item loadings and composite reliabilities for each reflective construct were all higher than the required minima of 0.5 and 0.7, respectively. Further, as shown in Table 3, construct AVEs were all above 0.5, and their square roots were all higher than their inter-construct correlations, as shown in Table 3, suggesting that each construct shared more variance with its indicators than with other constructs. Thus the measurement model satisfied established convergent and discriminant validity criteria.

In addition, two steps were used to examine the validity of the formative index that assessed interactive use of project follow-up reports. We first ran a redundancy model to assess the strength of the relationship between the formative and reflective measures. As shown in Fig. 2, the formative index explained 68% of the reflective measure, and the two measures were associated with a large and significant path $( \beta = 0 . 8 2 ; p < 0 . 0 1 ) .$ . In addition, the weights of the formative items were examined in relation to the underlying construct. Fig. 2 shows that items INT2, INT3, INT4 and INT7 had positive and significant weights, suggesting that the two properties that contributed the most to the interactive use construct were the project manager’s leadership style (facilitating, non-invasive involvement) as measured by items INT2 to INT4, and the intensity of MCS use (discussions even when there were no gaps between results and plans) assessed by INT7. The richness of the communications related to follow-up reports (face-to-face formal meetings measured by item INT1) did not significantly contribute to interactive use.

The non-significant formative indicators INT1, INT5 and INT6 were not eliminated because their non-significance may be specific to the study sample and their weights may change depending on the variables included in the model. The inter-item correlations and variance inflation factors (VIF), provided in Table 4, were also examined before deciding whether or not to keep these items. As can be seen, all correlations and VIF values of formative indicators of the interactive use construct were lower than 0.8 and 4, respectively, suggesting that multicollinearity between these items was not an issue. Thus, PLS analyses were conducted by keeping all seven formative indicators.

## 5.2. The structural model and hypothesis tests

The results of the analysis using SmartPLS2<sup>5</sup> are shown in Table 5. The model was run with both the formative and reflective measures of the interactive use construct, and the results were quite similar in terms of the direction and significance of the structural paths. Only reflective items of the interactive use construct were used to compute the interaction terms. As can be seen in Table 5, the model explained 54% of the variance in project performance, suggesting an adequate explanatory power. The significance of the model paths was estimated via the bootstrapping procedure with 200 samples.

Both the inter-construct correlations and the PLS path coefficients were used to analyze the study’s findings. It should be noted that most of the inter-construct correlations were in the direction predicted by the study hypotheses.

A positive and significant path was observed between project managers’ discretion and their level of interactive use of project follow-up reports $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting H1. As expected, managerial discretion was not significantly associated with the level of diagnostic use of follow-up reports $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 , p > 0 . 1 )$ with explained variance in diagnostic use of 1.4%, suggesting that the use of project follow-up reports for monitoring purposes did not depend on managerial discretion.

A positive and significant path was observed between managerial discretion and project performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting H2. As hypothesized in H3, the level of diagnostic use of project follow-up reports was found to positively and significantly influence project performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 3$ $p < 0 . 0 1 $ . Even though the level of interactive use was positively and significantly correlated with project performance, the path coefficient was not significant $( \beta = - 0 . 0 3 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , as expected. It should also be noted that INT5 and INT7 were the only two formative indicators which significantly contributed to explaining project performance (see Appendix 1), suggesting that the use of project follow-up reports to enable team members to focus on project success factors (INT5) together with the regularity and frequency of discussions of control information (INT7) were both important control aspects which enhanced overall project outcomes.

The interaction hypotheses for diagnostic use were partially supported. The diagnostic use-task complexity interaction had a negative and significant influence on project performance $( \beta = - 0 . 3 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , but the diagnostic use-task novelty interaction was not significant $( \beta = - 0 . 1 8 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . Thus, while H4b was supported, H4a was not.

As expected, the interactive use-task novelty and the interactive use-task complexity interactions both positively and significantly influenced project performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5$ and $\beta = 0 . 2 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 1$ , respectively), providing support for H5a and H5b.

To further investigate the interaction hypotheses, the sample was split into four subsamples based on the medians of project novelty and complexity. Then, a PLS model was run for each subsample, with diagnostic use and interactive use as independent variables and project performance as the dependent variable. The results are shown in Table 6. Overall, the type of MCS use explained a significant portion of the variance in project performance with all $R ^ { 2 }$ being greater than 23%. It is also interesting to note that the explained variance in project performance was greater when project novelty and complexity were low than when they were high (30% and 43% when low versus 28% and 24% when high). Moreover, project performance in the high project novelty and high complexity subsamples was mainly explained by the level of interactive use of project follow-up reports which was the only significant independent variable in either case.

## 5.3. Sensitivity analyses

In order to test the robustness of our model. we divided the sample into large and small projects, and compared the relationships between the model’s variables in each subsample. We could not analyze the full model with PLS because the number of links directed to the construct ‘‘project performance’’ was seven and this requires a minimum of 70 observations for PLS, which was larger than the number of observations in each subsample (46 and 47). Consequently, we performed a sensitivity analysis in three steps, as shown in Table 7: in Model 1, we tested the relationships among the main model variables (i.e., managerial discretion, interactive use, diagnostic use, project performance). In Model 2, we tested the impact on performance of diagnostic use and its two interaction terms related (i.e., diagnostic use  project novelty and diagnostic use  project complexity). Finally, in Model 3, we tested the impact on project performance of interactive use and its two interaction terms (i.e., interactive use  project novelty and interactive use  project complexity).

The results for Model 1 show that the positive impact of managerial discretion on performance was greater in large projects, which is consistent with past research.<sup>6</sup> Indeed, large project size has been considered a source of uncertainty for many years. Hence, in larger projects, project managers need more decisional discretion in order to be able to gather the information they require and make informed decisions about the project.

When they are granted discretion over decisions, the project managers in our sample seem to have fostered interactive use of their MCS, both for small and large projects, but more so in large projects. Indeed, the impact of managerial discretion on the level of interactive use appears to have been greater and more significant in large projects than in small ones.

The results also show that the relationship between managerial discretion and diagnostic use of MCS was positive and marginally significant in small projects (p < 0.1), but not significant in large projects.

Model 1 also shows that interactive use of project MCS had a negative impact on performance in small projects, but a positive impact in large projects. These results also provide an explanation for the non-significant path that was observed between interactive use and project performance when the model was tested on the whole sample.

According to the results of Model 2, the interaction between diagnostic use and project novelty had a negative and marginally significant impact on performance in small projects (this impact was non-significant for large projects). Further, the interaction between diagnostic use and project complexity had a negative impact on performance regardless of project size.

Finally, Model 3 results showed that the interaction between MCS interactive use and project novelty had a positive and significant impact on performance only in small projects.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

The objective of our study was to improve our understanding of the factors which influence how ISD project managers actually use project MCS, and the impact of this use on project performance. The research objective was motivated by a lack of research on the types of MCS use in the particular context of ISD which is often characterized by a great tension between the need to control and the necessity of managing uncertainty and change.

We found a positive relationship between project managers’ decisional discretion and the extent to which they interactively used project follow-up reports, regardless of the size of their project. ISD project managers who had considerable discretion over project decisions were more likely to call for the contribution of each team member through extensive and regular discussions of control information on project performance. Our study found that project managers deployed not only informal communication but also formal communication embedded in using project MCS interactively in order to satisfy their information needs.

However, project managers’ level of decisional discretion was not found to influence their diagnostic use of project MCS, especially in large projects. This is consistent with the argument that using project control information to monitor the achievement of pre-determined project objectives has become a common and institutionalized practice which does not depend on the specific characteristics of an ISD project.

Further, we examined the direct impact on project performance of managerial discretion, interactive use and diagnostic use of project follow-up reports. We found that the performance of ISD projects was more positive when managers had high levels of discretion over project decisions. As expected, we also did not find any significant link between interactive use of project MCS and project performance. However, when project size was taken into account, our sensitivity analyses showed that interactive use improved performance in large projects but deteriorated it in small projects.

Based on past research which has suggested that interactive use is more appropriate when uncertainty is high, and that diagnostic use is more appropriate when uncertainty is low, we tested the moderated effects of diagnostic and interactive uses of project MCS on project performance. Our results indicated that project novelty and complexity moderated interactive MCS use’s impact on project performance. More specifically, we found that interactive use improved project performance when project novelty and complexity were high, and reduced it when they were low.

Theoretically, the results indicate that formal controls can still be useful in enhancing project performance when uncertainty is high, provided they are used in ways that enable team members to understand and manage the sources of uncertainty in their activities. The results also suggest that the costs associated with project managers’ interactive use of project MCS may be outweighed by its benefits when the ISD task is new and complex and the project’s size is large, and that these costs may be too high and reduce the project’s performance when the ISD task entails low uncertainty. Practically, our findings suggest that ISD project managers who believe that informal communications are more efficient when the project is risky and uncertain, should rely more on formal meetings and discussions which explicitly communicate to the rest of the team the importance of new idea proposals and change management in such contexts.

Moreover, we found that diagnostic use of project MCS significantly contributed to explaining project performance when project novelty and complexity were low, but was not significantly associated with performance when these two project characteristics were high. These findings indicate that monitoring the achievement of pre-planned project outcomes is relevant only when the outcomes can be accurately predicted. Our study shows that, given the relatively low costs associated with it, diagnostic use of MCS did not hinder project performance when uncertainty was high. From a practical point of view, our findings indicate that monitoring the achievement of difficult to predict outcomes due to high project uncertainty may not be effective and may not have any effect on overall project performance.

While this study contributes to explaining the relationships between managerial discretion, type of MCS use and project performance in ISD contexts, its limitations must be taken into account when interpreting the results. As the sample used in our study was non-probabilistic, one must be careful in generalizing the findings to other contexts. While this research used existing measures to assess the level of MCS interactive use and was, to the best of our knowledge, the first to validate a formative measure of this construct, some of the indicators were not found to significantly contribute to the construct (e.g., INT1).

Another limit of this study pertains to its underlying assumptions that information received by project managers is complete and that they make rational decisions. These assumptions may not always hold true in practice. According to [13], ISD projects involve the development of intangible assets, which makes the assessment of their progress difficult and subjective. Hence, project managers rarely receive complete and accurate information about the status of a project. Further, human biases and social pressures can push project managers toward less than optimal decisions.

Overall, our study provided useful findings regarding appropriate ways to control ISD projects. The message it conveys to project managers is that formal controls do not always hinder project team flexibility and do not always positively affect project performance. The project context should therefore be taken into account when deciding on how to use project MCS as these might sometimes provide a useful source of information and at other times result in information overload; they can focus the team’s attention toward common objectives and issues or force the accomplishment of unclear objectives, all depending on how and under which circumstances they are used.
