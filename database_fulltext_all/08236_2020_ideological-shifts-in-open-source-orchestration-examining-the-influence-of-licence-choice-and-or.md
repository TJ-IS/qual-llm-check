# Ideological shifts in open source orchestration: examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of licence choice and organisational participation on open source project outcomes

Poonacha K. Medappa <sup>a</sup> and Shirish C. Srivastava <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, Tilburg School of Economics and Management, Tilburg University, Netherlands; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems and Operations Management Department, HEC Paris, France

## ABSTRACT

Though volunteer-driven Free (Libre) and Open Source Software (FLOSS) development were founded on the ideological beliefs of “openness” and “absence of any commercial appropriation”, in recent years, FLOSS movement has witnessed two ideological shifts. First, the emergence of “permissive FLOSS licences” that allow commercial appropriation of the collaboratively developed code, and second, “organisational ownership” of FLOSS projects. Because ideological beliefs shape the motivational needs of the volunteer contributors, and motivational needs in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dominant work structures, it is expected that ideological shifts could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the mechanisms through which work is orchestrated in FLOSS projects. Motivated by the need to understand the impact of these ideological shifts, we theorise the mechanisms through which the two ideological shifts alter the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of FLOSS work structures on project outcomes of popularity and survival. Adopting an instrument variable approach, our analysis of projects hosted on GitHub con<sup>fi</sup>rms the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of both the ideological shifts with some interesting contextual di<sup>f</sup>erences across the two project outcomes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the ideological shift pertaining to licence type has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on both the examined project outcomes, whereas organisational ownership has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence only on the popularity of FLOSS projects.

KEYWORDS Open source software; ideologies; structures of work; licence; superposition; instrument variable

## 1. Introduction

Digitally enabled transformations have led to profound changes in the way individuals and organisations undertake software development work. Amongst them, the transformation of software development from being traditionally in-house to one that is open source is particularly noteworthy (Ågerfalk et al., 2015). Central to this transformation is the ideological shifts that permitted the Free (Libre) and Open Source Software (FLOSS) movement, founded on the ideological beliefs of openness and absence of commercial appropriation (Ljungberg, 2000), to become more accepting of commercial participation. In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, two ideological shifts have shaped the way we see FLOSS today – (a) the emergence of permissive licences that allowed commercial appropriation of the collaboratively developed code (Daniel et al., 2018), and (b) the movement towards organisational ownership that allowed the emergence of a corporate-communal landscape in FLOSS projects (Fitzgerald, 2006; Germonprez et al., 2016). Because ideological beliefs shape the motivational needs of the volunteer contributors (Daniel et al., 2018), and motivational needs of contributors shape the emergent work structures in FLOSS projects (Howison & Crowston, 2014), it is expected that ideological shifts could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the mechanisms through which work structures in FLOSS projects are related to the project outcomes. Motivated by the need to understand these ideological shifts, considering their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the unique work orchestration mechanisms in FLOSS projects, our study attempts to answer the following research question:

RQ: How have the ideological shifts invoked by (a) the emergence of permissive licences, and (b) the shift towards organisational ownership, transformed the influence of FLOSS work structures on project outcomes?

The creation of the FLOSS movement, founded on the ideological beliefs of openness and no commercial appropriation, changed the nature of motivation of the developers from being largely extrinsic (as in employees of an organisation) to more intrinsic (as in volunteer contributors; Ke & Zhang, 2010). The intrinsically motivated volunteer contributors di<sup>f</sup>ered in their psychological needs for autonomy leading to the emergence of new work orchestration mechanisms that soon came to dominate FLOSS development work (Howison & Crowston, 2014). In this context, Howison and Crowston (2014), observed and conceptualised superposition of tasks as an e<sup>f</sup>ective work orchestration mechanism in FLOSS projects, wherein motivationally independent tasks are incrementally layered to create the software. This work orchestration mechanism is di<sup>f</sup>erent from that observed in the case of traditional software development, where the focus is towards co-work and concurrent task development through a modular task design (Baldwin & Clark, 2006). While superposition works well in environments that support the ideology of openness and attract a large proportion of intrinsically motivated contributors, its e<sup>fi</sup>cacy when community ideologies shift has not yet been examined. Changes in ideologies may not only change the contributor’s motivations but also create an ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>t, which alters their commitment to the project (Daniel et al., 2018; Stewart & Gosain, 2006). Therefore, understanding the changes to the contributors’ motivations and aligning the project attributes and social practices to the new ideologies are necessary to sustain development activities and produce high-quality software (Von Krogh et al., 2012). Motivated by the need to understand these changes and their implications on the orchestration of FLOSS work, we examine the changes brought to the relationship between FLOSS work structures and project outcomes in the context of the ideological shifts that are reshaping the FLOSS phenomenon.

Through this research, we aim to make the following contributions to theory and practice. First, we advance the understanding on FLOSS ideologies by examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of FLOSS ideological shifts on software development project outcomes. We do this by theorising the changes to the motivational mechanisms invoked by the ideological shifts and their subsequent in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the relationship between the work structures of FLOSS projects and their outcomes. This theorisation provides a novel understanding of the impact of ideological shifts, which links the literature on work structures in FLOSS projects (Howison & Crowston, 2014) and ideologies in FLOSS communities (Daniel et al., 2018; Stewart & Gosain, 2006). Second, we contribute to the theoretical understanding on the di<sup>f</sup>erent ways in which the choice of licence type in<sup>fl</sup>uences project outcomes in non-organisation and organisation-owned FLOSS projects. Licences provide the means to formalise and protect community ideologies in FLOSS projects and hence play an important role in shaping the motivations of the contributors who join the project. Prior research has examined many important aspects related to choice of licence types (e.g., Singh & Phelps, 2013; Stewart, Darcy et al., 2006) and organisational participation (e.g., Capra et al., 2011; Fitzgerald, 2006; Germonprez et al., 2016) in FLOSS projects, and has laid the groundwork for a deeper theoretical inquiry. Building on these prior research streams, we show that the ideological in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the choice of licence types on FLOSS project outcomes is sensitive to the organisational participation in the project. Lastly, the results from our study help advance theories related to the value creation in FLOSS and o<sup>f</sup>er practical insights for project owners. Management scholars have expressed considerable concern about the failure of academic research to penetrate the practitioner community (Rynes et al., 2001). We believe that a better understanding of the ideological in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the choice of licence type on the outcomes of the project can lead to better management practices for planning potentially successful FLOSS projects.

## 2. Theoretical background

To understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that ideological shifts have on the FLOSS project outcomes, four important concepts need to be developed: (a) FLOSS project outcomes, (b) ideological shifts in the FLOSS movement, (c) motivations of contributors, and (d) the structures of work in FLOSS projects. In this section, we set the context for each of the concepts and provide a brief background on the related literature.

## 2.1. FLOSS project outcomes – popularity and survival

Measuring the outcomes of FLOSS projects can be challenging because of the open-source nature of these projects, which precludes their association with the usual monetary measures such as prices, revenues, and sales (Fershtman & Gandal, 2007). Despite this di<sup>fi</sup>culty, extant empirical research literature considers di<sup>f</sup>erent project-related measures to operationalise FLOSS project outcomes (Crowston et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2009). Drawing from these studies, we examine two measures of FLOSS project outcomes: popularity and survival of the project. Popularity of the project is a particularly useful outcome measure because popular projects tend to engage a larger community of users which leads to an increase in task contributions to the project (Howison & Crowston, 2014). Moreover, measures of popularity are also correlated to the number of bugs, the number of participants in the bug trackers (Crowston et al., 2006), and the total number of developers (Krishnamurthy 2005; Subramaniam et al., 2009). These correlations suggest that project popularity can capture the essence of both quality and functionality of the FLOSS software. Given this salience, di<sup>f</sup>erent measures of popularity have been frequently used to determine FLOSS project outcomes. For example, number of downloads (Fershtman & Gandal, 2011; Rebeca & García, 2009), commercial success (Grewal et al., 2006; Midha & Palvia, 2012), user interest (Stewart, Ammeter et al., 2006; Subramaniam et al., 2009), OSS product awareness (Setia et al., 2012), and project viewership and downloads (Crowston et al., 2006). Following these examples and recognising the importance of attracting greater attention from the community for the success of the FLOSS project, we adopt popularity as the <sup>fi</sup>rst FLOSS project outcome measure in our study.

In addition to gaining in popularity, FLOSS projects are expected to see continuous enhancement and maintenance of the code, which results in multiple stable versions delivered one after another (Raymond 1999). Thus, the sustenance or survival of the project can be considered as an important measure of FLOSS project outcome. This attribute may di<sup>f</sup>erentiate FLOSS projects from commercial software development projects that often come with speci<sup>fi</sup>c end goals and milestones. Moreover, in their empirical analysis, Crowston et al. (2006) found that the common measures used for measuring FLOSS project outcome, such as popularity and quality were correlated to each other, but the project lifespan was unique and did not correlate with the other outcome measures. Given this <sup>fi</sup>nding, sustenance of FLOSS projects can provide an independent but important view on project outcome that has not yet been fully examined in the literature. We therefore consider project survival as the second measure of FLOSS project outcome in our study.

## 2.2. Ideological shifts in the FLOSS movement

Traditional (proprietary) software developed by individuals or organisations are protected by copyright laws, which make the software a private good (Lerner & Tirole, 2005). The Free Software Foundation (FSF, www.fsf.org) created in 1985 tried to create a legal basis which would allow collaboratively developed code to not only remain free for use, but to also ensure the free availability of the source code (Stallman, 1999). This movement gave rise to the free software movement which provided the early de<sup>fi</sup>nition of FLOSS built on four essential freedoms that the software needs to provide to its users (Ljungberg, 2000):

● The freedom to run the program as the user wishes, for any purpose (freedom 0)

● The freedom to study how the program works, and change it so that it does the computing as the user wishes (freedom 1)

● The freedom to redistribute copies so that the user can help others (freedom 2)

● The freedom to distribute copies of the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed versions to others. By doing this the user can give the whole community a chance to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the implemented changes (freedom 3)

Freedom 0 and 1 together supported the ideological belief of openness that ensured FLOSS would remain open for the user to study, modify and use in any way the user chooses. Freedom 2 and 3, ensured the user’s right to redistribute the current or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed versions of the software to others, centred on the ideological belief of no commercial appropriation. To develop high-quality software based on these ideological beliefs, the movement relied on volunteer contributors who supported these ideological beliefs and were willing to contribute time and e<sup>f</sup>ort with little or no commercial rewards (Crowston et al., 2012).

To protect the ideological beliefs of “openness” and “no commercial appropriation” and to allow its enforcement in FLOSS projects, FSF created the restrictive licencing policy. Software that adopted restrictive licences gave users the right to copy, modify, and redistribute the software. In addition, any enhancement made to the source code under a restrictive licence would be bound by the same licence and be forced to remain open (Lerner & Tirole, 2003; Singh & Phelps, 2013). This restrictive feature of the licence made sure that any o<sup>f</sup>shoots of the software would also remain free or open, thereby preventing the commercialisation and closure of the collaboratively developed code (Singh & Phelps, 2013). Thus, the development of unique open-source licences allowed the formalisation of the community ideology and provided project owners with the means to capture the community ideology in their projects and enforce it for all the project users and contributors.

## 2.2.1. First ideological shift – emergence of permissive licences

To relax the non-commercial ideology of the restrictive licences, the FLOSS movement saw the emergence of permissive licences (e.g., BSD, ASF and MIT). Such licences provided most of the open source features embedded in the restrictive licences but also gave the developer the freedom to change the licence policy of the built software. The Open Source Initiative (OSI; www.opensource.org) founded in 1998, spearheaded the creation of permissive licences to provide developers the freedom to use their developed code the way they chose to use it. In principle, the movements founded by FSF and OSI are grounded in di<sup>f</sup>erent ideologies. While FSF prefers restrictive licences that limit commercial appropriation of the code, OSI holds a more inclusive view that allows for commercial appropriation (Daniel et al., 2018).

## 2.2.2. Second ideological shift – organisational ownership of FLOSS projects

By early 2000, organisations began to realise the commercial potential of FLOSS. For example, in November 2001, IBM embraced the open-source approach by opening the source code for several of its software tools (estimated at 40 USD million) to the public domain (Lohr, 2001). This led to a transformation of FLOSS communities, which had primarily been volunteer driven, to communities that attracted organisations and commercial interests (Wagstrom, 2009). The concept of OSS 2.0 introduced by Fitzgerald (2006) captured the transformation that led to FLOSS becoming a mainstream, commercially viable form of software development. This transformation gave rise to the second ideological shift, which saw organisations initiating and taking ownership of FLOSS projects. The ideological shift in this case centred around the entry of paid employees into the mix of volunteer contributors, the provision of nondevelopmental support by organisations, and the inclusion of organisational management and control practices to the FLOSS projects (Capra et al., 2011). Apart from altering the ideological beliefs of openness and no commercial appropriation these changes also changed the team structure to a hybrid mix of organisational employees and volunteer contributors in the project (Germonprez et al., 2016).

## 2.3. Motivations of contributors

Community contributions sustain FLOSS projects and help it grow from an “itch worth scratching” to a scale that is hard to replicate within the boundaries of a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Raymond, 1999, p. 4). FLOSS project owners, both organisations and individuals, rely on volunteer contributors spread across the globe to create software that often surpasses in-house software projects in terms of quality and functionality at a fraction of the cost (Coverity Inc., 2013). For example, the popular projects – React and Pop, started by Facebook, see a greater proportion of contributions coming from outside of Facebook than from its own employees (Pearce, 2014). The ability of FLOSS projects to motivate contributors to dedicate time and e<sup>f</sup>ort, sometimes without pay, continues to intrigue open-source researchers and practitioners (Von Krogh et al., 2012).

While there are several underlying reasons that drive contributors in FLOSS, motivations of contributors are often distinguished as – intrinsic motivation, which refers to doing something because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable, and extrinsic motivation, which refers to doing something because it leads to a separable outcome (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In terms of task contributions and support for the project, both intrinsically and extrinsically motivated contributors are considered equally bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the project, but di<sup>f</sup>er in their innate psychological needs (Daniel et al., 2018; Roberts et al., 2006). From Self-Determination Theory (SDT), it is understood that the psychological need for autonomy and self-determination di<sup>f</sup>ers along the continuum from intrinsically motivated to extrinsically motivated individuals (Ke & Zhang, 2010; Ryan & Deci, 2000). The greater the degree to which a contributor is intrinsically motivated, the greater is her/his need for autonomy and self-determination (Roberts et al., 2006; Ryan & Deci, 2000). We can, therefore, infer that project teams consisting of a larger proportion of intrinsically motivated contributors tend to have a greater need for autonomy of work provided to the team.

Because ideologies can manifest as important mechanisms that shape the motivational needs of the contributors (Stewart & Gosain, 2006), it is expected that ideological shifts could have altered the underlying motivational mechanisms of the FLOSS project contributors. In this research, we study the impact of ideological shifts and understand how they can change the nature of motivations of the team, necessitating changes to the FLOSS work structures. In the next section, we provide a background on the literature surrounding work structures of FLOSS projects and describe the important role that they play in satisfying the contributors need for autonomy in work.

## 2.4. Work structures in FLOSS projects – superposition

Recent works of Howison and Crowston (2014) and Lindberg et al. (2016), have called attention to the characteristics of the software artefact, such as the emergent structures of work that are surprisingly e<sup>f</sup>ective at establishing the delicate balance between – managing developers’ contributions, and sustaining the contributors’ motivational needs. In this context, Howison and Crowston (2014), observed and conceptualised superposition of tasks as the dominant work orchestration mechanism in FLOSS environments that have high levels of openness and where there is limited time pressure. Superposition is the process through which software development occurs in a sequential manner, with changes to the software added incrementally, on top of one another. Each change represents a task that is independently built by a contributor and has its own functional payo<sup>f</sup> through the improvements that it brings to the application (Howison & crowston, 2014). Superposition is characterised by – (a) individual task work, and (b) incremental layering of motivationally independent tasks. This unique workbreakdown structure has been found to accomplish complex work through a process of “productive deferral”. In productive deferral, tasks that are envisioned to be too large are implemented through individual, moti vationally independent layers which are deferred until code written by someone else makes the envisioned task easy enough to be undertaken through relatively simple, quick, individual work (Howison & crowston, 2014).

Superposed work orchestration has been found to be well suited for non-organisation owned FLOSS projects that attract intrinsically motivated contributors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Howison and crowston (2014) show that in environments that see a high level of openness and limited time pressures, superposition creates an e<sup>f</sup>ective work-breakdown structure that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the psychological needs of the intrinsically motivated contributors. Further, by minimising the interdependencies among contributors and allowing for individual work, superposition tends to enhance the sense of autonomy in the intrinsically motivated volunteer contributors without compromising on their need for competence and relatedness.

Since FLOSS ideologies can encourage contributors’ identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the project and their motivational needs, di<sup>f</sup>erent FLOSS ideologies may invoke di<sup>f</sup>erent motivational needs in the project (Daniel et al., 2018; Stewart & gosain, 2006). Ensuring the right alignment between the motivational needs of the contributors and the project’s work structures can enhance the a<sup>f</sup>ective state of the contributors (Ke & Zhang, 2010; Roberts et al., 2006). Following this line of enquiry, in the next sections, we theorise the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideological shifts on the relationship between the FLOSS project work structures and their outcomes of popularity and survival.

## 3. Theory and hypothesis

## 3.1. First ideological shift–understanding the relationship between licence type, degree of superposition and project outcomes (Popularity and Survival)

While contributors to FLOSS projects are driven by a mix of motivations (Reiss, 2012), the ideological beliefs of the contributors may enhance/diminish one type of motivation over the other. In FLOSS projects, community ideologies are formalised and protected through the choice of licence. Therefore, FLOSS project owners often use licences as a means of signalling their intentions to other developers and users about the project (Sen et al., 2008). The ideologies captured by the restrictive and permissive types of licences di<sup>f</sup>er in their contrasting beliefs of commercial appropriation of collaboratively developed code. While the ideologies of FSF and restrictive licences stand against commercial appropriation of the code, the OSI holds a more inclusive view and supports permissive licences that allow commercial appropriation (Daniel et al., 2018). The ideological belief against the commercial appropriation of FLOSS software was echoed by the founder of FSF who was a strong proponent of restrictive licences – “when information is generally useful, redistributing it makes humanity wealthier no matter who is distributing and no matter who is receiving” (Stewart & Gosain, 2006, p. 294).

Studies on open source have shown that developers choose to participate in open source projects that are aligned with their motivations and attitudes (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2006; Roberts et al., 2006). When it comes to projects with restrictive licences, the rigid interpretation of the concept of “no commercial appropriation”, which limits the ability to commercialise the developed software, may deter extrinsically motivated individuals and organisations who look for tangible rewards from their participation in FLOSS projects. On the contrary, the concept of “information freedom” embedded in restrictive licences may appeal to intrinsically motivated contributors who do not expect any separable outcome for their contribution and instead contribute to support the community. Because the ideologies of extrinsically motivated contributors are more aligned to permissive licences, projects that adopt permissive licences are likely to attract a greater proportion of extrinsically motivated contributors than projects adopting restrictive licences. This ideological in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the motivation of the contributors has also been empirically observed, e.g., Sen et al. (2008), found that extrinsically motivated individuals tend to prefer permissive licences, while others who are intrinsically motivated by the challenge of the work prefer moderately restrictive over permissive licences. Further, FLOSS contributors who are driven by the intrinsic motivation of challenge (problem solving) prefer licences that are restrictive, while the extrinsic motivation of status (through peer recognition) is associated with developer’s preference for licences with least restrictions (Sen et al., 2008).

SDT postulates that along the continuum from extrinsically motivated to intrinsically motivated contributors there is an increasing psychological need for autonomy and self-determination (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Hence, projects that adopt restrictive licences need to align the project attributes to meet the higher need for work autonomy in the intrinsically motivated contributors. Among the project attributes, the structures of work in FLOSS projects have been found to provide an important mechanism through which the contributors’ need for autonomy can be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (Howison & Crowston, 2014). In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, the theory of collaboration through open superposition states that in the case of FLOSS environments which see a high level of openness and limited time pressures, superposition of tasks provides an e<sup>f</sup>ective workbreakdown structure for enhancing the intrinsic motivation to contribute, and at the same time allows for the creation of complex software (Howison & Crowston, 2014). Superposed structures of work are found to be more e<sup>f</sup>ective than concurrent work when intrinsically motivated contributors are involved because concurrent work creates dependencies across contributors, thereby reducing their autonomy (Howison & Crowston, 2014). While superposition is well suited for intrinsically motivated contributors in non-organisational settings, empirical work suggests that at high levels, superposition can also have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Medappa & Srivastava, 2019).

This is mainly because, delays due to the avoidance of concurrent work and co-work, which can occur with superposition may induce a sense of frustration and loss of control for the contributors. Hence, while superposition works well for projects that have a high proportion of intrinsically motivated contributors, its e<sup>fi</sup>cacy diminishes with increase in proportion of extrinsically motivated contributors (who may not have a high need for work autonomy).

By increasing the work autonomy of the contributors, superposition creates a positive a<sup>f</sup>ective state in teams that have a high proportion of intrinsically motivated contributors (Ryan & Deci, 2000). The increased positive a<sup>f</sup>ective state enhances the task e<sup>f</sup>ort that an individual will expend (Weiss & Cropanzo, 1996), encouraging greater contributions to the FLOSS project and leading to an increase in the quality, functionality, and usability of the software for the end user (Ke & Zhang, 2010). Thus, we posit that in the case of non-organisation owned FLOSS projects that adopt restrictive licences, the marginal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of degree of superposition on the popularity of a project is higher than in the case of permissive licences. Hence, we hypothesise:

Hypothesis 1a: In the absence of organisational ownership, the type of licence moderates the relationship between the degree of superposition and the popularity of FLOSS projects, such that, for projects with restrictive licences, the marginal influence of the degree of superposition on the popularity of the project is higher than for projects with permissive licences.

It is not only the contributor motivations, but also the perceptions of legitimacy that can change when we move from restrictive to permissive licences. The survival of FLOSS projects is determined by the contributors’ perceptions of legitimacy that the project is able to engender (Chengalur-smith et al., 2010). Suchman (1995), provides an inclusive and broadbased de<sup>fi</sup>nition of legitimacy as – “a generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, values, beliefs, and de<sup>fi</sup>- nitions” (Suchman, 1995, p. 574). Aligning project attributes, such as – its work structures to the ideological norms, beliefs, and values of the contributors, have been found to help project teams retain contributors and sustain collaboration (Stewart & Gosain, 2006). The ideologies of intrinsically motivated contributors align well with the beliefs of internal locus of control and autonomy in work (Ke & Zhang, 2010; Ryan & Deci, 2000). Since projects that adopt restrictive licences tend to attract intrinsically motivated contributors, provision of work autonomy through superposition may better engender the feelings of legitimacy in the project contributors. In contrast, because permissive licences adopt a more inclusive view that accepts commercial appropriation, contributors participating in such projects may be more open towards non-superposed work commonly seen in commercial software development environments. Thus, for projects with restrictive licences, the perceptions of legitimacy are more closely aligned to work autonomy provided by superposed work structures as compared to projects with permissive licences. Changes to the perceptions of legitimacy are re<sup>fl</sup>ected as changes to the FLOSS project’s ability to survive (Chengalur-smith et al., 2010), hence we hypothesise:

Hypothesis 1b: In the absence of organisational ownership, the type of licence moderates the relationship between the degree of superposition and the survival of FLOSS projects, such that, for projects with restrictive licences, the marginal influence of the degree of superposition on the survival of the project is higher than for projects with permissive licences.

## 3.2. Second ideological shift – in<sup>fl</sup>uence of organisational ownership on the relationship between licence type, degree of superposition, and project outcomes (Popularity and Survival)

The second ideological shift saw the transformation of FLOSS communities, which had mainly been volunteer driven, to communities that attracted organisations and commercial interests (Fitzgerald, 2006). The added in<sup>fl</sup>uence of organisational ownership on the FLOSS project contributors’ motivations and the owner’s potential need for control may alter the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the choice of licence on the relationship between the degree of superposition and project popularity. The changes to the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence can be attributed to the impact of organisational ownership on the – a) motivations and b) ideologies of the team members.

## 3.2.1. Impact of organisational ownership on team motivations

Organisational ownership changes the nature of the FLOSS project teams from being predominantly voluntary contributors, to a mix of organisational employees and volunteers external to the organisation (Capra et al., 2011; Germonprez et al., 2016). Here, paid employees of the organisation commit to writing the code that helps in <sup>fi</sup>xing bugs, customising the existing code, and building distribution packages (Capra et al., 2011). Unlike volunteer contributors, who often contribute during their free time, the employees of an organisation receive tangible rewards (in terms of salary) for their contributions. For example, Facebook contributed more than nine million lines of code to the open-source community through 200 di<sup>f</sup>erent projects in the <sup>fi</sup>rst six months of 2014 alone (Pearce, 2014). In addition to including extrinsically motivated employees in a FLOSS project, organisation ownership of FLOSS projects can also provide certain extrinsic motivations for the volunteer contributors. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, contributors’ ability to signal to the organisational owner for career advancement is an added extrinsic motivation for contributing to FLOSS projects (Lerner & Tirole, 2003). Therefore, when organisations own FLOSS projects, the addition of organisational employees to the team and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of signalling shifts the net motivation of the team towards extrinsic.

## 3.2.2. Impact of organisational ownership on team ideologies

Apart from its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the nature of motivation of the team, organisational ownership of FLOSS projects can also create an ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>t, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences contributor’s motivations. Daniel et al. (2018), conceptualise two ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>ts that may occur in FLOSS project teams – a) FLOSS ideology under-<sup>fi</sup>t, where a team member perceives that s/he embraces the community ideology more strongly than remaining members of the team, and b) FLOSS ideology over-<sup>fi</sup>t, where a contributor perceives that other members of the team embrace the community ideology more strongly than herself/himself.

In the case of organisation owned FLOSS projects that adopt restrictive licences, a perception of an ideological under-<sup>fi</sup>t can occur in intrinsically motivated contributors who believe in the ideologies on which restrictive licences were founded. In these projects, the intrinsically motivated volunteer contributors may perceive that the extrinsically motivated employees and contributors have a lesser belief in the ideologies of “openness” and “no commercial appropriation”. When a contributor perceives an ideological under-<sup>fi</sup>t with the team members, the contributor’s commitment to the team and contributions to the project decreases (Daniel et al., 2018). Therefore, with organisational ownership, intrinsically motivated contributors in projects with restrictive licences may perceive an under-<sup>fi</sup>t that reduces their participation in the project. In contrast, because ideologies of permissive licences accept commercialisation of FLOSS projects, a similar ideological under-<sup>fi</sup>t may not be prominent in projects that adopt permissive licences.

Overall, due to the inclusion of extrinsically motivated contributors to the project and the ideological under-<sup>fi</sup>t perceived by the intrinsically motivated contributors, organisational ownership shifts the project contributions towards extrinsically motivated contributors. The changes to the net motivations of the team when organisations own FLOSS projects mitigates the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of licence choice seen in hypotheses 1a and 1b. This is because the change in net motivations of the team when organisations own the project is greater for projects with restrictive licences than for projects with permissive licences. Our argument for the above is based on two reasons (see Table 1) – (1) non-organisational projects with permissive licences attract extrinsically motivated contributors as they o<sup>f</sup>er greater signalling opportunities than projects with restrictive licences (Sen et al., 2008). Therefore, with organisational ownership, the added in<sup>fl</sup>uence of signalling may result in a larger overall change in the net motivation of the team in projects with restrictive licences compared to projects with permissive licences. (2) The perception of ideological under-<sup>fi</sup>t is stronger for intrinsically motivated contributors in projects with restrictive licences than for projects with permissive licences. This is mainly because the ideologies of restrictive licences are less open to commercial appropriation than permissive licences.

Because of the larger shift in the net motivation of the team towards extrinsic in projects with restrictive licences as compared to projects with permissive licences, we expect that organisational ownership mitigate the di<sup>f</sup>erence in the team motivations that exists between the two licence types. Further, since provision of work autonomy through superposed work structures is better suited for projects that have a higher proportion of intrinsically motivated contributors, changes to the nature of motivation of the team re<sup>fl</sup>ect as changes to the marginal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of superposition on project popularity. Based on this understanding, we posit that in the case of organisation owned FLOSS projects, the positive moderation e<sup>f</sup>ect of the choice of licence type on the relationship between the degree of superposition and the popularity of the project will be lower as compared to nonorganisation owned projects (Table1). ## 4. Methodology

To understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the two ideological shifts and test our hypotheses, we conducted an empirical analysis of FLOSS projects hosted on GitHub. GitHub’s popularity among programmers, its developer-focused environment, its integrated social features, and the availability of detailed metadata makes it a popular environment for FLOSS research (Kalliamvakou et al., 2014). Our adopted methodology comprised three steps: data collection, measurement, and analysis. In the data collection step, we collected detailed project log data for a sample of FLOSS projects from the GHarchive database (https:// www.gharchive.org/). In the measurement step, using the collected project log data, we measured the dependent, independent, and control variables. Finally, in the analysis step, we developed our estimation models to test the hypothesised relationships.

## 4.1. Data collection

We employed Google’s bigquery tool to query the archived project log data available in the GitHub Archive database (Grigorik, 2012). Since this database is large (about 432 GB, with 134 million rows for the year 2014 alone; Google, 2014), we needed the bandwidth provided by a tool like Google’s bigquery to run queries and export the results. We restricted our analysis to projects that were started during the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>ve months of 2014 to reduce the number and size of queries.

While GitHub provides a rich dataset, care needs to be taken to overcome common perils in using this dataset (Kalliamvakou et al., 2014). For example, projects that do not involve software development, are too small, or are mirrors or personal stores should be avoided. Further, the complexity of the git work<sup>fl</sup>ow, such as the multiple approaches to merge <sup>fi</sup>les and multiple events (push, pull request, commit) for making contributions, makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure superposition. We adopted the methods and checks used by

Medappa and Srivastava (2019) to overcome these issues and measure the degree of superposition of a project. In addition to these issues, some projects did not have licences assigned or used licences that were di<sup>fi</sup>cult to be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being either restrictive or permissive. After <sup>fi</sup>ltering the dataset to address these issues, we were left with a sample of 4159 FLOSS projects, of which 2039 were nonorganisation-owned and 2120 were organisationowned that we considered for our analysis.

## 4.2. Measurement

GitHub o<sup>f</sup>ers a good environment for measuring the degree of superposition and studying its relationship to the project’s popularity and survival. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, two features of GitHub make it ideal for this research. First, the granularity of the data and the availability of time stamps for all events enabled us to measure the work structure in terms of the degree of superposition. Second, the availability of detailed contributor- and project-speci<sup>fi</sup>c data allowed us to measure the dependent variables and operationalise the necessary controls. The di<sup>f</sup>erent variables that we used in this research and their measures are detailed in the following subsections.

## 4.2.1. Dependent variables

We use two dependent variables in this research (a) popularity of the project and (b) project survival. As mentioned in the section “FLOSS Project Outcomes – Popularity and Survival”, we consider the popularity of the project to be an important measure of FLOSS project outcome. In GitHub, users can “star” projects in order to keep track of those that they <sup>fi</sup>nd interesting and also to show their appreciation for these projects (GitHub, 2017a). The number of stars a project has received indicates approximately the number of people who are interested in and show support for that project. Count of stars is therefore a commonly used measure for identifying popular projects in the GitHub environment. For example, GitHub uses stars to identify trending projects and in its project rankings (GitHub, 2017a), Jarczyk et al. (2014) used the log transformation of the number of stars as a measure of the quality and popularity of GitHub projects, and Tsay et al. (2014) used the number of stars as a measure of popularity and project establishment. In recognition of the usefulness of count of stars as a measure of popularity, we adopted it as the <sup>fi</sup>rst dependent variable to test our relationships.

In addition to project popularity, we included the survival of the project as an alternate measure of FLOSS project outcome. In this model, we considered the event to have happened (project failure), if the project became inactive within the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of its inception. Because we considered projects that were started in early 2014, a project was deemed inactive if no code changes were made to the source code from the year 2015 onwards. The choice of this timeline for project inactivity is in line with Stewart et al. (2006), who found that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of FLOSS projects cease to have activity within the <sup>fi</sup>rst year. In survival analysis, we estimate the hazard rates, which is the likelihood of project inactivity at time t given that it has lasted till time t (Hosmer et al., 2008).

## 4.2.2. Independent variables

We employed three independent variables in this study – restrictive licence flag, degree of superposition, and project ownership. The restrictive licence <sup>fl</sup>ag is the main independent variable used in our analysis. This <sup>fl</sup>ag takes a value of “1” if the project adopts the restrictive “copyleft” clause as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by FSF. The licence descriptions curated by GitHub was used to determine if the licence was restrictive or permissive (https://choosealicense.com/). Some of the projects in our sample did not have a licence assigned or adopted a lesser-known licence for which accurate descriptions were not provided. To avoid miss classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of such projects, we removed such projects from our sample.

We adopted the approach outlined by Medappa and Srivastava (2019) to operationalise the degree of superposition. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we measured the degree of superposition as the ratio of the total number of versions of the project to the total number of individual task contributions made to the project. This operationalisation is based on the concept of superposition introduced by Howison and Crowston (2014). Based on this operationalisation, the degree of superposition for a project takes a value between 0 and 1. If degree of superposition = 1, all the project’s tasks were implemented individually and added sequentially, with each individual task contribution representing a new version of the project. The degree of superposition decreases as the project adopts a concurrent development approach and approaches zero as greater number of individual task contributions are piled onto individual versions of the project.

To study the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of project ownership, we used a <sup>fl</sup>ag, project ownership, which takes the value 1 if the project is owned by an organisation and 0 otherwise. Organisations in GitHub are shared accounts that can be used to centralise a group’s code and adopt a work<sup>fl</sup>ow that is suitable for business (Neath, 2010). This work<sup>fl</sup>ow provides multiple levels of permission controls that enable companies to create nested teams with hierarchical access to the code, allowing them to replicate their organisational structure on GitHub. Most companies hosting projects on GitHub, use their own organisation accounts to consolidate monitoring and management of their FLOSS projects. GitHub recognises projects that are owned by organisations and makes this attribute publicly available through its API. By accessing this GitHub determined project attribute, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed if a project is owned by an organisation.

## 4.2.3. Control variables

We controlled for several project characteristics in our estimation models. Among the project characteristics, four measures were used to control for the contributor and owner-related attributes of the project and seven others were used to control for di<sup>f</sup>erent size and type related attributes of the project. First, we controlled for the experience of the project owner in terms of the number of GitHub FLOSS projects the owner has created. The existence and density of prior ties between the project owner and contributors has been found to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the probability that the project will attract more individuals (Rebeca & García, 2009). Based on this <sup>fi</sup>nding, we would expect that the experience of the project owner plays a role in enhancing the popularity of the project and its chance of survival. Second, we control for the number of contributors in the project. An increase in the number of contributors is often associated with an increase in the number of task contributions and consequently an increase in the success of the project (Stewart et al., 2006; Subramaniam et al., 2009). In addition to the number of contributors, the average number of contributions per contributor is also expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the project outcomes (Subramaniam et al., 2009). Considering this, we controlled for the average num ber of commits per contributor, where a commit is any change or set of changes that are locally saved (GitHub, 2017b). In Distributed Version Control Systems (DVCS) based development platforms like GitHub, we can delineate between core contributors, who can directly make changes to the main project code, and the peripheral contributors, who are not given write access to the project but still contribute to the project (Rullani & Hae<sup>fl</sup>iger, 2013). Community ideologies can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent to which contribu tors are given write access to the main project code With ideologies favouring self-governance tending to provide greater access to the source code. Further, access restriction to the main project code can function as informal governance mechanisms that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the popularity and survival of the project (Medappa & Srivastava, 2018). Considering the poten tial confounding e<sup>f</sup>ect of source-code access restric tions on the relationship work structures and project outcomes, we include number of core contributors in the project as a control variable.

In addition to the above four measures, we identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed seven other measures to control for di<sup>f</sup>erent size and type-related project attributes. First, we controlled for project size measured as megabytes of code. Smaller projects are usually associated with fewer contributors and contributions, than are larger projects. Thus, projects of di<sup>f</sup>erent sizes are expected to di<sup>f</sup>er in terms of their capacity for attracting contributors and coordinating new tasks (Setia et al., 2012). Second, we controlled for the number of programming languages. While projects that involve multiple languages may have greater functionality, coordinating contributions across di<sup>f</sup>erent languages can be challenging. Third, we controlled for the average task size, measured as the average number of commits made within the project’s tasks. As the tasks within a project increase in size and complexity, the average number of commits per task increases. It is important to control for the average task size in the project because projects with a greater number of large tasks will have a higher need for co-work than projects with smaller tasks. Fourth, we controlled for number of subscriptions in the project. In GitHub, subscriptions indicate the number of individuals who receive noti<sup>fi</sup>- cations regarding the project development activities. Like the number of contributors in a project, increase in subscriptions can attract attention to the code development activities, allowing better bug identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and opportunities for code improvement (Stewart, Ammeter et al., 2006). This can in turn lead to better project outcomes in terms of its popularity and survival. Fifth, time is expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the nature of routines associated with FLOSS projects (Lindberg, 2015). Because our analysis includes projects that commenced during the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5 months of 2014, it was necessary to control for the month of creation of projects. We also included two <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects: the <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect for the main programming language and the <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect for the project category. Despite mixed results in the extant literature, the type of pro gramming language used has been found to be an important antecedent to FLOSS project outcomes (Subramaniam et al., 2009). Further, the ease of coordinating development activities may di<sup>f</sup>er across programming languages, with languages more conducive to modular architecture displaying greater ease of coordination (Baldwin & Clark, 2006). Lastly, we control for the category or subject area of the project. It is very likely that certain categories of projects may prefer speci<sup>fi</sup>c licence types, and at the same time, may have a higher tendency to gain popularity and survive. We categorise projects into the following subject areas – data science and management, documentation and website software, gaming, system software, mobile development, general application, frontend and UI (User Interface) development. The project categories are included as <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects in our model.

## 4.3. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy

The empirical challenge in most studies on FLOSS licences is that the choice of licence type for a project may be endogenous with outcome parameters of interest – such as project popularity and survival. Since project owners choose a licence for their project rather than randomly assigning one, unobservable characteristics related to the project owner may get involved in their selection of the licence. For example, project owners considering the commercial potential of a project may choose permissive licences and at the same time target functionalities that are useful to the organisation rather than to the end-users (e.g., security frameworks vs. analytical frameworks). However, the functionality chosen may involve skills that are not widely available in the community, which would make productive deferral and superposition costlier, forcing greater amount of co-work. This would generate a downward bias to the marginal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of degree of superposition on the popularity of the project. To overcome this speci<sup>fi</sup>c issue, we included controls for the topic of the project and the main programming language. Yet there may be other such unobserved relationships, because of which, the observed association between the outcome and explanatory variable may be misleading. Instrument variables are often used to address endogeneity issues arising from omitted variables and hence o<sup>f</sup>er a good identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy for our research (Angrist & Krueger, 2001).

The project owner often chooses the licence type after envisioning the project and laying down its basic objectives. We leverage the fact that cultural and societal background of the project owner may play a role in her/his decision to choose a restrictive vs. a permissive licence. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identify that project owners belonging to more collectivist cultures align with the ideologies of restrictive licences (that prevent the commercialisation and closure of collaboratively developed code). At the same time, the undertones of the beliefs of socialism in restrictive licences vs. liberal beliefs in permissive licences suggest that project owners coming from welfareoriented economies may better accept the ideologies of restrictive licences (Forbes, 2017). To create our instrument for the choice of licence we leverage the location information of the project owner – using which we identify their cultural and societal a<sup>fi</sup>liation. Because the location entries are self-reported in GitHub and could be prone to errors and inconsistencies, we manually veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the location of the project owner by checking their LinkedIn pro<sup>fi</sup>le, home page, and headquarter location (in case of organisations). Using the location information, we measured two cultural and societal indicators for use as instruments for the choice of licence:

(a) Geert Hofstede cultural dimension Individualism vs. collectivism. This cultural dimension identi<sup>fi</sup>es the degree to which people in a society are integrated into groups. Compared to collectivist societies, individualistic societies have weaker ties that often only relate an individual to his/her immediate family. In other words, individualist cultures emphasise the “I” versus the “we” (Hofstede, 2011). Because of the rigid interpretation of information freedom, project owners coming from collectivist societies may be more willing to accept the ideologies of restrictive licences as compared to project owners coming from individualist cultures.

(b) Country’s social protection contribution as a percentage of total expenditure in the year 2014–2015. The social protection contribution of the country is a good indicator of the country’s focus towards social welfare. We leverage this macro indicator to create the second instrument for the licence type. Because restrictive licences are closer to social policies while permissive licences mimic liberal policies, we expect project owners coming from countries that have greater expenditure on social protection to align towards restrictive licences. To measure the social protection contribution, we use the government <sup>fi</sup>nal consumption expenditure data for each country maintained by the United Nations Statistics Division (UNSD, 2015). We choose expenditure data reported for 2014–2015 for the instrument because the projects in our sample were started in early 2014.

Our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy adopts an approach similar to Card (1993), who used residence location of the individual as an instrument for college education while examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of college education on earnings for di<sup>f</sup>erent income groups. In this research, we are interested in the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the licence choice on the relationship between superposition and project outcomes. Therefore, our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy relies on the assumption that the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of the cultural and societal background of the project owner on project popularity and survival (e.g., unobserved educational background and income of project owners which may be associated with the location; Hofstede, 2011) do not vary with di<sup>f</sup>erent work structures except through the hypothesised ideological and motivational mechanisms. Considering this assumption, the validity of the instrument rests on the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation tests for its strength and overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation restriction, which are provided below.

## 4.3.1. Checks for instrument validity

We provide three tests for the validity of the instruments – (a) under identi<sup>fi</sup>cation test, (b) weak identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation test, and (c) overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation test. First, we conduct the score test for the null hypothesis that the model is under identi<sup>fi</sup>ed (Breusch & Pagan, 1980). For both the non-organisation and organisational owned project subgroups, we reject the null hypothesis thereby supporting the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the model (LM statistic = 41.1 for individual-owned project subgroup and 98.3 for organisation owned project subgroup). Second, we check if the instruments are weakly correlated with licence type. The Cragg-Donald based weak identi<sup>fi</sup>cation test satis<sup>fi</sup>es the minimum criteria for strength for both the subgroups (Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic = 10.1 for individualowned project subgroup and 24.8 for organisation owned project subgroup; Stock & Yogo, 2005). Lastly, the Sargan-Hansen test for overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation restrictions fails to reject the null hypotheses that the instrument is valid for both the subgroups (Hayashi, 2000, pp. 227–228). The results of these three tests provide support for the strength and validity of our chosen instruments.

## 4.4. Analysis

Tables 2 and 3 provide the means, standard deviations, and correlation coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for the subgroups of nonorganisation- and organisation-owned projects, respectively. To estimate project popularity, we include two regression models – (a) 2SLS model with endogenous regressors, and (b) negative binomial model. To estimate likelihood of project inactivity, we include two models – (a) probit model with endogenous regressors, and (b) survival analysis using the accelerated failure-time (AFT) model.

## 4.4.1. Estimating Project popularity

2SLS with endogenous regressors. To estimate project popularity, we included a 2SLS model with log transformation of the number of stars as the dependent variable. In this model, the endogenous explanatory variable – choice of licence type, is estimated using an instrument created from the cultural and societal background of the project owner. The use of a log-linear OLS model helps us account for the overdispersed nature of the dependent variable – number of stars.

Negative binomial model. In addition to the 2SLS model, we included a negative binomial model with the number of stars as the dependent variable (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013, Ch 4). This model does not account for the endogenous e<sup>f</sup>ects of the explanatory variable and is included as a test for the presence and direction of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Since this model predicts the log of the expected number of stars as a function of the predictor variables, the interpretations of the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient are like the log-linear OLS model. That is, for a one-unit change in the predictor variable, the number of stars change by the regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, given that the other predictor variables in the model are held constant.

## 4.4.2. Estimating project inactivity

Probit model with endogenous regressors. To understand what determines the likelihood of inactivity of the project and test hypotheses 1b and 2b, we included a probit model with endogenous regressors. In this model, we use instruments to account for the endogenous explanatory variable – licence type. The dependent variable – likelihood of inactivity, takes a value of 1 if the project became inactive within the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of its activity, else it is 0.

Survival model. In addition to the probit model with endogenous regressors, we conducted survival analysis using accelerated failure-time (AFT) model (Hosmer et al., 2008). In this model, we estimate the likelihood that a project becomes inactive at time t given that it has survived till time t. This model is included as a test and benchmark for the instrumented model.

Further, to correct for any potential heteroscedasticity in the error terms, we used heteroscedasticity consistent standard errors in our models (Hayes & Cai, 2007). The following section details the results of the regression models and the checks we employed to ensure the validity of our results.

Second, our operationalisation of the constructs – degree of superposition, project popularity, and project survival was done based on existing de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the measures (e.g., Borges & Tulio Valente, 2018; Howison & Crowston, 2014; Samoladas et al., 2010). This allowed us to generalise our <sup>fi</sup>ndings over a large set of projects and test the relationships on the project outcomes – popularity and survival. However, we acknowledge that the subjective nature of these constructs makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop a measure that captures all aspects of the construct. When it comes to the measure of degree of superposition, potential spontaneous correlations of the measure of degree of superposition with project popularity and survival make it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to capture the causal e<sup>f</sup>ects. Further, empirical research supports the existence of non-linearity in the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of degree of superposition and project popularity (Medappa & Srivastava, 2019). Since this research examines the interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects of the change in licence type, the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings relies on the assumption that any confounding in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects does not vary with the choice of licence. By adopting an instrument variable approach and including a rich set of controls, we have attempted to minimise these confounding in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Third, motivations of contributions are multifaceted rather than being an unitary construct with intrinsic and extrinsic motivations at either ends (Reiss, 2012). Therefore, contributors of FLOSS projects are likely to be driven by a mix of motivations. Further, permissive licences may also engage intrinsically motivated contributors who <sup>fi</sup>nd it intrinsically rewarding to work on the project. Similarly, restrictive licences may also engage extrinsically motivated contributors who have the need for the functionality of the project. These issues may lead to a downward bias in the measured e<sup>f</sup>ect size in the empirical models. Considering these limitations, we need to exercise caution when interpreting the results of the analyses. Fourth, our sample tends to lean towards permissive licence with about 82% of the projects in our sample adopting permissive licences. Our selection of cultural and societal background of project owner as instruments for the choice of licence mitigates this issue as the measures of the instrument are more uniformly distributed. The cultural dimension measure of individualism has a mean of 70.5 and standard deviation of 22.8. The measure of social protection expenditure has a mean of 0.09 and standard deviation of 0.06. Further, trends in the open-source community indicate a shift towards permissive licences owing to legal di<sup>fi</sup>culties involved with its commercialisation and its integration with other software (Rubens, 2016). Despite the legal di<sup>fi</sup>culties, contributors and organisations who have a more rigid belief in information freedom may continue to use restrictive licences. Adopting restrictive licences necessitates certain changes to the project characteristics to ensure a seamless <sup>fi</sup>t with the ideologies of the licence. One important project characteristic is the project’s work structures, which this research has attempted to examine. In the next section, we take a deeper look at some of the important implications of this research for theory and practice.

## 7. Implications

The value creation mechanisms that have resulted in the success of the FLOSS phenomenon have inspired new sourcing strategies and engagement models (Ågerfalk et al., 2015). Moreover, organisations are now approaching FLOSS with the fabled idea of replicating the immense success of projects like Linux and Apache. Microsoft, once the “evil enemy” of open source (Ljungberg, 2000, p. 210), has been steadily shifting its focus towards FLOSS model of development (Microsoft News Center, 2018). On the other side, the free software movement, which was founded to prevent the “commercial hijack” of collaboratively developed code (Ci<sup>f</sup>olilli, 2004), has been evolving into a movement that now regularly witnesses commercial participation and organisational ownership (Fitzgerald, 2006; Germonprez et al., 2016). The fusion of the two vastly di<sup>f</sup>erent ideologies (open vs. commercial software) has resulted in the emergence of a new corporate-communal landscape with multiple interwoven, overlapping, con<sup>fl</sup>icting, and divergent practices that enable and constrain FLOSS engagements (Germonprez et al., 2016). Driven by the need to understand the ideological shifts that led to the evolution of the new corporate-communal landscape and examine their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the mechanisms that were embedded in the founding ideology of open source; this research sought to answer the following research question – How have the ideological shifts invoked by (a) the emergence of permissive licences, and (b) the shift towards organisational ownership, transformed the influence of work structures on FLOSS project outcomes?

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

By examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideological shifts that are associated with the choice of licence type and organisation ownership of FLOSS projects, we contribute to the theories on – FLOSS ideologies, licences, and organisational participation. First, our research adds to the existing understanding on FLOSS ideologies (e.g., Daniel et al., 2018; Ljungberg, 2000; Stewart & Gosain, 2006) as it takes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant step in delineating the dynamic nature of ideological beliefs in the FLOSS context. Because of this dynamism, our research <sup>fi</sup>nds that the mechanisms (e.g., motivational mechanisms) that are shaped by the ideological beliefs can also change over time and are therefore associated with boundary conditions of time (Suddaby, 2010). Moreover, changes to community ideologies can invoke the perceptions of ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>t which can alter the contributor’s commitment to the team (Daniel et al., 2018). At the project level, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>t can alter the participation structure of the project. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that con<sup>fl</sup>icting ideologies within the project (e.g., organisation owned projects with restrictive licences) can result in an ideological mis<sup>fi</sup>t in the team where one group of contributors face an under-<sup>fi</sup>t (e.g., intrinsically motivated contributors who believe in the ideology of restrictive licences) and the other group an over-<sup>fi</sup>t (e.g., extrinsically motivated contributors). Because the contributors who face an under-<sup>fi</sup>t may lower their commitment to the project and contributors who face an over-<sup>fi</sup>t may engage more with the project (Daniel et al., 2018), the participation structure of the project changes towards contributors who face an ideological over-<sup>fi</sup>t with the community.

Given the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideological shifts on the participation structure and motivational mechanisms, it is prudent to understand what triggers ideological shifts in the community. As a preliminary answer to this question, our research <sup>fi</sup>nds that ideological shifts can occur due to individuals and institutions questioning the tenets of the embedded ideological beliefs (e.g., creation of OSI and permissive licences) or from the mixing of di<sup>f</sup>erent ideologies (e.g., organisational ownership and the creation of the corporatecommunal landscape; Germonprez et al., 2016). Future research can build on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to provide a more complete typology of triggers for ideological shifts in the context of the FLOSS landscape.

Second, the choice of FLOSS licence type is one of the important decisions made by project owners (Fershtman & Gandal, 2007), which is instrumental in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing project outcomes (Stewart, Ammeter et al., 2006). Moreover, licences provide the means to formalise and protect community ideologies in FLOSS projects and hence play an important role in shaping the motivations of the contributors who join the project. This salience has attracted researchers across many disciplines who have tried to understand the mechanisms involved in choosing a particular licence type (e.g., Lerner & Tirole, 2005; Sen et al., 2008; Singh & Phelps, 2013) and the impact of licences on project outcomes (e.g., Colazo et al., 2005; Scotchmer, 2010; Sen et al., 2011; Stewart, Darcy et al., 2006). But the research on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the licences on project outcomes, has been less conclusive – with con<sup>fl</sup>icting <sup>fi</sup>ndings across the main types of licences (Crowston et al., 2012). To reconcile the mixed results, our research, grounded in ideological beliefs of FLOSS sought a novel way to understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of licences on project outcomes. From our analyses, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the choice of licence in<sup>fl</sup>uences motivational and ideological needs of the contributors, which requires careful alignment with di<sup>f</sup>erent project attributes. Thus, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of licences on the project outcomes depends on how well the project attributes, such as its work structures, are aligned to the ideological beliefs and motivational needs associated with the licence type. In addition to this <sup>fi</sup>nding, for future empirical research on FLOSS licences, we o<sup>f</sup>er an innovative identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy that mitigates the endogenous e<sup>f</sup>ects of licence choice. Leveraging the cultural and societal background of the project owner as instruments for the choice of licence, future empirical work can look towards generalising and examining the boundary conditions associated with the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of licence choice that extant research has painstakingly theorised.

Third, our research advances the literature surrounding organisational participation in FLOSS projects (Capra et al., 2011; Fitzgerald, 2006; Germonprez et al., 2016; Spaeth et al., 2015) by examining the changes to the community ideological landscape when organisations own FLOSS projects. The new corporate-community landscape that has emerged in software development due to the entry of organisations into the open-source movement have created new opportunities and challenges for project owners (Germonprez et al., 2016). Extant literature provides a rich understanding of the impact of organisational participation on governance practices (e.g., Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; O’Mahony, 2005; Wagstrom, 2009), motivations (e.g., Capra et al., 2011; Lerner & Tirole, 2003), and development practices (e.g., Fitzgerald, 2006; West & O’Mahony, 2008) of FLOSS projects. Our research adds to this literature by theorising the impact of organisational participation on the ideological landscape of community FLOSS projects, which invoke di<sup>f</sup>erent motivational needs and necessitates di<sup>f</sup>erent work organisation.

## 7.2. Practical implications

Practically, organisations and individual owners of FLOSS projects have constantly been trying to replicate the value creation mechanisms of successful FLOSS projects. Because the motivational mechanisms of the contributors can be embedded in their ideological beliefs (Daniel et al., 2018; Stewart & Gosain,

2006), it is important for project owners to understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideological changes that may occur when di<sup>f</sup>erent ideologies come together in FLOSS projects. Of particular importance in this relationship are the dominant work structures that emerge as a means to balance the motivational needs of the contributors and simultaneously overcome the challenges related to FLOSS task work orchestration (Howison & Crowston, 2014; Lindberg et al., 2016). While superposition is well suited to environments that support openness and limited time pressures (as in non-organisational settings; Howison & Crowston, 2014), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that changes to the community ideologies necessitate corresponding changes to the work structures to better facilitate project outcomes. This understanding is especially important for organisations who are constantly trying to balance e<sup>fi</sup>cient development practices but are faced with the challenge of meeting the motivational needs of autonomy amongst the intrinsically driven contributors.

Lastly, with increasing dependence on FLOSS, there is a growing concern among users (both <sup>fi</sup>rms and individuals), if they can expect future development of the software products that they are using today. Considering this concern, researchers have begun to call attention to the important research question – “why do developers sustain open source development?” (Von Krogh et al., 2012, p. 650). Further, the complex nature of FLOSS project outcome measures, suggests that di<sup>f</sup>erent project success measures are associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent value creation mechanisms. While popularity and task e<sup>f</sup>ort are more closely associated with the motivational mechanisms (Ke & Zhang, 2010), project sustenance may depend more on the perceptions of legitimacy (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010). Our research attempts to delineate the mechanisms through which ideological beliefs in<sup>fl</sup>uence project popularity and survival. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that licences and their underlying ideologies can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions of legitimacy in the minds of the contributors. Further, based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of hypotheses 2a and 2b, our research suggests that sustenance of FLOSS projects may not always align with the mechanisms that promote project popularity. Because of this di<sup>f</sup>erence, when project owners consider the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideologies, the type of project outcome desired by the owner needs to be an important part of their design decisions. Cognisance of these in<sup>fl</sup>uences can help project owners make sense of the complex in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ideological beliefs and facilitate better project outcomes.
