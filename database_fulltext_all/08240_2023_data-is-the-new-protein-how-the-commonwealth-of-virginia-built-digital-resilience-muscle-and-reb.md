# DATA IS THE NEW PROTEIN: HOW THE COMMONWEALTH OF VIRGINIA BUILT DIGITAL RESILIENCE MUSCLE AND REBOUNDED FROM OPIOID AND COVID SHOCKS <sup>1</sup>

Monica Chiarini Tremblay and Rajiv Kohli Raymond A. Mason School of Business, William & Mary, Williamsburg, VA 23187 U.S.A. {Monica.Tremblay@mason.wm.edu} {Rajiv.Kohli@mason.wm.edu}

Carlos Rivero Amazon Web Services, Worldwide Public Sector, State and Local Government Seattle, WA 98109 U.S.A. {rivercap@amazon.com}

During shocks, residents and businesses rely upon the government to ensure health, safety, and the continuity of services. The government’s ability to respond depends upon how well it utilizes its data resources and builds digital resilience. Yet governments often fail to integrate data from different agencies to respond effectively to shocks. We conceptualize digital resilience as a dynamic capability (DC). Although the DC framework provides a theoretical basis, it is unclear what actions managers can take to build DC. Through process tracing, we examine how the Commonwealth of Virginia (COVA) built DCs and rebounded from two shocks—the opioid crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. COVA managers leveraged statewide data assets, built routines to disseminate data, and reconfigured operational capabilities to build three DCs— relationship building, intelligence creation, and value extraction. Data functioned as the “protein” to build the digital resilience “muscle.” We found that the relationship building DC leveraged the operational capabilities of data management, integration, and governance structure to foster data sharing, the intelligence creation DC leveraged analytics, and the value extraction DC converted analytics into cost savings, revenue generation, and new services. Whereas COVA built robust digital resilience by facilitating data sharing, the agencies exploited data assets to develop scalable solutions.

Keywords: Resilience, digital, anticipate and rebound, learning, data sharing, government

## Introduction

Resilience is the ability of organizations to anticipate change (Meyer, 1982), absorb change and disturbance (Holling, 1973, p. 14), react, and bounce back (Shepherd & Williams, 2014; Weick, 1998) from sudden, extraordinary events or shocks (de Bruijne et al., 2010). Shocks manifest as natural disasters such as epidemics and earthquakes, technological failures of electric grids or wastewater systems, and civil disturbances such as looting and riots. Resilience implies that organizations are prepared for unknown threats (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2011) and can mitigate their impact. Resilience is a multifaceted concept built upon capabilities to anticipate and observe unusual and harmful events and capabilities to rebound, such as deploying assets and coordinating actions to combat shocks and learning capabilities that prepare organizations for future shocks. The types of threats vary, meaning that each response requires a different configuration of actors and resources. Therefore, building resilience requires a strategy that balances prevention and mitigation because not all shocks can be prevented (de Bruijne et al., 2010). To achieve resilience, organizational leaders must form a vision of what they view as the appropriate policies and capabilities to make investments in planning, forecasting, and preparedness to achieve the vision (de Bruijne et al., 2010).

The information systems community seeks to define digital resilience and examine how it enables organizations and societies to respond to shocks (Boh et al., 2021). Resilience requires building and activating capabilities to anticipate turbulence in the environment and rebound from unpredictable shocks. Therefore, the dynamic capabilities (DC) framework is a suitable theoretical basis for examining how an organization’s operational capabilities combine to create DCs to collectively deliver resilience. However, scholars argue that the conceptualization of DCs is rather undynamic and have called for a practice-oriented perspective to explain how organizational routines become dynamic (Wenzel et al., 2021). Augier and Teece (2009) argue that “The invisible hand [of organizations] must have fingers that can work in a coordinated fashion” to make the case that managerial agency plays a vital role in creating DCs (Di Stefano et al., 2014). Yet it is unclear what actions the managers’ fingers take during a shock to alter the organization’s resource base. How do managers decide which DCs to create to build resilience? We know that managerial attention plays a critical role in discerning the threats (Shepherd & Williams, 2014) and that building DCs is thus a prerequisite to taking action, but how managers should develop which DCs to build digital resilience needs to be better understood.

Building resilience is essential for government and public institutions that must mobilize physical and digital resources to ensure the safety of residents and facilitate commercial activities during a shock. Governments are multi-agency institutions that must coordinate with national, state, and local agencies and private enterprises to build data, appropriate assets, and capabilities in order to become resilient. The efficiencies extracted from digital technologies by commercial enterprises have stimulated public sector organizations to digitize back-end functions and citizen services in what is known as digital-era governance (Dunleavy et al., 2006). Understanding how governments can use data and digital technologies to redesign and deliver social services is even more pressing during shocks (Misuraca et al., 2018, p. 92).

According to a report by the OECD (2022), conveying timely, relevant information to the frontline staff at the local or regional level was a significant challenge for many governments during the COVID-19 pandemic. National governments encountered challenges in collecting data from multiple actors due to a lack of clear standards and homogeneous processes for reporting data. For example, when the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers observed that coronavirus patients overran hospitals in other parts of the world, they spent \$1.7 billion to build emergency field hospitals (Rose, 2020). The majority of these facilities went unused (Sonne & Ryan, 2020). Similar results were found in the United Kingdom (Meyer et al., 2020). However, countries that utilized data for targeted responses were successful in addressing citizens’ needs during the pandemic. For example, the Taiwanese government integrated its national health insurance database with travel-history data from customs databases to aid case identification (Ienca & Vayena, 2020). The South Korean government observed quarantine compliance among citizens using a mobile app, which contributed to “flattening the curve” (Zaimova, 2022).

As noted in the above examples, data and digital resources accentuate resilience. By recombining data assets, governments can create DCs that help decision makers anticipate environmental turbulence and rebound with targeted actions based upon evidence from data rather than a one-size-fits-all response. Building digital resilience against sudden shocks, such as earthquakes or contagious pandemics, demands a flexible and efficient way of accessing information so that the residents are protected and commercial activities and essential services are not interrupted. Therefore, our research question is: How can governments utilize digital assets to build and exercise digital resilience during shocks?

We examine how the Commonwealth of Virginia (COVA) used its data and digital assets to develop and exercise digital resilience during two shocks—the opioid crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. We conducted in-depth discussions with COVA officials and reviewed reports, documents, and public announcements to examine how COVA used its digital infrastructure to gather and analyze data and created capabilities to rebound from the two shocks. Our paper theorizes the dynamic development of the mechanisms that create digital resilience among governments and public institutions. We trace the processes of how data and digital assets, combined with operational capabilities and routines, coalesce to form DCs that deliver digital resilience.<sup>2</sup>

Our findings reveal that the relationship building DC leveraged operational capabilities of data management, integration, and governance structure that together fostered data sharing, the intelligence creation DC was built upon the operational capabilities of analytics, and the value extraction DC leveraged operational capabilities to convert analytics into cost savings, revenue generation, and new services. Together, the mutually reinforcing DCs created digital resilience and provided nuanced and targeted responses to the two shocks.

Our findings contribute to the understanding of how digital resilience is built in the government sector. We found that robust digital resilience emerges when the state government facilitates data sharing and analytical tools while its state agencies and local governments exploit data assets to develop solutions that can be scaled up to the state level. These insights contribute to the DC literature by uncovering the mechanisms that bring dynamism to DCs. Our interview data reveal the actions that managers must take to create DCs.<sup>3</sup>

## Theoretical Foundations

Drawing upon the resilience literature (Holling, 1973; Meyer, 1982) and the dynamic capabilities literature (Augier & Teece, 2009; Schilke et al., 2018), we theorize how governments employ data to build digital resilience. We identify mechanisms within state agencies that explain how data, combined with routines and operational capabilities, act as the “protein” to build digital resilience. Our theorization establishes the cumulative salience of the DCs that build the resilience “muscle.”

## Resilience

Holling (1973, p. 17) defines resilience as “the ability of these systems to absorb changes of state variables, driving variables, and parameters, and still persist.” Resilience is the capacity to tolerate disruption from significant shocks and learn to adapt and rebound from shocks (Boh et al., 2021). Resilient organizations learn from experience and engage in transformative actions in order to emerge stronger and more resourceful in the face of future shocks (Conz & Magnani, 2019). Broadly, resilience comprises three capabilities: anticipate, rebound, and learn. Resilient organizations anticipate impending disruptions (Bode et al., 2011), which includes the ability to detect the disruption, assess vulnerability (Linnenluecke, 2017), and recognize and interpret risks in order to solve problems when facing uncertainty (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2011). After interpreting the impending risk, resilient organizations rebound by reacting to mitigate the threat in times of crisis (Dentoni et al., 2020). They coordinate and adapt by managing and reconfiguring available resources (Ambulkar et al., 2015; Yoo et al., 2012)—for example, by developing situation-specific responses (Lengnick-Hall et al., 2011; Linnenluecke, 2017) with external partners to adapt to the new normal.

Resilience is not a one-time event; organizations must replenish resilience with periodic environmental evaluation and learning. Deliberate learning occurs when organizations bounce back to seminormal operations (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2011). Although organizations learn at both the anticipate and rebound stages during the shock, the overall learning is often local and scattered among operational teams. Learning is post hoc, formal, and emerges from the scrutiny of past responses when organizations reflect upon the overall effectiveness of the successes, near misses, and failures; synthesize learning across operational units; and formalize newly gained insights (Duchek, 2020). Organizations then institute new work practices (Lee et al., 2020) and assimilate the learning so it can be adapted to anticipate future challenges (Powley, 2009) and help them bounce back once again (Wildavsky, 1988).

As resilience requires the coordination of data resources with other organizational resources, the information systems discipline must first define digital resilience to examine how organizations leverage digital technologies (Boh et al., 2021). Digital enhancements can enable each resilience capability by nurturing routines that utilize data resources within their ranks and surroundings to anticipate a shock (see Table 1). Digital enhancements must then build analytical capabilities to recognize the data interdependencies within organizations and their partners and continuously monitor and interpret the environment to anticipate impending changes (Comfort et al., 2010). Failure to exercise detection routines during a shock can leave organizations unprepared (Meyer, 1982).

Rebound capabilities require that the infrastructure and data assets are in place such that the organization can anticipate and rebound from a shock in order to withstand it and bounce back to a steady state. Organizations utilize their rebound capabilities for proactive and reactive actions that help achieve resilience (Duchek, 2020). They accumulate physical, digital, and human assets and build capacity when they are not under direct stress (Pal et al., 2014), a period we refer to as the interlude. Although organizations learn at each stage, they devote time to reflect during the interlude after a shock is under control. In describing resilience capabilities, the extant literature describes the process through which organizations achieve resilience. However, it is unclear how organizations achieve resilience. What capabilities must organizations build to anticipate and rebound from shocks? How can organizations leverage existing resources to withstand a shock? Given that shocks are rare events, organizations cannot invest in solutions that can respond to all types of shocks. As such, organizations must improvise and reconfigure data and digital resources and coordinate activities to adapt to shocks.

## Digital Resilience as a Dynamic Capability

We define digital resilience as the dynamic capability of an organization to deploy data, technology, and analytics to anticipate, rebound, and learn from a shock. Enduring digital resilience requires organizations to rapidly leverage available data, acquire and store external data (Argote et al., 2022), and reconfigure their digital assets and operational capabilities to respond to shocks.

Dynamic capabilities are a cluster of self-renewing organizational abilities used to adapt, integrate, and reconfigure internal and external resources to achieve congruence with changing environments (Teece et al., 1997, p. 515). In contrast to operational capabilities, DCs are a particular set of organizational capabilities resulting from “purposeful modification” of the organization’s existing resource base, ecosystem, and strategy (Schilke et al., 2018, pp. 392-393). Although DCs build upon operational or ordinary capabilities, their repeated and reliable execution, or routines, combined with strategic intent, brings dynamism to normal operational capabilities. Contrasting DCs with operational capabilities, Winter (2003, p. 991) argued that DCs “extend, modify or create ordinary capabilities.” Therefore, although DCs can emerge from operational capabilities, they have the potential to create other operational and dynamic capabilities. For this reason, scholars have viewed the typology of organizational capabilities as nested within higher-order capabilities (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). Heimeriks et al. (2012) proposed a similar typology of nested routines in which organizational practices create organizational DCs.

An organization must nurture routines that utilize data resources within its ranks and its surroundings to anticipate a shock. It must then build analytical capabilities to recognize the data interdependencies within the organization and its partners and continuously monitor and interpret the environment to anticipate impending changes (Comfort et al., 2010). Failure to exercise detection routines during a shock can leave organizations unprepared (Meyer, 1982). Rebound capabilities require that the infrastructure and data assets are in place such that the organization can anticipate and rebound from a shock in order to withstand it and bounce back to a steady state, while learning during the interlude.

Data and digital technologies build capabilities that enable organizations to become alert, create flexible choices or options, and become agile in responding to environmental turbulence (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). The extant literature highlights the importance of data management, integration of data from disparate sources, and analytical operational capabilities as the mechanisms that make organizations resilient (Table 2).

Pavlou and El Sawy (2010) note three types of digital technologies that enable organizations to be responsive— project and resource management systems, organizational memory systems, and cooperative work systems. They argue that these capabilities are necessary to build improvisational capabilities that enable organizations to spontaneously reconfigure existing resources to address “urgent, unpredictable and novel environmental situations” (p. 444). However, researchers argue that to garner data and analytical capabilities, organizations must also implement a governance structure (Chen & Hsieh, 2014).

## Data Management

Digital resilience is built on accurate, secure, and accessible data. Given that data are generated from various sources, data management ensures that extracted data are defined with standard definitions and organized in a way that allows consumers to retrieve and utilize relevant information for decision-making (Hristidis et al., 2010). Data containing personally identifiable information must be deidentified before it can be analyzed and disseminated.

## Integration

The integration of data sources provides the support needed to quickly respond to emergency situations and rebound from shocks (Hristidis et al., 2010). Organizations invest in digital technologies to anticipate often unspecified events (Kohli & Grover, 2008). Digital technologies are malleable and reconfigurable and can be acquired with little or no friction (Yoo et al., 2012). These properties foster the agility needed to integrate and reconfigure assets and resources, such as human expertise, that are necessary to survive and bounce back from a shock. Data integration involves formal agreements about who will share what data and who is authorized to use the combined dataset.

## Governance Structure

Data governance aims to build a culture of collaboration and openness underpinned by clearly defined policies and processes (Hawn Nelson et al., 2020). Swanson (2021) proposed that data takes on the meaning of infrastructure when it is provisioned in a network for use by a community. For example, with a formal governance structure, local or state governments can authorize the integration of power outage data with incoming phone calls from emergency services and seismic activity data. This integrated data can then be used to build early warning capabilities to inform emergency management services and healthcare organizations about the severity and location of an earthquake. However, contractual governance is necessary for entities to share data. When entities are assured that their data will be interpreted correctly, it builds relational trust (Janssen et al., 2012). Governance structures provide legal and contractual frameworks that allocate decision rights to certain entities to utilize combined data when responding to shocks. Appropriate governance structures are necessary for governmental organizations because of the multi-agency, multi-location nature of their services. This means that data resources dispersed across the state must be properly integrated (Chen & Hsieh, 2014).

Analytics: To manage risks during disasters, organizations can build operational capabilities to use data analytics tools and methods such as data visualization and simulation (Araz et al., 2020). Integrated data capabilities allow organizations to customize such digital assets to develop a targeted, nuanced response for the affected constituency rather than a one-size-fits-all solution. The replicability of data resources makes it possible to distribute information to many constituents for a negligible marginal cost.

## Research Methodology

Our research methodology involves the process tracing of events, decisions, and activities related to the Commonwealth of Virginia’s government and its agencies. This type of methodology, based on the in-depth analysis of a case, is used in the social sciences to explore why and how questions (Tan et al., 2020). Process tracing a case presents an opportunity to examine rich, complex phenomena in realworld settings (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2018). We take a case-centric process tracing approach to construct a detailed narrative from our data (Beach & Pedersen, 2013). Data from interviews and document reviews expand our ability to understand individual parts and the whole, a hermeneutic-circle principle that is fundamental to interpretive research (Klein & Myers, 1999).

## Background

The Commonwealth of Virginia (COVA) is a state in the mid-Atlantic region of the United States of America (USA). The COVA government comprises more than 63 agencies and executive branch entities and 133 local governments. With approximately 8.6 million residents, COVA includes suburban white-collar workers, blue-collar manufacturing workers, and rural agricultural communities. The geography is diverse, with mountains, farmlands, and oceanfront cities. COVA’s governor serves a single fouryear term. These factors present a challenge to initiate and sustain public service projects and therefore require that COVA build resilient capabilities that will protect its residents regardless of the political party in office.

A state government is an appropriate setting to examine digital resilience because it is obligated to provide its residents with timely, secure, and convenient services. During shocks, such as natural disasters or pandemics, it is difficult for a single firm to assemble the resources needed to ensure the safety and well-being of millions of residents. The state government has the legislative authority to impose restrictions when needed. To make trade-offs to ensure the safety of residents while ensuring the smooth operation of commerce, COVA used its data resources to make targeted decisions about where to impose restrictions during shocks in a way that balanced freedom of movement and economic activity with the safety of the residents, instead of implementing a one-size-fits-all solution for the entire state. As such, ensuring the continued delivery of social and civil services to residents while raising tax revenue during a shock indicates resilience.

To make data-driven decisions during shocks, COVA must ask state agencies to share regulated data with other agencies and coordinate activities with private organizations. Most hospitals, suppliers of emergency equipment, and utility services in the USA are private entities and therefore have no obligation to share data with the state government. However, to provide essential services during shocks, a central entity must coordinate activities that span public-private boundaries. As the COVA state government does not compete with private business organizations, it considered building a digital platform to collaborate with government agencies and private institutions. Such a platform would deliver resilience by signaling to state agencies that they are authorized to share data and by assuring private businesses that their data will remain secure and not be shared with their competitors. This study documents how COVA leveraged data to modify its existing resources, built dynamic capabilities during shocks, and achieved digital resilience.

## Data Collection

As process tracing in case study research relies on multiple sources of data (Yin, 2018), we collected extensive data from semi-structured interviews, publicly available documents, archival data, and active discussions with members of COVA’s Office of Data Governance and Analytics. We examined how COVA utilized data and digital assets to build and exercise resilience when dealing with two shocks—the opioid crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic. The author team includes COVA’s former chief data officer (CDO) and an insider researcher (Adler & Adler, 1994). An insider participant also provided us with a clearer understanding of the culture and politics in COVA. It would have taken much longer to independently gather this information and understand its meaning (Bonner & Tolhurst, 2002).

Fleming (2018) identifies five challenges for insider researchers: the potential for the implicit coercion of participants, difficulty maintaining privacy and confidentiality, potential biases and ensuring trustworthiness, preconceived ideas and the desire for positive outcomes, and the potential of professional conflicts in the dual roles of being an academic and researcher within the same context. We took several steps to overcome these challenges. The internal researcher was a practitioner and was not involved in data analysis, except in instances when we needed to validate facts. The external researchers provided the necessary critical distance to the data collected; they captured information by documenting events during weekly meetings with the CDO team and independently sought and analyzed external material (e.g., additional interviews, archival data, and documents). The internal researcher joined COVA in 2018 and was able to be objective about events and actions taken because he did not feel the need to defend the failures or shortcomings of the past. The internal researcher identified critical stakeholders in COVA’s data journey, which allowed the external researchers to collect historical information about events and contact individuals to conduct interviews. The interviews were anonymized and the external researchers analyzed the results. The internal researcher read the manuscript and made factual corrections about the events, validated the theoretical assumptions, and provided additional examples to corroborate and refine the theoretical framework.

Our data collection included multiple sources of data (see Table 3). To explore the workings of COVA’s digital initiatives, we iteratively built a timeline. For creating the timeline, a key data source was 44 active videoconference discussions with COVA informants. The participants helped us identify external data sources to validate and enrich our understanding of the events included in the timeline. These external resources included published reports and publicly available news items (see Table 4 for a summary). Additionally, in order to learn about challenges and setbacks, explanations of events, and resilience-related outcomes, the participants provided contact information and introductions to internal COVA and external individuals to interview, which resulted in eight semi-structured interviews (Schultze & Avital, 2011) (See Table 5). Finally, we utilized data provided by COVA to provide rich examples throughout the manuscript and analyzed archival datasets to corroborate our findings from interviews and active discussions.

## Data Analysis

Our data analysis comprised a four-stage, iterative process to systematically trace the complex, case-specific mechanisms that produced resilience for COVA (See Table 6). Process tracing is an explanation-building approach primarily used in political science research (Yin, 2018) and is particularly wellsuited for assessing which mechanistic explanation accounts for an outcome (Beach & Pedersen, 2013; Bennett & Checkel, 2014). Process tracing studies seek to explain a particularly interesting and puzzling outcome—in our case, how COVA built digital resilience. Beach and Pedersen (2013, p. 18) view process tracing as “a single-outcome study, defined as seeking the causes of a specific outcome in a single case.”

To trace COVA’s journey to digital resilience (Louis & Bartunek, 1992), the insider researcher and his team constructed a chronology of steps, including previous attempts at data sharing by state agencies and learning from each initiative, resulting in a roadmap for the authors to identify where information resided. Although our focus was rooted in the period surrounding two shocks, we included efforts as early as 2006 to trace the groundwork that COVA put into place to provide contextual clarity. We searched for, selected, and appraised supporting documents and corrected the chronology when necessary (Bowen, 2009). We gathered and organized the documents and then stored them in a database until we achieved data saturation, defined as the degree to which new data repeats what has been expressed in previous data (Saunders et al., 2018).

We systematically reviewed and read the documents and extracted evidence of the data routines and capabilities identified in Tables 1 and 2. We analyzed interviews to add contextual clarity and offer deeper insights into failed attempts to create centralized data repositories, data sharing agreements, and COVA’s lessons applied to subsequent attempts. We coded all interviews and identified key themes and concepts using a cross-platform application for analyzing qualitative data, Dedoose Version 9.0.42 (2021). New concepts were created where necessary, resulting in 19 codes, 27 subcodes, and 93 excerpts. When necessary, we brought up questions during our active discussions with the CDO and his team to clarify ambiguity or request additional supporting documentation.

We cross-referenced data from discussions, documents, and interviews to identify linkages among routines, operational capabilities, and resilience activities, and uncovered three digital resilience capabilities during the two shocks (see Appendix B). ## Findings

The ability to anticipate and rebound from shocks demands that an organization possess a flexible, efficient way to access information and make sense of it as it flows through the event. Organizations that learn from an experience and reflect and plan for future shocks can succeed at building digital resilience. Starting in 2003, COVA undertook a series of legislative initiatives and projects to create a digital infrastructure to gather and analyze data from its multiple agencies while incrementally building operational capabilities to detect and interpret events in its environment. In doing so, COVA utilized its data infrastructure to confront a sudden increase in opioid use that became a crisis when reports emerged of increased numbers of deaths due to drug overdoses. Then, in 2020, COVA recombined its digital capabilities to respond to another shock—the global COVID-19 pandemic, henceforth COVID.

In this section, we describe how COVA integrated data shared by various state agencies and private organizations as it sought to rebound from the shock of a spike in lethal opioid overdoses. We examine how COVA reached a steady state in the opioid crisis, what it learned during the interlude, and how it reconfigured its operational capabilities and integrated external data to make crucial decisions in response to the COVID epidemic.

## Early Efforts to Integrate Data Sources

As early as 2003, COVA sought to identify what data and digital assets were available in the various state agencies and municipalities. The goal was to ensure the integrity, quality, security, and availability of digital assets to all qualified users.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, COVA leaders led efforts to streamline services for residents by integrating data. The COVA government asserted its oversight role and formulated a vision to build resilience so that it could respond robustly to unforeseen events or economic challenges. But they were unsure how to go about doing so. COVA agencies and local governments faced hurdles such as talent development to streamline their services. They also confronted the challenge of persuading agencies to share data because of their concerns about privacy and regulatory restrictions. Agency leaders were reluctant to coordinate with local governments and make decisions based on fragmented COVA data resources.

Early local data sharing success came from the Virginia Longitudinal Data System (VLDS), an initiative that linked disparate data across several COVA agencies. VLDS provided the capability to track students after they transitioned from public school systems to colleges and then into the workforce. In an early attempt to link data from disparate sources using entity resolution, COVA was able to measure the effectiveness of services, create new curricula, and promote its skilled workforce to employers. However, this data sharing was piecemeal and required agencies to create new sharing agreements for each use case, resulting in barriers to broader data sharing. Therefore, in 2009, educational agencies worked with the Office of the Attorney General to develop a model data-sharing agreement with oversight by a central coordinating body known as the Data Governance Council.

Building upon the model data-sharing agreement, COVA secretaries of Technology and Health and Human Resources formed the Secretarial Committee on Data Sharing (SCDS) (See Document 2). They issued an executive-level directive to establish a trust-agreement framework to support enterprise-wide data sharing, which established a governance committee comprising executive staff, data owners and stewards, business and technical leaders, legal and cybersecurity staff, and other representatives from COVA agencies tasked with overcoming the legal, regulatory, and policy hurdles to data sharing.

Meanwhile, a new federal health insurance law mandated that states must determine the eligibility of residents for Medicaid<sup>5</sup> benefits. COVA implemented a data-driven approach to managing the identities of residents, including compiling the names, addresses, and lists of services availed, to create a capability for entity resolution. However, many agencies resisted the data sharing that was needed for entity resolution because each agency interpreted its obligation and authority to share data differently. Although all agencies are a part of the COVA government, each is governed by different laws:

…one agency that freely trusts the process and provides the data and agrees to provide the data where maybe another agency says, you know, I could provide the data, but, but since this certain key data element exists in what you are asking for, you know, we have more strict guidelines than this other agency on the safekeeping of that data element, no matter where it goes. – INT3 (see Table 5)

Such resistance to data sharing was not without merit. Federal laws and privacy regulations restrict or prohibit data sharing or repurposing under the penalty of a fine or jail, as discussed below:

So literally the GDCDPA [Government Data Collection and Dissemination Practices Act] the Virginia government was, you know, governed by this you know 1976 Act, the government delays data and dissemination practices act, which had very little changes until 2001 where essentially, you know, agencies couldn’t share their data unless it was for the intent purpose that it was defined initially for the data collection. – INT4 (see Table 5)

Although initiatives to create a single data source accessible by COVA and its agencies were repeatedly impeded, each attempt created new datasets, incremental technology infrastructure components, and accumulated learning:

And so, we have a lot of the components in place, but the actual initiate, the program that, the application that, that [redacted] mentioned that has gone away, but there’s a lot of the pieces are still there. – INT4 (see Table 5)

Overall, such initiatives successfully built data sharing capabilities at the local level. COVA government’s capability to deploy data, technology, and analytics was tested when, in 2014, COVA faced a shock—the opioid and drug addiction epidemic, which we refer to as the opioid crisis.

## The Opioid Crisis

COVA was confronted with a dangerous prescription opioid and heroin abuse epidemic. Data sharing capabilities were generally local among agencies; COVA needed to expand data sharing so that it could detect and interpret data coming in from various parts of the state. The governor issued an executive directive that included the IT risk management (ITRM) framework and a memorandum of understanding that would establish a consistent methodology for sharing data between agencies with guidelines, policies, and technical standards. However, differences in the interpretation of the ITRM policies and guidelines led to inconsistent data reporting because agencies sometimes withheld data that they considered sensitive. Data-related discussions often evolved into an impasse: data owners would ask: “What data do you need?” and COVA officials would counter with: “What data do you have?”—resulting in inconsistent data sharing, causing reporting delays, as exemplified in the following quote:

We would have to go to each individual agency, DSS [Department of Social Services], even at a state level, we’d have to go to DSS, you know, the task force, law enforcement and asked for this data and it would take months to get this data together. – INT8 (see Table 5)

COVA’s governor issued another executive directive to catalog available data and established standards for data sharing and analytics. However, no single entity oversaw legal, regulatory, and trust-related issues. COVA recognized that it needed a single, neutral, trusted entity to establish relationships among data owners, coordinate agreements, and provide secure data storage.

In March 2018, COVA passed legislation to amend the Government Data Collection and Dissemination Practices Act and created a chief data officer<sup>6</sup> (CDO) position. The legislation also created a Data Sharing and Analytics Advisory Committee to propose guidelines and best practices, stating that it

…directs the CDO and the Advisory Committee to focus their initial efforts on developing a project for the sharing, analysis, and dissemination at a state, regional, and local level of data related to substance abuse, with a focus on opioid addiction, abuse, and overdose. – SB580, § 2.2-3803<sup>7</sup>

COVA’s Department of Criminal Justice Services partnered with the Winchester region’s Northern Shenandoah Valley Substance Abuse Coalition (NSVSAC) and The Office of the CDO to facilitate a regional pilot project to test the efficacy of opioid mitigation efforts at the community level, as agreed upon in the memorandum of understanding. The memorandum of understanding covered data sharing between two entities in which each agency entered into an agreement on what data could be shared and for what purpose. Any changes to data fields or their use required parties to renegotiate the agreement, which delayed data sharing. Further, the ITRM framework encouraged data sharing but was limited in its ability to rally agency leaders to agree upon a common set of data standards or even a structure for what data were needed by other agencies and what data could be shared. Data that contained personally identifiable information, such as medical records or bank account numbers, were required to be protected in compliance with privacy laws. As a result, agencies were hesitant to share data containing personally identifiable information. These data sharing constraints were not conducive to creating the capabilities needed to anticipate and rebound should another crisis escalate into a shock in the future.

To respond to the opioid crisis, the CDO’s office oversaw the Framework for Addiction Analysis and Community Transformation (FAACT), a pilot implementation that enabled data sharing. FAACT was an interagency, cloud-based, datasharing and analytics platform containing integrated datasets provided by various state agencies such as social services, health, education, law enforcement, and forensics. Like previous data-sharing initiatives, FAACT encountered resistance from agencies to sharing sensitive data. The pilot team addressed these issues by implementing a trust-based data sharing model with the CDO as the trustee, developing data standards and offering analytical capabilities to provide insights to stakeholders involved in mitigating the opioid crisis. In addition to accruing the benefits of opioid prevention, treatment, and recovery for Winchester-region residents, FAACT established interagency data sharing agreements, integrated data from disparate sources, and incrementally advanced COVA data sharing capabilities.

The increase in overdoses from January through March was primarily due to fentanyl-based overdoses. 100% of the fatalities were due to fentanyl-based overdoses in January and February. After the press release and law enforcement involvement, there were no fatal overdoses in April. Although there were 19 fatal overdoses from May through September, only four involved fentanyl and the percentage of fatal overdoses averaged 15%.

## Learning During the Interlude

Although opioid addiction and drug abuse continue to be major problems, by 2019, the opioid crisis had been contained. COVA had gained experience in determining the capabilities needed to be resilient in the long term. During the interlude, COVA administrators reflected upon the response and its effectiveness, notably whether data sharing resulted in effective interventions and how data sharing among agencies helped mitigate the opioid crisis. The objective was to assess weaknesses, find gaps in the response, and eliminate the failure points.

An advisory committee comprising CDO and COVA administrators met with agency leaders to learn about their data sharing practices, study the successes and failures of past data sharing initiatives, and assess COVA’s digital resilience capabilities in the event of future shocks. They determined that there was still no consistent statewide data sharing methodology and that the participation in data governance was ad hoc. To develop a data-driven culture, COVA needed to establish a governance structure, recruit talented individuals to build a data driven infrastructure with analytic capabilities, and share expertise with agencies so that they could address health, safety, and economic challenges.

## Applying the Learning: Workforce Referral Portal and FAACT

Building upon the advisory committee’s learning, in December 2018 the chief workforce advisor and the CDO integrated data from several agencies to build the Workforce Referral Portal, a cloud-based online platform to connect COVA residents with government-sponsored training, certification, and employment services. The Workforce Referral Portal was developed to serve as a resource for residents seeking employment, particularly during crises such as hurricanes or economic downturns, when people tend to be displaced. With a single interaction in the portal, residents were able to select government employment and training services, document obstacles to employment, and set up accounts for six employment-related COVA services. The portal’s workflow system notified service providers when an account was created. Building on the VLDS, COVA agencies partnered with community colleges to gather data and match eligible candidates with available training and employment services.

Although FAACT had shown promise in combating addiction and drug abuse, COVA leaders were concerned about the ongoing opioid crisis spreading into other communities and the nation. The learning from the Winchester region’s rebound from the opioid crisis indicated that more proactive outreach was necessary. Community leaders needed actionable intelligence—for example, from data sources able to detect an oncoming shock due to an oversupply of synthetic opioids— that would allow them to then assemble resources to prevent overdose deaths, interrupt the drug supply, and provide treatment.

COVA expanded FAACT to the Roanoke Valley, a southwestern region of the state, where opioid-related deaths had quadrupled between 2016 and 2017. As Nancy Hans, cochair of the Roanoke Valley Collective Response stated:

Access to the FAACT system will allow our region to share information and coordinate our response … This will enhance our abilities and understanding of what is happening in our community and enable us to better address the issues we’re seeing. (Document 19)

FAACT was no longer a pilot. Its expansion required COVA to build a secure, flexible infrastructure to integrate new data. COVA’s newly created Commonwealth Data Trust (CDT) platform served as the underlying technology infrastructure for extending FAACT and the Workforce Referral Portal. The CDT is a secure, interoperable, and scalable IT ecosystem where state agencies and private entities share and access data. Standard agreements ensured proper data use among participating parties to overcome the legal and regulatory concerns that were identified during the opioid crisis. For example, when additional data were needed, negotiations among agencies to amend agreements caused delays that could be catastrophic during a crisis. To overcome delays, COVA instituted a digital process involving explicit provisions for amendments or the addition of new clauses to an existing agreement. This capability overcame a previous condition that required an explicit description of data use. Changes to the agreement in the digital process could be enacted and electronically signed off on, often within one day.

Self-service data provisioning in the CDT gave data owners control over what data to share. COVA agencies documented the inventory of data sources in an online data catalog. Further, data were deidentified to ensure privacy and security when two or more datasets were joined. The CDT digital assets provided a more straightforward online process to request new data and faster online approval by data owners. Together, these steps reassured agencies of proper data use and engendered trust among them. Digital capabilities, combined with trust among agencies, resulted in the sharing of data assets, which created new opportunities for early anticipation and more effective response capabilities. Rapid modifications in data sharing agreements, a documented inventory of data sources, and the deidentification of sensitive data became routines that could be modified and recombined when responding to future shocks. Indeed, it would not be long before COVA was tested with the next shock—the COVID epidemic.

## The COVID Epidemic

When COVID cases were detected in the USA in January 2020, most Americans were unaware of how contagious or virulent COVID would be. It became a pandemic very quickly. Scientists warned of a potential pandemic contagion; however, the nation’s political establishment was divided on COVID’s intensity, veracity, and longevity, causing confusion among the public and government agencies. COVA leaders were unsure whether COVID would become a shock, and if so, how to prepare for it. On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared COVID a global pandemic, and the U.S. president declared a national emergency on March 13, 2020. Many U.S. states reported high COVID-related hospitalizations, deaths, and shortages of ventilators and personal protective equipment (PPE). They feared that hospitals would run out of intensive care unit (ICU) beds and rushed to construct makeshift facilities in preparation for a surge in COVID patients.

Resilience during COVID: To assess the intensity of the COVID shock and the needed facilities required that COVA combine data from various agencies and track the extent and location of the COVID spread. It required accurate data for hospitalization rates, available ICU capacity in private and public hospitals, supply of PPE and ventilators, COVID testing capacity in pathology laboratories, and demand for rapid test kits. COVA’s governor announced the expansion of FAACT to ensure an adequate supply of resources that were needed to rebound from the shock.

The digital resilience developed during the opioid crisis and strengthened during the interlude paid off during the COVID response despite having to onboard new agencies and the need to share different types of data. The learning from FAACT lowered data sharing barriers among state agencies. However, the COVID response required data sharing with external entities, i.e., private organizations that protect patient data for strategic reasons. A unified COVID response would require COVA to collaborate with hospitals, diagnostic laboratories, PPE suppliers, etc.

Meanwhile, the CDO’s office engaged the platform vendor that had previously built the digital infrastructure for the opioid dashboard (FAACT) to build a dashboard that extracted data from COVA’s internal digital infrastructure and integrated external data from hospitals and pathology laboratories (see Vignette 2). COVA’s governor and the secretary of Health and Human Resources approached the CEO of the Virginia Hospital and Healthcare Association (VHHA), a private organization. They asked VHHA to share data on the number of total beds, ICU beds, and ventilators, and how many were being utilized across COVA hospitals. Although the VHHA had already collected this information so that hospitals, health systems, and healthcare facilities could prepare for disasters such as Ebola and hurricanes, they hesitated to share the data with COVA, as reflected in the following quote:

It was a fine line that we had to walk and say this can [only] be used internally. We can’t have it posted publicly or media can have access to this because I know hospitals aren’t going to give us this information, and we know that we need them to. – INT1 (see Table 5)

Previously built data sharing agreement routines enabled capabilities for proper data use, and VHHA and COVA quickly reached an agreement. The CDO’s office was then charged with building the analytics necessary to support datadriven decisions in response to COVID. The goal was to build business intelligence tools and dashboards that required minimal training for COVA decision makers who would need to drill down into data bundles, coordinate activities, and take quick action.

The dashboard displayed the intensity of the COVID spread, but the governor and the leadership team needed actionable intelligence to support efforts to rebound from the COVID shock. The intelligence from data analytics became actionable when managers identified where COVID cases trailed the available hospital capacity. With this information, managers could determine which equipment was not needed and which locations did not require certain equipment and supplies. The ability to generate such intelligence from data enabled the targeted deployment of human and technological resources to where they were needed instead of positioning them throughout the state. The learning during the interlude that created the capabilities and routines enabled the intelligence capability discussed above to be built only eight days after COVA’s governor charged the CDO’s office for a plan to respond to COVID.

Although the dashboard met the objective to provide data regarding hospital beds, ICU patients, and the amount of COVID testing, it generated new demands for data. Health district directors wanted to monitor where in their district the testing was being done so that they could achieve the minimum testing requirement of 2% of the population per month and then increase testing to 4% of the population per month. Other state agencies and health districts extracted value from COVA data and analytics capabilities to make data-driven decisions and strengthen operational capabilities, such as managing their testing operations and quickly integrating data from long-term healthcare facilities where people were living in close proximity.

COVA detected that the COVID spread was faster in the southwestern and northern regions of the state and focused on mitigation efforts in those areas. Data analytics generated insights into where weekly positivity rates were higher in order to increase testing capacity in those places. When COVID positivity rates increased in communal living facilities, such as long-term patient care facilities and retirement homes, the CDT data sharing agreement was amended. Data from communal living facilities began to flow onto the COVID dashboards. The routine to make digital amendments to agreements, developed during the interlude, and the technology infrastructure tested with FAACT enabled COVA to share data and respond rapidly to the COVID spread.

## April 7, 2020

On March 12, 2020, the COVA governor declared a state of emergency and imposed restrictions on businesses, educational institutions, and public gatherings. COVA had to plan for an indeterminable surge in COVID cases. The governor summoned the Virginia Emergency Support Team Unified Command, the secretary of Health and Human Resources (HHR), and the secretary of Public Safety and Homeland Security. He needed to know the rate of infection, the location(s) of the surge in COVID cases, and the capacity of healthcare organizations to handle new cases. Hospitals in other states were setting up temporary triage and COVID treatment facilities in parking lots to prepare for future demand. State and city governments were competing to secure contracts with ventilator manufacturers. With finite resources to counter the COVID shock, accurate and timely information was critical to determine how best to allocate and deploy public and private resources. A statewide shutdown would have caused an economic slowdown, resulting in lower tax revenues and thus depriving COVA of the muchneeded funding to procure resources and rebound from COVID.

For COVA, resilience meant surviving the shock by remaining in operation to support residents and businesses. Although other factors influence tax revenue, it serves as a proxy for the continuity of economic activity during a shock. ## The Impact of Data-Enabled Intelligence

Post-restriction data indicated that the COVID positivity rate for the affected Hampton Roads region and the state declined to manageable levels. COVA’s overall COVID positivity rate remained below the U.S. national average. Taxation researchers, who examined the economic activity during COVID-19, found that state government sales and income tax revenues declined because personal consumption expenditures, particularly for health care, restaurants, and lodging, declined during COVID (Angelov & Waldenström, 2021; Clemens & Veuger, 2020). We provide a detailed analysis of comparative tax revenues in Appendix A.

The use of data during COVID indicated that the CDT and governance were key drivers for bringing the various agencies together and quickly getting the services up and running. The groundwork for data provisioning and the drafting of flexible digital agreements during the interlude lent speed and agility to the creation of customized dashboards so that decision makers could act quickly to help COVA rebound from the shock. State governments cannot serve their residents if a shock paralyzes them. Furthermore, residents cannot make responsible decisions if they are not empowered with data, intelligence, and insights. To this end, COVA relaunched the Open Data Portal and made it easier for residents to access the data underlying the publicly available dashboards via APIs (application programming interfaces). The Open Data Portal now facilitates data sharing between agencies and is open to all COVA residents.

## Discussion and Theoretical Integration

The IS and business strategy literature emphasizes that organizational performance results from how organizations acquire, configure, and deploy resources to build capabilities. Capabilities become critical when organizations confront uncertain, turbulent environments. Organizations must reconfigure their operational capabilities during such conditions to build DCs and become resilient. Our contribution to the IS literature lies in defining digital resilience as a dynamic capability buttressed by three DCs—relationship building, intelligence creation, and value extraction.

We theorize how and why these three DCs were essential for building COVA’s digital resilience. COVA’s reconfiguration of digital capabilities, which was built upon data assets and analytics, helped the state rebound from the opioid and COVID shocks and offers insights into how a state government can build DCs. Our detailed description of COVA’s journey towards digital resilience demonstrates the central role that data played as the protein used to build COVA’s resilience muscle through its operational capabilities and routines that aggregated, analyzed, and disseminated actionable information where it was needed, when it was needed. This targeted and nuanced response was the result of DCs that created digital resilience.

## Relationship Building

The relationship building DC leveraged operational capabilities of data management, integration, and governance structure. Data management capabilities began during the opioid shock, when health officials shared data with law enforcement agencies and then expanded data sharing to other regions. Linking disparate datasets resulted from routines of data sharing among various agencies. Gittell (2002) explains that the repeated enactment of routines leads to greater relational coordination and interaction, which in turn enriches the quality of relationships among actors.

The governor of COVA issued executive orders to publish a statewide data asset inventory, which further advanced data integration capabilities. As agencies began to access data, COVA’s routines, which deidentified personally identifiable information and credentialed authorized users, ensured that data were secure and privacy was protected. This strengthened agencies’ operational capabilities to convert data sharing into a relationship building DC, particularly with external partners such as VHHA. Once data began flowing through the CDT, more agencies began to develop value-added applications.

Our findings reveal that data owners initially resisted data sharing with other agencies—for example, the tracking of students in VLDS, one of the earliest COVA data sharing initiatives—due to concerns about data privacy, legal restrictions, and the fear of misinterpretation of their data by others. These concerns persisted through the opioid crisis, resulting in prolonged negotiations between agencies, which hindered COVA’s anticipate capabilities. These concerns were not only a technical issue, they also required data owners and COVA administrators to seek ways to address security and privacy concerns and build trust relationships among the agencies. COVA overcame these concerns by designating the CDO’s office as an impartial entity responsible for gathering and storing data, which provided data security and ensured access by authorized participants only. Alleviating these concerns eventually enhanced COVA’s rebound capabilities.

Leveraging the routines to facilitate agreements between agencies, COVA devised a digitally mediated process for executing data sharing agreements. Automated scripts to modify agreements that had otherwise required several meetings, ensured expediency when data needs changed. Digitally mediated processes allowed agency leaders to share relevant data and hastened the response during shocks such as COVID (Claggett & Karahanna, 2018). Indecisiveness about which content data to share and how to interpret them can hinder data sharing agreements and delay actionable intelligence.

COVA’s electronic data sharing agreement was a purposeful modification of the previous routine of one-on-one agreements. It resulted from changes in the environment that required assurance and trust between agency administrators (Schilke et al., 2018, p. 392). Similarly, by enacting legislation, COVA indemnified data owners and encouraged data sharing.

As a result, COVA developed trust relationships through shared goals, shared knowledge, and mutual respect (Claggett & Karahanna, 2018). Our findings shed light on the mechanisms of how shared understanding among actors builds relationships. Although agency administrators shared common goals, they were concerned that other agencies might misinterpret data and take inappropriate actions. Argote and Ingram (2000) refer to this as a knowledge transfer problem that occurs because interactions involving people can transfer more readily within an agency than between agencies. When the recipient of data is unable to interpret the data, it gives rise to the concern of data misuse.

The relationship building DC was also reinforced by a governance structure in which an independent data commission evaluated data sharing proposals. The data commission reviewed the agency’s data needs and made recommendations to the legislators for enacting regulations and funding information systems that followed data ethics, security, and privacy. The oversight structure provided confidence to agencies that data were secure and would be used only for authorized purposes. The agreement was written to apply broadly, such that it could be suitable for public as well as private entities. Amendments to data sharing agreements involved a one-page addendum that could be electronically completed and digitally signed.

In summary, the relationship building DC leveraged operational capabilities to standardize data, link disparate datasets, and govern oversight by a data commission (italicized text as shown in Figure 1). Further, the relationship DC was built with routines to deidentify personally identifiable information, facilitate rapid modification in data agreements, and provide prompt access to customized and integrated datasets.

## Intelligence Creation

The intelligence creation DC leveraged the operational capabilities of integration, governance structure, and analytics. With the integration capabilities needed to extend, recombine, and analyze data, COVA deployed the analytics capability to create dashboards for decision makers to take evidence-based actions. The intelligence creation DC is particularly important for conserving resources during shocks. Sharma and Shanks (2011) propose that DCs provide a theoretical basis to explain how data analytics lead to performance gains in organizations. Data availability is a necessary but insufficient condition for performance improvement. Therefore, the ability to extract intelligence from data within an organization’s information assets, combined with external data, is critical for performance improvements. The ability to convert digital resources into actions, or conversion effectiveness, has long been discussed in the information systems literature (Rai et al., 1996; Weill, 1992) and is a necessary routine in order for organizations to rebound. COVA’s conversion effectiveness routine is evidenced by how it used data assets to build dashboards (see Figure 1), conduct annual datathons to identify new uses of data, and recruit talent to formalize integrated intelligence creation. By leveraging the governance structure capability of a secure data reporting infrastructure supported by the CDT, agencies were able to access and analyze data combined with other agencies’ data resources, providing the ability to target their response and build digital resilience to rebound from a shock. Cross-functional team members exchanged and interpreted data from new datasets, for example, by deriving new datasets from drug overdose data to identify when and where a harmful batch of drugs had been distributed. This sharpened the opioid response and enabled COVA’s digital resilience.

Intelligence creation DC enabled COVA to provide a targeted and nuanced response, rather than a one-size-fits-all solution. For example, COVA used digital and physical resources and analytics to reveal that it did not need to build additional treatment facilities because there was sufficient hospital bed capacity to meet the demand (see Vignette 2). COVA used the analytics capability of forecasting with predictive modeling to develop an infection “doubling time” algorithm in order to forecast which localities would become COVID hotspots. This led to restrictions targeted to the Hampton Roads region instead of statewide restrictions (as shown in Vignette 3).

## Value Extraction

The value extraction DC leveraged the operational capabilities of data management, governance structure, and analytics. Organizations extract value from managing their data through savings resulting from reduced costs, by cultivating additional sources of revenue, or by creating new services (Mohamed et al., 2020). Exchanging data with private organizations and integrating data from partners expand the intellectual base of decision makers to efficiently create operational capabilities (Najjar & Kettinger, 2013). Such expanded datasets uncover redundant technology and software licenses that result in lower costs. Value extraction also occurs when an organization converts data and analytics into products and services with an economic value (Alfaro et al., 2019), such as by selling data services to generate revenue. For example, when data analytic capabilities are used to locate trouble spots, managers can extract value by quickly sharing data assets with decision makers responsible for responding to the shock. Such targeted and nuanced approaches to using data are valuable for formulating rapid responses for emergency situations and can free frontline personnel from expending scarce resources to search for solutions. During the opioid crisis shock, COVA extracted value from FAACT data by improving the internal processes of sharing data with law enforcement. Law enforcement officers interrupted the drug supply into the region and extracted value through fewer future drug overdoses while also seeking new opportunities to prevent overdose deaths.

FAACT data that showed opioid users’ “age of first use” enabled community outreach services and created new services—for example, delivering free drug testing kits for parents of high-risk youth and teaching school counselors how to plan rehab interventions. Further, FAACT data exhibited the value extraction DC by guiding a series of actions that lowered the operational costs of COVA services. Further, by bartering data with other states, COVA agencies interrupted cross-border drug smuggling. FAACT data also generated revenue when NSVSAC documented evidence of drug overdose challenges in a grant application to CVS Health Foundation. NSVSAC was awarded a \$1 million grant to support its Law Enforcement Overdose Intervention Program.<sup>9</sup>

Below we briefly cite three more examples of how COVA extracted value from its data and analytics capabilities. First, the operational capability of integration to determine Medicaid patient eligibility by linking disparate datasets and matching individuals across agencies created value by matching employment data with social services. In the past, the Department of Health and other agencies had accessed this service for a fee from external consultants. Second, regarding the data management operational capabilities of credentialing and transparent access made available through the CDT, COVA charges a \$2,500 a year licensing fee to access a suite of data sources and analytics tools. A commercial wastewater company licensed the security enclave to integrate COVA’s COVID data with its wastewater data to forecast COVID spread. Finally, by centralizing the technology procurements governance structure, COVA lowered costs associated with software licenses, cloud storage contracts, and maintenance fees.

## The Mutually Reinforcing Nature of DCs

We found that the three DCs are mutually reinforcing in building digital resilience. We provide illustrative examples in Figure 1 (shown as lines with arrows) to show how mutually reinforcing operational capabilities recombine to build DCs. Relationship building between parties is necessary to promote data sharing, and data sharing and interpretation improve the quality of intelligence creation because integrated data offer richer insights and promote value extraction. Linking disparate datasets, combined with the authority to integrate data to build dashboards, supports collaboration among cross-functional team members. Through this process, integrated data can be accessed via dashboards that yield richer insights and thereby contribute to the creation of the data analytics DC. Similarly, intelligence creation capabilities create new services that strengthen value extraction, a DC that creates nuanced and targeted solutions during shocks. In COVA’s case, predictive modeling to identify residents who were “at-risk” for drug dependency or job loss prevented allocating scarce resources to the statewide population during the shock, lowering treatment costs, and possibly saving human lives. Bartering data with other states extracted value by interrupting illegal drug smuggling from other states.

Overall, the more the data in a digital platform is used by agency administrators, the greater the economic value. When more agencies and businesses join a platform, it makes the data more valuable for each user (Gregory et al., 2021). When more organizations participate, they contribute and utilize more data, further strengthening the relationships and creating higher-order DCs. This mutually reinforcing nature of the three DCs—relationship building, intelligence creation, and value extraction strengthens digital resilience.

## Implications for Practice

Our process tracing approach outlines how COVA rebounded from two shocks and contributes to our understanding of how state governments can build digital resilience. Governments have the legislative authority to rally public and private resources to address the health and safety of residents and promote trade and commerce. As such, public officials must first focus on acquiring data and digital assets and then seek to build capabilities that deliver efficiency and reinforce resilience. Governments must implement efficient digital processes to extract value from data through data integration and analytics capabilities. Together these capabilities create digital resilience that can help governments rebound from shocks. When government agencies are responsive to the service demands of residents and businesses, they build trust, generate more data, and create demand for more services, thus further strengthening partnerships and digital resilience.

Further, although governments are not profit-making entities, they must deliver services with a quality and responsiveness that is comparable to that of private enterprises (Gil-Garcia et al., 2018). High-quality services require data and analytical capabilities to provide a targeted and nuanced response rather than a one-size-fits-all approach; such services can help governments quickly rebound from shocks. Likewise, digital resilience is created not only through data and computing; rather, political leaders and legislators must also have a vision of how best to serve their residents. They must foster a culture of trust relationships among agencies and businesses and promote evidence-based decision-making. Like the findings in Malgonde et al., (2023) we show that digital resilience is a continuous journey or process. As such, pilot projects to address health or safety issues are a practical way to build a culture of data sharing and data-driven decision-making that supports statewide digital resilience. Our findings suggest that state governments are likely to succeed in building digital resilience when they remove barriers to data sharing and enable local entities to utilize data assets to solve problems. COVA failed to build statewide digital resilience despite repeated attempts to share data until the Winchester region developed FAACT, tested a pilot, and expanded data sharing through the CDT. This journey involving data sharing was the protein that resulted in building the digital resilience muscle during the COVID shock.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study and its findings are subject to several limitations. First, our informants are people associated with the COVA government, its agencies, and private businesses. Other than the experiences of interviewees who were also COVA residents, we do not report how residents view COVA’s digital resilience. Second, although we based our findings on public reports and published legislation, our data collection did not capture whether political jockeying helped or hindered digital resilience. Finally, although we corroborated the interview findings for accuracy, we relied upon the candor of informants to interpret the underlying reasons and the meaning of event outcomes. Although we identified patterns emerging from the interviews using the Dedoose application program, which helped us analyze the unstructured text, we acknowledge that interviewee comments may be constrained by the interviewees’ interpretation of events or their recall ability.

Future research could further expand our understanding of the activities during the interlude. Researchers might investigate whether organizations develop plans for post-shock learning and how organizations can prepare to use technology for a targeted response to shocks during interludes. Finally, it would be informative to examine whether our findings from this case study are applicable to nongovernmental organizations, multinational corporations, or commercial conglomerates. Future research could build on the anticipate, rebound, and learn capabilities framework by providing process-level metrics for the key constructs (see Tables 1 and 2). This line of research could benefit from a mixed methods approach to develop metrics for a construct that would capture organizational digital resilience.

Our research contributes to the understanding of the mechanisms and actors that create dynamic capabilities. We explored relationship building, intelligence creation, and value extraction through process tracing and interviews. Future studies could test the causality between the relationships and outcomes in controlled settings. We anticipate that our study findings will advance the development of metrics to calibrate operational and dynamic capabilities that create digital resilience.

## Comparative Tax Revenues during COVID

To seek empirical triangulation of the impact of the restrictions imposed in the Hampton Roads region, we analyzed how taxable sales (transactions subject to state sales tax) varied when the governor imposed targeted restrictions. We compared taxable sales in COVA with taxable sales in Virginia Beach, a city in the Hampton Roads region. We selected Virginia Beach because it is a popular tourist destination and the data showed a higher COVID spread there.

Our findings in Figure 1 demonstrate that overall COVA taxable sales declined by 3.08% in the fourth quarter of 2020 (compared to the previous quarter) but increased by 0.72% in the first quarter of 2021. Virginia Beach, in contrast, saw an overall decrease of 4.27% and 9.85%, respectively, in taxable sales. We further examined two categories that are most affected by the restrictions: hotels and restaurants. Among hotels, Virginia Beach saw a reduction of 41.47% in taxable sales during the affected periods of the fourth quarter of 2020 and 52.41% in the first quarter of 2021.
