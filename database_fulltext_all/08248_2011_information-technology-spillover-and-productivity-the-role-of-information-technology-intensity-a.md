Ab strac t: We study interindustry information technology (IT) spillover wherein IT investments made by supplier industries increase the productivity of downstream industries. Using data from U.S. manufacturing industries, we find that industries receive significant IT spillover benefits in terms of total factor productivity growth through economic transactions with their respective supplier industries. More importantly, we find that two characteristics of downstream industries, namely, IT intensity and competitiveness, which have been shown to moderate the effect of internal IT investments, play an important role in IT spillovers as well. Our results suggest that IT intensity as well as competitiveness of the downstream industry moderate the effect of IT spillovers—industries that are more IT intensive and more competitive benefit more from IT spillovers. Finally, our results suggest that the long-term effects of spillovers are greater than short-term effects, suggesting that learning periods are required to reap the benefits from the IT spillovers.

Key words and ph rases: competition, industry analysis, industry characteristics, IT effects, IT intensity, IT spillover, productivity, total factor productivity.

Ever sinc e information tech nology (IT) became widely recognized as an important input to production, the impact of IT on business performance and economic growth has been an important area of inquiry. Not surprisingly, researchers in information systems (IS) and economics have extensively examined and found evidence for the impact of IT on economic performance at various levels and using various methodologies [15, 28, 29, 48, 62, 65, 79, 81]. While the primary focus of the extant academic literature has been on the value of IT investments accruing to the investing parties (i.e., firms, industries, or even countries), studies suggest that IT may also create spillover benefits to noninvesting parties [13, 26, 77]. For instance, suppliers’ IT investments can increase the quality of their outputs, which are subsequently used by downstream industries as intermediate inputs in the form of new products/services or improvements to existing products/services in terms of convenience, timeliness, quality, and variety [17]. If suppliers are not able to appropriate full rents from the quality improvements (e.g., because of competition among suppliers), part of the benefits will flow to downstream industries, resulting in IT spillovers [20]. For example, industrial equipment manufacturers such as Honeywell can improve the quality of their products by investing in state-of-the-art computer-aided design and computer-integrated manufacturing systems. They can also invest in Internet-based customer-care systems to enhance the quality of customer service. However, they may not be able to charge prices that reflect the full extent of quality improvements of their products and services (e.g., because of competitive pressures from their rivals), so part of the benefits from the investments is spilled over to their downstream customers.

Another important source of IT spillover is new process innovations and organizational practices enabled by IT investments [17]. Knowledge about IT-enabled innovations and practices can be transferred to trading partners through interactions over time so that the trading partners can learn and replicate them [19]. For example, suppliers and buyers in a supply chain often invest in interorganizational systems such as a vendor managed inventory (VMI) system to support transaction processing and information sharing [87]. When the supplier implements a new IT-enabled organizational practice, the buyer can learn about the practice and implement it as well.

A growing body of research has recently started to explore such IT spillovers. For example, van der Wiel and van Leeuwen [86] found a significant impact of IT spillovers on productivity growth in services industries in the Netherlands. Cheng and Nault [20] found that industries receive substantial spillover benefits in terms of contributions to output growth from their supplier industries’ IT investments through intermediate purchases. These studies provide valuable insights by demonstrating the existence and estimating the overall magnitude of IT spillover effects. However, our understanding of IT spillover effects is still in its infancy. For example, do all industries benefit equally from spillover effects? If not, what are some characteristics of downstream industries that allow them to receive more (or less) IT spillover benefits from supplier industries?

In order to better understand the mechanisms by which IT spillovers occur, we build on and go beyond these prior works by asking under what conditions are the benefits from IT spillovers greater?

Using Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data for U.S. manufacturing industries, we examine the impact of IT spillovers on the productivity growth of downstream industries. Our unique contribution to the literature is identifying industry characteristics that influence the magnitude of benefits from IT spillovers and estimating their effects. More specifically, we examine the moderating effects of two characteristics of downstream industries, namely, IT intensity and competitiveness, as these two characteristics influence downstream industries’ ability and motivation to capture and appropriate IT spillover benefits. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to rigorously examine the role of downstream industry characteristics in IT spillovers. While prior IT productivity studies (e.g., [32, 60, 82]) found that these two industry characteristics affect the returns to internal IT investments, we do not know whether they also have an impact on the benefits transmitted from external IT investments (i.e., IT spillovers). Our study extends the implications of prior IT value research by providing valuable insights into how industry characteristics influence the returns to IT investments from a broader ecological perspective.

Our empirical analyses measure productivity growth using total factor productivity (TFP) in lieu of the commonly used output-based production function. We do so since firms can simply augment output by using more capital and labor inputs [16], which we believe is less relevant in the context of spillovers. In addition, the use of TFP enables us to identify the portion that is attributable to technical progress, which is associated with more effective utilization of resources in the production process [4]. Given that technical progress (i.e., increase in TFP) can occur as a result of technology embodied in intermediate inputs and through the diffusion of IT-enabled organizational practices, we expect that IT spillovers will capture a significant portion of variations in TFP.

Overall, our results complement prior work by demonstrating that industries receive significant spillover benefits in the form of TFP growth from IT investments of their respective supplier industries. More important, we extend prior work by finding that the two characteristics of downstream industries, namely, IT intensity and competitiveness, play an important role in the appropriation of IT spillovers—industries that are more IT intensive and more competitive benefit more from IT spillovers. Furthermore, we find that the magnitude of IT spillover effects increases when we consider longer time horizons (i.e., long-run effects of IT spillover are greater than short-run effects), suggesting that learning periods and complementary investments are required to absorb the spillover benefits.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## IT Spillover and Productivity

Spillovers oc ur wh en th e b enefits from an investment are not fully appropriated by the investors but are spread to other noninvesting parties. Spillovers have traditionally been studied in the context of research and development (R&D) investments.

Pioneered by the work of Griliches and his colleagues [39, 40, 41, 43], the extent to which an industry’s R&D investments are spilled over to other industries and affect their productivity has long been investigated by economists. Studies have shown that R&D spillovers positively affect productivity at the industry level [36, 51] as well as at the country level [22, 52].

The R&D literature identifies two major channels through which spillovers occur [40, 41]. First, an industry’s R&D investments improve the quality of its goods and services, but if the prices of its intermediates do not fully reflect the quality improvements, the benefits of R&D are spilled over to the downstream industries. For instance, R&D studies show that an industry receives significant spillover benefits through the purchase of intermediate goods/services in which supplier industries’ R&D efforts are embedded [49, 51]. This is because supplier industries’ R&D investments improve the quality of their output by creating new products or improving existing products that are subsequently used as intermediate inputs by downstream partners. This type of spillover typically occurs in the presence of heightened competition within an industry—competitive pressures deflate the prices of intermediate inputs in such a way that they do not correctly reflect the extent of quality improvements resulting from the R&D investments [40, 41]. Second, since the end product of R&D is essentially intangible knowledge or information, which exhibits properties of nonrivalry and nonexcludability similar to public goods, spillovers occur because knowledge may be transferred (often unintentionally) to noninvesting parties [40, 41].

Similar to the process of R&D spillovers, an industry’s IT investments can improve the productivity of other industries, resulting in IT spillover.<sup>1</sup> First, the prices of the intermediate inputs often do not correctly reflect the full extent of quality improvement resulting from suppliers’ IT investments due to competition among suppliers. This leads to cost savings with respect to inputs and overall productivity gains for downstream partners [20]. Second, IT investments often involve organizational change and innovations in production processes [17]. In order to successfully implement IT projects, the investing firms must acquire or develop related knowledge and expertise. In such a case, the IT knowledge generated as a by-product of IT investments may spill over to downstream firms because of its nonrival properties [26]. As a result, productivity of downstream partners may increase because they can replicate the innovations and organizational change initiatives, which can help improve the efficiency of production and resource utilization.

Prior research in IS and economics has documented the impact of IT spillover on productivity at both the industry and country levels. At the industry level, Mun and Nadiri [67] found a positive effect of interindustry IT spillovers on cost savings and productivity growth. Similarly, Cheng and Nault [20] found that industries receive significant spillover benefits from their supplier industries in terms of an increase in output. At the country level, Lee and Guo [53] found significant intercountry spillover effects of IT investments. Similarly, Park et al. [71] estimated the effect of international transfer of IT through international trade on national productivity and found that imported IT makes a significant contribution to national TFP growth. To confirm the findings of these studies, we propose the following baseline hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: IT spillovers have a positive effect on the productivity growth of downstream industries.

The above discussion focuses on why we should expect IT spillovers to occur, but it does not fully explain under what conditions such spillover effects are likely to be greater. In order to better develop a theory of IT spillovers, we look to the role of downstream industry characteristics.

## Role of Downstream Industry Characteristics

The influence of industry structure and characteristics on firms has been well established in the industrial organization and management literature. For example, Bain [3] argued that an industry’s concentration ratio has a direct effect on the performance of firms within that industry. Recently, scholars in IS and economics have started to recognize the importance of industry characteristics in shaping how firms acquire and apply IT [32, 61, 82].

Although some studies qualitatively suggest that certain industries can attain higher productivity gains and greater cost reduction from IT (e.g., [56]), only a handful of studies have directly examined the effect of industry characteristics on IT business value [21, 61]. Among the various industry characteristics, IT intensity has received most attention as an important industry characteristic that affects the way IT is used and creates value [45, 66, 82]. Another industry characteristic that has been recognized as a major factor shaping the way IT is used and creates value is industry competitiveness [61]. While prior studies point to the importance of IT intensity and competition in explaining the differential productivity impact of industries’ own IT investments, to the best of our knowledge, no research to date has examined the role of these two factors in capturing the value from others’ IT investments—that is, IT spillover. We build on and extend these studies by examining whether IT intensity and industry competitiveness also matter in capturing value from IT spillover. IT intensity influences how able downstream industries are at understanding, absorbing, and utilizing the IT knowledge from upstream industries, whereas industry competitiveness determines how motivated the downstream industries are in extracting the spillover benefits.

## Industry IT Intensity

Prior studies on IT and productivity have used the notion of IT intensity to indicate industries’ different propensities to invest in IT (e.g., [66, 82]). Studies have shown that there is significant variation in IT intensity across industries, and that the level of IT intensity can affect industries’ economic performance. For example, Dumagan et al. [32] found that industries that are more IT intensive have experienced higher output growth and productivity growth than less-IT-intensive industries. Similarly, Stiroh [82] found that industries that are either producers or heavy users of IT (i.e., IT-intensive industries) made a significantly greater contribution to productivity acceleration in the United States in the late 1990s compared to the rest of the economy.

These studies point to the importance of IT intensity in explaining the differential productivity impact of own IT investments.

We argue that the level of IT intensity of downstream industries can also influence how much they can benefit from IT spillovers from suppliers. This is because the level of IT intensity of an industry is directly related to its IT capabilities, an industry’s ability to combine IT resources in ways that promote superior performance [8, 90]. Intensive IT investments can also foster organizational learning [2] and increase the productivity of other inputs [64]. By investing heavily in various types of IT, firms in IT-intensive industries can develop greater IT capabilities over time compared to those in less-IT-intensive industries [7, 64]. A related concept in the strategy and management literature is absorptive capacity, which refers to an organization’s ability to evaluate and utilize external knowledge [24]. Cohen and Levinthal [24] argued that an organization’s prior related knowledge is an important source of its absorptive capacity. When applied to the context of IT, this means that prior IT knowledge accumulated through intensive IT investments can help firms develop IT-related absorptive capacity [11]. With superior IT-related absorptive capacity, firms with higher levels of IT intensity can better exploit and benefit from the IT-related knowledge transferred from their suppliers. In a similar vein, Han et al. [45] found that industries with higher levels of IT intensity benefit more from IT outsourcing because they are better equipped with IT capabilities that facilitate the flow of IT services from the IT outsourcing industries.

In sum, industries exhibiting a higher level of IT intensity are more likely to have developed superior IT capabilities and IT-related absorptive capacity. As a result, such industries are better able to understand and utilize IT knowledge spilled over from supplier industries. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: IT spillover effects are greater to downstream industries that exhibit higher IT intensity.

## Industry Competitiveness

Industrial organization scholars have long examined the relationship between industry competitiveness and firm or industry efficiency and productivity [84] and have found that competitive pressures force firms to invest in innovations, leading to industrywide efficiency gains [9, 68]. One of the major theoretical perspectives used in this literature is the x‑efficiency theory [54]. Leibenstein [54] observed that social loss from the failure to attain the maximum output implied by economic theory is substantial even after accounting for inefficiency attributable to misallocation, which he called x‑inefficiency. He argued that the absence of competition would raise a firm’s costs above the technologically minimum levels since this discourages workers from making their best efforts. According to the theory, competition can lead to a reduction in slack and other inefficiencies such as opportunistic behavior [54, 74]. Efficiency gains occur because (1) competition forces managers to exert greater effort toward cost reduction; (2) competition renders comparison of the managers’ performance easier (as there are typically more players in a more competitive market), which sharpens managers incentives to outperform others; and (3) competition makes profits more sensitive to the efforts of managers, and since managerial compensation is closely tied to profits, managers have the incentive to increase efficiency [46, 68]. As a result, firms in more competitive industries are typically more efficient in their operations.

Empirical evidence of the x-efficiency theory can be found in the literature. Using data from UK manufacturing companies, Nickell [68] found that competition is associated with a significantly higher rate of TFP growth. Based on data from 19 UK manufacturing sectors in the 1970s and 1980s, Hay and Liu [46] found that in a more competitive environment, firms have a stronger incentive to improve their efficiency performance and investments. Examining the U.S. telephone industry for the 1951–90 period, Oum and Zhang [70] found that the introduction of competition in the long-distance telephone services market increased the allocative efficiency of the incumbent carriers by inducing more efficient use of capital. In a related study, Gort and Sung [35] found that competition in the telecommunications industry enabled incumbents to better utilize technology and existing capacity. Similarly, using industrylevel productivity data from the United States, Japan, and Germany, Börsch-Supan and Romer [10] found that the nature of competition facing companies in the market strongly influenced the efficiency with which capital is utilized. In the context of IT, Melville et al. [60] found that the productivity effect of IT capital is greater in more competitive industries.

In sum, the x-efficiency theory suggests that competitive pressures motivate firms to seek management initiatives to bolster operational efficiencies. In the context of IT practices, management initiatives to bolster operational efficiencies may involve operational refinements with respect to the firm’s existing IT (in which case competition would moderate the productivity effects of an industry’s own IT investments), or the introduction of novel IT-enabled processes (in which case competition would moderate the benefits from others’ IT investments, or IT spillovers). Whether firms seek to refine existing IT practices or seek to experiment with novel and innovative ones depends on the firms’ search distance. The notion of search distance relates to firms adaptation strategies of exploitation versus exploration [58]—the extent to which a firm will seek exploitative solutions by reconfiguring existing (i.e., local) knowledge or seek explorative solutions by combining far-flung (i.e., distant) knowledge elements. According to the behavioral theory of the firm [25], firms engage in problemistic search (i.e., firms search for solutions in response to organizational problems), and the intensity and distance of search is directly proportional to the firm’s assessment of the severity of the problem situation. In other words, firms facing more adverse situations are likely to search more intensively from more distant sources [57]. However, distant search does not imply that the target of search will be random. Firms engaging in distant search will seek solutions from the practices of competitors or business partners (e.g., customers and suppliers) rather than from disconnected random firms or industries [76]. Prior research has shown that imitation of practices is more likely with longer and more arduous relationships between firms [83, 88].

When we combine insights from the x-efficiency theory and the behavioral theory of the firm to the context of IT spillovers, we may posit that competitive pressures create adversity, which in turn motivates firms in downstream industries to not only seek to improve efficiency and productivity of their own IT (i.e., exploitation with local search) but also to utilize knowledge about IT practices from trading partners in upstream industries (i.e., exploration with distant search). Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: IT spillover effects are greater to downstream industries that are more competitive.

## A Total Factor Productivity Framework

As mentioned earlier, we use TFP as our dependent variable because it enables us to isolate the portion of output growth attributable to technical progress, and we believe that unmeasured quality improvements of intermediate inputs and IT-enabled organizational practices stemming from suppliers’ IT investments contribute to technical progress of downstream industries. To estimate the impact of IT spillover on TFP, we start with a standard Cobb–Douglas production function that has been extensively used in the IS literature [18, 20, 30]:<sup>2</sup>

$$
Y = A K ^ {\alpha} L ^ {\beta} M ^ {\delta},\tag{1}
$$

where Y is the industry output, and K, L, and M are physical capital (both regular and IT capital) and labor and intermediate inputs, respectively. α, β, and δ are output elasticities. A is a technology parameter that indicates the level of production technology of an industry.

The IS literature has stressed the secondary role of IT beyond its contribution to production as a conventional input [17, 18]. For instance, Brynjolfsson and Hitt [18] showed that the returns to IT investments are larger than their factor share. The divergence between estimated IT returns and factor share implies that IT may shift the production frontier by enabling new business processes and enhancing the level of production technology [80]. Similarly, Mittal and Nault [66] modeled and estimated the direct and indirect effects of IT. They found that IT not only contributes to output growth but also makes other inputs (i.e., regular capital and labor) more productive. Accordingly, we assume that an industry’s own IT capital (Z) contributes to technology (A) as well as output (Y). Also, based on our earlier discussion, we include IT spillover (SO) as a factor contributing to the technical progress of an industry. Therefore, we model A as a function of Z and SO as follows:

$$
A = C e ^ {F (Z, S O)},\tag{2}
$$

where C is a constant.

TFP is defined as

$$
T F P \equiv \frac {Y}{K ^ {\alpha} L ^ {\beta} M ^ {\delta}}.\tag{3}
$$

Substituting Equations (1) and (2) into Equation (3) and taking a natural log on both sides yields

$$
\ln (T F P) = \ln (C) + F (Z, S O).\tag{4}
$$

Following related studies on IT productivity and IT spillover (e.g., [18, 71]), we specify the function F(Z, SO) as the following additive form:

$$
\ln (T F P) = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \ln (Z) + a _ {2} \ln (S O).\tag{5}
$$

As is common in productivity studies based on time series or panel data, we take the time differences of variables in logarithms to yield growth rates. Similar differencing has been used by Bartelsman et al. [5] and Brynjolfsson and Hitt [18]. Such differencing helps control for unobserved heterogeneity across industries by removing time-invariant industry-specific effects [89].<sup>3</sup> Moreover, we examine various difference lengths, ranging from one to five years, to examine both short-run and long-run effects of IT spillover.

There are two reasons why we consider longer differences, in addition to the standard one-year difference, in our estimation. First, as Brynjolfsson and Hitt put it,

If input variables are measured without error, and factor adjustment to price and other exogenous changes is instantaneous, then the short- and long-difference estimates should be identical. However, as noted by Bartelsman, Caballero, and Lyons (1994) [5], when adjustment is not instantaneous, longer differences can be interpreted as long-run effects of factor input changes. Such changes include not only the direct effect of factor inputs, but also the effects of adjustment of complementary factors. [18, pp. 795–796, emphasis added]

Benefiting from IT spillover may require a time-consuming process of absorbing/ applying spilled-over knowledge and making necessary complementary investments (e.g., organizational change initiatives) on the part of the downstream firms. Using varying difference lengths in the estimation thus allows us to capture IT spillover benefits materializing over time. Second, Griliches and Hausman [42] suggest that when there are errors in measuring the inputs, estimates based on longer differences will be less biased than those based on shorter differences.

## Data and Methods

## Data and Variables

Our data c ome from two sourc es: the Multifactor Productivity (MFP) data set for three-digit SIC (Standard Industrial Classification) code manufacturing industries from 1987 to 1999 and the input-output tables from 1983 to 1999, both of which are provided by the BLS. By matching the two data sets, we end up with data from 1987 to 1999.<sup>4</sup> Annual TFP growth is calculated using the BLS’s historical MFP data for the SIC manufacturing industries. All the data have been converted to 1987 U.S. dollars using the implicit price deflator provided by the BLS. As Cheng and Nault, who have used the same data set, note, most of the manufacturing industries in our data set “purchase more than half of their intermediate inputs from other manufacturing industries in at least one of our sample years” [20, p. 1206]. Thus, we believe that our analysis captures a large proportion of the interindustry transactions in the economy.

The data from the BLS consist of a detailed breakdown of all the assets for each three-digit SIC code manufacturing industry in 1987 U.S. dollars. There are 30 asset types, and we aggregated the productive stock of the following five asset types to operationalize IT capital (Z): computers and related equipment, office equipment, communication, instruments, and photocopy and related equipment.

To construct the IT spillover variable (SO), we utilize the Use tables of the industry input-output account. The Use table contains the value of output produced by one industry that is purchased by another industry as an intermediate input for each pair of industries. Consistent with Bartelsman et al. [5] and Coe and Helpman [22], we use the following measure for the total IT spillover to industry $i ( S O _ { i } )$ through intermediate input purchase:

$$
S O _ {i} = \sum_ {j \neq i} \frac {i n t _ {i j}}{\sum_ {j \neq i} i n t _ {i j}} Z _ {j},
$$

where $i n t _ { _ { i j } }$ is the value of downstream industry $i \ ' _ { \mathrm { { S } } }$ intermediate input purchased from upstream industry $j ,$ and $Z _ { _ j }$ is industry j ’s IT capital.<sup>5</sup> In other words, an industry’s IT spillovers are measured as the intermediate-input-weighted sum of the IT capital of its supplier industries. Note that with this operationalization, the amount of IT spillover from industry j to industry i is proportional to industry $j ^ { \prime } { \bf s }$ share of the total intermediate inputs of industry i as well as industry j ’s IT capital stock. Put differently, the more industry i purchases from industry j and the more industry j invests in IT, the greater the spillover from industry j to industry i will be.<sup>6</sup> In an extreme case, industry i will not receive any spillover benefits from industry j when industry i does not purchase any intermediate inputs or industry j does not invest in IT.

IT intensity (ITINT ) is operationalized as the ratio of IT capital to industry output.<sup>7</sup> Because of the high correlation between this measure and IT capital (Z), we create an indicator variable by classifying industries into groups of high and low IT intensity based on a median split of the mean IT intensity values over the sample period— ITINT = 1 for the group high in IT intensity and 0 for the group low in IT intensity.

The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) is used as an inverse proxy to operationalize industry competition.<sup>8</sup> HHI is a measure of industry concentration and is defined as

$$
H H I _ {i} = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {i}} s _ {k i} ^ {2},
$$

where $s _ { k i }$ is the market share of firm k and $n _ { _ i }$ is the number of firms in industry i [63]. Higher values of HHI imply that the industry is more concentrated and, hence, less competitive. We calculated the HHI for each three-digit SIC industry in our data set for each year using data from Compustat.<sup>9</sup> Summary statistics and correlations are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

## Estimation Model

To estimate the moderating effects of industry IT intensity and competitiveness, we include interaction terms $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \Delta \mathrm { l n } ( S O ) \times I T I N T \mathrm { a n d } \Delta \mathrm { l n } ( S O ) \times H H I )$ in the estimation equation. We also include year dummies $( Y r _ { t } )$ to control for time-specific economic shocks that may affect TFP across all industries. We tested for joint significance of the year dummies and could reject the null hypothesis that the dummies are jointly zero (for one-year difference: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 2 4 7 . 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . This yields the following specification of our empirical model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Delta^ {n} \ln (T F P) = c _ {0} + c _ {1} \Delta^ {n} \ln (Z) + c _ {2} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) + c _ {3} I T I N T + c _ {4} H H I \\ \quad + c _ {5} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) \times I T I N T + c _ {6} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) \times H H I + \sum_ {t} \alpha_ {t} Y r _ {t} + \varepsilon , \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where $\Delta ^ { n }$ denotes n-year difference. We estimated the models with 20 sector dummies (at the two-digit SIC level) but could not reject the null hypothesis that sector dummies are all zero $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 4 . 3 5 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . This is not surprising, because our difference-based specification removes industry-specific (hence, sector-specific) effects [89]. Thus, we do not include the sector dummies in our subsequent estimations. To alleviate the potential multicollinearity between the main variables and the interaction terms and facilitate the interpretation of the coefficients, we center the variables that constitute the interaction terms by converting their values to deviations from their respective mean [1].

## Econometric Issues

There are potential econometric issues of heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation because of the cross-sectional time-series nature of our data set. In other words, the variances of the error terms are likely to differ across industries and the error terms may be correlated across time periods. Results of the Breusch–Pagan test for heteroskedasticity [37] suggest that we should reject the null hypothesis that the errors are homoskedastic $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 , 9 8 0 . 5 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Furthermore, given that most major economic variables including output are autocorrelated at short lags [23], it is important to explicitly account for potential autocorrelation in the estimation. The Wooldridge test [89] for identifying the autocorrelation structure indicates the presence of a firstorder autocorrelation (AR 1) in our panel data set $( F = 1 9 . 2 5 p < 0 . 0 1 )$ .

In the presence of heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation, the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimators will no longer be efficient and the standard errors will be incorrect [37]. Accordingly, we use the feasible generalized least squares (FGL S) procedure, which applies the appropriate corrections for both autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity [72, 89]. Further, because we have no prior reason to believe that the AR 1 process is common across industries, and the likelihood ratio test suggests that we cannot reject the null hypothesis that the data follow a panel-specific AR 1 process $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 , 3 1 7 . 5 $ $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , we adjust for the panel-specific AR 1 (PSAR 1) process in our FGL S regressions by allowing the AR 1 coefficients to differ across industries.

Another potential issue is cross-sectional correlations, wherein error terms across industries in the same period are correlated. This can happen when all the industries are simultaneously affected by a common exogenous shock (e.g., an economic depression). We are unable to adjust for cross-sectional correlations in FGL S because this requires the number of time periods to be greater than the number of cross sections (industries) [89]. Although not a perfect remedy, we alleviate this issue by including year dummies (e.g., [38]).

## Results

## Main Effects of IT Spillover on TFP Growth

Th e results of estimating th e main (i.e., unconditional) effects of IT spillover on TFP growth are presented in Table 3 (Model 1 columns).<sup>10</sup> Although the impact of IT spillover, ∆ln(SO), is not statistically significant for one-year and two-year differences, it becomes significant for the three-year difference (0.046, p < 0.1) and increases in size for longer differences, reaching 0.072 $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ for the five-year difference. Thus, Hypothesis 1 is supported. These estimates suggest that the TFP increase associated with a 1 percent increase in IT spillover is 0.05 percent for three-year differences and 0.07 percent for the five-year differences.<sup>11</sup> Put differently, the average TFP increase will be 0.05 percent for the three-year difference when each supplier industry increases its IT capital by 1 percent, which is equivalent to a \$17.8 million increase in 1987 U.S. dollars (cf. the mean IT capital stock is \$1.78 billion). This suggests that IT spillover does contribute to the technical progress of downstream industries, although the benefits from IT spillovers are not immediate and it seems to take time for the spillover benefits to materialize. Our result also suggests that the long-term benefits from IT spillover are greater than the short-term benefits.

Several additional results are noteworthy as they confirm findings from prior studies on IT value. First, the impact of IT capital, ∆ln(Z ), on TFP growth is positive and significant under all difference lengths, and in general, becomes greater as the difference length increases. This result is consistent with Brynjolfsson and Hitt [18], who also show that long-term benefits from own IT investments are greater than short-term benefits. Second, our results of the negative coefficients of HHI and the positive coefficients of ITINT suggest that less concentrated (i.e., more competitive) and more-IT-intensive industries experience stronger TFP growth.<sup>12</sup>

## Moderating Effects of Downstream Industry Characteristics

To examine the moderating effects of the two characteristics of downstream industries on the productivity impact of IT spillover (SO), we estimated the model with the interaction terms associated with IT spillover. As can be seen in the Model 2 columns of Table 3, IT intensity was shown to moderate the effect of IT spillovers on TFP growth. The positive and significant coefficient of the interaction between IT spillover and IT intensity, $\Delta \ln ( S O ) \times I T I N T ,$ implies that industries with high levels of IT intensity enjoy significantly greater IT spillover benefits compared with their low-IT-intensive counterparts. Hence, Hypothesis 2 is supported. The additional TFP increase enjoyed by the high-IT-intensive industries associated with a 1 percent increase in IT spillover ranges from 0.09 percent to 0.17 percent, increasing in difference lengths. The negative and significant coefficient of the interaction term, ∆ln(SO) × HHI, in Model 2 implies that industries that are less concentrated, and therefore more competitive, benefit more from IT spillover. Hence, Hypothesis 3 is supported. Because HHI is a continuous variable, the coefficient of the interaction term can be interpreted as the change in the slope of TFP growth on IT spillover given a unit change in HHI. In other words, under the one-year difference, for an industry with an HHI lower by 0.1 (or 10 percent), the additional TFP increase associated with a 1 percent increase in IT spillover is 0.025 percent.

## Additional Considerations and Robustness Checks

One estimation issue that we must consider is the potential presence of time-invariant industry-level heterogeneity. For example, industries are likely to use different technologies and produce different types of outputs. If not controlled for, industry-level heterogeneity is included in the error term and is likely to be correlated with the independent variables, resulting in biased estimates [44, 55]. For instance, a technologically savvy industry may invest more aggressively in IT when TFP is growing at a faster rate. We were able to deal with this concern as follows.

First, note that our difference-based empirical specification captures a significant portion of unobserved industry-level heterogeneity by removing industry-specific effects [73]. This was confirmed by the fact that the sector dummies, which are also known to capture industry-level heterogeneity, are jointly insignificant when they are included in the estimations. Second, our estimation using FGL S with a panel-specific AR 1 adjustment allows individual industries to have different autocorrelation structures. As a result, the industry-specific (i.e., panel-specific) AR 1 coefficients in our models are also capturing part of the industry-level heterogeneity.

Another potential issue is the endogeneity of input factors. For example, it is possible that IT capital is simultaneously determined with output. Also, because IT spillover is proportional to the intermediate inputs an industry purchases, IT spillover and output may be simultaneously determined as well. This would be the case if firms increase (reduce) their IT capital and spending in intermediate inputs along with their output when they experience higher (lower) demand for their output. For example, it is possible that firms reduced spending on IT capital, intermediate inputs, and outputs simultaneously when the recession started in the early 1990s. In this case, the IT capital or IT spillover variable could be correlated with the error term and the coefficient estimates would be biased. We formally tested for endogeneity of the independent variables (i.e., differenced IT capital and IT spillover variables) to verify whether endogeneity is indeed an issue in our estimations. First, we applied two-stage least squares (2SLS) and the two-step generalized method of moments (GMM) procedures after controlling for serial correlation and heteroskadasticity. Here, we treated our differenced IT capital and IT spillover variables (∆ln(Z) and ∆ln(SO)) as endogenous and used one- and two-year lagged values of these two variables as instruments. Specifically, we estimated Model 1 for one- and three-year differences while treating ∆ln(Z) and $\Delta \mathrm { l n } ( S O )$ as endogenous both independently and jointly. In general, we obtained large first-stage $R ^ { 2 } \mathrm { s }$ and significant first-stage F-statistics $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which validate the adequacy of our lagged variables as instruments. For the one-year difference, the first-stage $R ^ { 2 }$ was 0.79 for ∆ln(Z) and 0.49 for ∆ln(SO), and the first-stage F-statistic was 359.10 $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ for ∆ln(Z) and 74.12 $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) for ∆ln(SO). The R<sup>2</sup>s and F-statistics were even greater for the three-year difference. Now that we confirmed that our lagged differenced variables were not weak instruments, we next checked the endogeneity of our IT capital and IT spillover variables, both independently and jointly, using Hansen’s J and the C tests for exogeneity [6]. The results of all the tests indicated that we cannot reject the null hypothesis that IT capital and IT spillover are exogenous $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . Thus, we conclude that endogeneity is not a serious concern in our estimations.

Third, while our focus is on the impact of IT spillovers on TFP, an alternative way of examining the impact of IT spillover is through the estimation of a production function (e.g., [20]), wherein the contribution of IT spillover is measured by its output elasticity (see Appendix C for details of the estimation procedure and results). As a robustness check, we estimated an extended Cobb–Douglas production function that consists of five inputs—non-IT capital (K), labor (L), intermediate inputs (M), IT capital (Z), and IT spillover (SO)—along with the interaction terms as in our TFPbased model. As in the TFP-based model, we differenced the variables with respect to time to control for unobserved heterogeneity across industries. The estimation of the production function yielded similar findings—the moderating effects of IT intensity on both IT capital and IT spillover were positive and significant, and the moderating effect of industry competitiveness on IT spillover was negative and significant (see Model 2 columns in Appendix Table C1). This corroborates our results based on the TFP specification. While the main effect of IT capital is insignificant for the one-year difference, it becomes significant as the difference length increases, consistent with Brynjolfsson and Hitt’s [18] findings.

Finally, IT productivity researchers have noted that the surge in productivity in the late 1990s can largely be attributed to an increase in IT investments (e.g., [69]). Cheng and Nault [20] showed that while the mean IT intensity of U.S. manufacturing industries increased steadily during the period spanning 1987 to 1999 (i.e., our sample period), it started to increase at a more rapid rate from around 1994. Because the results based on longer differences rely more heavily on variations in the later years, the strong long-term effects we observe may be due to economy-wide IT productivity shocks in the late 1990s. To check the robustness of our results to this possibility, we examined whether the effects of IT spillover changed over time by dividing our sample period into two subperiods, 1987–93 and 1994–99, and estimating our models for each subperiod. The results are presented in Table 4.

The moderating effects of IT intensity and competition are not significant for the one-year difference for the earlier subperiod (1987–93), possibly due to the loss in efficiency as a result of reduced degrees of freedom. However, they do become significant and stronger under longer differences (n > 2). The results for the later subperiod (1994–99) are consistent with the overall results presented earlier—the moderating effects are significant and become stronger with longer differences. Interestingly, we also observe that the moderating effects are in fact stronger under the five-year difference in the earlier subperiod (1987–93) than in the later subperiod (1994–99). This implies that our long-term effects are not necessarily driven by productivity shocks in the late 1990s. Therefore, we conclude that the patterns of spillover benefits are similar between the two subperiods, supporting our arguments about short-term versus long-term effects of IT spillover.

## Discussion and Conclusions

## IT Spillover and Productivity

Our results sh ow th at IT spillover from supplier industries contributes to downstream TFP growth. According to our estimates, a 1 percent increase in IT spillover is associated with a 0.05\~0.07 percent increase in TFP. This indicates that supplier industries’ IT investments contribute to the technical progress (i.e., a shift of the production frontier) of downstream industries through interindustry transactions. We also found that the impact of IT spillover is not significant in the short run, but becomes significant and increases in magnitude as the difference length increases (n > 2). In other words, the benefits from IT spillover are not immediate, but rather take time to materialize. This suggests that learning periods may be necessary for industries to adapt themselves to the use of IT-related knowledge transferred through interindustry transactions.

Prior IT productivity studies estimated the impact of IT investments on TFP growth without considering the impact of IT spillover (e.g., [18]). Our results imply that if we omit the spillover variable in the TFP equation (Equation (5)), part of the impact of IT spillover is likely to be captured by IT capital (due to the positive and significant correlation between the two variables; see Table 2). This would result in an unusually high return to IT capital. To examine how much the coefficient on IT capital is affected by IT spillover, we estimated our model (Model 1) excluding the IT spillover term, ∆ln(SO), and all of the associated interaction terms. As shown in Table 5, the coefficient on IT capital indeed increases by as much as 23 percent (for the five-year difference) when IT spillover is not included. Therefore, we cautiously note that omitting IT spillovers in IT productivity studies may result in overestimation of the impact of IT capital on TFP growth.

## The Role of Downstream Industry Characteristics

Our understanding of IT spillover effects is further enriched by investigating the role of the characteristics of downstream industries in explaining the differential effects of IT spillovers. First, we found that the effects of IT spillovers are magnified when downstream industries are more IT intensive. We argue that this is due to such industries’ stronger IT capabilities to absorb the spilled-over IT knowledge. Second, we also find that more competitive industries tend to benefit more from IT spillovers in terms of productivity growth. We argue that this is due to such industries’ stronger motivation to be efficient in utilizing the higher-quality intermediate inputs. Prior studies have regarded competition among upstream suppliers as an important factor related to spillover because competitive pressures lower the ability of upstream firms to capture rents from their IT investments. For example, Bresnahan [12] found that downstream firms using IT produced by upstream sectors benefit from IT spillover due to competition in the upstream sectors. Our results suggest that competitiveness in downstream industries also affects how much benefit the firms in downstream industries can appropriate from IT spillover by providing firms stronger motivation to be efficient in utilizing the higher-quality intermediate inputs and IT knowledge from upstream. Overall, our results suggest that IT spillover benefits are not universal and unconditional, but rather depend on IT intensity and competitiveness of downstream industries.

## Contributions and Implications

Our study makes contributions to the growing body of IT spillover literature in several meaningful ways. First, we uncover the conditions under which IT spillover benefits may be greater by examining the moderating effects of downstream industry characteristics. This allows us to better understand the process through which IT spillovers materialize, which ultimately helps us explain and predict under what circumstances IT spillovers are likely to occur and be greater. In addition, we find that spillover benefits become greater over time. This implies that firms may not benefit from IT spillovers immediately but, rather, that it may take time to absorb and appropriate the benefits from IT spillovers.

We make contributions to the IT productivity literature as well. The two industry characteristics we examine, IT intensity and competitiveness, have been studied by IT productivity researchers and have been found to influence the returns to own IT investments [32, 60, 82]. Our results suggest that the influence of IT intensity and competition extends to the spillover value industries can capture from others’ IT investments.

Our results have practical implications for policymakers and managers. First, the countervailing effects of IT investments in the presence of spillover present an interesting situation for managers and policymakers. On the one hand, firms and industries will likely underinvest in IT so as to reduce the amount of their IT investments from spilling over to others [20, 59]. On the other hand, firms and industries may need to invest heavily in IT in order to benefit from IT spillovers from others. While we leave it to future research to identify what an optimal level of IT investments (i.e., the level at which IT spillover to other firms and industries is minimized and at the same time the level at which IT spillovers from other firms and industries are maximized) should be, policymakers may need to begin by developing incentive mechanisms to induce firms to invest in IT at an appropriate level to stimulate economic growth. Second, our results of the moderating effects of industry competitiveness suggest that policymakers need to foster competition to increase the IT spillover benefits. Third, our results imply that firms do not automatically receive spillover benefits from supplier industries. To fully reap the IT-related benefits transmitted from supplier industries, firms should develop their own IT capabilities and IT-related absorptive capacity by virtue of investing in IT themselves.

Our study is not without limitations. First, while our spillover index measures the transmission of IT-related spillover that occurs as a result of economic transactions between trading partners, there can be other possible channels through which downstream industries benefit. In a supply chain, for instance, there might be agreements of information sharing between trading partners, which may facilitate the transmission of IT knowledge to its trading industries. To the extent that these omitted mechanisms are correlated with supplier industry’s IT investment or the amount of interindustry transactions, our spillover measure would capture them as well.

Second, as noted earlier, our results based on longer differences may be driven by other factors such as complementary investments [14, 90]. Because these investments require time-consuming processes [48] and are likely to be associated with IT spillover, our estimates may also capture the impact of these investments in addition to that of IT spillover. We leave it to future research to disentangle the pure spillovers from suppliers’ IT investments from those associated with additional factors that are correlated with IT spillovers.

Third, although our explanation about the moderating effect of IT intensity is based on the IT capabilities perspective, we acknowledge that there may be alternative explanations. For example, due to unobserved heterogeneity across industries in production technology that are not related to capabilities or knowledge but are correlated with IT intensity, some industries may simply enjoy higher returns to their own IT investments as well as IT spillover. Although we removed any timeinvariant industry-level heterogeneity by using a difference-based specification (see [34] in the context of international economics) and by considering industryspecific autocorrelation structures, our results may still be subject to bias stemming from time-varying industry-specific factors. However, industry-specific factors such as production technology are relatively stable over time [47]. Therefore, we believe that this is not likely to explain the differential benefits from IT spillover. Nevertheless, identifying and measuring additional factors that can explain the differences in the benefits from IT spillover would be an exciting direction for future research.

Fourth, although our results suggest that competition helps an industry reap greater benefits from IT spillover from upstream, an offsetting effect of competition is that it can cause a greater portion of the benefits from its own IT investments to dissipate to downstream industries. Of course, the challenge here is to measure the amount of rent an industry fails to appropriate due to spillover. Nonetheless, it would be interesting to examine these countervailing effects of competition.

Finally, our data set exhibits a number of traits that may limit the broader generalizability of our results. As discussed earlier, our data set includes only U.S. manufacturing industries, ends in 1999, and uses a discontinued industry classification system (i.e., SIC). Even though we presented arguments toward why this data set is appropriate for this study (see note 4), we must nevertheless discuss the limitations and issues related to generalizability to the new industry classification system (i.e., North American Industry Classification System [NAICS]), which covers a broader segment of the economy (i.e., includes services industries) and more recent time periods (i.e., post-1999). We believe that our results based on the 1987–99 SIC data set will still hold for the following reasons. First, our results suggest that an industry’s IT intensity is positively associated with IT spillover benefits. Given that services industries that are not included in our data set typically exhibit higher levels of IT intensity than manufacturing industries [67], we expect to observe stronger IT spillover effects in the NAICS-based data set, which covers a broader segment of the economy including services industries. That said, several studies on IT productivity have suggested that the impact of IT investments may be different across manufacturing and services sectors [30, 85]. Given the increasing share of services industries in the total gross domestic product, future research should examine the impact of IT spillover in services industries as well. Second, compared to the SIC, which grouped economic units based on the similarity of products/services, in the NAICS those units that have similar production processes are grouped together. Because a large portion of IT spillover stems from the IT-related knowledge (e.g., IT-enabled organizational practices) transferred from upstream industries and the knowledge can be better applied to firms that have similar production processes, we believe that IT spillover effects will be even stronger with the NAICS-based classification. Finally, prior studies obtained similar results from the SIC-based 1987–99 data set and the NAICS-based data set. For example, Han et al. [45] examined the impact of IT outsourcing on output and labor productivity. Their analysis of the two data sets covering different periods yielded qualitatively similar results.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that examines the role of downstream industry characteristics in interindustry IT spillover. Our finding that IT intensity and competition moderate the impact of IT spillover on productivity provides novel insights into how upstream IT investments affect downstream productivity. As firms and industries become more interconnected and interdependent in today’s networked economy, IT spillover will continue to be an important source of IT value. We hope that this study will motivate future research on this important and exciting phenomenon.

## Notes

1. Throughout the paper, we define IT spillovers as dissipation of the benefits from IT investments made by upstream industries to downstream industries that purchase intermediate inputs from upstream.

2. Although a production function is a firm-level concept, it has been used at the industry, sector, and country levels [15, 20, 29, 30].

4. Our data set contains U.S. manufacturing industries only, and ends in 1999. This is because in 1997 the U.S. Department of Commerce changed its industry classification system from the Standard Industry Classification (SIC) to the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS). Although data for industry output, capital stock, and labor hours, and the input-output tables based on NAICS are available from 1998 to 2006, the NAICS-based data set unfortunately does not include the detailed TFP data we use as the dependent variable. Despite the datedness of our data set, the 1987–99 data set does offer some advantages. The 1987–99 data set consists of more detailed industries (92 manufacturing industries in three-digit SIC versus 60 manufacturing and services industries in three-digit NAICS) and is longer (13 years versus 9 years). Moreover, the same (or similar) data set has also been used in prior studies (e.g., [20]), which makes it possible to directly compare our results with the extant literature. That said, we will further discuss issues related to generalizability of our results to the new NAICS-based classification or to post-1999 periods in the section Discussion and Conclusions.

5. Intra-industry transactions were removed by setting the diagonals in the input-output tables to zero (i.e., int = 0 if i = j), as is commonly done in intercountry or interindustry spillover studies [20]. This enables us to capture the interindustry spillover effects only.

6. Some R&D spillover studies used the unweighted total R&D stock available within an industry (or a sector) as a measure of spillover (e.g., [75]). However, given that spillover is often embodied in the products or services purchased from other industries, and the amount of knowledge transferred is proportional to the amount of contact between trading industries, this measure is problematic because it does not account for the intensity of spillover [22]. In other words, under this measure of spillover, two industries receive exactly the same amount of spillover regardless of how much they purchase from their respective supplier industries. Our measure takes account of the intensity of spillover by using the intermediate input share as the weight for calculating the amount of spillover. In other words, an industry receives a greater amount of spillover when it purchases more intermediate inputs from a more IT-intensive industry. Our measure is in line with microeconomic studies of technological spillovers [50, 78] that use patent data as a proxy for “technological proximity” to gauge the intensity of spillovers between industries. A similar measure has been used in Park et al. [71] in the context of international IT spillover.

7. Another commonly used measure of IT intensity is the ratio of IT capital to labor [32]. Using this measure yields similar results.

8. HHI has frequently been used as an inverse proxy for competition in the economics (e.g., [33]) and strategic management (e.g., [27]) literature.

9. An alternative to the Compustat-based HHI is to use industry concentration data from the U.S. Census Bureau [60]. To check the robustness of our results, we estimated our models using the industry concentration data from the U.S. Census Bureau. The results are qualitatively similar. Additional details are provided in Appendix A .

10. For ease, clarity, and brevity of exposition, the tables only report the results based on one-, three-, and five-year differences.

11. Because our model is in a log-log form with differenced variables, each coefficient can be interpreted as the average percentage increase in TFP associated with a 1 percent increase in each independent variable.

12. To examine whether R&D intensity influences our results, we estimated our model with industry R&D intensity (defined as the ratio of industry R&D spending to industry output) as a control variable. As shown in Appendix B, the coefficient estimates on IT spillover and its interaction terms were similar to when R&D intensity was not included. The only major difference was that the coefficient on the interaction between IT spillover and IT intensity became insignificant under the one-year difference (p = 0.122), maybe due to the correlation between IT intensity and R&D intensity. Overall, the inclusion of R&D intensity does not qualitatively alter our estimates of IT spillover. We thank an anonymous reviewer for suggesting this.

## Appendix C: Production Function Estimation

Produc tion func tions h ave long b een used to estimate the impact of factor inputs on output growth. Among the various production function forms, the Cobb–Douglas form has been the most widely used because it is simple and has been shown to be a good approximation of the underlying production function [31]. Contribution of each factor input is measured by its output elasticity, the average percentage increase in output associated with a 1 percent increase in spending in each input. IT productivity studies [15, 29, 30] have used production functions, treating IT capital as an input in the production function, along with labor and non-IT capital. Building on these studies and considering IT spillover as a factor input, we consider the following extended Cobb–Douglas production function form:

$$
Y = A K ^ {\alpha} L ^ {\beta} M ^ {\delta} Z ^ {\gamma} S O ^ {\omega},
$$

where Y is the industry output, and K, L, M, Z, and SO are non-IT capital, labor, intermediate inputs, IT capital, and IT spillover, respectively α, β, δ, γ , and ω are output elasticities. A is a technology parameter which indicates the level of production technology of an industry. For our estimation, we take the natural log to create an additive form:

$$
\ln (Y) = a + \alpha \ln (K) + \beta \ln (L) + \delta \ln (M) + \gamma \ln (Z) + \omega \ln (S O).
$$

As we did in estimating the TFP-based equation, we difference the variables to remove industry-specific effects, include the interaction terms, and include year dummies (Yr ). This yields the following specification:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Delta^ {n} \ln (Y) = b _ {0} + b _ {1} \Delta^ {n} \ln (K) + b _ {2} \Delta^ {n} \ln (L) + b _ {3} \Delta^ {n} \ln (M) + b _ {4} \Delta^ {n} \ln (Z) + b _ {5} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) \\ \quad + b _ {6} I T I N T + b _ {7} H H I + b _ {8} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) \times I T I N T + b _ {9} \Delta^ {n} \ln (S O) \times H H I + \sum_ {t} \alpha_ {t} Y r _ {t} + \varepsilon , \end{array}
$$

where $\Delta ^ { n }$ denotes n-year difference.
