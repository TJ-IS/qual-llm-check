# User satisfaction with business-to-employee portals: conceptualization and scale development

Dewi Rooslani Tojib<sup>1</sup>, Ly-Fie Sugianto<sup>2</sup> and Sen Sendjaya<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to develop and validate the business-to-employee portal user satisfaction (B2EPUS) measure. Five sequential stages of scale development were undertaken to achieve this purpose: conceptual model development, item generation, content validation, exploratory study, and confirmatory study. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the five dimensions of the B2EPUS, namely Usefulness, Confidentiality, Ease of Use, Convenience of Access, and Portal Design as the dimensions of the construct. Competing model analysis indicates that the second-order factor model is the better fitting for the B2EPUS construct. The theoretical and practical implications of the study as well as directions for future research are discussed in the concluding sections of this paper.

Keywords: user satisfaction; business-to-employee portals; scale development

## Introduction

Since the introduction of the first personalized consumer portal by Yahoo in 1996 (Brosche, 2002), the global adoption of business-to-employee (B2E) portals in organizations has been on the increase (Hamerman, 2004). A B2E portal is the latest generation employee support system to be developed to facilitate the access and availability of customized and personalized information for employees (Davydov, 2001). It has two distinct advantages over web-based Intranets (Ryley, 2001; Smith, 2004). First, it utilizes user-recognition technology that enables the retrieval of information personally tailored to individual employees. Second, it provides integrated access to dynamic content from a variety of sources in a variety of source formats.

The literature indicates that scant attention has been paid to the successful implementation of B2E portals, although they have the potential to deliver significant financial and non-financial benefits in terms of reduced cost, increased competitive advantage, improved corporate communication, and higher employee morale and loyalty (Dias, 2001; Benbya et al., 2004; Sugianto & Tojib, 2006).Given the high cost of portal installation (Bannan, 2002) and significant failure risk of information system (IS) projects (Yeo, 2002), the measurement of B2E portal success is critical to our understanding of the value and efficacy of IS investments (DeLone & McLean, 2003). The quick and easy solution of using the return on investment (ROI) as an indicator of B2E portal success, as recommended in the practitioner-oriented literature (Braue, 2003;

White, 2003; Bisconti, 2004), often poses more logistical problems in the implementation phase as the portal’s intangible benefits are difficult to quantity (Zviran & Erlich, 2003). The shortcomings of the ROI approach in this context will no doubt restrict its application in the organizations. Unfortunately, empirical studies on B2E portals also fail to offer a solution to this conundrum given their myopic focus on portal adoption (Kotorov & Hsu, 2001; Corbitt et al., 2005; Rahim, 2007; Remus, 2007). Only very few studies have attempted to investigate the post-implementation stage (Lai & Yang, 2008), and none of them examined how B2E portal success can be measured from the users’ perspectives. The study reported in this paper seeks to address this gap in the literature by developing and validating a measure of the B2E portal user satisfaction (B2EPUS).

The focus on the user satisfaction dimension stems from the fact that the benefits of any IS are largely dependent on the users’ responses to the system (Au et al., 2002). Users who perceive that their needs might not be sufficiently fulfilled even by a technically advanced system, are usually less motivated to use the system, which may lead to dissatisfaction and eventually a failure in the system adoption (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988). Melone (1990) argued that user satisfaction is the most widely used and commonly accepted surrogate measure of system success. In keeping with the rigorous scale development procedures recommended by the authorities in the field (Churchill, 1979; Straub, 1989; DeVellis, 2003; Lewis et al., 2005), this study used five stages of scale development: conceptual model development, item generation, content validation, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA); each of which is explained in the following sections.

## User satisfaction studies

The concept of user satisfaction as a surrogate measure of system success was first proposed by Cyert & March (1963), then subsequently developed by Powers & Dickson (1973), Gallagher (1974), Swanson (1974), Jenkins & Ricketts (1979), Larcker & Lessig (1980), and Neumann & Segev (1980). However, not until the early 1980s were empirical studies on user satisfaction devoted to establishing a user satisfaction scale. In order to gain more understanding of the user satisfaction concept, we conducted a thorough review of past studies on user satisfaction. We classified these studies into three broad streams of research: user satisfaction with the overall ISs, user satisfaction with a particular IT application, and user satisfaction with web-based IS. A summary of this review is provided in Table 1.

As shown in Table 1, the three most commonly used, and hence validated, user satisfaction measures in the field of IS were developed by Bailey & Pearson (1983), Ives et al. (1983), and Doll & Torkzadeh (1988). However, none of the measures was developed to specifically measure user satisfaction with B2E portals. The first two measures were built to assess general user satisfaction in the traditional data processing environment, which was different from the B2E portal environment where end users interact with the portals without the intervention of IS staff ( Jones & Beatty, 2001). The third measure developed by Doll & Torkzadeh (1988) was designed to gauge user satisfaction with specific IT applications in the end user computing (EUC) environment. While this measure has been used to assess many different types of IS applications (e.g., Seddon & Yip, 1992; McHanney & Cronan, 1998; Zviran et al., 2005), research suggests that significant modifications are necessary for this scale to be used in the web-based environment (e.g., Otto et al., 2000; Simmers & Anandarajan, 2001; Xiao & Dasgupta, 2002; Abdinnour-Helm et al., 2005). Straub (1989) has long argued that a considerable modification to a measure will negatively affect its validity and reliability. In light of this comment, we believe that since B2E portals operate in a web-based environment, it would not be psychometrically sound to adopt this measure for B2E portals. There is a need, therefore, to develop a new user satisfaction measure for B2E portals in particular.

Similarly, Table 1 suggests that none of the studies focused on measuring user satisfaction with B2E portals. The closest are two studies on the B2E benefit system (Huang et al., 2004) and consumer portals (Xiao & Dasgupta, 2002). The former examined a system that provides opportunities for employees to purchase products and services online and the latter investigated consumer satisfaction with commercial portals. The concepts and measures addressed in these studies are not directly relevant to B2E portals whose functionalities such as search and retrieval processes, work flow systems, online self-service applications, and collaboration tools are unique and distinct. This confirms the need for a new measure to assess B2E portals user satisfaction (B2EPUS). The following sections outline the B2EPUS scale development process.

## The B2EPUS conceptual model development

Recognizing the importance of the initial domain identification stage in scale development to ensure the measure sufficiently captured the specific domain under examination (Hinkin, 1995), we meticulously followed the established practice in the field (Sonquist & Dunkelberg, 1977; Sekaran, 1992; DeVellis, 2003). First, we scanned the literature for a definition that best fits our study objective. While many researchers examined user satisfaction from various perspectives, as seen in Table 1, it was Doll & Torkzadeh (1988) who specifically argued that user satisfaction with a system manifests itself in user attitudes, and that an employee interacts with the system directly without the intervention of other staff. We therefore believe that Doll & Torkzadeh’s (1988) understanding of user satisfaction with IT applications in the EUC environment provides the closest parallel to our B2E portal study, and adapted it accordingly. Hence, the user satisfaction with B2E portals in our study is defined as an affective attitude toward the B2E portal by an employee who interacts with the portal directly. This definition served as a guide concurrently with findings from extant literature to identify the B2EPUS dimensions.

In order to adequately capture the relevant dimensions of the B2EPUS, we followed the inductive method recommended by Webster & Watson (2002) whereby the literature guides the selection of dimensions which were conceptually relevant to the B2EPUS domain. Instead of imposing our knowledge of portal-related dimensions a priori, we carefully reviewed extant literature (i.e., user satisfaction, B2E portal technology) and let it generate the most relevant dimensions. We also reviewed anecdotal evidence that appears in practitioner-oriented and trade magazines published by portal solution vendors (e.g., Plumtree Software, SAP Portals, Oracle, etc.), paying specific attention to the portal characteristics featured therein. Following this iterative process, we identified nine dimensions of B2EPUS: Ease of Use, Layout, Convenience of Access, Information Content, Communication, Timeliness, Efficiency, Confidentiality, and Security. These nine dimensions can be grouped into three categories which were typically used in various models of end user satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988; Hoffman & Novak, 1997; Yoo & Donthu, 2001; Au et al., 2002; Yang et al., 2005; Lin, 2007; Bai et al., 2008), namely information quality, system quality, and system design quality. The three categories and nine dimensions corresponded to our focus on, and definition of, user satisfaction with B2E portals, in that users of B2E portals will have direct, personal experience with the quality of the information the portal produces, the system performance, and the interface design quality. This generative or inductive method ensured that the domain of interest was sufficiently captured by the nine dimensions and conceptually irrelevant dimensions (e.g., management support, technology competency, and user involvement) were excluded from further investigation.

## Information content

The inclusion of Information Content as a dimension of user satisfaction is well established in the literature (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988; Chen et al., 2000; Muylle et al., 2004; Ong & Lai, 2004; Yang et al., 2005; Liang et al., 2007; Wang & Liao, 2007). It is a critical dimension in our construct as, unlike other types of employee support systems, B2E portals offer each employee information which is individualized, current, relevant, reliable, and accurate, thereby empowering employees to function more efficiently and effectively. These portal features ensure that end users are not overloaded with information that they do not require (Liaw & Huang, 2003).

## Convenience of access

Huang et al. (2004) and Yang et al. (2005) argued that the extent to which a system is accessible with no time and geographic barriers is an important dimension of user satisfaction. B2E portals are typically accessible by employees at work, from home, and when mobile through various means of access such as company intranet, any computer with an internet connection, and mobile devices such as web-enabled mobile phones or PDAs. Professionals who often work remotely from the office benefit from portals in various ways. They can keep up with the organization’s news and connect with their colleagues while at the same time performing their workrelated and personal tasks.

## Ease of use

Previous user satisfaction studies (e.g., Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988: Harrison & Rainer. 1996: Cho & Park. 2001: Smith.

2001; Wang, 2003; Muylle et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2005; Wang & Liao, 2007) unanimously retained Ease of Use as a stable and consistent dimension of user satisfaction construct (Tate et al., 2007). This dimension was also included in the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989). Since the use of B2E portals within organizations is often not mandatory, user-friendliness is crucial to attract first-time users. Research suggests, for example, that end users prefer organized, clearly structured, and easyto-navigate sites (Eighmey & McCord, 1998; Smith, 2001), all of which will enhance users’ overall satisfaction (Huang et al., 2004).

## Efficiency

Chen et al. (2000) and Loiacono et al. (2002) affirmed that the extent to which users believe that the system can fulfill their needs has an impact on user satisfaction. B2E portals potentially enhance employees’ efficiency by streamlining work processes, thereby increasing their productivity and overall performance (Mawhinney & Lederer, 1990; Vlahos & Ferratt, 1995).

## Security

Previous research has confirmed that Security is an important aspect of user satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Yoo & Donthu, 2001; Loiacono et al., 2002; Huang et al., 2004). However, their studies have used the term Security to represent misappropriation or unauthorized use of data. In our study, this term taps into a more encompassing aspect: the ability of the portal to provide secure access to all applications and facilities provided. We believe that providing a secure access is critical considering the nature of information and resources provided by the portal (e.g., Employee Self-Service) (Benbya et al., 2004).

## Communication

The system’s ability to assist users to interact with others does influence user satisfaction (Wang, 2003; Ong & Lai, 2004; Yang et al., 2005). This Communication dimension is also relevant in our study since B2E portals act as a medium of communication among employees and between employees and their organizations. Specifically, they facilitate collaboration among employees through the use of email, chat, or discussion threads. These facilities support mobile employees who have less frequent face-to-face interactions with their colleagues, thereby helping them to maintain a sense of attachment to their organization.

## Confidentiality

Privacy/confidentiality issues have been a serious concern in the online environment as Internet users’ personal information is often retrieved or submitted without warrant or authorization (Yoo & Donthu, 2001; Loiacono et al., 2002; Huang et al., 2004; Yang et al., 2005). B2E portals are not immune to these risks. The confidentiality and sensitivity of the submitted information in the ESS applications, for example, requires the portal to be accessible only by authorized individuals.

## Timeliness

This dimension was derived from Bailey & Pearson (1983), Yoo & Donthu (2001), and Muylle et al. (2004) who confirmed that response time or processing speed is an indicator of user satisfaction with IT applications. Similarly, employees working in a competitive work environment using B2E portals often require on-demand information reasonably quickly or the information becomes obsolete. Lengthy response time has been found to reduce user satisfaction and results in poor productivity (Hoxmeier & DiCesare, 2000). In our study, Timeliness is defined as the ability of the portal to deliver requested information in a reasonable response time.

## Layout

The final dimension of the B2EPUS is Layout, defined as the design of the interface and display of the information. Research suggests that interface design is an important gauge of user satisfaction (Chin et al., 1988; Cho & Park, 2001; Loiacono et al., 2002; Zazelenchuk & Boling, 2003; Huang et al., 2004; De Wulf et al., 2006; Zviran et al., 2006; Lindgaard, 2007). Muylle et al. (2004) stated that effective website design is vital for the success of an e-commerce initiative. Given the role that a portal plays as the interface between the employees and the organization, effective portal design must be seen as a prerequisite for successfully implementing B2E initiatives. Consequently, an attractive and aesthetic portal design is proposed as a dimension of the B2EPUS construct.

To conclude, there are nine proposed dimensions of the B2EPUS construct. While the terminology of the dimensions may be generic in the existing IS literature, the constructs measured in each dimension were specifically relevant for the B2E portal environment. In fact, some constructs were uniquely developed for B2E portals, such as Convenience of Access (the accessibility of B2E portals through different types of media), Communication (the ability of B2E portals to mediate information sharing or collaboration), and Efficiency (the extent to which B2E portals are useful to employees in terms of increasing productivity). Definitions of each dimension are provided in Table 2.

## Item generation

Following the deductive approach of item generation proposed by Schwab (1980), the nine dimensions of the B2EPUS conceptual model were used as a basis to generate scale items. Referring to the literature, multiple items were generated for each dimension to ensure reliability (Allen & Yen, 1979) and internal consistency (Nunnally, 1978). Whenever appropriate, items from existing scales which had been empirically tested were considered and adapted for the newly developed measure (Chang, 2001). The process established a pool of 47 items, which were subsequently refined and arranged in a sequence suitable for the purpose of content validation.

## Content validation process

The 47 items were subsequently tested for content validity. In order to ensure the adequacy of the dimensions and items representing the B2EPUS construct, an international panel of content experts was asked to provide their judgment. The panel members were selected on the basis of the following criteria: (1) hold a Ph.D. degree or currently pursue a Ph.D. study; (2) actively research in the domain of interest or have professional experience in the field of portal development (Grant & Kinney, 1992; Grant & Davis, 1997).

## First round of content validation

Six content experts were willing to participate in our study. First, they were asked to rate the importance of each of the nine dimensions using a 5-point rating scale (with 1 being ‘Extremely Unimportant’ and 5 ‘Extremely

Important’). The mean values for each of the nine dimensions were greater than 3, ranging from 3.67 to 4.83, which confirmed that all dimensions were essential for measuring the B2EPUS construct. Second, they were asked to match each item with its nominated dimension and rank the relevance of the item to the assigned dimension, using a 5-point rating scale (with 1 being ‘not at all’ and 5 ‘very well’). We calculated the weighted ratios for each item using the cutoff value of less than or equal to 0.50 (Fehring, 1987). The process yielded 15 items with average weighted ratios less than 0.50, which were subsequently excluded from further analysis.

The expert qualitative feedback indicated that the list of dimensions was comprehensive and no additional dimensions were required. They suggested combining dimension Security and dimension Confidentiality as they considered these two dimensions to be similar. Similarly, the experts suggested combining Efficiency and Usefulness as they capture a similar concept in the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989). To maintain the rigor of the scale development process, we conducted a second round of the content validation process.

## Second round of content validation

In the second round, three panel members agreed to participate. Lynn (1986) argued that three qualified content experts are sufficient for the purpose of the content validation. They were asked to rate the relevance of each item to the nominated dimension using a 4-point rating scale ranging from 1 being ‘irrelevant’ and 4 ‘very relevant.’ The 4-point rating scale was utilized in this round since the feedback was quantified by calculating the Content Validation Index (CVI) for each individual item and the entire scale, following Lynn’s (1986) recommendation. Each item had to be rated as 3 or 4 in order to be considered content valid. The CVI value for the entire scale was 91.89 %, which clearly exceeded the expected minimum CVI of 0.80 for a new scale (Davis, 1992). Corroborating the first round, the panel experts confirmed that the proposed dimensions were adequate to represent the B2EPUS construct and no additional dimensions were required. An important suggestion was to combine Timeliness with Information Content, and Layout with Ease of Use. All in all, a total of 28 items were retained under Usefulness, Communication, Convenience of Access, Ease of Use, Information Content, and Security dimensions.

## Field study data collection: sample and procedure

The field study was conducted in Australian universities, since B2E portals have been widely used in the higher education sector (Deans & Allmen, 2002). All 38 Australian universities were contacted, 10 of which indicated that they had built and implemented B2E portals. In response to our email invitations, five universities agreed to participate in the study. Using a non-probability convenience sampling method, a university global email was sent to university staff, inviting them to participate in the online survey. Three hundred and two useable responses were generated. We then randomly split them into two data sets: 145 cases for the exploratory analysis and the remaining 157 cases for the confirmatory analysis. The sample size for both data sets was considered adequate, following the parameter estimate ratio of 5 as suggested by Bentler (1995). The sample characteristics are outlined in Table 3.

## Exploratory analysis

The 145 cases in the first data set were subjected to principal component analysis. The Promax rotation method was used as the data demonstrated high correlations among the extracted factors. Three commonly used decision rules were applied to identify the number of factors underlying the B2EPUS construct (Comrey & Lee, 1992; Hair et al., 1998; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2001). Items with less than 0.45 loading and which cross-loaded on two or more factors at 0.45 or higher were excluded. An Eigenvalue of 1 was used as the cutoff value for extraction. The iterative sequence of factor analysis and item deletion was repeated, resulting in a final scale of 22 items belonging to five distinct factors associated with the B2EPUS construct. Following Tabachnick & Fidell (2001), we analyzed other possible factor solutions (e.g., three, four, and six) but confirmed that the five-factor solution is the most fitting solution, accounting for 73.63% of the variance. Table 4 summarizes the factor loadings for the condensed 22 item scale. The significant loading of all the items on the single factor indicates unidimensionality. The fact that no item had multiple cross loading was found to support the preliminary discriminant validity of the scale. Furthermore. the reliability coefficients for all five factors were above 0.80, indicating acceptable reliability (Nunnally, 1978).

The results of the factor analysis revealed a different pattern from that of the post-content validation model. Items initially generated to measure dimension Communication and Usefulness were formed together as Factor One (Usefulness). While conceptually different, the fact that these two dimensions emerged together is reasonable as respondents may perceive the B2E portal as a useful tool that enables them to perform their work electronically and to communicate with their colleagues. Items belonging to dimension Security were amalgamated with items associated with dimension Information Content in Factor Two (Confidentiality). Respondents may perceive that, as the portal provides a sense of assurance that any submitted information to the portal will remain confidential, the portal will present reliable, dependable, trustworthy information. It is thus logical to see the integration of these two dimensions as the co-relationship that may exist between these two dimensions. Although content experts suggested combining dimension Layout and Ease of Use, our exploratory analysis demonstrated that respondents were able to clearly differentiate one-item group as measuring user friendliness of the portal (Factor Three) and the other group as measuring the design of the portal (Factor Five). This finding is consistent with Muylle et al. (2004) who found that these two dimensions were indeed two separate constructs. Hence, we named Factor Three as Ease of Use and Factor Time as Portal Design. All items measuring Convenience of Access were loaded into Factor Four, thereby retaining the original name.

## Confirmatory analysis

CFA was performed on the remaining 157 cases in the data set using the maximum likelihood method. This method detects the unidimensionality of each factor, which indicates the presence of a single trait or construct underlying a set of measures (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Following Byrne (2001), we developed a measurement model which included the identified five factors as firstorder factors. The five factors were correlated, each item having a non-zero loading on its designated factors and zero loadings on other factors, and the measurement error terms associated with the item were uncorrelated. The hypothesized five-factor model turned out to be a poor model. As shown in Table 5, while the $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . }$ value suggested that the initial model was a reasonably good-fitting model, the other fit indices were all below the recommended threshold. Therefore, there was a need to re-specify the hypothesized model to detect ill-fitting parameters and achieve a clearer factor structure, in keeping with Yoo & Donthu’s (2001) recommendation.

Table 5 shows several models that we re-specified, taking into consideration the empirical and theoretical rationale in each step including standardized factor loadings (Raghunathan et al., 1999) and Modification index (Byrne, 2001). We followed Joreskog & Sorbom’s (1988) recommendation to drop one item at a time in order to avoid over modification. At the end of the re-specification process, four items were deleted. As shown in Table 5, the final 18-item model had acceptable fit indices $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } = 2 . 0 7 $ , CFI ¼ 0.93, TLI ¼ 0.92, RMSEA ¼ 0.83, GFI ¼ 0.85, AGFI ¼ 0.79, RMR ¼ 0.17, Normed Fit Index (NFI) ¼ 0.88). The AIC value for the final model is the smallest, suggesting that the final model is the most parsimonious model. The 18 items are provided in Appendix A.

## Investigation of higher-order factor model

Competing model analyses were performed to corroborate the correlated five-factor model. Following Doll et al. (1994), four plausible alternative models were specified (see Figure 1; only representative items are depicted). Model 1 is a one-factor, 18-item model, which was plausible in light of past studies that assumed that user satisfaction is a single first-order construct (Doll et al., 1994). In Model 2, the 18 items were loaded onto fivecorrelated factors. On the basis of the result of EFA, this was the proposed model in the study. In Model 3, the 18 items were loaded onto five uncorrelated factors. Model 4 comprised a second-order factor onto which the five firstorder factors were loaded. This model tested the extent to which the correlations among the five first-order factors were accentuated by the second-order factor.

Table 6 compares the fit indices of the four alternative models. A null model, which suggests that no latent factors underlie the observed items and that the correlations between items are zero in the population (Muylle et al., 2004), was also included to establish zero point for the NFI (Doll et al., 1994). The null model turned out to provide a poor fit with the data, as expected. Model 1 and Model 3 demonstrated a substantial improvement over the null model; however, neither of them indicated a reasonable fit with the empirical data.

It should be noted that the second-order factor merely explains the covariation among first-order factors in a more economical way (Stewart & Segars, 2002). Hence, even though the higher-order model is able to explain the factor covariations, the goodness-of-fit of the higherorder model will never surpass that of the corresponding first-order model (Doll et $a l .$ , 1994). This was shown in the fit indices of Model 2, which were superior to those of Model 4. As such, Model 2 provided a target or optimum fit for Model 1 and it acted as the target model. However, the target coefficient (T) of 0.883 (the ratio of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of Model 2 to the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of Model 4) suggested the existence of the second-order factor model. It indicated that 88.3% of the variation in Model 2 was explained by the secondorder factor. Hence, the factor covariances in Model 2 can be represented in a more economical manner with the existence of the second-order factor. Furthermore, as described in Figure 2, the paths from the second-order factor to the first-order factors were all significant. These results suggested that the second-order factor model is the best fitting model of the B2EPUS construct. In other words, the second-order factor model (Model 4) is a more accurate representation of the B2EPUS construct and should be accepted in preference to Model 2 (Segars & Grover, 1998).

## Assessment of criterion-related validity

The criterion-related validity refers to the extent to which a scale is related to pre-specified criteria (Yang et al., 2005). It was assessed by the correlation between the total scores on the scale (sum for 18 items) and the measures of the valid criterion (sum for two global items). In this study, the two global items were adapted from Doll & Torkzadeh (1988): ‘In general, I am satisfied with the staff portal’ and ‘In general, the staff portal is successful.’ As expected, a positive relationship was revealed (r ¼ 0.71, n ¼ 157, Po0.01), showing that the scale was capable of measuring the B2EPUS construct.

## Assessment of nomological validity

Nomological validity refers to the extent to which predictions based on the construct being measured are confirmed within a wider theoretical context or network of constructs (Bagozzi, 1981). To assess the nomological validity of the B2EPUS scale, this study assumed that there was a positive relationship between the user satisfaction construct and system use (e.g., Baroudi et al., 1986; Fraser & Salter, 1995; Igbaria & Tan, 1997). Hence, it was expected that a positive relationship between user satisfaction (i.e., the B2EPUS construct) and system use (i.e., the B2E portal use construct) did exist.

On the basis of the works of Teo et al. (1997) and Doll & Torkzadeh (1998), we developed six new items to assess the generic use of B2E portals, namely to check and reply to emails, to communicate with colleagues, to obtain organizational news, to search for information, to perform work-related and personal-related tasks (see Appendix B). A 7-point Likert-type scale (1 ¼ strongly disagree, 2 ¼ moderately disagree, 3 ¼ slightly disagree, 4 ¼ neutral, 5 ¼ slightly agree, 6 ¼ moderately agree, 7 ¼ strongly agree) was used to measure these items. The coefficient a of 0.85 clearly suggested that these six items were highly reliable. As expected, there was a strong positive correlation between the two constructs (r ¼ 0.63, n ¼ 157, Po0.01). This finding corroborated that of prior research, thereby confirming the nomological validity of the B2EPUS scale.

## Discussions

While IS researchers have been focusing on a qualitative approach to content validity assessment (Straub et al., 2004), this study extends IS research by demonstrating how the quantitative approach can be applied in content validating the B2EPUS scale. Compared with previous studies (Bhattacherjee, 2002; Templeton et al., 2002), which reported only one round of the content validation process, this study performed its content validity assessment in two rounds to ensure that there was sufficient rigor in the measure. This step addressed the concern posed by Hinkin (1995) who argued that many scale development practices include measures that lack content validity in the item development stage. We also believed that a second round was necessary as some dimensions and items were revised and several items were added to the scale in the first round.

The finding of the exploratory study suggested that the rotated five-factor solution met the following three criteria: simplicity (Sethi & King, 1991), interpretability (Lederer & Sethi, 1992), and a reasonable percentage of variance explained (Bernstein, 1988). The CFA undertaken in this study confirmed the stability of these five factors. In addition, the competing analysis also revealed the existence of the second-order factor model in the B2EPUS construct. This finding provides evidence that the B2EPUS is a single second-order construct, in alignment with previous user satisfaction studies (e.g., Doll et al., 1994; Huang et al., 2004; Muylle et al., 2004). Our findings support the view that user satisfaction is not a single construct.

This study also concluded that the two most frequently applied user satisfaction scales, the user information satisfaction (UIS) scale proposed by Ives et al. (1983) and end user computing satisfaction (EUCS) scale developed by Doll & Torkzadeh (1988), were not appropriate for measuring user satisfaction with B2E portals. A comparison of the underlying dimensions among the UIS, EUCS, and B2EPUS constructs, as shown in Figure 3, reveals that there are similar dimensions among the three constructs: (1) Information Content in the UIS and EUCS constructs appears to be a part of Confidentiality in the B2EPUS construct; (2) Ease of Use in the EUCS construct is similar to the one in the B2EPUS construct. These overlapping dimensions were expected since we used their scales as references when developing our own scale (see Wang & Liao, 2007 for another example). The fact that Information Content (partially embedded in dimension Confidentiality) and Ease of Use became a part of the B2EPUS construct was also important because they were the two most common dimensions of the user satisfaction construct, thereby strengthening the face validity of the B2EPUS scale as a user satisfaction measure. More fundamentally, nearly all dimensions in our measure (i.e., Confidentiality, Usefulness, Convenience of Access, and Portal Design) and their respective items are specifically developed for the B2E portal.

## Implications of the study

This study has conceptually defined the domain of the B2EPUS construct and operationalized this construct into conceptually distinct indicators that could be observed and assessed. The B2EPUS scale developed in this study is exceptionally important in the development of the B2EPUS construct since a psychometrically sound measurement is a prerequisite for any theoretical advancement (Schwab, 1980). The findings provide a better understanding of the multidimensionality of the B2EPUS construct, which comprises five empirically distinguishable dimensions (Ease of Use, Convenience of Access, Confidentiality, Portal Design, and Usefulness). The existence of the second-order factor in the B2EPUS construct also confirms the view that the user satisfaction construct should not be treated as a single construct, as commonly suggested. This study may contribute to and stimulate further interest in the study of user satisfaction within the web-based environment and its link to other outcome variables, such as employee loyalty and job satisfaction.

As for its practical implications, the 18-item B2EPUS scale is an accessible and easily administered measure of B2EPUS. Organizations may administer the scale to evaluate their B2E portal performance and examine specific dimensions (i.e., Convenience of Access, Usefulness, Ease of Use, Portal Design, and Confidentiality) of the B2E portal. The portal project manager can also administer the scale over successive periods to assess the extent to which employees are satisfied with the portal in both the earlier and later implementation stages. Doing so enables the organization to strategically manage and control the most important aspects of B2E portals during portal implementation.

## Study limitations and directions for future research

Although the resulting scale has been subjected to rigorous development and validation procedures, the study is not without limitations. After a series of re-specifications, our final five-factor model still had a significant $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistics. It is important to note however, that the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ index is always sensitive to, and can be adversely affected by. sample size (Hair et al.. 1998).

A good fitting model could be rejected merely because of small differences between the observed and predicted covariance matrices if the sample size were large. On the contrary, ill-fitting models may be accepted as having adequate fit if the sample size is small. Bentler & Bonett (1980) recommended nearly three decades ago that accepting a model with a small $\chi ^ { 2 }$ signifies nothing but dubious logic, since one can purposefully produce a small $\chi ^ { 2 }$ by simply reducing the sample size. Given the fact that mere reliance on $\chi ^ { 2 }$ does not warrant any statistical merit, alternative types of indices were used in the current investigation to assess the overall fit of the measurement model ( Joreskog & Sorbom, 1988; Hair et $a l .$ , 1998; Gefen et $a l .$ , 2000). In the IS literature, it has been common practice to report a model fit assessment with various fit indices $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Lewis & Byrd, 2003; Chiou, 2004; Xia & Lee, 2005), and ours is no exception. As seen in Table $5 ,$ most of the fit indices we reported $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ GFI, AGFI, RMR, RMSEA, TLI, NFI, CFI, AIC, CAIC) were within the recommended thresholds. Consequently, the decision to retain our model was reasonable and substantiated (Kim & Umanath, 2005).

Second, the participating organizations were predomi nantly Australian universities. While the unit of analysis is the individual B2E portal user who exhibits characteristics typical of B2E portal users, the generalizability of the findings remains yet to be determined. The sampling method may have potential bias since a sample of willing respondents may not be generalized. Future research should test the B2EPUS scale with new data sets in other settings. For instance, researchers could perform crosscultural studies using different larger samples from different industries for greater generalization of the scale. Test–retest reliability investigation should be considered in order to examine the stability of the construct over time (Webster & Martocchio, 1992). Future studies might also investigate the perceptual differences in their satisfaction with B2E portals among employees in various roles. With different types of work arrangements in today’s workplaces, employees may perceive the B2E portal differently. For instance, telecommuting homebased employees may view them as collaboration tools. The portal’s accessibility through the Internet allows employees to stay connected with colleagues and retains their sense of community. On the other hand, officebased employees may perceive the portal as their desktop destination since they can perform their allocated tasks, administrative responsibilities, and individual-related tasks through the portal. It would be interesting to examine whether their perceptual differences affect their level of satisfaction. Such examination is crucial to ensure that the portal can satisfy the needs and wants of all employees. Finally, this study has incorporated multiple rounds of theory building through literature review and expert opinion. Furthermore, a rigorous approach of theory testing has been adopted that seems to confirm the adequacy of measurement. However, since the establishment of psychometric properties of a measure is a complex and lengthy process, there exists a need for continued refinement and validation of the measure. Thus, future work should replicate the current one in a variety of settings in order to ascertain the generalizability of the findings.

## Appendix A: The final 18-item of the B2EPUS scale

1. The staff portal enables me to share or exchange project/task information with my team member colleagues.

2. The staff portal facilitates my collaboration work with all colleagues.

3. The staff portal enables me to discuss work or project issues with my immediate work colleagues.

4. The staff portal enables me to share general information through email or on website with other colleagues in the whole organization.

5. I feel confident in submitting personal information through the staff portal because it will be properly used by authorized people.

6. The information presented on the portal can be trusted.

7. Information presented on the portal is dependable.

8. I feel the staff portal is secure.

## Appendix B: Six items measuring the B2E portal use construct

1. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to check and reply my emails.

2. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to communicate with my colleagues.

3. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to get the latest organizational news.

4. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to search for any information I need.

5. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to perform my job-related tasks.

6. Since the staff portal is now available, I prefer to access the portal to perform my personal-related tasks.
