# How Do Individuals Understand Multiple Conceptual Modeling Scripts?

Mohammad Jabbari,<sup>1</sup> Jan Recker,<sup>2</sup> Peter Green,<sup>3</sup> Karl Werder<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Université Laval, Canada, mohammad.jabbari@fsa.ulaval.ca <sup>2</sup>University of Hamburg, Germany, jan.christof.recker@uni-hamburg.de <sup>3</sup>Queensland University of Technology, Australia, p.green@qut.edu.au <sup>4</sup>University of Cologne, Germany, werder@wiso.uni-koeln.de

## Abstract

Because most real-world domains intended to be supported by an information system are complex, practitioners often use multiple different types of conceptual modeling scripts to understand them. We performed two experiments to examine how two theoretical factors of multiple scripts— combined ontological completeness and ontological overlap—influence how users develop an understanding of a real-world domain from multiple scripts. Results of the first experiment show that to some degree, ontological overlap improves participants’ understanding of a domain, more so than combined ontological completeness. In the second experiment, we tracked the eye movement data of participants to understand how ontological overlap between scripts impacts users’ information search and cognitive integration processes. We found that some occurrence of semantically similar constructs between scripts helps individuals to identify and relate constructs presented in different scripts. Users, therefore, can identify and focus on script areas that are relevant to their problem tasks. However, a high level of ontological overlap decreases the attention paid by participants to relevant task-specific areas because they spend more time searching for relevant information. Together, our findings both refine and extend existing conceptual modeling theory. We clarify the dialectics between the full and parsimonious real-world representations offered through multiple scripts and the individual’s understanding of the domain that is represented by those scripts.

Keywords: Conceptual Modeling, Combined Ontological Completeness, Domain Understanding, Eye-Tracking Experiment, Ontological Overlap, Representation Theory

## 1 Introduction

A key activity in systems analysis and design is to develop and use representations, which are often graphical, of relevant features of a real-world domain. These representations are called conceptual modeling scripts (scripts from here on). Scripts are used to describe relevant aspects of a real-world domain for the purpose of understanding, communicating, and reasoning about it (Mylopoulos, 1992). They are developed using modeling grammars (Wand & Weber, 2002), that is, sets of constructs and rules for modeling real-world domains.

The use of scripts in systems analysis and design is a persistent activity of information systems (IS) professionals. Professionals use scripts in large-scale software engineering (Whittle et al., 2014), in agile projects (Gupta et al., 2019), and in open-source software development (Robles et al., 2017). Professionals also use scripts to assist with business analytics (Nalchigar & Yu, 2018), big data projects (Storey & Song, 2017), and even AI-related work (Lukyanenko et al., 2019; Nalchigar et al., 2021).

Consistent with the prevalence in practice, research on conceptual modeling is an active area of IS scholarship (Recker et al., 2019; Recker et al., 2021). But the research to date on conceptual modeling has largely focused on aspects of single scripts created with one grammar and on how professionals use them. When multiple scripts are used in a study, these studies have typically compared one type of script created with multiple grammars to evaluate which single script is better (Figl et al., 2013; Bera et al., 2019). Few studies have examined how users work with multiple scripts of the same type, an example being Parsons (2002); and even fewer studies exist about how professionals use multiple scripts created using multiple grammars, let alone how they generate domain understanding from them, which is our focus. One exception is Kim et al. (2000) who focused on how external representation aids (in the form of visual cues and contextual information) aided users in these tasks. Another exception is Burton-Jones and Meso (2006, 2008) who focused on how scripts can be decomposed into multiple (sub)scripts to make them easier to comprehend.

Our study complements and extends this work. We focus on two theoretical properties that characterize multiple scripts directly: their levels of combined ontological completeness (COC) and ontological overlap (OO) (Recker & Green, 2019). COC refers to the extent to which a combination of two or more scripts provides a maximal representation of all relevant aspects of a real-world problem domain. OO refers to the extent to which two or more scripts share model constructs that provide the same representation of some relevant aspect of a real-world problem domain. We ask two research questions:

1. What are the effects of combined ontological completeness and ontological overlap between multiple types of scripts on users’ ability to develop domain understanding?

2. How do users develop domain understanding when interpreting multiple types of scripts with different levels of combined ontological completeness and ontological overlap?

Answering these questions allows us to make several contributions. First, we provide extensions to a prominent theoretical basis of conceptual modeling research, namely representation theory (Weber, 1997; Burton-Jones et al., 2017), from which ontological guidance is derived for conceptual modeling practice (Saghafi & Wand, 2020). We add theoretical precision to existing predictions concerning the use of multiple types of scripts (Recker & Green, 2019), and we add the first empirical evidence about these predictions through laboratory experiments. Second, we develop a finegrained understanding of users’ cognitive processes involved in the interpretation of multiple scripts. Eyetracking has been used in IS research since the early 2000s (e.g., Jacob & Karn, 2003) but only recently in conceptual modeling studies (Bera & Poels, 2019; Bera et al., 2019), and its application to conceptual modeling has so far focused on the cognitive processing of information from one type of script alone. Third, our theoretical explanations and empirical analyses culminate in new guidelines for IS professionals to use when they are working with multiple types of scripts during systems analysis and design tasks.

We proceed as follows. Following a brief review of conceptual modeling research, we extend Recker and Green’s (2019) predictions for how domain understanding is obtained from multiple types of different scripts. Then, we describe how we performed two laboratory experiments to answer our two research questions. We then synthesize the findings from both experiments and discuss their implications and limitations.

## 2 Background

Conceptual modeling is a key technique used in systems development practice (Whittle et al., 2014; Jabbari & Recker, 2017) because it improves the efficiency and effectiveness of daily practices carried out by IS professionals (Brambilla et al., 2017). In IS research, several streams of research address conceptual modeling. One line of work has focused on model creation, i.e., acts of designing, evaluating, or improving conceptual modeling grammars, such as UML (Irwin & Turk, 2005; Siau, 2010), ERD (Shanks et al., 2008), BPMN (Recker et al., 2010), and others, and how they can be used to construct scripts (Gemino & Wand, 2005) to serve purposes such as comprehension (Figl et al., 2013), database querying (Bowen et al., 2009; Allen & Parsons, 2010), problemsolving (Khatri & Vessey, 2016), and creative idea generation (Figl & Recker, 2016). Several recent reviews of this stream of literature exist (e.g., Malinova & Mendling, 2021; Recker et al., 2021).

A second line of work has focused on model readers— that is, individuals who, during systems analysis and design, engage in solving problems and making decisions with the use of previously built scripts (Gemino & Wand, 2004). Research in this stream has mostly focused on how the reading of scripts created with conceptual modeling grammars can be improved. For example, we have learned how ontological guidance can make a script more understandable (Shanks et al., 2008) and how learning the semantics of a script can be improved by choosing the most appropriate syntactical constructs (Recker, 2013; Soffer et al., 2015) or by adding additional elements such as graphics (Kummer & Mendling, 2021), text (Gemino & Parker, 2009), and other customizations (Kim et al., 2000). Research in this area has also examined how reading scripts is influenced by pragmatic factors, such as prior domain knowledge (Khatri et al., 2006; Khatri & Vessey, 2016) or experience of the model readers (Mendling et al., 2012). A review of the literature examining the influence of individual difference factors is provided by Mendling et al. (2019).

A third line of research has focused on how conceptual modeling scripts represent a domain and how domain representations through scripts can be improved. This work has often focused on different ontological models that can be used to capture the relevant entities and aspects of real-world domains such that a baseline exists against which conceptual modeling grammars or scripts can be compared (e.g., Wyssusek, 2006). Different ontological models have been proposed and compared (Verdonck et al., 2019; Eriksson & Ågerfalk, 2021). Work has also been conducted in the area of ontology specification and engineering (Kishore et al., 2004; Gailly et al., 2016), and on ways in which different scripts of a real-world domain could be compared or integrated by aligning or mapping their underlying ontologies (Li et al., 2009). Again, reviews exist that summarize this stream of literature and its application to conceptual modeling (e.g., Verdonck et al., 2015).

Our study relates primarily to the second line of research. We study how individuals read multiple scripts to develop an understanding of the real-world domain represented by the scripts. As a theoretical basis, we have chosen one particular ontological foundation, representation theory (Wand & Weber, 1993, 1995).

## 3 Combined Ontological Completeness and Ontological Overlap of Multiple Scripts

Representation theory has provided research on conceptual modeling with guidelines for evaluating conceptual modeling grammars as well as for building and understanding high-quality scripts, namely those that are ontologically complete and ontologically clear (Wand & Weber, 1995, pp. 205-207). Ontological completeness is a measure of the extent to which stakeholders’ perceived meaning of a real-world domain is fully articulated in a script. Ontological clarity is a measure of the extent to which stakeholders’ perceived meaning of a real-world domain is articulated clearly so that the script can be unambiguously interpreted avoiding redundancy, overload, or excess. Reviews of this literature are presented by Burton-Jones et al. (2017) and Recker et al. (2019). There are four conclusions about this body of literature that are relevant to our work: (1) following ontological guidelines in principle improves conceptual modeling processes and outcomes (Saghafi & Wand, 2020); (2) most studies have focused on evaluating one conceptual modeling grammar or one type of script developed using one grammar; (3) there is no single ontologically complete modeling grammar, and therefore, no single script can ever be an ontologically complete representation of a domain (Opdahl & Henderson-Sellers, 2002; Recker et al., 2009); (4) there are equivocal arguments about the role of ontological clarity on model users performance in understanding a domain (Burton-Jones et al., 2017).

The literature to date has provided sound support for the relevance of ontological guidelines for governing the creation and use of single scripts (Burton-Jones et al., 2017; Recker et al., 2019; Saghafi & Wand, 2020). Our goal now is to extend this literature to cover situations in which users employ multiple types of conceptual modeling scripts created with different grammars. In this line of research, our focus is new.

Because ontological completeness and clarity pertain to single grammars or scripts, Recker and Green (2019) suggested two new theoretical constructs pertaining specifically to sets of multiple scripts to evaluate the level of domain understanding that can be generated from reading them: the scripts’ levels of combined ontological completeness (COC) as a measure of completeness and ontological overlap (OO) as a measure of clarity.

COC and OO are constructs that measure the completeness and clarity of scripts, not grammars. This distinction is important. Conceptual modeling grammars, such as UML, BPMN, or others, can be more or less ontologically complete or clear (Recker et al., 2009). For example, the BPMN grammar does not offer a grammatical construct to represent things (Recker et al., 2010) and neither does the UML use case modeling grammar (Irwin & Turk, 2005). Both grammars, however, offer several constructs to represent classes of things. Whether or not one or more scripts contain representations for certain classes of things—or ought to include representations for certain things—is a question of the script(s) developed using one or more grammars. Any one script can contain none, some, or all grammatical constructs from a grammar, and any one script may have a need for additional representations that are not provided by a chosen grammar. COC and OO are constructs that cover the actual representation offered by a certain script, not the potential representations that could be achieved by using a certain grammar (Recker & Green, 2019).

Recker and Green (2019) suggest that the level of understanding that users can generate from reading scripts increases when additional scripts present more elements of a domain than one script alone, that is, when COC is high. However, the level of understanding that can be generated will vary with levels of OO between scripts such that when the level of OO increases, there is a point at which the positive effect of COC decreases.

Recker and Green’s (2019) theory about the roles of COC and OO has not been tested to date. Moreover, we believe the theory needs to be extended in at least two ways. First, we believe their theoretical arguments about the role of OO in understanding scripts in light of empirical findings regarding the use of scripts in practice (e.g., Kim et al., 2000; Jabbari & Recker, 2017). Challenging and updating the theoretical argument requires rigorous experimental data on performance outcomes. Second, we believe that more insights are needed to understand the information processing that occurs when users interpret multiple scripts that have different levels of COC and OO. The current theory makes no predictions about the cognitive processes involved in searching for and integrating one or more overlapping constructs between more or less complete scripts. Hence, exploratory data is needed to better understand the actual cognitive processes that users employ when reading multiple scripts. Our goal is to provide both types of insights to advance the theory.

## 4 Method

To answer our two research questions, we performed two experimental studies in an expansion mixedmethod setup (Venkatesh et al., 2013): one laboratory experiment to evaluate how the existence of COC and OO affect users’ domain understanding when interpreting multiple types of scripts, and a second replication experiment to expand our understanding of how individuals cognitively process multiple types of scripts with different levels of COC and OO. We designed and used the same materials and tasks in both studies but used additional measures (collected through an eye-tracking device) in Study 2 to elaborate and expand the findings from Study 1. Our experiments followed prior examples and guidelines for conceptual modeling experiments in IS (Burton-Jones et al., 2009; Bera et al., 2014). In what follows, we first discuss design elements common to both experimental setups before we present the unique aspects of each study separately.

## 4.1 Experimental Design

Both studies followed a between-group experimental design with two between-group factors: OO and COC. The first factor, ontological overlap, had three levels: Combinations of scripts with no, medium, and high levels of OO between them. The second factor, combined ontological completeness, had two levels: low and high COC.

## 4.2 Measures

In both studies, we employed the dependent variable level of domain understanding generated by users. As is common in conceptual modeling experiments, we focused on deep-level understanding (Parsons, 2011; Bera et al., 2019). To measure deep-level understanding, we collected the total number of correct answers to two inferential problem-solving tasks and evaluated the appropriateness of each given answer, on a scale from 1 (absolutely inappropriate) to 5 (absolutely appropriate). Similar to Parsons and Cole (2005), the appropriateness of answers was evaluated with respect to whether a user provided a reasonable answer that made sense given the domain presented by the scripts.

We chose to measure understanding absolutely rather than relatively (such as using the percentage of correct answers in relation to the total number of answers given, see Burton-Jones & Meso, 2008; Bera et al., 2014). In systems analysis and design practices, it is important to identify how many potential root causes analysts can develop for an issue in total and how many (re)design options they can identify for an information system in total so that the plausible root cause for each issue or the feasibility of each suggested (re)design option can be identified. Evaluating the overall quality of answers in this way enabled us to capture whether participants had described solutions to the denoted problem that were realistic and to develop likely hypotheses of how to actually solve the problem described. We explain how we constructed the tasks in more detail below. We hired two independent coders to code the number, correctness, and appropriateness of answers. Details are provided in Appendix A.

In both studies, we also measured several control variables. First, we measured participants’ levels of familiarity with (1) conceptual modeling in general, and (2) with different UML scripts. The rationale was to ensure that participants had some but varying experience in conceptual modeling and to evaluate their level of familiarity with UML scripts specifically. We used (a) a self-reported familiarity scale, and (b) scores on a UML familiarity test (Figl & Recker, 2016). Second, we measured participants’ prior domain knowledge, which can influence the interpretation of scripts (Bera et al., 2014; Khatri & Vessey, 2016). We measured participants’ prior domain knowledge using self-reported scale measures (e.g., Burton-Jones & Meso, 2008; Bera et al., 2014). To be able to examine the extent to which participants relied on the information presented by scripts when completing the tasks, we created self-reporting measures to evaluate (1) the extent to which participants relied on the information in the scripts, (2) the extent to which they relied on their prior knowledge of the domain, and (3) the extent to which they made assumptions. We preregistered all measurements on an open science server (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/FJVQA).

## 4.3 Materials

In both studies, we developed six sets of two scripts, constructed with two different UML grammars (activity and class diagrams) to operationalize three levels of OO and two levels of COC. As a case, we selected a library management system (i.e., the real-world domain was a library in a university and involved concepts such as students, borrowing, returning, books, and articles). All scripts are available on an open science preregistration server (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/GTPWM). We also developed a set of two scripts for a second case, a bicycle rental shop, for use in a sample test to familiarize participants with the materials prior to the actual experiment (see procedures below). Both cases feature in a standard information systems analysis and design textbook (Whiteley, 2013, pp. 228-263). We used the instructions and diagrams in the textbook to develop different types of scripts to represent different aspects of the domains. The cases we used are similar to those used in prior conceptual modeling studies (e.g., Bera et al., 2014). They are also commonly used in IS education (Topi et al., 2017).

We deliberately selected the cases to be representative of a low complexity domain. In low complexity domains, users do not require high levels of domain-specific knowledge to understand scripts that are representing features of that domain. However, in high complexity domains (such as, for example, bioinformatics, Batra, 2007), users must deal with extraordinary cognitive complexity because they need to process scripts representing a domain with which they are typically not familiar (Bowen et al., 2009; Allen & Parsons, 2010). This situation would have distorted our experimental focus. Instead, both the bicycle rental shop case and the library management system case describe real-world domains about which we could assume that students have some but varying prior knowledge. The scripts we created for these domains were of average to high script complexity. The complexity of scripts is evaluated based on the size of the script, i.e., the number of constructs presented in the script (Allen & Parsons, 2010) and the connections within the script, that is, the number of arcs connecting constructs (Recker, 2013). Our scripts feature over twenty constructs and arcs, suggesting average to high script complexity (Recker, 2013, p. 679).

We chose UML because it is a widely used conceptual modeling grammar with widespread adoption in the software industry (Akdur et al., 2018) and extensive coverage in IS education (Fettke, 2009). UML provides a wide variety of scripts, each of which is designed to convey different information. We focus on activity and class diagrams because they are the two most widely used script types in UML (Ozkaya & Erata, 2020) and they focus on two notably distinct aspects of a real-world domain: one describes form and substance, and the other, behavior and change. Both aspects are needed to understand what an information system represents and what it is meant to do. The grammatical constructs of UML have also been analyzed by researchers (e.g., Opdahl & Henderson-Sellers, 2002; Evermann & Wand, 2005; Siau, 2010), allowing us to evaluate the levels of COC and OO in sets of UML scripts.

To manipulate the different levels of COC and OO in the scripts, we performed an interpretation mapping (Wand & Weber, 1993). That is, we mapped the meaning of each grammatical construct in each script to ontological constructs that describe things in the real world. We performed this process for two reasons. First, not all constructs provided by a grammar are presented in the developed script using that grammar. Second, one grammatical construct can feature multiple times in a script with different meanings. For example, the classes University, Student, and Employee in a class diagram are all classes of thing, but of different things (e.g., an institution versus different roles), but they are all represented using the same grammatical construct—boxes that contain the name of the class. We then conducted an overlap analysis (Green et al., 2007) to establish different levels of OO and COC for the possible script combinations for each set. Appendix B provides details.

Manipulation of OO: We defined ontological overlap when the same real-world construct was represented in two scripts using different grammatical symbols, that is, when constructs were redundant across two scripts because they conveyed the same semantics. For example, when the real-world construct, customer, (interpreted as the ontological construct of class), was represented both in an activity diagram (through the graphical construct swimlane) and in a class diagram (through the graphical construct class), or when the real-world construct, check order, (interpreted as the ontological construct of transformation), was represented in both an activity diagram (through the graphical construct activity) and in a class diagram (through the graphical construct operation).

To illustrate our manipulation of OO, consider the example shown in Figure 1. It shows two sets of scripts of the same excerpt of a domain, one with ontological overlap and one without. In Figure 1a, we count seven instances of ontological overlap: two classes of things (customer, sales) and five transformations (submit order, check order, check stock, send invoice, and pay invoice) that feature in both the activity diagram and the class diagram. As a contrast, Figure 1b shows a set of scripts of the same excerpt of a domain but without any ontological overlap. Classes of things (customer, sales) feature only in the class diagram, transformations (submit order, check order, check stock, send invoice, pay invoice) only in the activity diagram. Note that both sets of scripts have the same level of combined ontological completeness. In the same manner, we manipulated the level of OO in the sets of scripts in our experiment by adding or removing redundant constructs such that the level of overlapping constructs in the set of scripts varied from zero to three to twelve.

## 4.4 Tasks

In both studies, we created inferential transfer tasks to measure the development of deep-level understanding (Mayer, 2009), as is common in conceptual modeling experiments (Burton-Jones et al., 2009). Transfer tasks examine users’ ability to use the knowledge gained from reading the combination of scripts, cognitively integrating them with their own prior knowledge (Gemino & Wand, 2005), and developing creative solutions based on their mental model of how the domain works (Mayer, 2009).<sup>1</sup> In inferential transfer tasks, users are required to use information beyond what is provided in the scripts when they assimilate information represented in the scripts with prior knowledge stored in their memory to form a composite, implicit mental model of the domain in their mind (Gemino & Wand, 2004). Thus, a deep understanding of the domain after reading the scripts is required in addition to reasoning about them, rather than simply using the surface understanding of the scripts’ features (e.g., Recker, 2013).

For our study, two specific aspects were particularly important when designing the experimental tasks. First, our specific interest was in analysis and in designing problem-solving tasks that would require an integrated understanding of both the structural and behavioral aspects of the domain, rather than either-or. Therefore, we created high-integration transfer tasks (Tetlock, 1986). In such tasks, users have to synthesize in their minds the different aspects of the problem domain from multiple individual scripts, correctly identify specific overlapping constructs between scripts, infer underlying construct relationships between scripts, and develop creative solutions for a given problem. For example, to understand how changes in the availability of a resource (e.g., absence of the librarian) might influence the library service provision, participants needed to understand both structural aspects (e.g., which information the librarian is able to access or who else might have similar access to information) and behavioral aspects (e.g., which service provision activities such as authorization or signoff are normally carried out by a librarian and which other role could carry out these activities). In other words, to solve high-integration problem tasks, participants needed to develop a deep understanding of scripts and compare, contrast, and synthesize multiple perspectives about the problem in order to be able to solve it (Tetlock, 1986). We defined two highintegration tasks for our experiment:

1. Proposing innovative ideas to improve the functionality of the current IS, and

2. Updating the structure and behavior of the current IS to reflect an externally triggered change.

To understand the current functionality of the system, users had to understand both the structure and the dynamic perspectives of the system. Users also had to integrate the different perspectives to develop an understanding of how they relate to each other. For the second task, users had to evaluate the scope and impact of an external change—the definition of a new role— on both the structural and behavioral aspects of the system. For control purposes we designed two additional, low-integration transfer tasks that are less complex than high-integration tasks:

1. Proposing solutions involving how the system should be updated to address a structural change, and

2. Identifying how the system’s behavior may change due to an external event.

The second relevant aspect was that we required our tasks to incorporate elements of both systems analysis and systems design such that the tasks resembled common activities undertaken by professional analysts and designers. To solve the first task, users had to understand the scope of functionality offered by the current system (an analysis challenge) and then identify suitable extensions that would improve its utility (a design challenge). The second task involved evaluating the current structure and behavior (an analysis challenge) and then proposing an update (a design challenge).

## 4.5 Procedures and Manipulation

In both studies, the experiment involved six steps. First, participants read information about the study and they were asked to sign the consent form. Second, participants were randomly assigned to one of the six treatment groups (Table 2 and Table 6 show the distribution of participants in the two experiments). Third, participants completed a pretest that collected demographic information and information about participants’ domain and modeling familiarity, followed by a short tutorial about the basics of UML diagrams. Fourth, participants received a sample problem-solving task to solve in the bicycle rental shop domain. The objective of the sample test was to ensure that participants became familiar with the experimental tasks. Fifth, participants were given a combination of two scripts, and they were asked to answer problem-solving questions using the given combination of scripts for the library management system domain. Finally, after the problemsolving questions, participants were asked to answer some self-reflective questions to evaluate the degree to which they relied on their prior knowledge, scripts, or assumptions in answering the problem-solving questions.

## 5 Study 1

The objective of Study 1 was to evaluate the levels of domain understanding that users generate when reading scripts with no OO, a medium level of OO, or a high level of OO, and with a low or high level of COC. With this focus, to the best of our knowledge, this laboratory experiment is the first direct test of Recker and Green’s (2019) theory.

## 5.1 Participants

Student participants are frequently used in conceptual modeling research (Recker et al., 2019), in turn allowing a comparison of the findings to the body of knowledge. We invited third-year bachelor IT/IS students enrolled in an IS lifecycle management course from a large university in Australia to participate in the experiment. These students had previously completed courses on systems analysis and design and conceptual modeling. Students received both remuneration (i.e., 20 AUD for participation) and performance incentives (i.e., 5% of their final course grade) for participation. Participation in the experiment was voluntary and students could withdraw their participation at any time without penalty or judgment. The students were also offered an alternative assessment item to gain equal incentives and were given the option of neither completing the experiment nor the alternative task. Out of the 237 invited students, 134 students participated in Study 1. Our sample size is considered a mediumto-large sample size when compared to other experiments on conceptual modeling (Bera et al., 2019; Recker et al., 2019).

## 5.2 Experimental Hypotheses

For Study 1, we developed three experimental hypotheses. Our starting point was the understanding proposition developed by Recker and Green (2019, pp. 1219-1220). They suggested that the level of understanding users can generate from reading scripts increases when additional scripts present more elements of a domain, that is, when COC is high. However, the level of understanding that can be generated will diminish when the level of OO between scripts increases such that the positive effect of COC weakens.

We believe that this argumentation needs to be refined because the findings in conceptual modeling research to date are inconclusive regarding the effects of ontological clarity on domain understanding (Fickinger & Recker, 2013). The primary basis of our argument is a set of equivocal arguments about the role of ontological clarity in the understanding of scripts.

On the one hand, scholars argue that the existence of OO will introduce additional extraneous cognitive load (Recker & Green, 2019, p. 1220). Understanding scripts involves users’ cognitive activity in searching for and integrating relevant information (Gemino & Wand, 2004). When overlapping constructs exist in several scripts, additional cognitive load will be produced because users need to identify and reconcile redundant constructs across scripts and they need to evaluate whether the redundant construct is identical in meaning to some other construct or whether it stands for some other type of phenomenon (Weber, 1997). Hence, the expectation is that using cognitive capacity for construct reconciliation will diminish the extent of cognitive capacity available to decide or solve a problem with the help of the scripts, thus reducing performance.

All the while, however, empirical studies have shown that when professionals use different scripts that depict different features of a domain, they prefer to select scripts in which they can link (or integrate) the different information presented (Woods & Watts, 1997). Jabbari and Recker (2017), for example, report that practitioners preferred to use scripts that had some correspondence between them. Correspondence in this context means that a construct in one script is semantically related to or has equivalent elements in another script. Practitioners indicated that the existence of such corresponding constructs between scripts helped to better integrate<sup>2</sup> different scripts and in turn improved the understandability of the information presented by multiple scripts. Similarly, Kim et al. (2000) showed that visual cues that indicate how a representation in one script relates to a representation in another script helped users in searching the scripts for related information. Kim et al. (2000, p. 289) concluded that “[the] integration of information from the multiple perspectives was indeed necessary to thoroughly understand the business case.”

We believe both lines of argumentation have merit. OO will help integrate information presented across different scripts, but it will also increase cognitive load. Overall, we believe that the existence of overlapping constructs in multiple scripts will improve, not reduce, the level of domain understanding obtainable from them. But this improvement will not follow a linear gradient as originally suggested by Recker and Green (2019). Rather, we expect an inversely V-shaped effect of OO. In what follows, we explain our logic in the form of three experimental hypotheses.

First, we expect that COC will elevate the ability to generate domain understanding from multiple scripts. Combining scripts that together provide a more complete representation will make more information available for assimilation into users’ mental representations about the real-world domain, improving the level of domain understanding that can be gained from the scripts.

The availability of different sources of relevant information improves the quality of decision-making and problem-solving capabilities. Problem solving requires the development of complex comprehension and integrative processing ability to compare, contrast, and synthesize multiple perspectives on an issue (Goldman, 2011). Integrative processing ability requires the ability to evaluate information within and across multiple sources to determine the relevance of the information and how the information aspects are connected (Goldman et al., 2013).

Multiple scripts with a high level of COC will therefore allow readers to develop a more complete mental model of the phenomena. Prima facie, the more aspects of a real-world domain represented through grammatical constructs in a set of scripts, the more information is available for cognitive integration with existing domain knowledge (Kim et al., 2000; Gemino & Parker, 2009). Having a more complete mental model of the phenomena provides users with a better understanding of the problem domain, allowing them to suggest more feature extensions and solve more problems associated with the IS.

H1: Performance in problem-solving tasks will be higher for the users of script combinations with a high level of combined ontological completeness than for the users of script combinations with a low level of combined ontological completeness.

Second, developing an understanding of a problem domain from multiple sources of information requires determining how different sources of information relate to each other (Goldman, 2011). Determining how different sources are related to each other will be challenging if participants fail to identify any connection points. However, when constructs exist that overlap between scripts, they will provide the connection points for users to link the different perspectives together, which will facilitate developing a more complete understanding of the problem domain. We therefore expect that some level of OO between scripts will be better than no OO between scripts.

In the situation with no OO, readers will not be able to integrate the information presented in the different scripts because users will find the perspectives provided by the different scripts to be completely isolated, and integrating the information presented in different scripts cannot be performed randomly or sequentially (Kim et al., 2000). Model readers need to develop some conceptual connection between scripts. When there is no OO between scripts, the semantics represented in the scripts are entirely separate. In this situation, users will either fail to interpret alternative representations of a domain or they will fail to see any linkage between them. As a result, the mental model of the domain developed by interpreting multiple scripts will be insufficient.

By contrast, as the level of ontological overlap between scripts increases, readers will be able to integrate information presented in different scripts more easily. The existence of some overlapping constructs in scripts will provide explicit points of connection with clearly demarcated corresponding semantics. Consider a class diagram that describes the composition and structure of a library management system (e.g., with representations for entities such as users, librarian, book, and author) and an activity diagram that describes the process of returning a book (e.g., with representations for activities such as shelve book, update book status, and update user status). A user will not be able to understand who is responsible to shelve the book and update the user’s status if they cannot link the activities to the entities. Adding swimlane constructs in the activity diagram that specify roles such as librarian or user will allow readers to link activities in the activity diagram to entities and their properties in the class diagram. Semantically related constructs between scripts help users to identify how two scripts are related to each other and this situation will enable model readers to identify integration points between scripts. Therefore, the overlapping constructs will simplify the processes of search and integration of related information between scripts. Having these integration points focuses users mental efforts toward reasoning about multiple scripts (Woods & Watts, 1997).

H2: Performance in problem-solving tasks will be higher for the users of script combinations with a medium level of ontological overlap than for the users of script combinations with no ontological overlap.

Third, we expect that when the level of OO between scripts is high, the effects on domain understanding will be detrimental instead of beneficial. Generating domain understanding from multiple scripts that involve high levels of OO is more difficult because the ability to integrate information between scripts more easily must be balanced against the additional cognitive load (Sweller, 1988; Gemino & Wand, 2005) required to process scripts that contain higher levels of OO. When the level of OO increases, the number of semantically similar constructs between scripts increases. All these constructs need to be located and interpreted by readers, which consumes mental effort. Identifying overlapping constructs requires a complicated cognitive search process of locating visual constructs in a script and identifying relevant attributes and relationships (Larkin & Simon, 1987). Once identified, readers must then integrate, that is, reconcile the meaning of overlapping constructs, which adds complexity because users must compare the semantics of all the overlapping constructs to understand whether they stand for the same real-world phenomenon. Because the cognitive capacity of the human working memory is limited (Miller, 1996), reading combinations of scripts with a high level of OO will require much of their available capacity for purposes of search and integration, which leaves less capacity for reasoning about the task at hand. This situation means that readers will have less cognitive capacity available to apply the obtained information to solve the problems with which they are tasked.

H3: Performance in problem-solving tasks will be lower for the users of script combinations with a high level of ontological overlap than for the users of script combinations with a medium level of ontological overlap.

## 5.3 Data Analysis

In what follows, we report statistical information about our experimental results honestly, openly, and in context, and we separately offer our interpretation of these statistical findings to the readers for inspection and critical appraisal (Mertens & Recker, 2020).

We began our analysis by checking our data against the covariates to see if there were significant differences between domain knowledge, modeling familiarity, and UML diagrams familiarity across the six treatment groups. Appendix C provides relevant statistics. An ANOVA test indicated that there were no statistically significant differences across groups in terms of their domain knowledge $( F ( 5 , ~ 1 2 8 ) ~ = ~ 0 . 4 6 , ~ p ~ = ~ 0 . 8 1 )$ modeling familiarity $( F ( 5 , 1 2 8 ) = 0 . 9 5 , p = 0 . 4 5 )$ , and UML familiarity $( F ( 5 , 1 2 8 ) = 1 . 1 4 , p = 0 . 3 4 )$ . These results suggest that participants were effectively randomized across the treatment groups.

Next, we checked whether participants indeed relied on the scripts provided to them, rather than their own knowledge or assumptions, when completing the problem-solving tasks. Paired-sample t-tests showed the difference between relying on scripts (M = 5.23, SD = 0.98) versus assumptions $( M = 4 . 6 4 , S D = 1 . 4 4 )$ to be statistically significant $( t ( 1 3 3 ) = - 4 . 4 0 , p = 0 . 0 0 )$ and the difference between relying on scripts (M = 5.23, SD = 0.98) versus prior knowledge $( M = 5 . 0 4 , S D = 1 . 2 5 )$ to be not statistically significant $( t ( 1 3 3 ) = - 1 . 8 7 , p =$ 0.06). We also estimated a regression model to evaluate whether the covariates or proxy measures affected participants’ performance in the two highintegration problem-solving tasks. The results (Appendix C) do not show any statistically significant relationship between these variables and participants’ performance on problem-solving tasks.

To evaluate our three hypotheses, we ran an ANCOVA analysis with COC and OO as independent factors; domain knowledge, modeling familiarity, and UML familiarity as covariates; and problem-solving performance as the dependent variable. We used the total number of correct answers weighted by their level of appropriateness for the two problem-solving tasks as the dependent measure, as described above. Table 2 presents descriptive results, Table 3 summarizes the test results, and Figure 2 plots the results graphically.

While the level of COC did not have a statistically significant effect on understanding $( F ( 8 , 1 2 5 ) = 0 . 0 2 , p$ = 0.89), contrary to our expectation in H1, the level of OO had a statistically significant effect on understanding $( F ( 8 , 1 2 5 ) = 6 . 0 6 , p = 0 . 0 0 )$ , in line with our expectations in H2. The partial eta-squared value of 0.09 for OO suggests a small effect. When the level of OO increased from none to a medium level, the average performance for participants of script combinations with low COC increased from none (M $= 6 . 3 2 , S D = 5 . 4 8 )$ to a medium level of OO (M = $1 0 . 1 1 , S D = 7 . 1 0 )$ . Similarly, the average performance for participants of script combinations with high COC increased from none $( M = 6 . 9 3 , \ S D = 5 . 6 7 )$ to a medium level of OO $( M = 1 1 . 4 2 , S D = 8 . 4 0 )$ . Post hoc contrast tests showed that OO had a statistically significant effect on understanding when the level of OO increased from none to a medium level (contrast estimate 4.31, SE = 1.54, 95% CI [1.26, 7.37], p = 0.01).

While the contrast from a medium to high level of OO was not statistically significant (contrast estimate -0.28, SE 1.57, 95% CI [-3.39, 2.83], p = 0.86), these results still follow our hypothesized direction. When the level of COC was high, OO had a negative effect on participants’ performance as OO exceeded the medium level. Average performance decreased from medium $( M = 1 1 . 4 2 , S D = 8 . 4 0 )$ to a high level of OO $( M = 1 0 . 2 9 , S D = 7 . 0 8 )$ . However, for participants who used combinations of scripts with a low level of COC, increases in the level of OO from low to medium to high continued to have a positive impact on participants’ performance in the problem-solving tasks. But, as presented in Figure 2, the positive effect of OO slightly decreased in the rate of improvement when OO exceeded the medium level.

## 5.4 Our Interpretation of the Results

Our results suggest that the role of COC and OO in understanding multiple scripts is neither uniform nor trivial. Some level of OO appears to aid domain understanding more so than its absence. However, depending on the scripts’ level of COC, too much OO can become detrimental to problem-solving performance. We believe that the results we obtained, in fact, support the logic of our proposition: the presence of OO in multiple scripts helps users relate and integrate the information presented in the different scripts. However, when the scripts articulate many aspects of a real-world domain (i.e., when their level of COC is high), high levels of OO apparently introduce too much additional cognitive load. In such cases, the ability to generate domain understanding diminishes. In fact, it diminishes so much that it offsets any gain in understanding accrued by higher levels of COC at a medium level of OO.

The results can be interpreted as a testament to the well-known limits of human working-memory capacity, and they resonate with the 7+/-2 rule (Miller, 1956). Highly complete and highly overlapping scripts likely contain too many representational constructs for humans to process. The demands for cognitive search, evaluation, and integration processes are too high to result in effective problem-solving. Trade-offs must be made. Our results indicate that the trade-off should be in favor of OO, not COC.

## 6 Study 2

The objective of our second experiment was to obtain insights into the cognitive processes that users employ when reading multiple scripts with varying levels of COC and OO. We sought to evaluate why the existence of overlapping constructs, to some degree, increases domain understanding and explain how participants identify and integrate—or not—overlapping constructs between scripts. Answering why and how questions can best be performed through inductive, exploratory research, which is why we did not develop a priori hypotheses for Study 2. Instead, we carried out our experiment with the aim of developing post hoc inferences (Mertens & Recker, 2020) by using the same experimental setup as in Study 1 but now with the goal of generating new theory about the cognitive processes users employed when reading multiple scripts.

To that end, we performed a replication of our first experiment, in which we used eye-tracking devices to capture participants’ information-seeking behavior and their cognitive processes in reading, scanning, and understanding the information presented in the two different scripts. Eye-tracking helps expose cognitive processes in understanding scripts (Bera et al., 2019). It can be used to collect data to demonstrate how users integrate specific information from scripts to perform tasks and to evaluate the mental effort related to participants’ processing of information in multiple scripts (Petrusel et al., 2017).

## 6.1 Variations to Procedures and Manipulation

In Study 2, we used the same manipulation and followed the same procedure as in Study 1, with one addition. In Step 1, participants were additionally informed about the recording of their eye movements using an eyetracking device. We used Tobii Pro X3-120 eyetracking devices with external processing units. We calibrated the devices for each participant before starting the experiment to ensure accurate and precise measurements. We followed existing best-use recommendations for the eye-tracking devices. Once the device was calibrated, participants started the experiment following the same procedure as in Study 1.

## 6.2 Additional Measures

Understanding multiple scripts involves two main cognitive activities (Gemino & Wand, 2004). First, users need to search for relevant elements across scripts and identify their meaning and relationships. Then, they need to integrate the meaning of relevant information they have identified across the different scripts. To generate post hoc inferences for theory generation about the search and integration processes employed by users when reading multiple scripts, we therefore developed new measures based on data collected through the eyetracking device in order to capture information about cognitive search and integration processes.

To operationalize new measures for our study, we first specified the areas of interest (AOIs)—that is, those parts of the visual display that are relevant to our theoretical interest (Bera et al., 2019). We defined AOIs on two levels, the script and the overlapping construct areas. The script-level AOIs contain each entire script. Defining AOIs on the script level allows us to examine, broadly, how much participants focused on which script and how often they moved from one script to the other.

Because our specific theoretical interest was in the processing of information from overlapping constructs across scripts, we also defined construct-level AOIs. They define in each set of scripts those grammatical shapes and textual information that are visually placed around constructs that semantically overlap between the scripts. This task was important so that we could see whether and how visual attention to overlapping constructs mattered to solving a particular problemsolving task. For example, in our second transfer task, we asked participants to identify what changes should be made if a new admin role is added to the library management system to delegate some duties from the librarian to the admin role. This role assumes some responsibilities (i.e., properties in the class diagram) from the entity librarian and it also assumes some of the task allocations (i.e., activities in the activity diagram). While the number of overlapping constructs within taskrelevant areas (AOIs) varied between the experimental groups, the size of AOIs was consistent for all groups. To illustrate, Figure 3 shows script-level AOIs with dashed rectangles and construct-level AOIs with solid rectangles. It also displays the focal overlapping constructs with dotted ovals.

Eye-tracking devices collect two types of data: saccades and fixations. Saccades refer to the continued movements of the eyes to locate the interesting parts of a screen (Bera et al., 2019). Saccades capture participants constant scanning of the scripts with rapid ballistic eye movements. They, therefore, indicate whether participants are searching for information. Fixations capture periods in which eye gazes are relatively stable. They indicate whether participants focus on a specific area in one or more scripts. The brain interprets the information presented in that part of the script (Bera et al., 2019)—that is, fixation indicates that new information is integrated with the existing knowledge and assumptions of the participant’s mental model.

With AOIs defined, we developed three measures based on saccade and fixation data (see Table 4) to study the cognitive search and integration processes involved in reading multiple scripts.

1. Normalized AOI run count is a measure that tracks the number of saccadic movements to enter and exit a given area of interest from another area of interest (Bera et al., 2019), relative to the total task completion time. This measure indicates how participants search for information among relevant areas of interest (Bera et al., 2019). A high normalized AOI run count indicates that a participant moves often between relevant AOIs searching for information. Because our objective was to explore how participants searched for and integrated relevant information from multiple scripts, we did not consider saccadic movements between and within nonrelevant areas, nor did we consider the saccadic movement within relevant AOIs (Bera et al., 2019).

2. Normalized AOI fixation count is a measure that tracks how many times participants focused on specific areas within the two scripts while engaging in their problem-solving tasks, relative to the total number of fixations on the scripts. This measure indicates the level of cognitive processing involved in integrating information. A high level of normalized fixation count indicates that participants identified a high amount of information to integrate (Poole & Ball, 2006; Shojaeizadeh et al., 2019).

3. Normalized AOI fixation duration is a measure that tracks the time spent fixating on specific areas in two scripts, relative to the total time fixating the scripts. This measure indicates engagement of attention (Jacob & Karn, 2003) and is thus also an indicator for the level of cognitive processing involved to integrate information. A high level of normalized fixation duration suggests that participants spent more time integrating the information.

## 6.3 Participants

We again recruited university students with prior conceptual modeling knowledge. Students were enrolled at a large university in Austria. Our invitation indicated that participation in the experiment was voluntary and that it was not connected to any course credits. Table 7 presents descriptive statistics for normalized AOI run counts (indicative of search) and for normalized AOI fixation counts and normalized AOI fixation duration (indicative of cognitive integration).

In terms of how participants searched for relevant information, we found that search behavior varied by levels of OO and COC. For users of the script combination with a high level of COC, the data suggests that participants moved more often between relevant AOIs when searching for relevant information as the level of OO increased. Normalized AOI run count increased when the level of OO increased from none (M = 0.09, SD = 0.07) to medium (M = 0.15, SD = 0.22). This result was however not the case when participants used a script combination with a low level of COC. They devoted almost the same amount of effort to searching for relevant information when the level of OO increased from none (M = 0.11, SD = 0.10) to medium (M = 0.10, SD = 0.09). Nevertheless, independent of the level of COC, the number of times participants moved between AOIs searching for the task-relevant information was highest when the level of OO was high (M = 0.14, SD = 0.16 for low COC; M = 0.25, SD = 0.21 for high COC).

In terms of how participants integrated information, the results also show sensitivity to variations in OO and COC. First, as the level of OO increased, the normalized AOI fixation count and the normalized AOI fixation duration increased, with only one exception (normalized AOI fixation duration in the no OO, low COC condition was higher than in the other two low COC conditions). Second, as the level of COC increased, the normalized AOI fixation count and the normalized AOI fixation duration also increased, with one exception (normalized AOI fixation count in the no OO, low COC condition was higher than in the no OO, high COC condition).

Summative performance analysis: Finally, to understand how differences in cognitive processing of script combinations with different levels of COC and OO relate to task performance, we produced a scatter plot of the average scores of search and integration behavior (Figure 6). To center the plot, we perused the average search $( M = 0 . 1 4 ; S D = 0 . 1 5 )$ and integration (M $= 0 . 4 9 ; S D = 0 . 2 0 )$ scores across all participants.

Visual analysis suggests that participants’ information processing behavior can be categorized into three main clusters, indicated visually in Figure 6 by dashed ovals. The first cluster contains users of script combinations with no OO and low COC, no OO and high COC, and those with medium OO and low COC, all of whom invested less-than-average cognitive effort in searching for information and on- or below-average cognitive effort to integrate information. The second cluster contains users of script combinations with medium OO and high COC, and those with high OO and low COC. Participants in this cluster devoted about average cognitive effort to searching and on- or somewhat above-average cognitive effort to integrating information. Finally, the third cluster contained users of script combinations with high OO and high COC. These users invested relatively high amounts of cognitive effort in both searching for and integrating information from the two scripts.

To understand how different cognitive efforts affect participants’ performance, we compared the average problem-solving performance of participants in each cluster (Table 7), noting an inverse V-shaped distribution. <sup>3</sup> Participants who devoted average cognitive effort to searching for and integrating information, that is, Cluster 2, performed relatively better on the problem-solving tasks $( M ^ { 4 } = 1 1 . 6 9 , S D =$ 7.01) than participants who spent less than average cognitive effort, that is, Cluster 1 (M = 8.32, SD = 5.73). Our results indicate that users of a script combination with no OO and low COC devoted more cognitive effort to integrating information compared to users of a script combination with no OO and high COC, as well as those with medium OO and low COC. As participants in this cluster of conditions scored relatively low on task performance, these results suggest that in conditions of low completeness and/or low overlap, users cannot meaningfully reason about the limited information available to solve problemsolving tasks and they are also not able to establish correspondence between the limited available information. Our results also indicate that for the users of a script combination with low COC, when the level of OO increased from none to high, there was a slight decrease in their integration behavior; however, they invested more cognitive effort in searching for new information.

For users of script combinations with a high level of COC, as the level of OO increased from none to a medium level, participants moved more often between relevant areas when searching for relevant information. They also identified more information and they spent more time integrating the information they discovered. In turn, their problem-solving task performance scores were the highest in our sample.

For users of script combinations with a medium level of OO, when the level of COC increased from low to high, participants apparently devoted more cognitive effort to processing the information, as they had more information available to search for and integrate. However, as the level of OO increased to high, participants devoted even more cognitive effort to searching for relevant information from the script combination, but they did not devote substantially more time and cognitive effort to integrating them. This result could be seen as indicating the onset of confusion in participants’ information processing behavior. They likely were unable to integrate all the information they discovered, which in turn resulted in somewhat lower task performance. Therefore, it would appear that high levels of OO decrease the efficiency of the information search and integration process. This effect was even more pronounced in the data of users of script combinations with high levels of COC. The existence of a high level of COC together with a high level of OO required a high level of cognitive effort to search and integrate task-relevant information. Given that human memory has limited working capacity (Miller, 1996), a high level of cognitive effort seemed to shrink participants’ ability to perform the problemsolving tasks, compared to the condition where participants needed to invest average cognitive effort in order to process the information.

## 7 Discussion

## 7.1 Synthesis of Findings

Through two laboratory experiments, we studied how individuals interpret combinations of scripts when solving systems analysis and design problems. Our focus was on the COC of two different types of scripts and especially on the OO between these scripts. We suggest that the effect of OO is more complicated than previously assumed—that is, the effect of OO on understanding multiple scripts is neither uniform nor unequivocally beneficial, it is inversely V-shaped. Our findings from the two laboratory experiments show that overlapping constructs (1) improve understanding, to some degree, because (2) they facilitate more efficient information integration and processing, but only until the OO reaches some bearable level (Recker & Green, 2019).

Figure 7 provides a conceptualization of the synthesized insights we developed through our experiments about how readers process multiple scripts. The results of Study 1 indicate that participants’ performance improved when the level of OO increased from none to medium. These results indicate that OO helped participants search for and integrate related information from multiple scripts. In addition, our studies point to the boundary conditions of this positive effect: The results of Study 1 indicate that participant performance decreased when the level of OO increased from medium to high. Additionally, our data from Study 2 suggests that when the level of OO increased to a high level, participants had to devote disproportionally more effort to searching for relevant information. However, as they found more information to pay attention to, the amount of cognitive effort they invested in interpreting information presented in those areas increased while their performance did not. These findings suggest that while a high level of OO enabled participants to search for and identify relevant information, it did not allow them to integrate the information they identified, which in turn explains lower performance scores. We explain these findings based on the limited cognitive capacity of human beings. While participants identified related information, they could not process the high amount of overlapping information and they were confused about which information to integrate. A high amount of overlapping information increased the cognitive load needed to process that information and therefore, decreased participants’ mental capacity to integrate that information. These interpretations align with those of prior studies that suggest that fixation duration is related to the cognitive effort devoted to information processing (Shojaeizadeh et al., 2019) and that a higher number of saccades indicates a less efficient search for relevant information (Sharafi et al., 2015).

Overall, our findings suggest that a medium level of OO facilitates an easier, more efficient integration of multiple scripts than either low or high levels of OO. No OO complicates the integration of relevant information from multiple scripts because it is difficult to establish correspondence between information. Conversely, the less-efficient information processing that occurs when presented with highly overlapping scripts seems to decrease participants’ ability to focus on task-relevant information areas, in turn leaving too little cognitive capacity to effectively solve problem-solving tasks.

## 7.2 Completeness and Clarity of Multiple Different Scripts

Our findings help clarify the role of ontological clarity (Fickinger & Recker, 2013; Burton-Jones et al., 2017) and, specifically, the dialectic of completeness versus clarity. First, we found that participants performed worst in problem-solving when using a script combination with low levels of OO and COC. Simply put, not enough information was provided and the information across scripts lacked integration points. However, participants performed best in problemsolving when there was a high level of OO between scripts and a low, rather than high, level of COC. This finding suggests that interpreting multiple scripts may primarily follow the principle of parsimony. For complex real-world domains, scripts should provide a representation that must be appropriately complete but, more importantly, easily integrated through overlap.

Second, through eye-tracking, we developed an understanding of users’ cognitive processes of reading and integrating information across multiple scripts when the scripts were relatively complete and overlapping. Our findings suggest that overlapping constructs are identified and perused by users as integration points between scripts. How many overlapping constructs are present in scripts will affect the level of cognitive effort required by a person undertaking a given task. Specifically, we found that script combinations with high overlap and high completeness resulted in inefficient, scattered information processing that required more cognitive effort. Some overlapping constructs, however, seemed to help focus attention on relevant areas of scripts.

## 7.3 Informational and Computational Differences in Multiple Different Scripts

Our findings also help clarify discussions about the informational and computational equivalence of conceptual modeling scripts (Siau, 2004; Parsons & Cole, 2005; Burton-Jones et al., 2009). Informational equivalence between scripts means that “all information in one [script] is also inferable from the other and vice versa” (Larkin & Simon, 1987, p. 67).

![](/api/attachments/6HGTBEZC/fulltext/images/2e25f5aaff0f201ea59465e9cd4787f58f105d1693c37b4c8ce1f0140ad25ab7.jpg)  
Figure 7. Synthesis of the Findings from the Two Experiments

Computational equivalence exists “if [the scripts] are informationally equivalent and, in addition, any inference that can be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the one can also be drawn easily and quickly from the information given explicitly in the other, and vice versa” (Larkin & Simon, 1987, p. 67).

When dealing with sets of multiple different scripts, these scripts could theoretically be designed to be informationally and computationally equivalent. However, designing informationally equivalent multiple scripts makes no sense. If different scripts are deemed necessary to depict the full complexity of a realworld domain, then the idea would be for each script to describe a different partial aspect of the real-world domain such that the scripts together present the full information. The question is then how should multiple, different scripts be designed such that, together, they are informationally and computationally superior to some other way of creating the set of scripts. This question is exactly what our study examines.

Our findings show that problem-solving performance is not simply a function of informational supremacy (i.e., higher levels of completeness). The results from Study 1 (Figure 2), for example, clearly show a tipping point—performance is highest when completeness is low but overlap is high. Moreover, performance drops when completeness is high, but overlap varies from medium to high. These findings suggest that performance is driven by an appropriate balance of both informational and computational considerations. Model readers perform best in their tasks when they have sufficient (not necessarily maximized) information and when the set of scripts are relatively straightforward to integrate mentally—that is, when the scripts have some overlap, but neither too little nor too much.

## 7.4 Future Research Directions

We see seven main directions for future research to extend our work.

First, future studies could compare the benefits and disadvantages of overlapping constructs on users’ performance when they engage in tasks that require different levels of understanding, such as deep- versus surface-level understanding (Mayer, 2009) or tasks that are different in nature, such as explorative as opposed to exploitive problem-solving tasks (Recker & Green, 2019, p. 1239).

Second, practitioners sometimes use even more than two different scripts (Jabbari & Recker, 2017). Future studies could thus investigate how different levels of overlap across more than two scripts may help to identify or prevent inconsistencies across scripts.

Third, we focused on the semantic qualities of scripts (Burton-Jones et al., 2009). Pragmatic qualities such as individual differences in domain knowledge also influence how people process information (Bera et al., 2014; Khatri & Vessey, 2016). Future studies could therefore extend the predictions of our theory and provide a more detailed explanation of how individuals performance may vary by considering semantic and different pragmatic factors together (Burton-Jones et al., 2009). Another meaningful extension would be to consider syntactic qualities such as notational deficiencies (Figl et al., 2013) or visual aids like color highlighting. For example, semantically matching overlapping constructs could be color coded, which may help individuals identify and peruse them as integration points when processing information scattered across scripts.

Fourth, we defined ontological overlap when one realworld construct was presented in two scripts conveying the same semantic meaning. Future research could extend our definition of ontological overlap and evaluate whether sets of constructs, i.e., multiple constructs that are connected through relationships, are overlapping across scripts.

Fifth, evaluated the effect of ontological overlap on integrating information across multiple scripts without considering external enablers such as modeling tool support. Many modeling suites provide a variety of features that support script interpretation, for example, grammar-independent visualizations (Kim et al., 2000). Future research could examine the potential benefits of systematically using additional cues through tool support in identifying and integrating overlapping information across multiple scripts.

Sixth, we evaluated the effect of ontological overlap on the cognitive integration of information represented across multiple different types of scripts. Evaluating the role of the existence of ontological overlap between multiple scripts of a single type (e.g., multiple class diagrams representing different modules of a system), would be another meaningful extension of our research. For instance, ontological overlap could be the starting point for developing the next version of the same type of scripts on different abstraction levels. Future studies could also investigate how systems could be decomposed into smaller parts or subsystems that can be presented by multiple types of scripts, and how the presence of ontological overlap could affect the quality of decomposition (Burton-Jones & Meso, 2008).

Seventh, we used lab experiments to evaluate how individuals interpret multiple scripts. While the use of eye-tracking devices enabled us to get deeper insights into the information processing behavior of individuals when they engage with multiple scripts, we encourage future studies to employ other research methods, e.g., verbal protocol analysis or inductive field studies of conceptual modeling experts. A potential advantage of such observational methods might be to provide deeper insights into how individuals process information when there are different levels of overlap and completeness in the combination of scripts. This approach could lead to identifying the tipping point of ontological overlap more precisely and identifying users’ coping mechanisms in response to the cognitive demands resulting from overlapping constructs.

## 7.5 Implications for Practice

Our findings inform a largely neglected aspect of conceptual modeling practice—how users work with multiple types of scripts in combination. The use of multiple scripts is more common than often assumed in the literature (Whittle et al., 2014; Robles et al., 2017) and it is important for conceptual modeling research to inform these practices. Our findings provide practical guidance that can assist conceptual modeling practitioners to select and use appropriate combinations of scripts for different systems analysis and design tasks. Furthermore, our findings suggest that designers of conceptual modeling scripts should design, and even highlight, overlap in their scripts. Design for overlap may even trump design for completeness—our findings suggest that even a complete set of scripts will not help practitioners solve their problems if they are too complex to process.

## 7.6 Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. First, our experimental procedures, tasks, and scripts may not faithfully reflect the scale of real-world modeling in practice. For example, one could construe our experimental tasks as being simple activities undertaken by analysts and designers, the scripts as small, or the chosen case domains as simplistic. We deliberately chose relatively well-known domains to eliminate the lack of domain knowledge as a cofounding variable, and we also focused our experimental tasks on what we believe to be the main requirements of the role of a typical analyst or designer. Yet extensions would of course be laudable—in particular, replications of our experiment with a focus on other tasks carried out by analysts and designers, such as requirements verification (e.g., Parsons, 2002) or query development (e.g., Allen & Parsons, 2010).

Second, experimental and eye-tracking studies are primarily conducted in laboratories where ecological validity is low. Therefore, it could be argued that eyetracking behavior in a laboratory setting may not be generalizable to a real-world setting in which individuals use multiple scripts in different organizational settings. However, the primary objective of our study was focused on controlling variables and maximizing internal validity (Calder et al., 1981).

Third, as is common in experiments, we used a sample of students rather than a sample of practitioners. Despite concerns regarding the limitations of using students as a sample (Compeau et al., 2012), we believe that our sample was still suitable. Our experiment was designed to test interpreting multiple scripts in combination. There is no reason to expect that the cognitive processes of model interpretation would vary drastically between students and nonstudents (Parsons, 2011).

Fourth, our results in Study 2 have low statistical conclusion validity as our sample size was too small to have sufficient power for inferential statistics.

Fifth, we designed our experimental material to correspond to a relatively high level of script complexity while keeping the level of domain complexity low. Prior research has shown that users interpret scripts of domains with varying complexity differently (e.g., Recker, 2013; Khatri & Vessey, 2016); thus, future research should investigate such interaction effects more closely to establish the boundary conditions of COC and OO.

Sixth, our experiment was designed to evaluate, in particular, the role of OO and COC on understanding. We intentionally created sets of scripts that were computationally and informationally different (Burton-Jones et al., 2009). This situation could be seen as a limitation because the computational effect of overlapping constructs cannot, for example, be fully separated from the informational difference— overlapping scripts contain both more information and more representations of the same information. However, we note that computational and informational differences are dependent on the subjective experience of the users consuming the scripts, which is exactly what our focus was.

Finally, we did not evaluate how an individual’s ability might vary in identifying overlapping constructs. Thus, future research could redesign our experiment, as discussed in the third future research direction, or use a baseline group to evaluate whether and how an individual’s ability varies in detecting OO between scripts.

## Appendix B: Interpretation Mapping of Graphical Constructs in UML Scripts

We followed established procedures for our interpretation mapping of script constructs (Rosemann et al., 2009). We used multiple coders to establish reliability. Table B2 summarizes the results of our mapping, the detailed mapping is available on an open science server (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/GTPWM). We followed three steps: First, three researchers individually read the constructs represented in each script and then interpreted and mapped the ontological constructs to constructs represented in the scripts. In conducting the mappings, we relied upon previous literature describing ontological analyses of UML grammars (e.g., Opdahl & Henderson-Sellers, 2002; Evermann & Wand, 2005; Irwin & Turk, 2005). We identified grammar constructs represented in each script. Second, we evaluated the meaning ascribed to each construct and the mapping rationale to map it to the corresponding ontological construct. We did so because not all constructs provided via a grammar are presented in the scripts. For example, the constructs, System, or Extend, do not feature in any of the scripts we used for the High Peak Bicycles or the Library Management System domain. We then met as the entire author team to discuss interpretations of the mapping. Jointly, we discussed and refined the mappings through several meetings with all of the researchers until a 100% agreement on mappings was reached.

Level of OO is measured based on the number of instances of ontological constructs featured in both scripts. To measure OO, first, we identified types of ontological constructs featured in the activity diagram and class diagram. For instance, for the combination of scripts with a medium level of OO, we identified the ontological construct of class presented in the activity diagram (through swimlanes), as well as, in the class diagram (through classes). Then, we counted the number of instances that ontological constructs presented in each script. For instance, we identified the ontological constructs of class presented three times in the activity diagram (i.e., swimlane of user, online transaction services, and librarian). Then we counted the instances of the ontological constructs featured in both scrips that convey the same semantics. For instance, we identified three classes (i.e., classes of user, online transaction services, and librarian) in the class diagram that covey the same semantics of the classes featured in the activity diagram. Therefore, the level of OO was counted as three in the script combination with a medium level of OO.

The level of COC was measured based on the number of different types of ontological constructs featured in both scripts. To evaluate the level of COC, we counted the types of ontological constructs presented in each script. Then, we identified which types of ontological constructs are overlapping across scripts. For instance, we identified that there are four ontological constructs (i.e., class, property in general, binding mutual property, and state law) in the class diagram and six ontological constructs (i.e., class, event, transformation, lawful transformation, stability condition, and system) in the activity diagram for the combination of scripts with a medium OO and low COC. The ontological construct of class was overlapping between two scripts. Therefore, in total, there were nine different types of ontological constructs in the combination of scripts with a medium OO and low COC.

Karl Werder is an assistant professor at the Cologne Institute for Information Systems of the University of Cologne. His research interests focus on the design of digital innovation and the development of digital technology. His work has been published or is forthcoming in leading journals, such as Journal of Management Information Systems, California Management Review, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, and Decision Support Systems, among others. He serves as an associate editor at the Communications of the Association for Information Systems.
