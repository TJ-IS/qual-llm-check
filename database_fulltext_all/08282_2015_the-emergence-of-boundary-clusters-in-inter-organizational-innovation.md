# The emergence of boundary clusters in inter-organizational innovation

Sven-Volker Rehm <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Lakshmi Goel <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> WHU – Otto Beisheim School of Management, Burgplatz 2, D-56179 Vallendar, Germany

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 22 August 2013 Received in revised form 13 November 2014 Accepted 3 December 2014 Available online xxxx

Keywords: Boundary object Boundary cluster Knowledge boundary Inter-organizational cooperation Coordination Innovation

## a b s t r a c t

Firms that want to innovate often do so by conducting cooperative innovation activities with business partners. In such inter-organizational cooperations, business partners depend on each other's knowledge and bring in different types of expertise. In our research we focus on the knowledge exchanges across knowledge boundaries in interorganizational activities for innovation. Information systems (IS) play an important role as enablers in this context. The concept of boundary objects has been used to describe interactions between actors with varying information and knowledge needs. While the vital role of boundary objects has been recognized in IS literature, the process of emergence of boundary-spanning has received less attention. Recent literature has hinted that, contrary to initial conceptualizations, boundary objects may not manifest as only single information technology artifacts. In this study, we offer the view of boundary objects as a cluster of several artifacts. By means of a qualitative case study, we describe the process of emergence of such boundary clusters and provide evidence for how they advance inter-organizational innovation activities. We describe how such clusters emerge from differences and dependencies in the knowledge of cooperating actors and explore the clusters' signi<sup>fi</sup>cance to knowledge exchanges in inter-organizational innovation contexts. In doing so, we offer an explanation of cluster emergence which links the interplay of artifacts, uses, practices, and knowledge to the coordination of cooperative inter-organizational innovation activities.

## 1. Introduction

Today many <sup>fi</sup>rms strive to network with business partners in order to improve their capabilities for innovation through cooperative activities (Davenport, Leibold, & Voelpel, 2006). In this context, acquiring and integrating knowledge from outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm is vital (Chesbrough, 2003). One way to gain access to external knowledge is through establishing cooperative partnerships for New Product Development (NPD) (Davenport & Prusak, 2000). A characteristic of inter-organizational NPD teams is the need to integrate knowledge about the cooperating <sup>fi</sup>rms' objectives, contributions of involved actors, and information systems (IS) (Malhotra, Majchrzak, Carman, & Lott, 2001).

The role of IS in inter-organizational cooperation in general, and in cooperative innovation activities in particular, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Carlsson, 2003). IS enable and constrain the sharing of knowledge within teams that come together across organizational and functional boundaries for a particular innovation project (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Wenger, 2000). Rarely are the IS used isolated pieces of software; they are more often parts of a larger portfolio of systems and information technology (IT) artifacts employed to accomplish cooperative activities (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). It is vital to understand, manage, design, and use these IS resources by establishing appropriate management processes (Krishnan & Ulrich, 2001; Nambisan, 2003; Saraf, Langdon, & Gosain, 2007).

It is a widely accepted understanding that IT artifacts support knowledge exchanges between actors (Doherty, Karamanis, & Luz, 2012; Jonsson, Holmström, & Lyytinen, 2009; Schmidt & Simone, 1996). In this paper, we study interactions in a dispersed, inter-organizational NPD project team, and take a close look at the knowledge exchange between actors. We observe how they choose and make use of IT and other artifacts during their cooperation. In the early phases of innovation, the tasks an NPD team needs to accomplish are often unknown or unclear. They only become apparent step by step through an ongoing dialog (Hardy, Phillips, & Lawrence, 2003). Intermediate results as well as external events in<sup>fl</sup>uence this dialog and divide it into what we call “cooperative episodes”. This dialog endures through the later phases of the innovation process when tasks are matched with the help of systematic planning. The dialogical negotiation, reassessment and agreement on tasks, as well as the particular distribution of work, are accomplished through coordination. Our level of analysis is not an IT artifact, a single user, team, or <sup>fi</sup>rm; rather it is the emergence of artifact clusters during knowledge exchanges that establish cooperative episodes.

The concept of boundary objects is well established in literature on knowledge sharing and re-use (Carlile, 2002; Star & Griesemer, 1989). Boundary objects are used to understand interactions between actors with varying information and knowledge needs. While the vital role of boundary objects has been recognized in IS literature, we don't yet fully understand the processes of how boundary objects develop and how they function to overcome knowledge boundaries (Star, 2010). Lee (2007:335) hints at the opportunity to gain new insights into cooperative practices, when “avoiding the temptation to treat the boundary object as a black box”. While boundary objects were often studied in their manifestations as single IT artifacts such as a database or spreadsheet (e.g. Gal, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2008), recent literature has hinted that they are rarely solitary artifacts. Evidence of the use of multiple boundary objects to accomplish group tasks has been seen in various contexts such as in an aerospace laboratory setting (Bergman, Lyytinen, & Mark, 2007), marketing and sales (Levina & Vaast, 2005), systems development projects (Boujut, 2003; Kimble, Grenier, & Goglio-Primard, 2010; Levina, 2005), healthcare administration (Bossen & Markussen, 2010), and urban planning (Schmidt & Wagner, 2004; Wagner, 2012). In each context, boundary spanning was achieved through employing multiple artifacts and practices for knowledge exchange. While evidence of the combined usage of artifacts exists, prior literature does not explicitly consider the combination of artifacts as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c concept. Following this line of research, we explicitly extend the idea of boundary objects, by using their conceptual foundations to describe clusters of artifacts that facilitate knowledge exchange.

We use an in-depth case study of an innovation project which we followed closely through a three year period. We focus on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of artifacts for knowledge exchanges during cooperative episodes. Previous studies state that the combination of boundary objects, agents and organizations could account for coordination and performance (e.g., Barrett & Oborn, 2010; Bergman et al., 2007; Koskinen, 2005; Lee, 2007). We advance the existing literature by analyzing how the interplay of a constellation of artifacts in<sup>fl</sup>uences knowledge exchange and coordination. Interestingly, we see evidence that such “boundary clusters” can explain the occurrence of coordinated interaction, leading to progress in the innovation project, even though no coordinated changes were intended by the actors. We propose boundary clusters as a new concept in the context of cooperating teams which might be useful for further studies because it sheds new light on the potentiality of managing inter-organizational innovation projects. Our study thus contributes a conceptualization of boundary clusters, a description of the emergence of such structures, and an exploration of their role in facilitating knowledge exchanges in cooperative inter-organizational innovation activities.

In the next sections we review relevant literature on boundary objects, knowledge boundaries, artifacts and boundary-spanning. We then describe our research method and case selection. We analyze three cooperative episodes to highlight our proposed concept of boundary clusters. We next discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and summarize our contributions in the conclusion section.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Types of boundaries and types of objects

The concept of boundary objects was introduced by Star and Griesemer (1989) to explain how actors with different knowledge backgrounds or viewpoints establish a shared point of reference during interactions (Carlile. 2002). Boundary obiects can be concrete, like a spreadsheet or prototype, or abstract concepts like a symbol or shared experience, which possess some “interpretative <sup>fl</sup>exibility” (Bijker, Hughes, & Pinch, 1987; Pinch & Bijker, 1984). Carlile (2002:453) states that in NPD the concept of boundary objects “provide an infrastructure or process where current and more novel forms of knowledge can be jointly transformed producing more shared knowledge or syntax at the boundary.” This motivates our adoption of boundary ob jects for our study in the context of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm innovation projects.

At least four types of objects that enable boundary-spanning have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: Repositories (standardized repertoires of objects), ideal types (a model or symbolic description which can be concrete as an atlas, or abstract like a concept), coincident boundaries (shared conventions of boundaries that contain functionally differentiated tasks, e.g. maps), and standardized forms and methods (Star & Griesemer, 1989:410). All of these can potentially enable knowledge exchange between actors across knowledge boundaries and the occurrence of coordinated interaction. Star (2010:603) remarks that there might be also other types of objects that enable boundary-spanning.

Boundary obiects are temporal and based in action. Temporality suggests that they exist for a limited period of time and eventually change. They are also subject to re<sup>fl</sup>ection and local tailoring (Star, 2010) and are eventually modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in course of actions (Boujut & Blanco, 2003; Doolin & McLeod, 2012; Gal et al., 2008; McLeod & Doolin, 2011). Star (2010:602) emphasizes that boundary objects “are a sort of arrangement that allow different groups to work together without consensus" and that they are “organic infrastructures that have arisen due to (…) information and work requirements”. She also notes that a boundary objects' “materiality derives from action”. If an object is studied for its use as a boundary object, it must be understood as “a set of work arrangements that are at once material and processual” (Star, 2010:604). This characterization of objects that have the potential to become boundary objects in use embraces both the possibility for single concrete IT artifacts such as a database to become a boundary object, as well as the notion that any other (set of) means of cooperative work can become a boundary object.

Carlile (2002) suggests that for boundary objects to be effective in NPD, they need to span three boundaries. Syntactic knowledge boundaries are about establishing terminologies, semantic boundaries are about aligning interpretations, and pragmatic boundaries are about arranging mutual understanding between actors. He proposes a corresponding categorization of boundary objects following this tripartite boundary de<sup>fi</sup>nition. Table 1 characterizes these types of boundaries and illustratively positions the types of objects introduced by Star and Griesemer (1989) in relation to them. Ideal types might reside on each of the boundaries in a particular case.

Syntactic knowledge boundaries arise due to inconsistencies in the terminology used or due to unavailability of facts and data that need to be shared between actors. This boundary can be the source of misunderstandings or lack of information. It is apparent in the form of inaccurate rules for classifying information or incomprehensible representation of thoughts or concepts (Bowker & Star, 2000:10). Establishing a shared and stable syntax solves the information processing, or knowledge transfer, problems of such a boundary. Repositories such as databases provide a common reference point of data, measures or labels across functional areas establishing shared de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and values for problem solving.

Semantic knowledge boundaries arise due to differing interpretations or differing contexts (Tyre & von Hippel, 1997). They cause dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in communicating despite a common syntax. This can be considered as a problem of knowledge communication between the actors. Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) suggest making tacit knowledge or implicit interpretations explicit. Techniques include verbal approaches such as storytelling (Brown & Duguid, 1998) or codifying knowledge in standardized forms and methods, such as establishing best practices (Davenport & Prusak. 2000:68). Utilizing such means in order to translate knowledge between actors helps overcome this boundary.

Pragmatic knowledge boundaries arise when knowledge needs to be transformed between actors in order to reach a shared understanding or stimulate new insights. This involves the notions of knowledge creation and alteration by each actor. Thus, pragmatic knowledge boundaries shape boundary objects that enable collaborative sense-making (Orlikowski, 2002). In terms of IT artifacts, this can comprise models or maps which are exchanged and collaboratively altered by the actors. Pragmatic boundary objects encapsulate all three characteristics — representing, learning and transforming

The categorization of objects into each of the types of boundaries expresses the affordance of an object as a boundary object when used to span a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of boundary. Data repositories, for instance, can provide a shared point of reference, and thus become a boundary object on a syntactic boundary. However, when not used as a shared point of reference, the same artifact is not a boundary-spanning object. In the same vein, models, e.g. business process models, can be used to stimulate interchange of ideas and learning, and are thus predisposed to be used as pragmatic boundary objects. What matters in this case is the understanding that cooperating actors have about the artifact, and how it is actually used to span a speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge boundary.

## 2.2. Managing knowledge across boundaries

Carlile (2004) proposes a framework for managing knowledge across the three types of boundaries (see Table 1) that takes into consideration the degree of novelty of the cooperative task concerned. Cooperative tasks can vary within a spectrum, or “continuum” (Star, 2010:602) of tasks that range from “known” to “novel”. Actors interact within this spectrum, eventually spanning boundaries of all three kinds, syntactic (for known tasks), semantic (for tasks involving some novelty), and pragmatic (for highly novel tasks) (Carlile, 2004).

Task novelty plays a role in creating distinct boundaries that arise from the differences in the kinds of knowledge reguired for a task and from the dependencies across the differences between the knowledge the actors bring into their cooperation (Carlile, 2002; Carlile & Rebentisch, 2003). For example, two cooperating actors rely on data stored in a shared repository to accomplish known, regular tasks. The repository here represents a common point of reference on the syntactic knowledge boundary, and enables knowledge transfer. At some point, a new work requirement arises. Since the work of both actors relates to the repository, there may exist a dependency between their individual tasks and there may be differences in interpreting the data in the new context that need to be resolved. The actors exchange and discuss their interpretations of the data, eventually arriving at a new shared mental model to classify or evaluate the data enabling knowledge translation on the semantic boundary. On the other hand, a highly novel task might involve a product prototype which is examined by two actors from diverse contexts (maybe a designer and an engineer) each from their individual perspective, for prototype development. During a conversation each actor may learn about the technical requirements and constraints of the other's task, and by incorporating these aspects into a shared idea of the product prototype, an alignment of work may be achieved. This is an example of knowledge transformation on the pragmatic boundary. In this example, the differences in the knowledge the actors have cannot be eliminated—they are indeed required to accomplish the task—but the dependencies in the context of the cooperative task need to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and discussed, using the product prototype as a shared reference. As can be seen in the example, each situation places unique demands for boundary-spanning in terms of differences and dependencies.

Often, cooperative work subsumes multiple situations that each bring out unique knowledge differences and dependencies. Thus, collaborations that involve crossing complex knowledge boundaries are naturally adaptive (Hsiao, Tsai, & Lee, 2012). Carlile (2004:563) proposes an iterative approach to managing knowledge that traverses the three boundaries from known to novel tasks. This view suggests that for complex task arrangements as in the context of NPD teams, multiple boundaries and tasks interact. Similarly, Seidel and O'Mahony (2014) emphasize the iterative use of boundary-spanning practices to achieve conceptual coherence in teams.

In cooperative work settings in NPD, interactions between actors involve multiple boundaries, boundaryspanning practices, and artifacts. Extending the boundary object concept in this direction may shed light on how the boundary-spanning function is accomplished and to what extent the uses of artifacts are determined with respect to spanning particular types of boundaries. Further, the literature on boundary objects, while rich in evidence of their importance to coordination, says little about their emergence and change over time (see as an exception, Doolin and McLeod (2012)).

## 2.3. Artifacts and boundary-spanning

Studies describing boundary-spanning in cooperative work often provide a comprehensive account of the work arrangements in place. This involves stating: the employed artifacts, including IT and other physical artifacts, and abstract or concrete objects and concepts; the processes and uses observed, cooperation methods, and organizational routines (Pentland & Feldman, 2008); as well as the effects of boundary-spanning, like processes of individual and team sense-making, or coordination (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Levina & Vaast, 2004, 2005, 2006).

The role of IS in spanning syntactic and semantic boundaries has been studied extensively in IS literature (e.g. Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Boland, Tenkasi, & Te'eni, 1994; Levina & Vaast, 2005, 2006; Pawlowski & Robey, 2004), including the context of NPD (Merminod & Rowe, 2012). In some cases, the use of a single IT artifact, e.g. a database, might be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to coordinate between actors, forming a boundary object (e.g. Pawlowski & Robey, 2004). In other cases however, this might not suf<sup>fi</sup>ce, and a coordinative effect is established only by the involvement of several artifacts in the cooperation process. Prior studies provide evidence of combined usage of multiple IT artifacts in the cooperative work context (Bergman et al., 2007; Levina & Vaast, 2005). It has also been acknowledged that elements interacting across boundaries can both facilitate and constrain knowledge sharing and even generate con<sup>fl</sup>icts in teams (Barrett & Oborn, 2010). Groups of artifacts which have been observed in this context comprise sketches, CAD drawings, photographs, and newspaper clippings (Schmidt & Wagner, 2004). Exemplarily, Schmidt and Wagner (2004:401) write: “Coordinative practices and the coordinative artifacts upon which they rely obviously form complexes of interrelated practices and artifacts. We have proposed to call these complexes ordering systems.”

The observation that several artifacts may be used together for boundary-spanning is in line with a recent reconsideration of the notion of boundary objects. In revisiting the original formulation of the boundary object concept which Star and Griesemer (1989) had introduced, Star's (2010) revised characterization states that the ‘object’ is “a set of work arrangements that are at once material and processual”. This is not just a single artifact or device and “resides between social worlds (or communities of practice) where it is ill structured” thus being subject to identity-forming and tailoring processes (Star, 2010:604). These processes may lead to standardized forms of work, but potentially also “into things and yet other processes, which have not yet been fully studied as such” (Star, 2010:605). Consequently, Star (2010:613) suggests considering the materiality and infrastructural properties of elements of work arrangements for studying boundary objects. These considerations entail the notion that multiple artifacts may function jointly as a boundary object. Identifying such boundary objects requires describing how multiple artifacts are embedded in work arrangements from a material and processual point of view.

Concerning the processual aspect of boundary objects, Star (2010) suggests focusing on the analytical usefulness of the selected artifacts in relation to the studied work arrangement (Star, 2010:612). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, she hints at the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of information and work requirements as triggers for boundary object formation (Star, 2010:602). The ‘boundary’ of the boundary object as conceived by Star refers to a “shared space” within a group's understanding (Star, 2010:602). This shared space contains a number of elements and involves several aspects of a work arrangement. Recognizing that today in most cooperations various artifacts may be used in parallel and that practices evolve simultaneously, the study of boundaries becomes complex. We conceive that from the total number of artifacts in a work arrangement, particular artifacts will be used to accomplish a speci<sup>fi</sup>c cooperative task, e.g. a <sup>fi</sup>le which is exchanged between team members and a product prototype which is concurrently examined and discussed in a team meeting. They could potentially serve as a boundary object together. However, the artifacts by themselves may not necessarily exhibit characteristics of boundary objects. This implies that it is the speci<sup>fi</sup>c uses of artifacts that de<sup>fi</sup>ne a boundary object, not the artifacts themselves.

Concerning the material aspect of boundary objects, Star emphasizes that the materiality of boundary objects derives from action. This implies that some artifacts, in particular physical ones, may never become boundary objects because they are not used as such. The absence of this aspect of use is often referred to when resolving the conundrum of ‘Isn't then anything a boundary object?’ (Star, 2010). It follows that artifacts may come together in particular constellations for tasks and together constitute materiality through collective action or practices. In these cases, the involved artifacts together with practices delineate the shared space of understanding and action; together they confound the involved boundaries. Therefore we can conceive the role of a boundary object as accomplished by several artifacts at a time.

Hence, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the set of artifacts which through collective action in combination obtain materiality as a boundary object, as a boundary cluster. The cluster should be understood as the shared space showing boundary object characteristics, which is established by a logically coherent bundle of artifacts. The idea of the combination of artifacts into clusters entails the notion of a process, which combines artifacts in-use through a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed period of time, with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c trigger, and with a resulting outcome, purpose or consequence, which lie beyond the individual artifacts' capacity in-use. This conception is fundamentally different from other concepts that have been proposed in literature, as for example systems of boundary objects and boundary infrastructures (Bowker & Star, 1999; Star, 2010) which rely on the existence of multiple boundary objects. In our conception, artifacts observed in a work arrangement may not exert boundary object characteristics in isolation, but the cluster of artifacts within the particular work arrangement does.

Because of their dynamic and emergent nature, innovation processes impose continuously changing requirements on work practices and deployed artifacts to accomplish tasks. Thus, the infrastructure of work arrangements changes with the progressing process. In the innovation team context, the lifetime of a boundary object will not necessarily end in the development of a ‘residual category’ or standard (Star, 2010) or relatively stable ‘ordering systems’ (Schmidt & Wagner, 2004), but instead with the accomplishment of a particular task or the resolution of disruptions in the work arrangement. This implies the need to look at speci<sup>fi</sup>c work processes to determine what artifacts act as boundary objects at what periods of time.

While IS literature has applied the concept of boundary objects to understand coordination (Levina & Vaast, 2004, 2005, 2006), the idea of explicating their role in knowledge exchanges that occur due to the use of multiple artifacts has not been considered. IT artifacts play a decisive role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing and facilitating practices for inter-organizational team cooperations. They exhibit characteristics to span all three types of knowledge boundaries (see Table 1). In addition, they are able to carry or represent information about all other elements of a work arrangement. In this respect, it is not so much an ‘information system’, ‘software’ or ‘spreadsheet’ as a physical entity, or as a single boundary object, which is relevant to our study, but the capacity or affordance of the IT artifact to carry information. This affordance can be understood as an artifact's action potential that is dynamically put to use by actors who engage in processes of knowledge transfer, translation and transformation across boundaries. In particular with respect to the context of inter-organizational NPD teams, this makes IT artifacts central ‘levers’ for managing the formation process of cooperative tasks. Hence, the study of the use of artifacts in cooperative task arrangements could inform management on means to manage knowledge across boundaries in NPD project teams.

Our research explores how boundary clusters emerge from boundary-spanning knowledge exchanges. Our interest is in cooperative episodes that bear collective action in complex settings of multiple knowledge boundaries and in which the affordances of several artifacts are jointly enacted to form boundary clusters. To describe how this happens, we conduct a qualitative case study of an inter-organizational NPD team.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Methodology

We followed a network of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the course of an NPD project. During the 3 years of long in-depth case study we closely examined interactions and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the role of artifacts in knowledge exchanges within the NPD project team (Walsham, 1993; Yin, 2003). Our data, comprising largely of text transcribed from interviews and meeting conversations, our own notes, as well as from secondary data gathered, lent itself to an interpretive epistemological stance (Myers, 1997). We used episodic interviews as particular form of a narrative inquiry (Denzin & Lincoln, 2005; Myers & Newman, 2007). Rather than strictly following a prepared series of questions, episodic interviews encourage the participant to give a story-like account of personal experiences, in particular experiences of changes or disruptions in their daily work (Murray, 2008). We pre pared sample questions to prompt interviewees to discuss such disruptive experiences (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). We formulated questions based on our theoretical lens that allowed us to look for evidence of crucial themes such as boundary-spanning or the existence of boundary objects. During the interviews, we tried not only to get a general impression about the course of the knowledge exchange, but also to identify incidents or events that were momentous in changing the cooperative work arrangements in the NPD project team.

In order to identify coherent phases of knowledge exchanges, or cooperative episodes, we built on the assumption of previous literature that boundary objects enable knowledge exchange between actors across boundaries. This assumption comprises the notion that boundary objects are capable of resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts or of mediating between diverging expectations. Thus, we asked for the current status of work, the interviewee's role and tasks, team members they collaborate with closely, and whether these had changed recently. We focused on boundary-spanning by asking how the collaborative work was organized in the team, and which software and other artifacts were utilized. This included questions such as ‘How do you make sure collaboration works?’ and ‘How do you keep each other updated on new developments?’ We also explicitly inquired about recent noteworthy situations, surprising moments or major turns in the project and the consequent reactions and interpretations of team members.

## 3.2. Case selection

Our case site is a network of <sup>fi</sup>rms and research institutions engaged in a cooperative innovation project. The network was part of a larger research initiative supported by the European Commission's Research Framework Programme to investigate ways to help small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs) establish and manage inter-organizational relationships for innovation and production. One of the authors was part of the overall initiative's team of researchers and responsible for studying the necessary transformation of involved SMEs. The goal of the particular NPD project selected for this study was the development of a new type of safety helmet for motorbike drivers utilizing a novel dampening material. Such helmets usually contain a thin outer shell made of composite or thermoplastic material, and a thicker inner shell made of a dampening material. The chemical composition of the dampening material decisively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the helmet's designs (thickness and geometry) that can be attained while providing for adequate impact absorption in case of an accident. The network comprised <sup>fi</sup>ve organizations from Germany, Italy and the United Kingdom (UK). At its core were three partners; all located in Italy. The “Product Design & Manufacturing Company” is a larger mid-sized business with subsidiaries in several countries and experienced in design and prototype development for sports and safety products. It acted as the designated helmet manufacturer in the network and was tasked with the external design of the helmet and its mechanical parts. The “Chemical Company” is a familyowned micro-company, which produces customized paints and <sup>fi</sup>nishes. The entrepreneur, a chemical engineer, had synthesized a new chemical substance for improving the dampening function of safety helmets. The “Engineering Services Provider” is active in a wide range of markets and applications for innovative materials and production processes and took over engineering tasks associated with production process and product engineering. Supplemental services were provided by a Consulting & Engineering Services Provider, an Industrial Research Institute and further <sup>fi</sup>rms such as e.g. a specialized service provider in the area of chemical engineering.

The NPD team involved around 25 members in total. Our analysis focused on eight people central to the team's work as they cooperated closely with each other. Further engineers and consultants were occasionally involved or provided with speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks.

The study was conducted over the <sup>fi</sup>rst three years of the project and concluded about one year before the planned market entry. It provided us with a representative case (Yin, 2003) for our enquiry in multiple ways. First, the team had not worked together in this network before, which made it possible for us to observe the learning curve regarding knowledge exchange and growth of relationships during this cooperation, which could be detected during the interviews. Second, the targeted new product represents a development that is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently complex in that no standard procedure for its development could be chosen a priori, but the route towards reaching the common goal had to be worked out step by step. The reason for this complexity is that the newly synthesized chemical to be used as dampening material caused novel engineering requirements for the helmet design and production process. Hence, the helmet design and production process had to be developed from scratch. Market requirements such as legal safety regulations provided further constraints which had to be continuously monitored and checked against the developed prototypes.

The NPD team shared efforts and responsibilities. None of the team members was in charge of the overall process in the sense that there was no direct authority to give directives. Also, there was no blueprint for the development process they could follow. Thus, they had to ensure consistency and alignment between individual activities and objectives in view of the shared goal. This demanded signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts for establishing a mutual understanding. As the core members were located in different regions of Italy, they heavily relied on electronic communication for information exchange. Usually, virtual status report management meetings were conducted at least weekly, but face to face meetings took place on a regular basis as well. This situation provided a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context for observing how team cooperation developed and how practices emerged in the team.

## 3.3. Data collection and analysis criteria

During the three years' time frame of our study, the initiative's team of researchers continuously maintained close contact with the NPD project team. Formal interviews took place face-to-face or via phone or telephony software: and informal conversations were held alongside numerous proiect meetings, More than 20 full day work meetings and more than 5 half-day workshops which were conducted in scope of the NPD project where attended by the team of researchers and contributed to our data collection. Some of the formal interviews took place at the central work place of each actor, some meetings in external settings. The frequency of interviews and informal exchanges was high in order to ensure that episodes brought up in the conversations were fresh in the minds of the involved actors, to minimize validity concerns of recollection (Morse,

2004). Formal interviews comprise more than 32 h; 25 h of them were recorded, and 19 h transcribed. In some of the interviews, several NPD team members were present. The transcribed texts were interpreted independently by the authors in an iterative approach by sharing and (re-)evaluating comments (Klein & Myers, 1999). In addition call-backs were conducted after a <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interpretation of the gathered text in order to further investigate speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions or to gain clari<sup>fi</sup>cations. From the responses gathered, we were able to achieve theoretical saturation from the data sample for the purpose of this study (Morse, 2004). In order words, we observed recurrent themes and concepts in our data such that gathering more data would potentially provide only incremental insights.

Our data analysis was guided by our literature review and focused on identifying characteristics of boundary-spanning. We looked for speci<sup>fi</sup>c themes when interpreting and coding the data. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed cooperative episodes characterized by narrated disruptions in the team work and analyzed each of them by reconstructing the process of team interactions. In particular, we looked for evidence of the six analysis criteria described in Table 2.

Our interviewees form the core NPD team. Two of the most prominent interviewees in our analysis were General Managers, and one a Process Engineer, all with multiple years of professional experience. Forming the core of the project team, the three interviewees are supported by other colleagues in their companies. In orde to obtain a representative sample of various narratives (Klein & Myers, 1999), people in different roles in each company were interviewed. Table 3 gives an overview of the roles of interviewees included in our analysis.

## 4. Analysis

## 4.1. Cooperative episodes

We present three cooperative episodes based on anecdotes that were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to NPD team members during the project. In analyzing the team members' narrations through the criteria presented in Table 2, we found that several artifacts were used in combination, to achieve knowledge transfer, translation, or transformation within coherent phases of knowledge exchanges. In this way we were able to identify them as boundary clusters within cooperative episodes. We also observed the process of emergence of these boundary clusters. While the clusters themselves were dynamic, temporal, and unique to the situation, the process appeared similar across all episodes. Each episode followed trigger incidents like unexpected <sup>fi</sup>ndings, spontaneous communications, a story to tell, or explicitly agreed changes to work arrangements, such as the introduction of a collaborative workspace in the NPD project. What these trigger incidents had in common was that they imposed new information and work requirements. The team cooperatively tried to understand the new situation. During the resulting dialog, differences between the actors' interpretation of the incidents became apparent. Each actor was constrained by his or her own technical background knowledge, or scheduled contribution, and the team had to <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to deal with their divergent interpretations. They did so by engaging various artifacts, which were used to balance the multiple dependencies between the individual contributions and the cooperative task. These balancing processes led to particular outcomes, which made it possible to identify cooperative episodes and to distinguish them from other types of team interactions.

A cooperative process of work planning, including stepwise re-de<sup>fi</sup>nition of tasks, emerged from the episode as a shared practice. We classify the arrangement and use of this set of artifacts in the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed episode as one boundary cluster. While each artifact had the affordance for knowledge exchange, they were not used as boundary objects in isolation. The partners gradually developed the practice to try to personally attend prototype tests and instantly discuss the obtained results. In consequence, novel solutions were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and developed which spurred the NPD process, for instance a revision of the overall helmet design.

This episode highlights how new information and work requirements were triggered by a helmet prototype test. A dialectic process emerged in which team members re<sup>fl</sup>ected on and tailored multiple artifacts available to them, while spanning multiple knowledge boundaries to resolve the knowledge differences and dependencies. During that process, some of the artifacts were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed, or their uses changed. An outcome of the episode was a new process of work planning with new practices of cooperative task de<sup>fi</sup>nition and joint test visits.

During the NPD project, relevant news about market tests or publications in related technical domains were captured, shared between actors, and when pertinent, incorporated into the product development. This became relevant when on one occasion a journal article triggered the need to reconsider the shared product idea and to evaluate the innovation process. In the resulting cooperative episode, strategic objectives of the project were rede<sup>fi</sup>ned. An overview of this episode structured by our analysis criteria is provided in Table 5.

The team's awareness that actively searching for information about markets and competitors was important was evident:

“We try to be in continuous contact with the market in terms of the market demands and so on. (…) We look for news etc. with specific tools which we have defined for the project, for the specific issue that we want to check. We try to look at what the world is developing.” (Process Engineer/Project Manager at Engineering Services Provider)

The team established practices to obtain relevant external information on a regular basis, and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed viable channels to reach out to potentially interesting markets.

“Because we are a very small company and it is not easy to establish the right communication, the right contacts… To find some materials is not easy, okay there is the internet, but (available information there is usually) limited… Also to increase the possibility to put our idea on the market…” (Chemist/Business Owner)

In addition to information sources such as professional social networking communities or services like Facebook, the following instruments were cited:

“Personal network of contacts, specific information collectors, magazines, books which publish some research. Also, the patents data base…” (R&D Manager/Business Owner at Product Design & Manufacturing Company)

The team made an effort to incorporate the external information gathered to what they knew.

“Normally my way to knowledge is to have information from the [chemical industry] suppliers. But I don't go to them and ask ‘Do you have materials? Do you have a product to solve this problem’ But instead, I ask them to explain to me what they sell that's new. A practice of frequent interaction emerged such that team members regularly exchanged information to keep up with relevant and interesting news. This also involved information that was generated during the project. Information that was deemed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant was documented in the team's CWS and communicated via e-mail, Skype, or Dropbox, as well as through phone and face-to-face meetings. The team members were aware that sharing such information aided to setup and maintain a common base of information as a shared reference. Thus, the CWS as a repository and the captured information helped to span the syntactic knowledge boundary. This shared reference point increased the team members' awareness towards the market reality, and enabled them to link their knowledge and perspectives, as one of them notes:

“Alex, the designer, immediately understood the possibilities of the materials and he proposed me to go deep into this application. (…) Because he needs me for the materials, and I need him for the product, because I am not able to produce helmets or understand how helmets truly function. Because I understand, there is a shell, but it is not so easy.” (Chemist/Business Owner)

Following this incident, a re-evaluation of the role of the test performances was carried out. It became obvious that obtaining appropriate product parameters satisfying legal requirements was at the core of project success. It can be seen as a standardized method on a semantic boundary that is used to align contributions and interpretations between the team members. QFD as well as further complementary methods supported the complex balancing between effectively formulating requirements, considering interacting technical parameters, and meeting objectives.

Using this procedure, the NPD project's <sup>fi</sup>nal target moved from producing a product to licensing a production model for the helmet. The uncertainty regarding customer awareness that had triggered this cooperative episode could not be resolved. However, in response to the new work requirement to reconsider the shared product idea and to evaluate the innovation process, the team managed to move ahead and rede<sup>fi</sup>ne its strategic objectives.

The experience gained in this episode engendered a practice of activity orientation, which emphasized obtaining high quality results through an iterative innovation process, rather than just passing prede<sup>fi</sup>ned de velopment stages. The use of the CWS as a shared point of reference was emphasized.

“The common point is evident, and it is to go through a tool, through a method. (…) we are trying to learn how to use the CWS. As a by-product from this experience of re-aligning the innovation process various product and service ideas emerged which extended the original scope of the NPD project.

In this cooperative episode, the inclusion of external information led the team to recognize the uncertainties with respect to consumers' awareness. This imposed a new work requirement to re-evaluate their innovation process in view of the shared product idea. A dialectic process emerged in which the team re<sup>fl</sup>ected on their shared ideas and re-interpreted their testing and other development procedures. They reinforced the use of the CWS as a shared repository to maintain a common point of reference, and introduced the Quality Function Methodology to collectively modify their approach. Also during this process, they dedicated multiple artifacts to enable knowledge exchanges on different boundaries. As a result, the overall project strategy was rede<sup>fi</sup>ned. An outcome was a new practice of activity orientation that allowed the team to iteratively advance the innovation process. We classify the set of artifacts involyed in this episode as a boundary cluster. All these artifacts together materialized as a cluster through the team's discourse which leads to re-alignment of the innovation process.

Shortly after the start of the NPD project, a CWS, implemented as a web-based enterprise wiki, was introduced. It served as a central repository for team members to create and edit wiki pages, document their development activities, manage task lists, tag and subscribe to speci<sup>fi</sup>c pages, and chat and blog on technical topics. When the CWS was introduced, activities were already underway, team members were familiar with each other, and had developed a certain kind of shared mode of working together. For instance, there were unspoken practices to avoid information overload and to share only what was important for all involved, as the process engineer explained:

“…generally I inform them with a short call or a short message via Skype in order to check if they have interest in the topic with two phrases or something like that. And if they are interested I am going to e-mail. Just not to lose time, to waste time, explaining things that they are not interested in or to hide information they could be interested in. (…) Yes, it is not easy, because I can say what I am interested in but I cannot say what Alex is interested in with absolute sureness.” (2, Process Engineer/Project Manager at Engineering Services Provider)

The CWS offered new opportunities to the team to mutually arrange their work. This stimulated re-evaluation of their established practices for capturing knowledge, documenting, and information exchange. As a central repository, the CWS served as a pragmatic object that stimulated the team's awareness on work practices. A sustained discourse emerged in the team in trying to understand how to effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently utilize the CWS. One team member re<sup>fl</sup>ects on how to change his work practices from his previously used software to the CWS:

“For the mind map I use Mind Jet. I have been using it since three years. It allows you to mind map your ideas and to manage schedules, profiles, and store information. The team members had to <sup>fi</sup>nd out step by step how they could include the CWS in their collective work practices. They actively sought new ways of using the functionality of the CWS collectively. Team members recognized that the existing wiki functionality did not meet all of their needs in supporting the innovation process. They engaged in an active dialog with the IT services <sup>fi</sup>rm that provided the CWS and came up with constructive proposals for extending the workspace's functionalities. An R&D Manager recounts this re-<sup>fl</sup>ection and tailoring process that led to several modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the CWS:

## 5. Discussion

Lee (2007:335) suggested “(opening) the box around boundary objects” to gain new insights into cooperative practices. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on cooperative episodes during NPD suggests that in the context of certain incidents, artifacts, practices, knowledge differences and dependencies, processes and uses, cooperation purposes and constraints, and cooperative interactions can form intricate textures of relationships which involve knowledge exchanges on several different boundaries at a time. We built on a framework developed by Carlile (2002, 2004) to categorize artifacts along three knowledge boundaries based on their affordances. Other authors report that boundary objects themselves can provide for coordination (e.g. Bechky, 2003; Bergman et al., 2007; Kimble et al., 2010; Koskinen, 2005; Lee, 2007; Levina & Vaast, 2005; Pennington, 2010). However, in our case study we found that coordination of the project team is not accomplished by the artifacts individually, and not on a single boundary.

Our study was conducted in one project, with a particular type of NPD and collaborative partnerships. Thus, the empirical evidence is limited to this case context. However, it does suggest analytically generalizable processes that will need to be investigated in different settings. Since we had paramount access to the NPD team we studied and could gain deep insights into their considerations, opinions and judgments, we strove for triangulation when possible, such as through data collection from multiple sources, to enhance internal validity and reliability (Stake, 2005). We adhered as closely as possible to best practices for conducting reliable interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld research, such as those suggested by Klein and Myers (1999). For example, we were sensitive to inherent biases and possible differences in interpretations when conducting interviews and analyzing collected data. Our study provides only one perspective of knowledge exchange via boundary clusters; other interpretations are of course possible (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004; Majchrzak, Rice, Malhotra, King, & Ba, 2000). While our research context has been inter-organizational, we assume that such effects can also be examined in intraorganizational settings with comparable knowledge boundaries. Our approach presents one perspective to analyze knowledge exchanges with a focus on artifacts in use; other approaches might center on team dialog or dynamics with a focus on creating new knowledge or on emerging modes of coordination

Our considerations open up new avenues for IS research. We have shown that clusters of different types of artifacts, both virtual and physical, with affordances on different knowledge boundaries are engaged to resolve knowledge differences and dependencies in cooperations. It has been suggested that such intertwining of material and virtual representations might allow for an extension of team capabilities (Robey, Schwaig, & Jin, 2003). Our approach explicates aspects involved in this yet invisible interrelation. It calls for a shift of focus in IS research on boundary management from single artifacts to environments or boundary-spanning clusters of artifacts.

Across the three episodes we observed a general pattern of the emergence of boundary clusters. The episodes were triggered by distinct incidents that created new work requirements for the team and initiated knowledge exchanges. The new requirements caused the team to re-evaluate the work or project situation. Starting from the triggers reported to us in the interviews we reconstructed the team discourse. The discourse can be described in relation to artifacts, their uses, and work arrangements, which lead to the materialization of boundary clusters. We provide a representation of this process in Fig. 4. From our analysis we can arrive at several insights which we outline in the following paragraphs.

## 5.1. The concept of boundary clusters

During cooperative episodes, groups of artifacts become signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for collective action when utilized by several actors for knowledge exchanges. In combination, these groups of artifacts obtain materiality as a boundary cluster. The episodes are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the sense that work arrangements of a team are altered through a discourse involving knowledge exchanges—transfer, translation, and transformation—across different boundaries. While the particular composition of a cluster may only be valid during a particular episode, they are de<sup>fi</sup>nite, traceable structures during that episode. The clusters arise from new information or work requirements, and materialize through the use of artifacts in an emerging discourse. They are con<sup>fi</sup>ned by the resolution or balancing of requirements, the accomplishment of a particular task, and/or the transition into a new work arrangement. Hence, it is possible to conceptualize these structures and to study their role for knowledge exchanges and team coordination. These structures may be referred to as boundary objects that are composed of several artifacts. We offer the conceptualization of these structures as boundary clusters as a contribution of this study.

These structures provide a link between groups of artifacts and their knowledge exchange and coordination effects, thus constituting a distinct concept. In this respect, they are different from what has been proposed before, e.g. as “ordering systems” by Schmidt and Wagner (2004), because they are not intentionally deployed, or sustained through local practices, but lead to an emerging coordination process, and novel coordinative capabilities.

The process model in Fig. 4 explains why single artifacts failed to serve as boundary objects in our context. One reason is the boundary-spanning required across multiple boundaries. The observed trigger incidents established new work requirements. Various knowledge differences and dependencies came into play, and boundary-spanning on multiple boundaries became necessary. Episode A, for example, was triggered by an incident on the syntactic knowledge boundary, but the discourse involved artifacts on all three boundaries before a new joint practice emerged. Another reason is that resolving or learning how to deal with new work requirements in the episodes observed stimulated team learning which led to various unexpected outcomes that cannot be attributed to single artifacts. Such outcomes involved changes and modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of multiple artifacts and their uses. In addition, new practices or uses appeared, or new artifacts were introduced into the work arrangement, as in episode B when the QFD was introduced as a standardized method.

Thus, boundary clusters resemble boundary objects in the sense that they coordinate, provide interpretative <sup>fl</sup>exibility through a dialectic process, and eventually result in new capabilities or practices. They are different in that their material nature and their effects cannot be drawn from single artifact uses.

## 5.2. The role of differences and dependencies

Based on our understanding of boundary clusters, we arrive at an explanation for the function of boundary clusters in facilitating coordination of cooperative work in the context of inter-organizational NPD projects

Following trigger incidents and the appearance of new work requirements, team members tried to arrive at an understanding of the situation. Due to the divergent knowledge and experiences of actors, differences became apparent in the interpretation of incidents and their consequences. Through a discourse, the team determined how to resolve such differences. Various artifacts were available to the team as means for interaction, which enabled knowledge exchanges across different knowledge boundaries. During the discourse, the actors were constrained by their individual knowledge, their particular tasks, their expected contribution to the co operation, and their involvement in the team through established practices. The team's interactions were constrained by established shared practices, or modes of working, and the available artifacts. In addition, dependencies arose between the various interrelated tasks of the team, the boundary conditions of the overal work arrangement, and the overall purpose of the cooperation.

We observed that, within this texture of relationships of differences, dependencies, and constraints, the team engages various artifacts to resolve the multiple differences and dependencies between their individual estimations and contributions, vis-à-vis the cooperative process. At this point the formation of boundary clusters becomes visible, as the actors attempt to transfer, translate or transform knowledge within the team Through an iterative process, multiple inferences eventually lead to re-evaluations, re-assessments, rede<sup>fi</sup>nitions or re-con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of cooperative tasks and processes as well as individual or shared practices.

## 5.3. The role of IT artifacts in boundary clusters

We observed that during the team discourse, novel information was documented with help of IT artifacts. In an iterative, dialectic process, this information was dynamically put in context during processes of knowledge transfer, translation and transformation across various knowledge boundaries, which involved other types of artifacts.

One relevant affordance of IT artifacts we observed was their capacity to carry information, or to represent knowledge about other elements of a work arrangement (knowledge transfer). Another affordance was their capacity as a means for knowledge translation and transformation. Within each iteration of the team discourse, a single IT artifact's potential affordance was re-de<sup>fi</sup>ned. This represents a dialectic, divergent use of IT artifacts which evolves as a function of previous uses (as seen particularly in Episode C). From this dialectic use we infer that—apart from practices—IT artifacts constitute a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant means to capture and stimulate novel insights in the team. This dialectic process substantiates persisting characteristics of a team that can be used in further iterations. We thus surmise that IT artifacts within boundary clusters act as containers for representing and merging coordinative capabilities of a particular cooperation.

## 5.4. Work arrangements, boundary clusters and IS

Our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms and extends recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of dialogs for knowledge sharing (Majchrzak, More, & Faraj, 2012:963) and inter-organizational cooperation (Hardy et al., 2003). Star (2010:611) writes, “(the) normally invisible quality of working infrastructure becomes visible when it breaks”. This notion of disorder is, to some extent, visible in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, where trigger incidents create new work requirements that highlight knowledge differences. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several such incidents that triggered cooperative episodes of knowledge exchanges. The perceived differences indicate “ill structured” arrangements (Star, 2010:613) or gaps in understanding, which the actors attempt to bridge. In this respect, boundary clusters represent one constituent in the “back and forth between ill structured and well structured” (Star, 2010:613) work arrangements.

In studying the characteristics of boundary clusters, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that they corroborate with concepts in prior literature on boundary object functionality, and extend it in several ways: First, boundary clusters come into existence during complex interactions between artifacts, actors, and work arrangements, involving actors' differing knowledge and interpretations of situations or contexts. Second, they temporarily gain materiality within distinct cooperative episodes. This materiality may dissolve with the re-balancing of work requirements, or the resolution of differences, in an episode. In our analysis, each cooperative episode culminated in distinct novel outcomes as coordination effects, such as the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of new tasks or processes. Third, boundary clusters are composed of several artifacts, and this composition as well as the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of artifacts, might change during an episode. In our analysis, we observed that the involved artifacts are subject to re<sup>fl</sup>ection and tailoring as well as changes and modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. These observations show that boundary clusters comply with the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions set forth by Star (2010), stating that boundary objects need to be “based in action”, and to be subject to re<sup>fl</sup>ection and local tailoring.

The boundary cluster concept helps explain how coordination can take place even though single artifacts may not enable coordination by themselves. It also helps explain coordinated interactions in the absence of intended coordination by the actors. In our understanding, the ‘coordinator’ is not a distinct entity, but an emerging process involving—and enabled by—boundary clusters. We see the functional role of boundary clusters as containers of coordinative capabilities of a particular cooperation. Awareness of the clusters' function may guide a systematic analysis of the outcomes of cooperative episodes in terms of developed practices, overcome differences, identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dependencies, or created novelties. Such awareness might open up opportunities to improve capabilities for cooperative interactions. In this respect, we observed substantial changes in the business approaches of involved organizations, e.g. an enlarged scope of the NPD project (Episode B) or the change in the business strategy of a partner <sup>fi</sup>rm.

The insight that IS often are part of a larger ‘bundle’ of resources is not new (Hanseth, Jacucci, Grisot, & Aanestad, 2006; Monteiro, Pollock, Hanseth, & Williams, 2012; Orlikowski, 1992). Our research offers one way to reconstruct how boundary clusters emerge from work arrangements that involve multiple (IT) artifacts. Future research needs to further investigate the challenges emerging in this respect (Butler & Murphy, 2007), bearing in mind that often a larger infrastructure of IS is employed to accomplish cooperative activities (Whyte, 2013). We have intentionally adopted the term “cluster” as part of the concept proposed by us, to retain terminological proximity to these models. As a further supposition, management processes for introducing, managing and using IS resources in teams ought to consider effects of boundary-spanning on coordination. This would guide considerations on how and which IT artifacts can support coordination in clusters that span multiple knowledge boundaries.

## 6. Conclusion

We see our major contributions in a <sup>fi</sup>rst conceptualization and de<sup>fi</sup>nition of boundary clusters; in describing their emergence in the context of inter-organizational innovation activities; and in exploring their role in facilitating knowledge exchanges. A boundary cluster is a set of artifacts which through collective action in combination obtain materiality as a boundary object. In our study we focus on an inter-organizational NPD team whose cooperation can be divided into cooperative episodes. These episodes were triggered by distinct incidents that created new work requirements for the team and initiated knowledge exchanges. The new requirements caused the team to re-evaluate the work or project situation. Starting from the triggers reported to us in the interviews we reconstructed the team discourse. The discourse can be described in relation to artifacts, their uses, and work arrangements, which lead to the materialization of boundary clusters.

One important question in IS research is how to support cooperative work in a way that viable relationships are reinforced and the inventiveness of actors is stimulated. Knowing how newly introduced artifacts, evolving practices, and external in<sup>fl</sup>uences impact the coordination of actors, knowledge exchanges and creation of new knowledge, could inform better development and selection of IS. In this respect, with our concept of boundary clusters we propose to take into account particular differences, dependencies and constraints in relation to a team's coordinative capabilities, and how these change during cooperation. This might enable to anticipate, attenuate, or amplify, the consequences of intentional actions or unexpected incidents on a project team—an idea which will need to be examined through further studies.

Future research on boundary clusters might shed new light on understanding better the management of cooperations in cases which thus far were believed to be broadly unstructured, or uncoordinated. An advance in management capabilities—and in design of IS infrastructures—for inter-organizational innovation could re sult from that. The presented <sup>fi</sup>ndings are proposed as a building block in this endeavor.
