# A conceptual framework for the implementation of enterprise information portals in large organizations

Rens Scheepers<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

Correspondence: Rens Scheepers, Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Victoria 3010, Australia. Tel: +61 3 8344 1575 Fax: +61 3 9349 4596 E-mail: r.scheepers@unimelb.edu.au

## Abstract

Addressing the diversity in information requirements across the enterprise is a central challenge in enterprise content management. Enterprise information portals (EIPs) hold promise in this regard, offering each user a gateway to customized and personalized content. However, in large organizations EIP implementers face the complexity of tailoring portals for potentially thousands of users. A conceptual framework based on marketing fundamentals is developed to inform EIP implementation in this respect. It is argued that EIP implementers should view their user community as a number of distinct segments in order to address divergent needs more specifically (as opposed to a ‘one size fits all’ approach). For each identified segment, a ‘mix’ of content (product), distribution (place), promotion and price (cost) should be considered. The framework is applied to two cases of EIP implementation in large organizations. It is proposed that segmentation enables better planning of the overall EIP implementation effort. Considerations such as the number of user segments, customization, and personalization of content, and the funding of EIP development in large organizations are also discussed. The concepts elaborated here also hold theoretical relevance for other IS implementation contexts that involve very large user communities with diverse information requirements.

## Introduction

Enterprise content management (ECM) is a comprehensive, integrated approach to information management, focusing on the information requirements of the entire organization (AIIM, 2005). As such, ECM also encompasses managerial strategies for the implementation of information technology infrastructure in support of information management across the enterprise (Pa¨iva¨rinta & Munkvold, 2005). As a promising element of ECM infrastructure, enterprise information portals (EIPs) offer each user a gateway to customized and personalized content (Shilakes & Tylman, 1998).

Considerations about supporting infrastructure such as EIPs are only part of an overall ECM strategy. However, as gateways to enterprise content, a poorly conceived EIP implementation strategy raises the risk of underutilization of portal functionality (Rose, 2003; Kakumanu & Mezzacca, 2005), dissatisfaction with content available via the portal (Detlor, 2000;

Benbya et al., 2004; McCubbrey et al., 2005) and even user resistance (Kim et al., 2002). Ultimately this could mean that the organizational benefits of ECM are not achieved (Smith & McKeen, 2003).

While a carefully considered EIP implementation strategy clearly has merit, formulating such a strategy is complex. First, from a content perspective, the increase in access and global connectivity made possible by Web technology has exacerbated the phenomenon of information overload faced by many workers (Turoff & Hiltz, 1998). Even a modest company intranet can contain in excess of tens of thousands of web pages. This complicates the decision making of what content to make accessible via the portal. Second, compared to information systems with a more specific focus, the scope of EIPs could span multiple communities within the organization. In large organizations, this could literally involve thousands of users with quite divergent information needs.

While some quite detailed recommendations and ‘action steps’ for EIP implementation have been documented (especially in industry sources), the following question remains: what are the fundamental considerations during the formulation of an EIP implementation strategy?

In this paper, the question is addressed by drawing on the concepts of Trumbly & Arnold (1989) who applied marketing fundamentals to the implementation of management information systems (MIS) in organizations. Trumbly & Arnold’s framework is extended to include the notion of target market segmentation (thus accommodating diverse user communities) and their original concepts are adapted to the EIP context. The extended conceptual framework is applied to retrospectively analyze two case studies of EIP implementation that were conducted in large organizations in Australia. Based on the application of the framework concepts to the cases, several propositions are derived for further research in other EIP contexts. The relevance of the framework concepts is discussed in terms of the implementation literature in general and implications for practice. Limitations of the study are also pointed out.

## Enterprise portals

To date, a commonly agreed portal definition has remained elusive in the academic as well as the industry literature. This situation is confounded by the number of commercial stakeholders with rival interests, product offerings, and divergent views in this market space. This paper builds on the recent definition of Smith (2004) who defines a portal as

an infrastructure providing secure, customizable, personalizable, integrated access to dynamic content from a variety of sources, in a variety of source formats, wherever it is needed (p. 94).

Aspects of this general portal definition are now further discussed and distinctions made between enterprise portals and related concepts.

Smith’s (2004) portal definition encompasses a diverse range of portals, including Public Internet portals (e.g. Yahoo!) and EIPs. Shilakes & Tylman (1998), acknowledged in the industry literature as being first to coin the concept, have defined EIPs as

applications that enable companies to unlock internally and externally stored information, and provide users a single gateway to personalized information needed to make informed business decisions.

EIPs aggregate access to related information content within and outside the enterprise (ibid.).

When applied within organizational contexts, it is argued that EIPs should provide members of the organization with access to relevant internally and externally sourced information, via a (Web-based) gateway which can be customized and personalized to meet individuals’ specific information requirements.

EIPs are related to, but conceptually different from other information technology infrastructure support for ECM. For example, intranets are also the application of Web-based technology for defined communities within the organization (Scheepers & Rose, 2001). Intranets, however, contain information content whereas portals (by definition), only provide points of access to such content (Chan & Chung, 2002; Daniel & Ward, 2005). As opposed to most intranet applications, portals also offer both customization and personalization facilities (van Brakel, 2003). These two portal attributes are often used interchangeably in the EIP literature (Coner, 2003). In this paper, customization is viewed as a supply-side function, that is, facilities that enable providers to tailor content for specific portal users or communities in line with their unique needs. Personalization as used in this paper refers to demand-side activities, that is, facilities that enable individual users to set specific preferences in terms of their own portal access (such as content interests, layout, and personal links). This distinction is consistent with many sources in the EIP literature (e.g. Kotorov & Hsu, 2001; Martin & Metcalfe, 2001; Hazra, 2002; van Brakel, 2003). This is also consistent with the business strategy literature where customization clearly refers to supply-side activities such as the mass-customization of products by vendors (e.g. Treacy & Wiersema, 1993; Ives & Piccoli, 2003). Lastly, intranets are mostly a ‘pull’ type organizational innovation (Zmud, 1984); users seek information on demand. Compared to most intranets, portal technology also allows content providers to ‘push’ information to targeted users or communities. For these reasons EIP access typically requires user identification (which is often not the case with intranet use) (van Brakel, 2003). Given such information about the user, EIPs can support additional functionality including role-based access to applications, and single logon to different systems (Detlor, 2000; Dias, 2001;

Kotorov & Hsu, 2001; Brosche´, 2002; Schwabe & Salim, 2002).

Portal technology is highly malleable and can be configured for a range of organizational purposes. For example, portals can be set up as ‘one-stop shops’ for relevant content about specific functional domains (Human resources, Marketing, etc.) or as ‘employee portals’ to address employee-specific interests (e.g. leave, payroll, benefits) (Munkvold, 2003a). Portal technology has also been positioned as supporting infrastructure for knowledge management (Schwabe & Salim, 2002; Firestone, 2003; Benbya et al., 2004). As such a complex portfolio of portals could exist within the enterprise, offering a plurality of services (Daniel & Ward, 2005) including access to content, applications, search functionality, and collaborative applications to different user communities.

While a sizeable body of literature exists (especially industry sources) on the potential advantages of EIPs for organizations, the contemporary nature of the phenomenon is reflected in the lack of reported empirical results on EIP implementation. Unanswered questions remain on how to address the content requirements of large communities of EIP users with diverse needs (White, 2000). Some sources have documented detailed ‘action steps’ for EIP implementation (e.g. Collins, 2003; Rose, 2003; van Brakel, 2003). While operational-level recommendations are useful, the conceptual principles behind these guidelines remain unclear, leaving questions about the applicability and adaptability of such advice to specific organizational contexts. From the perspectives of project and change management, some approaches relating to portal implementation have been proposed (e.g. Kotorov & Hsu, 2001; Chan & Chung, 2002; Weiss & Datta, 2002). However, more recent sources (e.g. Corbitt et al., 2005; Daniel & Ward, 2005; Kakumanu & Mezzacca, 2005; McCubbrey et al., 2005) suggest that EIP implementation may be more complex than anticipated earlier. For example, Daniel and Ward (2005) propose that EIPs should not be conceptualized as ‘single’ innovations, but rather as a range of distinct services and functionality that need to be coherently combined as part of an organizational implementation strategy. While a rich body of research literature exists on implementation approaches of related IT innovations such as intranets (e.g. Bansler et al., 2000; Damsgaard & Scheepers, 2000), the plurality of EIP services (Daniel & Ward, 2005), diversity in user interests (McCubbrey et al., 2005), utilization concerns (Rose, 2003; Kakumanu & Mezzacca, 2005) and the sheer scale of EIP implementation (Mack et al., 2001) suggest the need for a richer conceptual framework in the case of EIP implementation.

## Framework development

In this section a conceptual framework is developed to inform the implementation of EIPs in large organizations. It is argued that the divergent and unique needs of various user communities within large organizations represent an internal market that EIP implementers should consider. The conceptual framework draws on fundamentals from the marketing discipline that inform marketers’ decision-making. An approach based on marketing fundamentals can be justified, given the largely volitional nature of EIP use and the focus of this paper on informing (supply-side) implementation decision-making.

Below, two fundamental concepts from marketing theory are discussed: the marketing mix and target market segmentation. Trumbly & Arnold (1989) previously applied the marketing mix to inform MIS implementation. The conceptual framework developed here, builds on Trumbly & Arnold’s interpretation of the marketing mix for MIS, but adapts their original concepts for EIPs. Furthermore, Trumbly & Arnold assumed a homogenous internal market in the context of MIS users. To accommodate the diversity and potential size of EIP user communities, their original framework is extended, again drawing on marketing theory, via the additional notion of target market segmentation.

The resulting conceptual EIP framework is summarized in Table 1 at the end of this section.

## The marketing mix

The marketing mix is seen as one of the key concepts in modern marketing and represents all the elements that the firm needs to integrate to generate a desired response in its chosen target market (Kotler et al., 2002). Borden’s (1964) seminal article established the concept of the marketing mix, originally including the following considerations: product, pricing, branding, distribution, personal selling, advertising, promotions, packaging, display, servicing, physical handling, fact finding and analysis. McCarthy (1971) condensed the concept of the marketing mix into the so-called ‘4P’ framework: Product, Place, Promotion, and Price.

Product includes decisions about the product itself, features, design, brand name, product lines, and packaging; the essential concern is to develop the right product for the target market. In this sense product also includes services or product/service ‘bundles’ (see also Freiden et al., 1998). Place deals with decisions such as where, when and by whom the product will be delivered. This includes considerations such as distribution channels, locations, and transportation. Promotion encompasses all methods of communicating with the target market to persuade customers to buy the product/service (e.g. advertising, sales promotions, personal selling, public relations). Lastly, Price represents considerations about the ‘right’ price for the target market (list price, discounts, credit terms) (McCarthy, 1971; Kotler et al., 2002). Despite some criticism (e.g. van Waterschoot & van den Bulte, 1992) the 4P’s remain one of the most widely used frameworks of the marketing mix of the firm (Kotler et al., 2002).

While the mentioned marketing strategy decisions are most often associated with addressing the needs of an external target market, similar considerations have been voiced with respect to the firm’s internal market, that is, business units or individual employees of the organization. Within the field of organizational behavior, the concept of the internal customer is seen as instrumental in ensuring effective internal exchanges between coworkers that ultimately benefit the external customer (George, 1990; Tansuhaj et al., 1991). It has been argued that many in-house services such as information systems, market research, training, and other staff functions can improve their overall service quality when the recipients of these services within the organization are treated as (internal) customers (Harrell & Fors, 1995; Parasuraman et al., 1988; Jayasuriya, 1998; Watson et al., 1998). As such, the needs of the internal target market should be considered in a similar manner as those of the external customer base. The internal marketing approach blends well with scenarios of voluntary information systems use where it is up to the implementers of such systems to persuade their potential user base of the merits of the system.

## The marketing mix in the context of MIS

Trumbly & Arnold (1989) applied the 4P marketing mix to MIS implementation, viewing MIS users as an internal market. Their interpretation of the marketing mix in an MIS context is as follows.

Product Trumbly & Arnold do not regard the product as the MIS (technology) itself, but rather the information content of the system (their view in this respect is consistent with other sources; e.g. Pasqueletto, 1989; Freiden et al., 1998). This view is based on the fact that a manager would only use the MIS because it provides information outputs which, for example, are more timely, more accurate and in a better format than can be obtained by other methods. Drawing on the principles of internal marketing, the aim is to satisfy the information needs of the user as customer. As such, the particular technology that delivers this information is largely the means to this end and not an end itself.

Viewing the product as the information content rather than as the system itself has important implications for other aspects of the internal marketing process. It is widely argued in the marketing literature that support services should be offered to augment the value of the product as perceived by the customer. Services such as training and user support should thus be tailored to help users extract more value from the product. This view is consistent with sources on groupware implementation (e.g. Orlikowski, 1996) who have argued for an emphasis on value during training (e.g. exploring how a system could transform ways of working) as opposed to only ‘mechanistic’ training on systems features.

Place (distribution) Trumbly & Arnold (1989) argue that place addresses the distribution of the information product to users (the target market). To maximize the user experience from a distribution perspective, it must be convenient and easy for users to access the product. Implementers should also consider if users have adequate facilities to access the information product. This includes the necessary computing infrastructure, printing, etc., as well as adequate training and support to utilize this infrastructure (ibid.). These aspects correspond to considerations such as usability and facilitating conditions in the technology acceptance literature (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

Promotion Implementers of information systems should consider a variety of mechanisms to promote the merits of the information product to the wider user community. It is often the case that potential users/user communities are unaware of the existence of a particular system. Promotion could include personal selling (e.g. presentations to targeted users, opinion leaders) and non-personal selling (e.g. newsletters, internal advertising campaigns) (Trumbly & Arnold, 1989).

Price (cost) Finally, applying the concept of price to the implementation of MIS in organizations encompasses considerations such as internal charges associated with the cost of development and delivery of information technology and services (ibid.). These typically include the costs associated with systems development, computing and networking infrastructure, content development, and maintenance. Trumbly & Arnold advocate the negotiation of internal chargeback arrangements to recoup such costs from business units (ibid.).

## The marketing mix adapted for EIPs

Drawing on Trumbly & Arnold’s concepts, the 4P marketing mix is adapted to an EIP context as follows:

Product (content) In line with Trumbly & Arnold’s (1989) interpretation, the product in the EIP context is regarded as the information content and not the technology itself. Given the phenomenon of information overload (the raison d’eˆtre for portals), the product is, however, more narrower defined as relevant and related information content, applications, etc., taking into account the user’s role(s) in the organization. While portals, by definition, do not contain content per se, from the user’s perspective content and access to that content are indistinguishable. For example, poor quality content accessed via the portal will likely detract from the user’s overall portal use experience. Hence, consistent with Trumbly & Arnold, it is argued that EIP technology is a means to an end – that end being relevant content, ultimately judged by individual users. The scope of EIP implementation thus encompasses both technology and information content considerations. In this respect, EIP implementers face the challenge of responding systematically and economically to the diverse content needs of large user communities. The notion of target market segmentation, discussed below, offers a mechanism for EIP implementers in this regard.

A slight departure is made from Smith’s (2004) portal definition in suggesting that the EIP product is not necessarily ‘integrated’ content, but rather related content. The modern organizational computing landscape (especially in large, complex organizations) is often characterized by numerous information systems, databases, communication technologies, and different computing platforms. Complete integration between all these components is often impractical and too costly (see also the discussion of price below). The product view adopted here implies that related information content could be aggregated for the user, taking the role of the individual user into account for relevancy. While some of this may in fact be truly integrated behind the scene, this need not necessarily be the case. For example, a portal could aggregate access to the organization’s leave application system, leave policies, and external links to holiday destinations, none of which may be integrated (this corresponds to the notion of ‘artificial systems integration’ – see Firestone, 2003).

The view of the product as content holds a number of implications for EIP implementers in deciding about the features and design of portals in their specific organizational contexts. Portal technology enables providers to customize content for user communities (even masscustomization for individual users), as well as personalization features that end users themselves can tailor. Marketing principles, however, dictate that implementers should make such design considerations against the backdrop of the rest of the marketing mix and the characteristics of their target market. For example, the degree of customization should be considered in terms of whether this would indeed increase the value of the information content for a specific target community, if the associated costs are acceptable, and if such benefits can be effectively promoted.

Place (distribution) Consistent with Trumbly & Arnold, it is argued that place addresses the distribution of information content to the target market. In this respect, EIP implementers need to consider the distribution of content on both a pull and push basis, usability of the portal interface, as well as issues such as training and support to enable users to utilize portal functionality effectively. Distribution also encompasses convenience considerations. For example, in the era of nomadic work (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002) that would include aspects such as remote or wireless access to portals.

In terms of relevancy of the information product, push and pull modes of content distribution should be carefully considered. Too much pushing of content via the portal could lead users to experience information overload, or that some content is irrelevant. On the other hand, inadequate mechanisms for users to locate (pull) information via the portal could diminish the perceived value of the product.

Promotion Consistent with Trumbly & Arnold’s advice, EIP implementers should consider a variety of promotional avenues, both personal and non-personal, first to raise product awareness in the potential user community, and subsequently to communicate the benefits associated with portal use. Marketing techniques such as branding of the portal could prove useful in promotional messages.

Once product awareness is established, the marketing literature stresses that promotion should focus on the benefits of the product to the customer and not merely on its features (e.g. Kotler et al., 2002). This approach to promotional objectives maps onto the individual innovation-decision process (Rogers, 1995). At the onset, potential adopters of complex innovations require what Rogers terms ‘software information’, that is, information about the innovation itself and its capacity to solve an individual’s problem. Later in the decision process, the individual seeks ‘innovation-evaluation information’ about the innovation’s advantages and disadvantages pertinent to his/her situation. The innovation-decision process culminates in an adoption/rejection decision (ibid.).

Price (cost) In line with Trumbly & Arnold’s interpretation, the price element in the framework encompasses the internal costing arrangements associated with portal and content development, including support services. Weill & Broadbent (1998) have argued that shared IT infrastructure (which could include EIP infrastructure) in organizations should largely be funded centrally to lower costs by harnessing economies of scale and shared expertise across the firm. Similarly, unit-specific requirements should typically be charged back to the individual business unit (ibid.). These arguments are also expected to apply to the EIP context.

## Target market segmentation

As mentioned, Trumbly & Arnold assumed a homogenous internal target market of users in the case of MIS. One of the central challenges in ECM concerns addressing the diverse information requirements of large numbers of users across the enterprise. This challenge also manifests at the level of supporting IT infrastructure, such as EIPs (cf. Mack et al., 2001; McCubbrey et al., 2005). It is hence necessary to extend the Trumbly & Arnold framework to enable a large, heterogeneous user community to be subdivided.

Market segmentation represents a fundamental concept enabling marketers to consider the divergent needs of a large, heterogeneous market. Marketers typically subdivide such a target market into different, more homogenous, segments (Treacy & Wiersema, 1993). Segmentation enables marketers to focus more specifically on each segment’s unique attributes (needs, profitability, etc.) in terms of an appropriate marketing mix for that particular segment (Kotler et al., 2002). Segmentation also enables prioritization; given resource, access constraints and profitability, segmentation helps marketers decide which segments to target first, etc.

Following this logic, the community of potential EIP users in a large organization can be regarded as a heterogeneous internal target market whose divergent needs are to be addressed during implementation. This target market is best conceived as consisting of a number of distinct market segments. Each market segment represents a grouping of users with relatively homogenous, though unique, content needs. Segmentation as a means of dealing with user diversity has been applied previously to IT, for example, in the area of humancomputer interaction (e.g. Degen, 2000).

Segmentation of the target market implies that EIP implementers can more specifically consider the diverse needs of these distinct groupings of users. Overall implementation activities can be prioritized by targeting the needs of key segments first. Over time additional segments could be added as the implementation progresses and initial priorities are met, or as additional segments emerge over time. Also, larger segments can subsequently be subdivided to respond more specifically to needs. In segmenting the target market, it should however be noted that some potential users may likely belong to more than one segment (e.g. to a particular business unit, and also a project team). In the EIP context, technical mechanisms such as role-based access would typically support such scenarios.

## Application of the framework

In this section the conceptual framework for EIP implementation is applied to two case studies of EIP implementation that were conducted in large organizations. The evidence from the cases was retrospectively analyzed using the framework. The names AusBank and AusUni (both pseudonyms) are used for the case organizations.

## Data collection for the cases

Case study research is especially appropriate in new topic areas (Eisenhardt, 1989) and is a research strategy that allows for an in-depth description of the relationships in a particular situation (Darke et al., 1998; Dube´ & Pare´, 2003). In this research, the case study method enabled an in-depth exploration into EIP implementation and a detailed assessment of the theoretical concepts in the framework. Sources such as Benbasat et al. (1987) and Yin (2003) have confirmed that the case study method is well justified in these respects.

These two case organizations were selected for the following reasons. First, the organizations had large-scale EIP portal implementations (in terms of user base) at the time of this study. Second, both organizations had been implementing portal technology for some time, and hence some outcomes from their experiences could also be examined. Lastly, these two organizations enabled an illustration of the framework to both off-the-shelf and inhouse developed portal environments as elaborated below.

The data for this study was collected during 2003, with some informal follow-up data collection in 2004. Interviews with implementers of the technology in the two organizations formed the main source of data. In total five interviews (three at AusBank and two at AusUni) were conducted with senior EIP implementers. In addition to the formal interviews, informal meetings and e-mail contact formed further sources of data. Given the focus on EIP implementation, the most senior manager directly responsible for the implementation of the portals in each organization was interviewed. In addition, members of staff who were mainly involved with the project management and technical aspects of the EIP implementation in both organizations were also interviewed.

The formal interviews lasted between 60 and 90 min each. The interviews were semi-structured and comprised of open-ended questions which were derived from the implementation literature and similar research studies. These questions were structured using an interview guideline for use by the interviewers. The formal interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. To enable more thorough and efficient analysis of the case study data, qualitative analysis software (Atlas.ti) was used.

The process of data analysis was retrospective, seeking to replicate findings between the cases (Yin, 2003, p. 50). The conceptual EIP framework was used as theoretical device to examine the case study evidence. The framework was also used to consider some ‘what if’ questions arising from problems reported by the interviewees or anticipated in the cases. Care was taken to consider all pertinent case study evidence to reduce the risk of researcher bias. In each case, contextual data that could further explain the observations made were taken into account (Klein & Myers, 1999). In addition, the framework concepts and analysis of evidence in the cases were discussed with fellow researchers as well as EIP practitioners from other organizations.

Below, some background to each case is presented followed by an analysis of the case study evidence according to the concepts in the framework.

## AusBank

AusBank is a large Australian banking group (approximately 24,000 employees in total) with representation in New Zealand as well as the U.K. Around 19,000 of their employees have access to the portal environment. AusBank implemented commercial off-the-shelf portal software procured from a well-known international vendor. A ‘first proof of concept’ was made available in November 2002. The implementation team consisted of 28 members of staff (managerial and technical).

## AusUni

AusUni is a large public university in Australia. Their implementation reflects a typical campus portal targeting around 50,000 students and members of staff. In 2003, approximately 95% of the students and 30% of staff had access to the portal environment. AusUni developed their own portal environment, having significant development skills within their IT section. The implementation team consisted of a project manager, eight technical development staff and five members of staff in their ‘client relations’ team.

## Target market segmentation

The AusBank implementation team initially identified seven portal user communities, including specific business units, frontline banking staff, and the general employee community as a whole. Their planning was to target additional communities over time. This is consistent with the framework in terms of segmentation of the target market and prioritizing the needs of key segments.

In the AusUni case, the target market was only segmented into the staff and student communities (with the alumni community as a possible future consideration).

Both AusUni and AusBank employed specific mechanisms to determine the needs of their EIP customers. AusUni ran surveys, held focus group meetings and did phone interviews with key users. The AusUni portal implementers also interacted directly with key staff and student users via the client relations team. The AusBank team operated similarly and worked closely with representatives in specific business units.

## Product (content)

In both cases evidence was found that is consistent with the view in the framework that information content is the product, with portal technology being the means to that end. An AusBank implementer stated:

The portal has no content per se, but since the portal is the door to that content, it needs to be managed properly to be useful, otherwise people won’t bother with the portal in the first case.

An AusUni implementer made a similar observation:

It’s about hiding the technologyy

The following comments from implementers are consistent with the definition of the information product as relevant and related information content and applications, taking into account the user’s role(s) in the organization. An AusUni implementer remarked:

The staff portal is a one stop service to the relevant web-based resources you are likely to need while at [AusUni].

In both cases, evidence was found that customization and personalization were seen as important mechanisms to help ensure the relevancy of portal content. An AusBank implementer summarized:

Today it is pretty much one view doesn’t fit everyone.

This view was echoed by an AusUni implementer:

y to get rid of the stuff [users] never wanted to see and just put in the stuff that they used frequently.

The evidence further suggests that in both cases implementers saw (supply-side) customization as more effective in terms of ensuring content relevance than users’ personalization of content. An AusUni implementer observed:

It’s very much better for us [implementers] to try and get it right than it is for fifty thousand students and staff trying to make their portals work for them.

## An AusBank implementer stated:

[Portals]y bring together all the things relevant to you in a web environmentyThe whole idea with the portal technology is that once you [log in] and we [implementers] know who you are, we can actually target the view.

Indeed, at AusUni implementers recorded statistics on the number of users who chose to personalize portal content (e.g. add/remove different sources of content). At the time of this study (nearly 2 years after the portals were launched), their statistics showed that only 6% of users did so.

An efficient approach to the aggregation of related content via the EIP was noted at AusBank. They developed a generic aggregation profile that included collaboration tools (e-mail, discussion groups), transactional tools (access to systems, data bases), and personal utilities (e.g. leave and pay details). The particular content a worker would see via the portal would depend on her role. For example, the portal for a relationship banker would include access to customer details, legal databases, and personal utilities, while cashiers’ portals would include access to transactional systems and personal utilities. This approach enabled the AusBank implementation team to ensure related content is made available via the portal pertinent to a worker’s role.

The view that information content is the product was also reflected in the composition of the steering groups, which oversaw the implementation in each organization. In both cases, members of the steering groups included key representatives who had the organizational responsibility for content and applications in their specific environments. Close collaboration between these representatives and EIP implementers was evident in both cases, underpinning the view that the scope of EIP implementation encompasses both technology and content considerations.

## Place (distribution)

In both cases evidence was found that the portal implementers were considering how and where portal content was distributed.

In terms of the balance between ‘push’ and ‘pull’ modes of content distribution some differences were noted in the approaches followed in the cases. At AusUni, the approach was to limit the pushing of content in favor of content pull by users. An AusUni implementer noted:

Lean is good. So if you fill it up with that kind of junk, people go ‘well, this is irrelevant to me; I’m outta here.’ One of the big values with the portal is what we call narrowcasting, which is the opposite of broadcastingy

AusBank followed a different approach. Some content was deemed important (‘not negotiable’) and was pushed to users by default (taking their role into account). Access to most of the other content was distributed on either a push or pull basis, and individual users could personalize that further.

In both cases, implementers ensured that sufficient computing and networking infrastructure existed for users to access the portal. Apart from providing regular desktop access in AusBank, the organization also installed a number of kiosks around their headquarters for additional access. At AusUni, wireless access to portals via mobile phones and personal digital assistants was on the agenda at the time of this research. An AusUni implementer elaborated on their vision in this regard, in line with the notions of convenient access, role awareness, and portal technology as a means to an end in the framework:

So [students] don’t have to think ‘I’m using something from the faculty of engineering, because that’s where I’m a student. And now I’ve gone to the library, but that’s a different organization and their website works all differently.’ But just to say ‘I’m doing this, and whether that service came from this area or that area I didn’t know.’

## Promotion

The implementers in both cases engaged in various personal and non-personal promotional activities to foster awareness of the portal product in their organizations. AusUni ran promotional events aimed at both staff and students during the launch of their portal. In this respect, specific brand names were established for their staff and student portals, which were used in communication with these user communities. AusBank pursued promotional activities in line with marketing principles that suggest focusing on product benefits and not only product features. An AusBank implementer elaborated:

We have internal workshops to say what the capabilities are that we delivery. We also have case studies to show to business units what [they] get out of it. We have open day presentations where we talk about how we engage, what the solution is, and what the benefits arey

## Price (cost)

Both AusBank and AusUni funded their portal developments centrally at the time of this research. AusBank, however, indicated that they were starting to consider charging back the costs of portal development to business units, especially for business-unit-specific needs.

In terms of the organizational funding for portal development costs, some key challenges emerged from the cases. First, users often only realize the benefits of the portal concept once they actually see it in action. Hence it seems beneficial to fund some initial upfront pilot development centrally to show users the possibilities and gain commitment, and thereafter to consider other funding arrangements. An AusUni implementer noted:

In the portal environment, we’ve got stakeholders saying ‘yeah, yeah that’s really what we wanted’, by the time it gets to resource the second part, we’ve got more stakeholders, so more people are willing to put up money and say, ‘yes, this is something that should be done.’

Second, as an AusUni implementer summarized, tradeoffs may need to be made between what users ideally would like to have, and the reality of funding such developments:

Sometimes [users] do say they want big, big things and they don’t really have the follow throughy

## Discussion

The two cases highlight some of the complexity faced by EIP implementers in tailoring IT support to address the content needs of large, diverse communities of users across the enterprise. Implications of the framework are now discussed in terms of further research and the contribution to the implementation literature in general. The practical implications of the framework as well as the limitations of this research are also pointed out.

## Implications for further research

This paper extended the Trumbly & Arnold framework by including the notion of target market segmentation to accommodate diversity in user requirements, and by adapting their original ideas for the specifics of EIP implementation. The vocabulary and conceptual ideas provided by the resulting framework, as well as the considerations emerging from the two cases, point to even further theoretical development in this research area. Below, a number of propositions are derived to serve as a basis for further research in other EIP implementation contexts. The propositions are formulated in a manner to enable subsequent confirmation/refutation in follow-up research (cf. Dubin, 1969; Whetten, 1989).

Incorporating the notion of segmentation, in addition to Trumbly & Arnold’s concepts, is a crucial evolution in applying marketing fundamentals to the implementation of collaborative technologies that address diverse content requirements across the enterprise. The EIP framework implies that implementers should consider their user community (target market) as a number of distinct segments, each with their own unique needs. Indeed, in both cases EIP implementation was approached by considering the needs of different user communities as opposed to a ‘one size fits all approach’. For each identified user segment, an appropriate mix of product (content), place (distribution), promotion, and price should be considered (see Table 1). A portfolio of different portals may need to be implemented in line with the divergent needs of user segments, especially in large organizations (this corresponds to different ‘product lines’ in marketing terms). It also means that portal implementation could unfold in different stages, depending on segment priorities across the organization (cf.

Munkvold, 1999). This is consistent with the argument that EIPs should not be viewed as ‘single’ innovations (Daniel & Ward, 2005). In addition, given resource constraints, it is argued that segmentation also represents a useful mechanism for implementers when prioritizing EIP implementation activities, for example, by first considering the needs of key segments.

Proposition 1a: Segmentation of a large, heterogeneous user community into smaller, more homogenous segments enables better management of the overall EIP implementation effort.

Marketing principles would suggest that very large segments be further subdivided, given the likelihood of divergent information needs in such segments. For example, in the AusUni case more precision would have been gained if their staff segment were further subdivided, for example, to respond more specifically to the different information needs of academic and general staff. The same applies if their student segment were also subdivided to reflect the divergent needs of undergraduate and postgraduate students. In comparison, the finer segmentation at AusBank suggests more homogeneity in the content of the different types of workers in the segments identified. The gain in precision associated with smaller, but more homogenous segments thus reduces the risk that some users would eventually perceive content to be irrelevant, given their role in the organization.

At the same time, gains in precision should be considered against the increased cost in servicing segments, since each additional segment requires its own marketing mix. In the extreme case, each user could represent a unique ‘segment’, but in large organizations servicing the specific content needs of every individual would be prohibitively expensive. Indeed, the size of the portal implementation teams in the cases appears to be related to the number of segments chosen (taking into account the total number of users in each organization). In terms of the number of market segments, implementers thus need to strike a balance between the gains in precision associated with more, but smaller market segments, and the increase in cost associated with serving many diverse segments.

Proposition 1b: Progressively finer segmentation of the user community increases the precision with which users’ content requirements can be addressed.

Proposition 1c: Progressively finer segmentation of the user community increases the overall cost of the EIP implementation effort.

Comments made by EIP implementers in the cases support the arguments that portal technology (as a means to an end) is largely transparent to users, and that the technology itself contains no content. At the same time, the product view in the framework implies that content considerations are fundamental to the overall EIP implementation effort. Successful implementation of a content-based product delivered via the portal thus necessitates cooperation between those responsible for content and applications in the organization, and those responsible for the portal technology. Examples of such cooperation were noted in both cases, including cooperation in formal structures such as steering groups. Also noted in the cases was that members of both implementation teams actively engaged with user representatives in the different segments they identified.

Proposition 2: Close cooperation between implementers, content providers and user representatives is necessary for the success of the overall EIP implementation effort.

Viewing the product as information content delivered via the portal suggests that supply-side customization and personalization by users represent mechanisms to increase the perceived relevancy of content and thus the overall ‘product value’. Evidence was also noted in both cases to suggest that (supply-side) customization may be more effective in terms of ensuring relevancy of content compared to users own personalization, especially in the early stages of implementation. The low level of personalization in the AusUni case seems to be in line with marketing principles suggesting customers expect suppliers to deliver a product to them that meets their needs, without the customer having to ‘fix’ it afterwards. However, the level of personalization may increase in the longer run as awareness of portal features spreads. For example, past research into groupware support has shown that users often adhere to initial system defaults (Palen & Grudin, 2003).

Proposition 3a: Appropriate customization and personalization increases the relevancy of content as perceived by users.

Proposition 3b: Customization has a stronger influence than personalization on the perceived relevancy of content, especially in the early stages of implementation.

In the cases, the use of both pull and push modes of content distribution was noted. In the AusUni case the preference was for minimal pushing of content to users, whereas AusBank utilized pushing more (although only for important, user-specific content). Only follow-up research can reveal the full effect of these choices. However, this hints at the existence of an inverted Ushaped relationship between the level of content pushing via the portal, and relevancy of the content as perceived by users. A strategy of zero pushing implies that users themselves are responsible to pull all content to the portal. Such a strategy will likely result in very low perceptions of content relevancy; in fact, users may be completely unaware of the existence of content that is relevant to them. Hence, a moderate level of (pertinent) content pushing would likely be reflected in increased perceptions of content relevancy. However, if the level of content pushing is further increased, it is likely that users will become overwhelmed and eventually perceive some content to be irrelevant. As implied by the comments made in the AusUni case, a very high level of content pushing could even result in user resistance.

Proposition 4: A curvilinear relationship exists between the level of content ‘pushing’ and the relevancy of content as perceived by users.

At the onset of implementation the majority of potential users will likely be unaware of what an EIP is. As such, EIP implementers first need to establish the portal concept in the minds of their potential users. This corresponds to providing ‘software information’ (Rogers, 1995) about the innovation itself in order to trigger individual innovation-decision processes. In the cases implementers used both personal and non-personal promotional avenues to foster awareness of the portals in their organizations. This included the establishment of ‘brand names’ for the portals. As awareness increases over time, the need for continued promotion of the EIP concept diminishes. Once awareness is established, marketing principles advocate an emphasis on product benefits, rather than features, in promotional messages that seek to persuade potential users to evaluate and eventually adopt the product. This corresponds to adopters’ need for ‘innovation-evaluation’ information in the later stages of the innovation-decision process (Rogers, 1995). As such, EIP implementers should then base their promotional messages on the individual benefits associated with portal use (e.g. reduction in information overload, time savings because relevant information is in one place) in their communication with the potential user community.

Proposition 5a: Promotion of the EIP concept is necessary to generate awareness during the early stages of implementation.

Proposition 5b: Promotion based on the benefits associated with EIP use is effective in encouraging user adoption in the subsequent stages of implementation.

The price element in the framework encompasses the internal costing arrangements associated with portal and content development. Current perspectives in the literature suggest that shared IT services in organizations should be funded centrally, while unit-specific requirements should be charged back to business units (Weill & Broadbent, 1998). The same logic holds in the EIP context, however, two complexities emerge. First, as the AusUni case illustrates, a dilemma arises since it was only after key individuals experienced the portal concept that they would commit to further funding. As such, centrally funded pilot initiatives appear necessary to generate user interest in the early stages of implementation. Second, as understanding of the portal concept spreads and different market segments start articulating their own unique requirements for further development, subsequent funding approaches (e.g. chargeback) could be considered as in the AusBank case. However, segments may span multiple business units, and specific users could belong to more than one segment. This could result in overly complex chargeback formulas to apportion EIP costs either centrally or to specific business units. It may be simpler to consider chargeback costs for each EIP initiative on a case-by-case basis.

Proposition 6a: Central funding for pilot EIP initiatives is necessary to generate user interest in the early stages of implementation.

Proposition 6b: Considering EIP developments on a case-by-case basis will simplify internal costing arrangements in the later stages of implementation.

## Contribution to the IT implementation literature

A rich body of literature exists on IT implementation in organizational contexts, including process, factor, diffusion, individual, change, and political perspectives (e.g. Markus, 1983; Kwon & Zmud, 1987; Srinivasan & Davis, 1987; Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Lucas et al., 1990; Galliers & Sutherland, 1991; Sabherwal & Robey, 1993; Marble, 2000; Munkvold, 2003b). While documented IT implementation approaches are diverse, Munkvold (2003b) has argued they can often broadly be typified as either topdown or bottom-up. Top-down approaches tend to be more aligned with the strategic direction of the organization, but users may perceive such approaches as management ‘dictates’, raising the risk of user resistance and underutilization (ibid.). Bottom-up implementation approaches are more driven by individual needs, but run the risk of being uncoordinated due to a lack of management support (ibid., see also Ciborra, 2000; Lamb & Davidson, 2000; Scheepers & Rose, 2001). The conceptual framework presented here suggests some fruitful ‘middle ground’ exists between top-down and bottom-up extremes. The EIP framework blends elements of a top-down, planned implementation approach with marketing concepts to enable a more precise focus on the needs of specific user communities in the organization. This underpins the interdisciplinary ties that exist between Marketing and IS in the case of large numbers of customers (users) with diverse needs.

## Implications for practice

The framework offers a ‘meta-level’ planning structure for implementers who are responsible for the overall organizational EIP implementation strategy. Concepts in the framework could usefully be applied during the onset of strategy formulation (e.g. when segments are decided upon and prioritized), but also to periodically consider if implementation activities (during different stages for various segments) are still consistent with the fundamental concepts.

Decisions about the nature and range of content, degree of sophistication, support for personalization, etc., should be taken against the backdrop of the other framework elements, especially price, aiming for an appropriate mix for each particular segment. Certain implementation considerations, however, apply to more than one segment. For example in the AusBank case, the generic content profile was developed that could be configured and re-used for different user segments thus harnessing internal ‘economies of scale’ (cf. Marshall, 1920). The same applies to promotion which could apply to multiple user segments at the same time.

Like good marketers, portal implementers should seek to gain as much feedback from their target market as possible. This of course means frequent interaction with key users to identify emerging needs (as noted in both the AusBank and AusUni cases). In addition, implementers should consider collecting statistics about various aspects of portal use to enhance the product over time.

The intent with the framework is not to present a universal approach that any organization or portal implementation team must mechanistically follow. Rather, the framework is positioned as a conceptual device for EIP implementers to reflect on their overall approach and systematically consider an appropriate implementation strategy. The application of the framework should be cognizant of the uniqueness of each organizational context, culture, and political dynamics.

## Limitations

The research has the following limitations. First, the focus was specifically on the supply-side considerations given the paper’s objective to inform EIP implementers’ decision-making. Further research is needed to explore the effect of such decisions on the demand-side in terms of user experiences and satisfaction. Second, while the interrelationships between the 4P elements in the framework were illustrated, the dynamics between the four elements were not fully investigated. Further research should for example examine which combinations between the components are most appropriate in fostering the implementation process. Third, the present cases represent large, hierarchical organizations with user bases numbering in the thousands. Further research is needed to assess the applicability of the framework in other contexts, for example, smaller and/or differently structured organizations. Fourth, the developed framework seems to be applicable irrespective of how the portal technology is sourced (built or bought). However, further research is needed to investigate possible nuances associated with the sourcing of the technology such as price/ product/place tradeoffs pertaining to different sourcing options. Last, the case evidence reported in this paper pertained largely to intra-organizational users. Further research should examine the applicability of the framework in the case of extra-organizational user segments.

## Conclusion

As a key component of ECM infrastructure, EIPs hold much promise, but also present implementers with a high degree of complexity. Drawing on marketing fundamentals, a conceptual framework was developed to inform the decision-making of EIP implementers. The framework was applied to two case studies of EIP implementation in large organizations. Based on the framework concepts and cases, several propositions were formulated for further research in other EIP contexts.

EIPs represent a means to an end; that end being relevant and related content, taking into account users’ roles in the organization.
