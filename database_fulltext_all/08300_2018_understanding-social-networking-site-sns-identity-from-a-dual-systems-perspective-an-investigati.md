# Understanding social networking site (SNS) identity from a dual systems perspective: an investigation of the dark side of SNS use

Greta L. Polites<sup>a</sup>, Christina Serrano<sup>b</sup>, Jason Bennett Thatcher<sup>c</sup> and Kevin Matthews<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management & Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA; <sup>b</sup>Computer Information Systems Department, College of Business, Colorado State University, Collins, CO, USA; <sup>c</sup>Management Department, College o Business , Alabama University , Alabama , SC , USA

## ABSTRACT

Using a pathological perspective (e.g., addiction), some research suggests that technology engagement leads to poor self-regulation of behaviours. Rather than pathologise predictors of negative outcomes of IT use, we ofer an alternative, identity-based explanation to investigate the role of identity in relation to an individual’s perceptions of his/her media use and the potential negative consequences of such use. We build on recent advances in conceptualising IT identity, defined as the extent to which one perceives an IT as central to who he/she is, to explore one’s self-identification with social networking sites (SNS) within a broader nomological net of deficient self-regulation, IS habit, use, and normative pressures. Results of a survey of 214 college students indicate that if an individual has strong self-identification with a particular SNS, it will increase perceptions of poor self-control over his/her time. Our study contributes to the literature by highlighting the utility of an identity-based (vs. pathology-based) explanation for such outcomes, which has distinct implications for interventions aimed at improving these outcomes. We suggest future research to advance understanding of IT identity’s antecedents and consequences, and discuss practical implications for managing SNS use in workplace and academic settings.

## 1. Introduction

A growing literature examines the “dark side” of information systems (IS) use and highlights the negative consequences of IS use, such as technostress (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011), addiction (Turel & Serenko, 2012; Xu, Turel, & Yuan, 2012), social overload (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2014), and work–life conflict (Boswell & Olson-Buchanan, 2007). Some scholars also point to negative forms of IS use, such as problematic Internet use (Caplan, 2010) and dangerous mobile phone use (e.g., texting while driving) (Steelman, Soror, Limayem, & Worrell, 2012). By studying the “dark side” of IS, scholarship contributes to a better understanding of factors that predict negative consequences of IS use and informs the design of appropriate interventions to prevent and mitigate these negative outcomes.

Much “dark side” research has focused on psychopathologies or maladaptive factors (e.g., addiction, obsessive-compulsive traits) to predict negative IS use and outcomes, particularly in the context of social networking site (SNS) use. For example, Krasnova, Widjaja, Buxmann, Wenninger, and Benbasat (2015) found that

SNS use can lead to feelings of envy, a negative emotion that in turn reduces one’s well-being. Turel (2015) describes the “vicious cycle” of SNS use: SNS use leads to SNS addiction, which fuels even more SNS use and a stronger SNS addiction. Rosen, Whaling, Rab, Carrier, and Cheever (2013) propose that SNS use may be giving rise to “iDisorders”, such as personality disorders (narcissistic, antisocial, obsessive-compulsive, paranoid, and histrionic) and mood disorders (major depression and bipolar). The thrust of this growing body of research is that maladaptive technology use and outcomes are associated with psychopathologies and, thus, should be treated and managed in the same manner as other mental health disorders. In practice, a growing number of mental health clinics are ofering clinical services to combat technology-related psychopathologies such as Internet addiction (although no formal medical diagnoses exist) (McNamee, 2015).

Rather than focusing on psychopathologies as drivers of negative outcomes of IS use, we propose that IT identity, defined as “the extent to which an individual views use of an IT as integral to his or her sense of self ” (Carter & Grover, 2015, p. 932), may also lead to negative IS use outcomes. IT identity is a recently conceptualised type of identity and, as such, there is little research that explores its implications (Carter & Grover, 2015). Our identity conceptualisation of “dark side” outcomes is important because whether concerns about one’s behaviour stem from one’s identity or a mental health pathology has very diferent implications for the interventions that would be employed to ameliorate such concerns.

Consider, for example, debates that have surrounded identities and psychopathologies associated with gender. In the past, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) specified mental disorders involving gender (e.g., gender identity disorder) that have since been removed or renamed so that individuals gender identities are no longer pathologised and stigmatised (Parry, 2013). Shifts in understanding of gender identity have afected organisations’ human resource and customer engagement strategies, which are increasingly embracing practices that aim to integrate individuals’ gender identities into policies and norms rather than treat these identities as pathologies that warrant medical interventions (De Cuypere, Knudson, & Bockting, 2011; Giang, 2015).

Similar shifts in policies and strategies can be seen with respect to IT identity. In one case, a large financial company adjusted its SNS workplace policy specifically for its digital native hires (Koch, Gonzalez, & Leidner, 2012; Leidner, Koch, & Gonzalez, 2010). While the company had previously banned SNS access on workplace computers, efectively disallowing digital natives’ SNS (IT) identity in the workplace, it eventually reversed this policy and went a step further to implement an internal SNS for employees (Koch et al., 2012; Leidner et al., 2010). In this case, the company realised that an IT identity integration approach yielded more productive and satisfied digital native employees.

Despite these recent shifts in both understanding and policies, the literature has not converged on how to best address personal use of technology and the potential for deficient self-regulation it presents. For example, “cyberloafing” and “cyberslacking” have become mainstream labels for individuals engaging in the personal use of Internet and mobile technologies when they are supposed to be working on assigned tasks (Vitak, Crouse, & LaRose, 2011). In workplace settings, recent US industry surveys indicate that digital distractions contribute to the most time wasted at work, with SNS use (Facebook, in particular) ranking among the top work-related distractions (Gouveia, 2014; Poppick, 2016). In academic settings, research indicates that students’ SNS use negatively impacts academic performance, primarily by increasing distractions and of-task behaviours (Rosen, Carrier, & Cheever, 2013; Wood et al., 2012). Clearly, understanding deficient self-regulation of technology use is of interest to both industry and academe.

Some research suggests that deterrence approaches, such as acceptable use policies combined with monitoring, detecting, and sanctioning inappropriate use, are efective strategies to encourage better self-regulation of technology use and combat cyberloafing (Urgin & Pearson, 2013). One deterrence strategy that is popular with supervisors is to simply block certain websites (e.g., SNS) altogether (Poppick, 2016). Such institutionalised deterrence approaches are consistent with beliefs that stigmatise and pathologise personal technology use (particularly SNS use) that is assumed to be inappropriate and, as such, have been the focus of research associating SNS use with psychopathologies (e.g., Garcia & Sikström, 2014; Rosen, Whaling et al., 2013; Turel, 2015). However, as we have alluded to earlier, such deterrence approaches may not resonate well with employees who have a strong sense of SNS (IT) identity, such as digital natives. According to a survey of over 3500 professionals across six countries, the majority of digital native employees would leave their jobs if their employer banned technology use for personal tasks during work hours (Donavan, 2015).

In shifting the discussion away from psychopathologies toward an identity-based perspective, we explore the impact of IT identity on a particular negative consequence of SNS use, namely, deficient self-regulation of time (i.e., the inability to manage one’s time well). We use the term “deficient self-regulation” based on the prominent work by LaRose, Lin, and Eastin (2003) to describe “a state in which conscious self-control is relatively diminished” (p. 232). Further, to better understand interventions that could address negative consequences associated with IT identity, we probe the antecedents of IT identity. Hence, our aim is to examine the nomological network of IT identity within the context of a negative outcome related to SNS use. Accordingly, the research questions are as follows:

• How does SNS (IT) identity impact deficient self-regulation of time due to SNS use?

• What factors impact an individual’s SNS (IT) identity?

To answer these questions, we situate IT identity within a dual systems framework, investigating whether, and how, IT identity – in a broader nomological net including IT use and habit – influences one’s perceived lack of control of his/her time in the context of Facebook use.

This study makes two important contributions. First, it points to an alternative perspective on the “dark side” of IS – namely, a negative IS use outcome can be predicted by IT identity rather than stigmatised or pathologised factors. Second, as IT identity is a new theoretical construct in the IS domain, this study is one of the first to empirically investigate its nomological net and, thus, answers a recent call to explore IT identity in “contemporary contexts, relative to established IS constructs” (Carter & Grover, 2015, p. 950).

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Dual systems model of self-related processes

According to dual systems theories, individuals have two parallel and competing information processing systems that determine their behaviours (Gawronski & Creighton, 2013; Kruglanski & Orehek, 2007; Strack & Deutsch, 2015). The reflective system involves deliberate, conscious, and controlled processes, whereas the reflexive system invokes processes that are automatic, unconscious, and dificult to control (Lieberman, Gaunt, Gilbert, & Trope, 2002). Thus, reasoned actions and beliefs are typically governed by the reflective system, while habits are driven by the reflexive system (Kahneman, 2003; Kruglanski & Orehek, 2007). Because both systems of information processing influence the processes underlying self-identity (Berzonsky & Luyckx, 2008; Oyserman & James, 2011; Vignoles, Schwartz, & Luyckx, 2011) and the self-regulation of behaviours (Gunn & Finn, 2015; Hofmann, Friese, & Strack, 2009), our conceptual model (Figure 1) includes both reflective and reflexive systems leading to self-related processes.

Theories of the self specify systems of assumptions and beliefs concerning the way the self interacts with the world (Epstein, 1980). These theories explore the attentional, cognitive, and regulatory aspects of the self that underlie emotion and motivation and include the study of such concepts as self-identity, self-regulation, and self-worth, as well as their interrelationships to each other (Andersen & Chen, 2002; Berzonsky, 2011; Leary & Tangney, 2012). Although scholars have debated definitions of “the self ”, ranging from personality to executive agent to “the total person” (Leary & Tangney, 2012), many agree that the self can be helpful in guiding instrumental behaviours and beliefs while, at the same time, it can invoke irrational thinking that leads to undesirable outcomes (Guay, Marsh, McInerney, & Craven, 2015). The two interrelated self processes explored in this study are self-identity – more specifically, IT identity – and self-regulation.

## 2.2. IT identity as an expanded self-identity

Self-identity answers the question “Who am I?” and is a concept that is increasingly explored in IS research (Carter & Grover, 2015; Whitley, Gal, & Kjaergaard, 2014). While prior IS research concerning individual-level concepts of identity has explored IT as an antecedent to or a consequence of identity, or as a medium used to express one’s identity, there has been little research or theorising concerning IT as a core dimension of one’s self-identity (Carter & Grover, 2015). Thus, a new conceptualisation of identity that is specific to the IS domain, referred to as IT identity, captures the extent to which an individual perceives use of an IT as central to who he/she is (Carter & Grover, 2015). IT identity becomes part of one’s identity when an individual expands his/her self-identity to include IT. In this sense, the loss of an IT that has become an important part of one’s identity would feel like losing a part of one’s self, perceived as “shrinkage of the self ” (Carter & Grover, 2015, p. 940).

IT identity draws upon material identity (Belk, 1988), which views material possessions and other objects as extensions of one’s self. According to Carter and Grover (2015), IT as a material possession is defined as “a unit of technology (hardware device, software application, or software application environment) that an individual consciously engages with, as an end-user, to produce, store, and communicate information; that could be accessible to that person across time and space; and that may provide breadth of access to others in the person’s social world” (p. 932). Going beyond answering the question “Who am I?”, IT identity specifically answers the question “Who am I in relation to this technology?”, and is reflected in individuals’ perceptions of relatedness, dependence, and emotional energy with respect to the IT (Carter & Grover, 2015).

Relatedness captures the extent to which the boundary between the self and IT becomes obscured and users exhibit a sense of connectedness with the IT. Individuals experience strong relatedness with an IT when their interactions with that IT become fused with their sense of self. Dependence refers to the extent to which individuals rely on IT to fulfil important instrumental goals, such as supporting communications or managing schedules. Note that the concept of dependence in the context of IT identity is diferent from the notion of dependence in the context of IT addiction, in which dependency necessarily involves withdrawal symptoms and negative life consequences (Kandell, 1998). Dependence in the identity literature captures the notion that the material object meets the individual’s salient functional needs; thus, the individual relies on the material object for goal fulfilment. For example, one reason people strongly self-identify with their home is because they depend on it to provide them with shelter (Anton & Lawrence, 2014). The same can be said for IT dependence in the context of IT identity: individuals more strongly identify with IT that they rely on to fulfil important goals (Carter & Grover, 2015). Lastly, individuals with a strong IT identity also experience feelings of emotional energy (i.e., enthusiasm and energy) in relation to an IT. That is, in order for individuals to develop and maintain a solid identity in relation to a particular IT, their interactions with that IT must be emotionally significant and facilitate emotional attachment to the IT. IT identity is shaped by preexisting behaviours and beliefs tied to the IT, and is capable of influencing a wide range of related behaviours and aspects of one’s self-concept (Carter & Grover, 2015).

## 2.3. Perceived control of time as self-regulation

Another self process included in this study is self-regulation, or self-control, specifically related to time management. Perceived control of time refers to an individual’s “sense of mastery over how one allocates one’s time” (Macan, 1994, p. 382). Managing one’s time well has been shown to be important for lowering stress, increasing work and life satisfaction, lowering role ambiguity and role overload, and improving job and academic performance (Macan, 1994; Macan, Shahani, Dipboye, & Phillips, 1990). Because perceived control of time represents individuals’ beliefs concerning their ability to exert control over their time management behaviours, it captures perceptions of self-regulation of time. Selfregulation, or self-control, refers to an individual’s ability to exert control over one’s self, i.e., how one thinks, feels, and behaves (Muraven & Baumeister, 2000).

In line with contemporary “dark side of IS” literature, we specifically explore deficient self-regulation, which refers to “a state in which conscious self-control is relatively diminished” (LaRose et al., 2003, p. 232), as it directly relates to concerns over the pervasiveness of “cyberloafing” or “cyberslacking” (Vitak et al., 2011). Thus, deficient self-regulation of time in this context captures perceived deficiencies in being able to control how one allocates one’s time as a result of SNS use.

Self-control and self-identity are related, as both are self-related processes that operate within one’s goal hierarchy. However, they difer in terms of both their malleability (with self-control considered as more malleable and self-identity regarded as more stable) and their impact on actions (O’Leary, Uusberg, & Gross, 2017). Self-identity is associated more with long-term goals; thus, actions that align with one’s self-identity are given more weight, whereas self-control is invoked when there is a conflict between two action impulses: one that aligns with a shorter-term goal and one that aligns with a more enduring goal (Duckworth & Gross, 2014; O’Leary et al., 2017).

Having discussed the self-related processes involved in this research, we now turn our attention to the reflective and reflexive systems that influence both IT identity and deficient self-regulation of time.

## 2.4. Reflective system: Reasoned actions and beliefs

Commonly employed reasoned action frameworks in IS research, including those based on the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and its extended model, the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991), propose that intentional behaviours are a function of conscious and deliberate socio-cognitive processes, including normative beliefs and attitudes. We focus on normative beliefs specifically due to their close relationship with self-identity. Most IS studies have incorporated normative beliefs as a single construct, subjective norm, representing overall normative pressures or “perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform the behaviour in question” (Ajzen, 1991, p. 188). However, given that TPB-based research has found subjective norm to be a weak predictor of behavioural intentions (Armitage & Conner, 2001), behavioural scholars have expanded the conceptualisation of normative pressures to include various distinct types of norms (see Cialdini, Reno, & Kallgren, 1990; Elek, Miller-Day, & Hecht, 2006). Accordingly, in addition to the most commonly studied type of norm (injunctive norm), we investigate personal norm, which is the least studied of the norms yet also the most relevant to self-related processes (Conner & Armitage, 1998), such as self-identity and self-regulation.

Injunctive norm represents direct pressure from others to engage in a particular behaviour – i.e., “what others want me to do”. We use this term in place of “social norm” or “social influences” (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003) to clarify that we are in fact only interested in a particular social norm – that is, perceptions that engaging in SNS use is what members in one’s reference group think ought to be done. Using this terminology in place of the more popular “subjective norm”, which in its pure form also takes into account one’s willingness to comply (Ajzen, 1991), also allows us to better align our research model with extant identity work in other disciplines (e.g., Christensen, Rothgerber, Wood, & Matz, 2004), thus furthering a cumulative research tradition. In self-theories, injunctive norms “serve as self-standards that specify whom people would ideally like to be”, leading to pride, relief, and other positive emotions when afirming the injunctive norms of their relevant social groups (Christensen et al., 2004, p. 1296).

Personal (or moral) norm represents personal rules of conduct, or “what I think is right to $\mathrm { d } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { o } } ^ { \flat }$ (Cialdini et al., 1990). Because personal norm captures values that are personal, rather than social, in nature (Conner & Armitage, 1998), it has been included in expanded TRA and TPB models that investigate ethical behaviours and decision-making (Kurland, 1995; Parker, Manstead, &

Stradling, 1995). Both types of norms influence conscious, intentional behaviours (e.g., SNS use), which in turn shape self-identity and self-regulation processes.

In our study, the intentional behaviour, or reasoned action, that we investigate is the extent of SNS use. While studies based on popular IS behavioural models such as UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) have already demonstrated that social influences can impact behavioural intention, which in turn impacts usage behaviour, most such studies either stop at predicting general usage intentions (e.g., “I intend/predict/plan to use the system in the next < n > months”) or include only a general measure of actual or self-reported usage as a consequence of these intentions (e.g., Igbaria, Parasuraman, & Baroudi, 1996; Liang, Xue, Ke, & Wei, 2010; Taylor & Todd, 1995). Further, this line of research has argued that social influences only matter in the early stages of mandated, not voluntary, usage settings (see Venkatesh et al., 2003). Thus, there is still a need to improve our understanding of the role of normative pressures in impacting specific aspects of one’s actual extent of SNS use, a voluntary IS behaviour that nevertheless has a critical social com ponent (Al-Debei, Al-Lozi, & Papazafeiropoulou, 2013; Cheung & Lee, 2010).

## 2.5. Reflexive system: habit

Recognising that past behavioural IS research emphasised theories rooted in the reflective system, IS scholars have recently turned to examining factors within the reflexive system as well, highlighting habit as an important reflexive construct to investigate (Kim, Malhotra, & Narasimhan, 2005; Limayem & Hirt, 2003). Habits are “learned sequences of acts that have become automatic responses to specific cues, and are functional in obtaining certain goals or end-states” (Verplanken & Aarts, 1999, p. 104). Habit is a specific type of goal-directed automaticity in which people are generally unaware of the triggers to their behaviour, and the behaviour itself is both mentally eficient to perform and dificult to control (Bargh, 1994). Habits develop over time as an individual satisfactorily repeats a particular behaviour in a stable context. Eventually, that behaviour comes to be controlled less by cognitive processes and more by habit. In the IS literature, habit is most often investigated from the perspectives of the Technology Acceptance Model (e.g., Kim & Malhotra, 2005), Expectation–Disconfirmation Theory (e.g., Limayem, Hirt, & Cheung, 2007), and status quo bias (e.g., Polites & Karahanna, 2012), where it is considered either an enabler or obstacle to IS acceptance.

Although some scholars (e.g., Verplanken & Orbell, 2003) have considered identity to be a facet of habit, IS researchers and other scholars have argued that habit and identity are distinct constructs (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). Psychology research has primarily explored the relationship between habit and identity in an indirect fashion, with a focus on self-identity. Most studies investigating the relationship between habit and self-identity (e.g., Charng, Piliavin, & Callero, 1988; Conner & Mcmillan, 1999; Fekadu & Kraft, 2001) have focused on predicting intentions or (rarely) actual behaviour, rather than individual perceptions of the consequences of that behaviour. Further, what little we do know about the relationship between these two constructs has been largely based on operationalising habit as past behaviour as opposed to a psychological construct with genuine facets of automaticity (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). Thus, such studies may not fully explain the relationship between habit and IT identity (as an expanded self-identity).

Further, because habits are automatic, unconscious responses to triggers associated with previously reinforced behaviours, they are hard to bring under conscious self-control (Verplanken & Orbell, 2003). Therefore, when habits conflict with the goals of competing desired behaviours, such as time management, the habitual behavioural response is dificult to override (Webb, Sheeran, & Luszczynska, 2009) and can lead to deficient self-regulation (Larose, Kim, & Peng, 2010).

We now present our research model, which incorporates aspects of both the reflective system (represented by norms and usage) and reflexive system (represented by habit), to demonstrate the dual systems’ impact on self-related processes from an identity perspective.

## 3. Research model

To further illuminate our identity perspective on “dark side” behaviours, we present an integrative research model (Figure 2) that illustrates the antecedents and consequences of IT identity. Based on self-discrepancy theory (Higgins, 1987) and building upon the IT identity framework introduced by Carter and Grover (2015), we posit that the impact of normative pressures on one’s SNS (IT) identity is fully mediated by one’s extent of SNS usage. In regard to the model’s reflexive component, we break from prior literature (e.g., Kim et al., 2005) that recognises habit and past usage as distinct theoretical concepts yet considers past usage a suitable proxy for habit, to posit that habit and extent of usage will have independent efects on SNS (IT) identity. For example, it is possible for individuals to have a strong habit of only updating their Facebook status at the start of each day or to have a strong habit of making frequent posts throughout the day. Next, we draw from theories of the self to argue that individuals with a strong SNS (IT) identity are more likely to perceive that they have issues controlling how they allocate their time due to SNS use. Finally, we expect that SNS (IT) identity will only partially mediate the efects of extent of usage and habit on deficient self-regulation of time, due to the nature of SNS as a “distracting technology” (Rosen, Carrier et al., 2013) and the fact that habit performance occurs outside of awareness and is dificult to control. We situate our theorising within the context of voluntary, personal SNS use, which can span a variety of settings – work, school, or one’s personal life. ## 3.1. Reflective system predicting IT identity

We propose that normative pressures’ influence on SNS (IT) identity is fully mediated by individuals’ extent of SNS usage. The relationships between normative pressures, extent of usage, and SNS (IT) identity are rooted in how self-identity (and, specifically, IT identity) is shaped and maintained over time, according to self-discrepancy theory (Higgins, 1987).

Self-identity – which includes IT identity – is both personally and socially constructed (Breakwell, 2015; Carter & Grover, 2015). At any given time, individuals consist of their actual self, which is the current self-identity they believe they actually possess (“who I really am”), and their desired self, which consists of both their “ought self ” and “ideal self ” (Higgins, 1987; Higgins, Bond, Klein, & Strauman, 1986). The ought self represents identity attributes an individual believes he/she should possess (“who I should be”) based on obligations, while the ideal self is composed of identity attributes an individual wishes to possess (“who I wish to be”) based on aspirations (Higgins, 1987; Higgins et al., 1986). Both domains of the desired self are defined by one’s personal and injunctive norms and beliefs (Higgins, 1987; Higgins et al., 1986).

Because individuals are motivated to maintain congruence between their actual and desired selves, this motivation often prompts action (Breakwell, 2015; Higgins, 1987). When individuals perceive a discrepancy between their diferent selves, they will often modify their behaviours to achieve congruence. Further, when their actual and desired selves are congruent, they will maintain their existing identity-relevant behaviours for the sake of self-continuity (Breakwell, 2015; Higgins, 1987). Hence, identity development and maintenance, in part, are functions of conscious and deliberate self-evaluations of both individuals’ personal value system (represented in our study by personal norms) and interpersonal feedback (represented in our study by injunctive norms), which together define the desired self, and comparing these self-evaluations with perceptions of the actual self – or SNS (IT) identity, as captured in our study. The result of these comparative evaluations is that SNS (IT) identity-relevant behaviours (represented in our study by extent of SNS usage) are often either changed or maintained (Breakwell, 2015). Thus, we expect the influence of personal and injunctive norms on SNS (IT) identity to be fully mediated by the behaviours (i.e., usage) enacted to align normative feedback (both personal and injunctive) with one’s self-identity.

We expect normative pressures from individuals peers, specifically those tied to one’s perceptions of “what others think I ought to do” (i.e., injunctive norms; see Cialdini et al., 1990), to lead to more extensive SNS usage as part of their eforts to reduce any discrepancies (or to simply maintain congruence) in their comparative self-evaluations. For example, because one’s family or peer group uses SNS to coordinate activities or share news, an SNS user may feel substantial pressure to participate in specific platforms, such as Facebook, or to use specific features, such as Facebook Instant Messenger. While one may be aware that many people use Facebook, absent the injunctive norm, that individual may never try the new features or engage in sustained use.

We measure extent of usage in terms of frequency, volume, relative proportion, and breadth of SNS use, with a focus on active information sharing rather than simple “lurking”. Intuitively, a perceived obligation to members of one’s reference group to use a particular SNS for sharing information should lead to an increase in both the frequency and volume of doing so (whether via increased status updates, direct messages, posting of photos and videos, or even commenting on the posts of others). This will also lead to the SNS being used more for this purpose in comparison to alternative channels (e.g., texting, email, phone, other social media tools).

Theoretical support for this view can be found in the literature on perceived social overload resulting from SNS use (Maier et al., 2014). Specifically, injunctive pressures (which Maier et al. termed “subjective social support norm”) may arise as an SNS user’s friends expect both entertainment and reciprocity in terms of reactions to their own posts, leading to a perceived obligation to provide such benefits to one’s online social network. These norms may be communicated verbally (e.g., “please send me a Facebook message about meeting up this weekend”), or through the SNS itself. For example, one of the authors has previously tried to take a week-long “Facebook break”, only to end up tagged in Facebook messages from geographically distant friends, asking “where they had gone” and wondering whether they were okay, simply because they had stopped posting regularly. Such actions impose an implicit social pressure to stay actively engaged on Facebook, if only for the sake of one’s friends or family, shaping one’s “ought self ” and subsequent usage behaviours that will align the ought self with one’s actual SNS (IT) identity (Higgins, 1987).

Further, as a particular SNS comes to be viewed as the acceptable means of communicating with one’s friends, the desired richness and timeliness of information exchange should lead to the use of more features (e.g., image, video, and link sharing, instant messaging), and a “spillover” efect may result, such that the SNS comes to be used for other activities as well (e.g., joining groups with allied interests, following the activities of favourite companies or nonprofit organisations). Therefore, we posit:

## H1. Injunctive norm positively influences extent of SNS usage.

We also expect personal norm to influence SNS (IT) identity via one’s extent of SNS usage. Personal norm is rarely incorporated into IS behavioural models. It difers from attitude in that it has a moral component, representing personal rules of conduct or “what I think is right to do” (Cialdini et al., 1990), whereas attitude simply represents “an individual’s positive or negative feelings (evaluative afect) about performing the target behaviour” (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975, p. 216). Compared against the various types of social norms, personal norms consist of “internalised values and expectations for behaviour, irrespective of external reward or evidence” (Elek et al., 2006) and align with the “ideal self ”, or who one aspires to be (Higgins, 1987). Many people open a Facebook account simply to maintain a network of contacts and passively “lurk”, without actually posting anything themselves. Some of them strongly believe that the “proper” way to communicate with friends and family (even those in their Facebook network) is to call, send a text message, or even write a letter. These individuals, thus, have not internalised a personal norm that Facebook is the appropriate tool for sharing information about their lives with friends (Gross, 2014). This is manifested by infrequent posts and a propensity for communicating through other channels, which will likely also reduce the range of other activities for which they use Facebook.

Conversely, an individual who has internalised a strong personal norm regarding the appropriateness of Facebook for information exchange will more often choose Facebook over alternative channels – posting more frequently, posting in greater volume, and using more of the Facebook’s available features. For example, while one may feel low afect towards Facebook as a source of news, given a strong moral belief that one should disclose important personal news with close family and friends, an individual may feel compelled to disclose or announce personal events, such as promotions, weddings, and births via Facebook – not necessarily because of perceived social pressure to do so but because of a belief that it is the right thing to do. Thus, we posit:

## H2. Personal norm positively influences extent of SNS usage.

IT usage behaviours that are important and deeply embedded in users’ daily lives are posited to be core predictors of IT identity development and maintenance (Carter & Grover, 2015). Individuals can form and maintain multiple IT identities (e.g., an iPhone identity and a Snapchat identity) with respect to specific technologies that are salient to them, but these IT identities will be weighted diferently according to their relative importance to one’s identity (Carter & Grover, 2015). Generally, technologies that ofer wide applications across a variety of social contexts and are significant and valuable to the individual are more apt to be integrated into their identities (Carter & Grover, 2015). Because SNSs ofer a broad set of applications across diferent social contexts (e.g., family, friends, work) that serve many purposes viewed as instrumental and rewarding to individuals (e.g., maintaining relationships, seeking and sharing information) (Błachnio, Przepiórka, & Rudnicka, 2013), a particular SNS such as Facebook is more amenable to being incorporated into users’ identities. This SNS (IT) identity is strengthened as the extent of SNS usage increases the embeddedness of the SNS in one’s life, boosting feelings of emotional energy, relatedness, and dependence with respect to the SNS (Carter & Grover, 2015). Even tech nologies with a narrow scope of application can become part of one’s identity, however, if a substantial amount of time is spent using them (Carter & Grover, 2015). Thus, frequent, high volume use of an SNS, leveraging a wide array of its available features (e.g., sharing information, posting photos or videos, following news, finding information), across various situations where it is possible to do so, should lead to a stronger SNS (IT) identity.

H3. Extent of SNS usage positively influences SNS (IT) identity.

## 3.2. Reflexive system predicting IT identity

Self-identities can be activated automatically and unconsciously along the reflexive path. For example, many people are unaware of how they automatically self-identify with certain social or role categories (e.g., gender) and how these identity constructs emerged in the first place (Vignoles et al., 2011). In fact, many daily activities and decisions occur automatically, outside of our conscious awareness (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999). Prior research provides support for the view that habit will strengthen one’s self-identification with a particular behaviour (e.g., Sparks & Guthrie, 1998).

Conventional habit theories stipulate that a stable context is a necessary condition for habit formation and subsequent activation (Wood, Tam, & Witt, 2005). However, media consumption habits (covering both traditional and electronic forms of media such as newspaper, TV, email, and online music streaming) have been argued as oper ating diferently from other habits in that a stable context (e.g., location, time, mood, presence of certain individuals) is only required for the initial media habit formation but not for subsequent activations (LaRose, 2010). Because social media is so ubiquitous, an SNS habit can be triggered automatically and unconsciously by numerous stimuli and cognitive associations that span contexts (LaRose, 2010), which deeply embeds the SNS habit in the user’s life, just as Limayem et al. (2007) have previ ously argued that specific IS habits (i.e., the development of habits to use a given IS for specific tasks) can ultimately lead to a more general habit of using that IS across a wide range of situations. This habit-driven embeddedness increases the user’s feelings of emotional attachment to and dependence upon the SNS, key facets of an SNS (IT) identity (Carter & Grover, 2015). Furthermore, people often use their habits to infer self-concept meanings after the fact (Burkitt, 2011; Wood & Neal, 2007), such as when individuals who have a habit of using a particular SNS come to see themselves as individuals who strongly self-identify with that SNS. Thus, we posit:

H4. SNS habit positively influences SNS (IT) identity.

## 3.3. IT identity predicting deficient self-regulation of time

The influence of SNS (IT) identity on deficient self-regulation of time involves relationships between two inner “self ” processes within one’s overall self-concept, akin to the “intra-psychic” strategies described by Breakwell (2015) that operate purely as cognitions and emotions, rather than behaviours. Due to its highly interactive nature and fulfilment of hedonic goals (Turel, 2014), social media (including SNS) is characterised as a “distracting technology” (Rosen, Carrier et al., 2013). When individuals strongly self-identify with a “distracting” technology, this potentially creates inner goal conflict with another self-related process, namely, self-regulation of time. Deficient self-regulation of time due to SNS use could pose a threat to one’s SNS (IT) identity because it implies that the IT identity is problematic in some way. Likewise, individuals’ SNS (IT) identity can threaten their self-perception of how well they control their time. In either case, the inner self-conflict threatens the continuity of one’s self-concept. Because individuals are motivated to achieve and maintain congruence among their various self-processes (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999), they engage in reconciliation strategies when they experience inner-self conflicts. One particular strategy that individuals pursue to resolve these inner self-conflicts is identity-restructuring responses (Petriglieri, 2011).

Identity-restructuring responses involve changing an aspect of the threatened identity itself, such as changing the meaning or importance of the threatened identity (Petriglieri, 2011). We propose that individuals will respond to the aforementioned internal identity threat by pursuing the identity restructuring responses of meaning change and/or importance change. With meaning change, the individual reframes negative self-concepts as positive self-concepts, thereby eliminating the inner tensions altogether (Petriglieri, 2011). For example, individuals who strongly identify with a “distracting technology” and subsequently have dificulty managing their time may reframe their self-regulation of time as digital multi-tasking rather than time-wasting. In this case, it would make sense to the individual that he/she falls behind schedule sometimes because he/she is juggling many tasks at once. With importance change, the individual lessens the importance of threatened self-concepts to minimise their motivational significance and potential harm (Petriglieri, 2011). This is exemplified by individuals who do not necessarily perceive deficient self-regulation of time as problematic, as long as they eventually complete their important tasks (Warner, 2013). Unlike more extreme forms of deficient self-regulation, such as IT addiction, deficient self-regulation of time does not necessarily have to lead to negative life consequences.

These identity-restructuring responses are consistent with existing research concerning self-identity and self-regulation, which finds that individuals associate stable identities with long-term goals and place greater value on achieving these goals. When faced with a choice between two competing goals, which invokes self-regulation, individuals will give more weight to identity-relevant choices vs. those that are identity-irrelevant (O’Leary et al., 2017). IT identity becomes stable after the IT has become embedded within individuals’ personal social networks and use of the IT becomes routinised (Carter & Grover, 2015). Thus, once individuals have a stable SNS (IT) identity, they will prioritise identity-relevant choices over identity-irrelevant ones.

Using the identity restructuring responses of meaning change and importance change, an individual can readily admit to having both a strong SNS (IT) identity and deficient self-regulation of time due to SNS use but without the cognitive dissonance associated with the conflict between the two self processes. Thus, if individuals self-identify very strongly with a particular SNS (manifested through feeling related to it, dependent upon it, and drawing emotional energy from its use), that SNS will be viewed as so important to their life that, via identity restructuring strategies, they may readily acknowledge that the time spent using the SNS interferes with how they allocate their time.

H5: SNS (IT) identity positively influences perceived deficient self-regulation of time.

## 3.4. Reflective system predicting deficient selfregulation of time

We expect that the efect of extensive intentional use of an SNS on deficient self-regulation of time will be only partially mediated by SNS (IT) identity. While not all planned technology use (for example, extensive use of productivity tools, such as Excel, in the workplace) leads to deficient self-regulation of time, we have already dis cussed how SNS is considered a “distracting technology” (Rosen, Carrier et al., 2013; Turel, 2014). As such, SNS encourages deep engagement by serving as a mood-stabilising distraction from important but stressful tasks (Reinecke & Hofmann, 2016); some scholars have gone so far as to label this behaviour “Facebocrastination” (Meier, Reinecke, & Meltzer, 2016). Because SNS pro vides immediate positive reinforcement, users may prioritise this short-term afect regulation over other important self-regulatory goals, as has been shown with other afect-regulation behaviours (Tice, Bratslavsky, & Baumeister, 2001). Furthermore, research suggests that users who actively engage in SNS find it dificult to ignore distracting stimuli and channel their attentional control to targeted tasks when compared to passive users (Alloway & Alloway, 2012). This efect will be amplified as SNS use increases in frequency, volume, and breadth, as well as when the SNS has become the primary channel for communicating with one’s friends. Thus, we posit:

H6. Extent of SNS usage positively influences deficient self-regulation of time.

## 3.5. Reflexive system predicting deficient selfregulation of time

We also expect habits associated with a particular SNS to have a direct impact on deficient self-regulation of time, beyond their efect mediated by IT identity. As we have previously described, habits are behavioural responses that over time can come to be triggered automatically by any number of diferent features of the performance context, e.g., one’s location, time of day, social surroundings, mood, or task definition (Polites & Karahanna, 2013). By their very nature as a form of automaticity (with both triggers and responses occurring at least in part outside of awareness), they are dificult to bring under conscious control (Bargh, 1994; Verplanken & Orbell, 2003). For example, some individuals automatically reach for their smartphone and click on the Facebook app the moment anything even slightly interesting, exciting, or annoying happens, in order to share it with their friends, only to then become distracted by reading and responding to other posts that they see on their screen. As a consequence of this lack of real-time awareness of the trigger and response, individuals may only recognise after the fact (i.e., after they have already “slipped up” and used the SNS more than they intended to) that their habitual use has had negative consequences on time available for other tasks.

Even when individuals do recognise that their habits conflict with the goals of competing desired behaviours, and sincerely intend to practice better time management, habits are still dificult to override (Webb et al., 2009). For this reason, several studies have found that habits may lead to various forms of deficient self-regulation of behaviour, up to and including addiction (LaRose, Connolly, Lee, Li, & Hales, 2014). This problem is likely exacerbated as the development of several task-specific habits tied to a particular SNS can easily morph into a more general consumption habit that is less sensitive to specific contextual triggers (LaRose, 2010; Polites & Karahanna, 2013) and, therefore, more dificult to reprogram via such popular habit control techniques as the development of implementation intentions (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). Thus, we posit:

H7. SNS habit positively influences deficient self-regulation of time.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Research context

Despite recent growth in membership on alternative social media platforms (e.g., Snapchat, Instagram), Facebook continues to be a leader in social networking, with more than 1.4 billion active monthly users; of these, approximately 65 per cent access the site daily, primarily through mobile devices (Facebook Newsroom, 2015). Digital natives, in particular, are heavy users of Facebook. It is commonly reported that almost half of 18 to 34 year old Facebook members visit their Facebook feed when they first wake up to view posts, make comments, and upload photos (Statistic Brain, 2015). Although 76 per cent of digital natives report using Facebook primarily to find out what others are doing, 42 per cent still report sharing content of their own as their main reason for using Facebook over other social media (including SNS) tools (American Press Institute, 2015). Thus, we believe that Facebook represents an appropriate platform on which to investigate key aspects of SNS use and SNS (IT) identity, particularly among digital natives. Carter and Grover (2015) have also argued that technologies such as Facebook that have multiple uses (e.g., sharing status updates and photos, instant messaging), and are both portable and networked, are particularly amenable to IT identity formation. Thus, we use a sample of college students who are Facebook users.

## 4.2. Operationalisation of constructs

In following Polites and Karahanna’s argument (2012, 2013) that specific IS habits should be measured in regard to a particular technology/task combination, we selected “sharing news with friends” as our focal Facebook activity. This activity encompasses not only the posting of status updates (which may include photos, videos, and links in addition to text), but also responding to the posts of others with new information, thus indicating a more active role in using Facebook (vs. simply “lurking”) that would be more likely to aid the development of a Facebook (IT) identity. Survey items for other constructs were also worded in regard to the focal activity for consistency.

All latent constructs except for IT identity were measured on a 7-point Likert scale (Table A1 in Appendix A). Following Carter (2013), IT identity was conceptualised as a superordinate construct with three reflective dimensions, and measured using a semantic diferential scale. We drew from theory and extant literature in developing and refining scales for both injunctive and personal norms (details of this process are available upon request). Items for deficient self-regulation of time were adapted from an unpublished study extending Chen (2007), based on Macan (1994) and Macan et al. (1990).

To avoid respondent fatigue, we measured habit using a short form measure based on Polites and Karahanna (2012) and Limayem et al. (2007). Specifically, habit was operationalised as a first-order formative construct, using one item each for the dimensions of awareness, controllability, eficient use, and intentionality (see LaRose, 2010; Polites, Roberts, & Thatcher, 2012). A reflective item representing perceived habit as a means to achieve model identification in covariance-based SEM.

Extent of usage was captured through items designed to tap various facets of Facebook use that illuminate the overall embeddedness of this use in the respondent’s life (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006; Carter & Grover, 2015; Limayem et al., 2007), including frequency of daily use, volume of use (number of messages posted per day), relative proportion of time that they selected Facebook for the focal task (as opposed to selecting available alternatives), and breadth of use (a sum of the various tasks for which they indicated that they used Facebook). To capture any diferential efects for the various types of usage, each item was included in the model as a separate variable. Finally, we included basic demographic questions, such as respondents’ age, gender, and years of Facebook experience, as well as three “attention check” items.

## 4.3. Sample

We surveyed students at a large public university in the south-eastern United States, with respondents receiving extra credit for completing the questionnaire. We randomised the order of all survey items except for the nine IT identity items, which were measured on a semantic diferential scale and appeared together toward the end of the survey, along with the demographic questions and questions about the various facets of Facebook usage. We invited a total of 464 students to participate and received 264 responses. We eliminated 50 of these responses due to their failing one or more attention checks, giving patterned responses (e.g., answering “4” to all questions), giving invalid or out-of-range responses for years of Facebook experience or the size of their Facebook “friends” network, or indicating that they no longer actively used Facebook, leaving us with a final sample size of 214 (Table 1).

## 6. Discussion

This work was motivated by a desire to understand how IT identity influences “dark side” IT behaviours. Rooted in a dual systems framework, we built a theory-driven model of SNS (IT) identity’s antecedents and consequences. By empirically testing SNS (IT) identity’s influence within a nomological network incorporating two types of normative pressures and four diferent measures of embedded use, as well as habit, to predict a specific negative consequence of SNS use (deficient self-regulation of time), we answer the call of Vodanovich, Sundaram, and Myers (2010) by investigating the role of IT identity as an explanation for how digital natives, in particular, interact with ubiquitous information systems.

Our results demonstrate that SNS (IT) identity has a positive impact on deficient self-regulation of time, indicating that individuals who strongly identify with Facebook realise that their interconnectedness with the tool is causing them problems with organising their time. Furthermore, we found that the efects of extent of SNS usage (reflective system) and SNS habit (reflexive system) on deficient self-regulation of time were mediated by SNS (IT) identity. Contrary to existing research relating SNS usage to self-regulation of time, extensive Facebook usage did not significantly impact deficient self-regulation of time; instead, its impact on deficient self-regulation of time was fully mediated by SNS (IT) identity. Also, consistent with our hypotheses, we found that Facebook users do in fact infer at least part of their SNS (IT) identity based on their SNS usage habits, and these automatically triggered usage behaviours can have negative impacts on their perceived ability to control their time.

While both the reflective and reflexive systems predict SNS (IT) identity, the nature of their efects on deficient self-regulation of time difers. SNS habit (reflexive system) exhibited both direct and indirect efects, while the extent of SNS usage (reflective system) exhibited indirect efects only. However, our post hoc analysis showed that normative pressures (reflective system) directly impact one’s perceived ability to control one’s time. Specifically, respondents indicated that if they feel pressured by their friends and family members to use Facebook, this pressure (injunctive norm) impacts their perceived ability to efectively manage their time above and beyond the impact of their actual level of Facebook habit, extent of Facebook usage, or self-identification with Facebook. However, if they have strong personal norms that using Facebook is an appropriate way of sharing news with friends, they are far less likely to think that Facebook use is harming their ability to control their time.

All but one of our measures of extent of usage (relative proportion) led to higher levels of SNS (IT) identity. In other words, the more an individual uses Facebook to connect with others, and uses it for a wide variety of tasks (both social and utilitarian in nature), the higher the likelihood that the individual will feel emotionally energised by and linked to Facebook, and the more reliant upon it he/she will become.

Results also indicate that normative pressures impact several facets of the extent of Facebook usage. Injunctive norms primarily impacted volume of usage (i.e., the number of posts that a respondent makes per day). Both injunctive and personal norms afect the relative proportion of times that Facebook is selected when one wants to share news with friends. One’s own personal norms primarily afect breadth of use (representing the number of diferent tasks or activities for which Facebook is used). Neither type of normative pressure impacted the daily frequency of Facebook use. However, our post hoc analyses indicated that individuals who felt pressured to use Facebook by people important to them (i.e., injunctive norm) perceived more problems with managing their time, regardless of their actual levels of Facebook use. This may be due to SNS users feeling a sense of “social overload” from the high level of SNS use pressure and requests from their inner circle (Maier et al., 2014). This efect is ofset to some extent if the individual has strong personal norms concerning the acceptability of Facebook use.

## 7. Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, while student respondents are appropriate for a study of phenomena related to SNS usage, our sampling strategy afords opportunities for future research that investigates whether there might be diferential impacts of age or life stage or culture (e.g., attending school versus having entered the workforce, or United States versus other countries) on SNS use, IT identity, and deficient self-regulation of time. Our construct measures are generic enough that they should be applicable across a variety of contexts (e.g., SNS use in a personal versus work or academic context). Nevertheless, future studies should explore the impact of SNS (IT) identity on deficient self-regulation of time under a variety of conditions. For example, some research indicates that personal Internet use in the workplace may result in productivity gains, rather than losses, for knowledge workers under certain conditions (see Jiang, 2016); this work could be expanded to include an IT identity focus.

Second, this is a cross-sectional study and, as such, causal inferences must be based on the underlying theory. It is possible that some relationships in the model may actually be bidirectional in nature. For example, many individuals who heavily engage in SNS use have readily acknowledged that a core deliberate motive for such use is procrastination (Quan-Haase & Young, 2010; Reinecke & Hofmann, 2016), which is a form of deficient self-regulation of time with the intention of delaying and avoiding important tasks (Steel, 2007). This implies that there may be a reciprocal efect between extent of usage and deficient self-regulation of time.

Third, our results may be impacted by our decision to use “sharing news with friends” as the focal task for which to measure IS habit and other related constructs in the model. Diferent results might have been obtained had we focused on another task, such as “keeping up with news on friends”, as many respondents indicated that they used Facebook more for reading other peoples’ posts than for making their own. Nevertheless, our results were still significant, even when using this much narrower focal task. Finally, we note that while extant literature has recognised a link between extent of usage and the development of IS habits (e.g., Limayem et al., 2007; Soror, Hammer, Steelman, Davis, & Limayem,

2015; Turel & Serenko, 2012), we excluded this link because relationships between the reflective and reflexive antecedents of self processes are not a focus of the current study.

## 8. Implications and future research directions

Our study makes important contributions to understanding the roots and implications of IT identity, particularly among college-age digital natives. Nevertheless, we believe that our results can be generalised to other contexts as well.

## 8.1. Theoretical contributions and implications

An important theoretical contribution of our study is conceptualising and investigating IT identity, as opposed to the increasingly studied concepts of psychopathologies (e.g., addiction and compulsion) (Turel & Serenko, 2012; Turel, Serenko, & Giles, 2011; Xu et al., 2012), as a driver of a negative consequence of IS use. Negative outcomes that stem from one’s identity vs. a mental health pathology will point to diferent interventions that should be implemented to address these concerns. This distinction is important, as recent work on gender identity issues in academe and practice illustrate. People’s evolving understanding of identity issues has shaped human resource strategies employed by companies and universities, which are increasingly embracing gender concepts that span beyond the binary-gendered notions of male and female. Strategies such as this one reflect an identity integration approach in organisations, which has been shown to yield positive workplace outcomes (Koch et al., 2012; Leidner et al., 2010).

Our work implies a need for research that examines how to craft identity integration strategies that incorporate SNS use into workplace norms, rather than employ strong deterrence strategies that aim to control and limit SNS use in organisations. Some deterrence approaches, such as banning SNS use, align with a view that pathologises undesired SNS use. Our study shows an alternative explanation to the psychopathologies that have been attributed to SNS use (e.g., IT addiction) in predicting a negative IS outcome. Future research should examine these two perspectives in parallel. For example, a study could probe into theoretically distinguishing IT identity and IT addiction to gain a deeper understanding of negative consequences of IS use and investigate the eficacy of diferent interventions aimed at addressing these issues.

Given we found that self-identification with Facebook increases (perceived) deficient self-regulation of time, future research should investigate boundary conditions on the identity-behaviour relationship. For example, given our sample was limited to relatively young college students, it would be interesting to see if the influence of identity varies across digital natives versus digital immigrants (Vodanovich et al., 2010) by exploring additional explanatory constructs or behaviours such as multi-tasking behaviours. Notions of polychronicity (preference for performing two or more tasks simultaneously – i.e., multi-tasking) and monochronicity (preference for performing one task at a time sequentially) (Lee, 1999) may be particularly relevant to study in relation to individuals’ time management. Monochronicity is associated with higher levels of planning and selective attention on tasks (Goonetilleke & Luximon, 2010), whereas polychronicity is related to increased distractions and reduced focus (Cameron & Webster, 2005). It would be interesting to examine the relationship between IT identity and these task management styles and whether the relationship difers across generations.

Related to understanding identity and polychronicity is further investigation of how types of technology afect individuals’ attention quality and quantity, which some suggest can diminish in the presence of constant interruptions and distractions, an increasingly prevalent phenomenon given the widespread use of various digital media (van Knippenberg, Dahlander, Haas, & George, 2015). Some scholars even suggest individuals interactions with multiple media has wired their brains to exhibit attention-deficit traits (Small & Vorgan, 2008). Referred to as the “shallowing hypothesis” (Carr, 2010), this phenomenon suggests that the widespread difusion of SNS usage has led to a stark decline in conscious, reflective thought (Annisette & Lafreniere, 2016).

Thus, our work opens up avenues for future research regarding IT identity and deficient self-regulation of time to include investigating the role of additional constructs, such as the traits of polychronicity and monochronicity, task switching, working memory, and attention quality, in mediating or moderating the relationship posited in Hypothesis 5. Furthermore, our logic supporting H5 is based on concepts explained by self-discrepancy theory (Higgins, 1987) and, more specifically, the identity-restructuring responses of meaning or importance change of a threatened identity (Petriglieri, 2011). Future empirical research could also unpack the relationship between IT identity and deficient self-regulation of time to investigate this and other reconciliation strategies.

Although we examine a particular negative outcome (deficient self-regulation of time), existing studies on SNS use have pointed to other negative outcomes, including self concepts. For example, Krasnova et al. (2015) found that SNS use can lead to feelings of envy of others in an individual’s SNS network, which in turn negatively afect the person’s sense of well-being and prompts him/her to engage in self-enhancement behaviours on the SNS. While our research points to SNS use influencing the self concepts of IT identity and deficient self-regulation, future research could explore how SNS use afects other self concepts in tandem, such as self-promotion and self-esteem. Additionally, although we found that normative pressures impact IT identity through SNS use, some research suggests that excessive normative pressures can lead to technostress (Maier, Laumer, Weinert, & Weitzel, 2015) and social overload (Maier et al., 2014). Thus, it is unclear where this tipping point may be, where normative pressures may decrease SNS use and thereby have little impact on IT identity, and this opens avenues for future research.

We investigate IT identity from the perspective of how interaction with a particular SNS can become an integral part of who one is, such that an inability to use it leaves the person feeling as though he/she has in fact lost a part of him/herself (Moss, 2014). This approach difers substantially from prior IS research that has investigated the impact of SNS use on shaping and presenting one’s self-identity (e.g., Krasnova et al., 2015). Nonetheless, we recognise that there is a link between identifying with an IT artefact itself and being able to use that IT artefact to shape other facets of one’s overall identity. Future research should explore the interrelationships between IT identity and other aspects of identity processes (e.g., identity development, maintenance, experimentation, etc.).

Research investigating diferent types of SNS, as well as diferent types of IT in general, is another potentially fruitful direction for future research. For example, where Facebook presents a more stable “wall of information”, Twitter presents a dynamic, fast-moving feed and Snapchat presents an ephemeral, limited-release feed. It would be interesting to examine whether the speed, trajectory, and ephemerality at which information is disseminated afects the salience of an individual’s IT identity as well as their ability to self-regulate SNS use.

## 8.2. Practical contributions and implications

Given that the individuals with strong IT identities in our study readily acknowledged their challenges with time management, identity-based strategies (as opposed to pathology-based interventions) to overcome these challenges may prove to be most efective in workplace and academic settings, and recent studies appear to support this idea. Employing boundary theory to explore digital media use in the workplace, scholars are finding that managerial strategies aimed at integrating employees’ IT identity into workplace practices results in improved outcomes as compared to enforcing segmentation approaches that tightly control the boundaries of employees’ IT identity in the workplace (Koch et al., 2012; Leidner et al., 2010; Stanko & Beckman, 2015). For instance, we have already described how managers at a large financial company shifted their boundary control strategy from one of IT identity segmentation to one of IT identity integration to assimilate the SNS (IT) identity of their digital native hires (Koch et al., 2012; Leidner et al., 2010). Similarly, a study involving the US Navy found that imposing excessive boundary controls to limit digital media use among its employees resulted in employees’ feeling disconnected and powerless; however, integrating employees’ IT identity into the workplace in conjunction with creative, or “artful”, applications of boundary controls facilitated Navy employees’ focus and attention on the job (Stanko & Beckman, 2015).

Within academic settings, Rosen, Carrier et al. (2013) propose similar identity integration interventions to help college students stay focused and attentive. As with the US Navy example, Rosen, Carrier et al. (2013) also recommend combining an integration approach with some level of boundary controls – e.g., when not on a technology break, students must silence their cell phones and place them face down in plain sight as a visual cue that they will be rewarded another technology break after an extended period of focus and attention on learning tasks. Future research should investigate organisations perspectives on these interventions and policies to further probe the advantages and disadvantages of such strategies.

Still unexplored is an IT identity integration approach that leverages IT specifically designed to facilitate focused, uninterrupted work for individuals. One such approach would be to encourage individuals to use apps geared toward enhancing attention and focus in performing tasks, such as “StayFocused”, “Anti-Social”, and “Concentrate”. Future research should investigate the role and eficacy of IT designed to overcome challenges associated with negative IT use behaviours.
