# An investigation of neural network classi<sup>fi</sup>ers with unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and group sizes

Jyhshyan Lan <sup>a</sup>, Michael Y. Hu <sup>b</sup>, Eddy Patuwo <sup>b</sup>, G. Peter Zhang <sup>c,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> College of Business Administration, Providence University, Taiwan

Keywords: Neural networks Group sizes Medical diagnosis Misclassification costs

## a b s t r a c t

Despite a larger number of successful applications of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial neural networks for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation in business and other areas, published research has not considered the effects of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and group sizes. Without the consideration of uneven misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs, the classi<sup>fi</sup>er development will be compromised in minimizing the total misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors. The use of this simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed model will not only result in poor decision capability when misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly unequal, but also increase the model bias in favor of larger groups. This paper explores the issues of asymmetric misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and imbalanced group sizes through an application of neural networks to thyroid disease diagnosis. The results show that both asymmetric misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and imbalanced group sizes have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on the neural network classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that increasing the sample size and resampling are two effective approaches to counteract the problems.

## 1. Introduction

The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem arises when an investigator wishes to classify objects into one of several groups on the basis of their attribute measurements. Many business decision making situations such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial distress detection, company performance evaluation, target marketing, production process monitoring, quality control, bond rating and credit scoring can be considered as classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems also exist in many other <sup>fi</sup>elds such as medical diagnosis, <sup>fi</sup>nger print detection, and speech and hand writing recognition.

Arti<sup>fi</sup>cial neural networks (ANNs) are one of the popular methods for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems [1,6–8,10]. Compared to most traditional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approaches, ANNs are nonlinear, nonparametric, and adaptive. They can theoretically approximate any fundamental relationship with arbitrary accuracy. They are ideally suitable for problems where observations are easy to obtain but the data structure or underlying relationship is unknown. Other important features of ANNs that make them attractive for general classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems are (1) their link to Bayes decision theory in terms of posterior probability estimation [27] and (2) their link to traditional statistical classi<sup>fi</sup>ers such as discriminant analysis, logistic regression, classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation tree, and nearest neighbor methods [33].

Despite the growing popularity of ANNs for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, few studies in the literature take asymmetric misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs into consideration. In many cases, researchers simply make the assumption of equal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs without due process. Under this assumption, the objective of ANNs is equivalent to minimizing the total number of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed cases, and not the total misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost. The equal cost assumption can simplify the model development and the selection of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation cutoff point. This simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation is not appropriate for situations where misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs have severe unequal consequences for different groups. Depending on the situation and the perspective of the decision maker, the differences in misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost can be quite large. For example, in bankruptcy prediction, a misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation resulting from classifying a well-managed bank as an out-of-control one may have less severe consequences than misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation derived from failure to detect an out-of-control bank for government regulators. A classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model based on equal cost assumption cannot provide enough opportunity for an early identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of potential <sup>fi</sup>nancial decline to closely monitor those problem institutions and to take immediate corrective actions. A classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model with higher capability to detect insolvent institutions will be more appropriate for those information users, given the magnitude of the banking crisis and the enormous costs of resolution.

Medical diagnosis is another common case of asymmetric misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs. Medical examiners typically assign higher cost for misclassifying a malignant tumor as a benign one to save patient's life or avoid legal issues. It is clear that in these situations, ignoring the unequal consequences of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation will generate bias and result in a classi<sup>fi</sup>er with little practical value.

Several previous studies have provided evidence that the unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ANN performance and optimal decision making. Kohers et al. [12,13] utilized different penalty cost functions in the context of overestimating and underestimating the actual future values to examine the effectiveness of ANNs as forecasting composite models. Salchenberger et al. [30] evaluated the ability of ANNs to predict thrift institution failures by considering the effect of different cutoff points on the Type I and Type II errors. Philipoom et al. [24] used a cost-based due-date assignment scheme to suggest that the cost of early completion may differ in form and/or degree from the cost of tardiness. They found that implicitly ignoring asymmetric consequences in the due-date assignment could be costly and ANNs could be more appropriate for problems with unequal costs for earliness and tardiness than linear programming approaches. They also suggested that ANNs can be used for a wide range of cost functions, whereas other methodologies are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more restricted. Berardi and Zhang [2] investigated the effect of unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs on neural network classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance. Their results suggested that different cost considerations had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on the neural network classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance, particularly for smaller groups, and that appropriate use of cost information could aid in optimal decision making in a situation in which correct identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of some groups is of utmost importance.

Another issue associated with ANN application is imbalanced group sizes. The imbalanced problem occurs when there are many more instances in some groups than in others. The ability of ANNs to perform static pattern discrimination stems from their potential to create a speci<sup>fi</sup>c nonlinear transformation into a space spanned by the outputs of the hidden units in which class separation is easier [17,18]. This transformation is constrained to maximize a feature extraction criterion, which may be viewed as nonlinear multi-dimensional generalization of Fisher's linear discriminant function. Since this criterion involves the weighted between-class covariance matrix, adaptive networks trained on a multi-group classi<sup>fi</sup>er problem exhibit a strong bias in favor of those classes that have the large membership in the training data. The bias toward a large group is also an undesirable feature of networks in situations where information on one particular class may be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or expensive to obtain than other classes.

In practical applications, the level of imbalance can be drastic, with the ratio of the smallest group size to largest group size as high as 1 to 100, 1 to 1000, 1 to 10,000, or higher [20,25,32]. Even though it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for ANNs to learn from imbalanced data sets, a large number of studies in the literature ignore the issue as though the data are balanced [4,17]. However, some previous researchers in areas such as fraud detection, telecommunications management, and oil spill detection provide evidence that the imbalanced data set can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ANN performance and the optimal decision making [3,5,15].

Therefore, developing a neural classi<sup>fi</sup>er that takes into consideration both cost and group imbalance is very important for practical applications. Unfortunately, a majority of the studies in the literature focus on either cost or group imbalance and are often limited in both scope and size. Kotsiantis et al. [14] review several common methods in addressing imbalanced data sets, which include data sampling and cost-sensitive learning. Li [16] shows how a bagging ensemble variation method can be used to classify imbalanced data. Zhou and Liu [34] empirically evaluate several sampling methods in addressing training cost-sensitive neural networks. Kamimura and Uchida [9] propose a cost-sensitive greedy network algorithm with Gaussian activation functions. Peng et al. [23] use a cost-sensitive ensemble method for breast cancer diagnosis. Pendharkar [21] and Pendharkar and Nanda [22] develop neural network training methods based on threshold varying and genetic algorithm.

This research aims to explore the effects of asymmetric misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and imbalanced group sizes on ANN performance. In addition, through a comprehensive and systematic experimental study on a medical diagnosis problem (thyroid disease diagnosis), we are able to suggest strategies to deal with classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs and uneven group distributions. In thyroid diagnosis, the goal is to determine whether a patient has a normally functioning thyroid, an under-functioning thyroid (hypothyroid), or an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroid) with a number of patients' attributes such as age, gender, and health condition, as well as results of parents' various medical tests. Thyroid diagnosis represents a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult yet interesting classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem because this is a three-group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem with extremely unbalanced group memberships. Because of the large total sample size, we are able to use a cross-validation approach to study the effect of sample size as well.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section discusses the methodology regarding research design and data sets used in this study. Results are then analyzed and reported. Finally, summary and conclusion are provided.

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. Data set

The data set used in this study is selected from the well-known UCI (University of California, Irvine) data repository which has been used as a benchmark for various machine learning techniques. There are 7200 cases in this thyroid disease data set, which classi<sup>fi</sup>es a patient as having a normally functioning thyroid, an under-functioning thyroid (hypothyroid), or an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroid). The hyperthyroid class represents 2.3% (166 cases) of the data points, the hypothyroid class accounts for 5.1% (367 cases) of the observations, while the normal group makes up the remaining 92.6% (6667 cases). The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of thyroid level is a challenging task because the data set is highly imbalanced. For each of the 7200 cases, there are 21 attributes with 15 binary and 6 continuous variables used to determine in which of the three classes the patient belongs. These attributes represent information on patients such as age, gender, health condition, and the results of various medical tests [19,26,31]. The original data set is further divided into two parts: The test set is composed of 3700 observations while the rest of the data set is used for training purposes.

## 2.2. Research design

To systematically investigate the effect of unequal cost, uneven group size, and sample size on neural network classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, a 6 by 5 by 4 (a total of 120 cells) factorial experiment is administered. The <sup>fi</sup>rst factor in our investigation is the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost. We consider six different levels of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost for each group. These six levels are represented by misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost ratio (CR) among the three groups: (1:1:1), (3:1.5:1), (4.5:2.25:1), (7:3.5:1), (12:6:1), and (27:13.5:1), where the ratio denotes relative magnitude of one group misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost over others. For example, (1:1:1) means that all misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs are equal while (3:1.5:1) indicates that the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost for group 1 (hyperthyroid) is twice as severe as that for group 2 (hypothyroid) and is three times as much as that for group 3 (normal thyroid). Although many other alternative cost values can be selected based on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c situation and consideration, these selected levels re<sup>fl</sup>ect a reasonable range of possible values in this study as they have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the subsequent classi<sup>fi</sup>cation decision as discussed in the next section. In addition, we choose these ratios to match the relative differences in group size levels as discussed below.

The second experimental factor is the imbalanced group size. We consider <sup>fi</sup>ve levels of the imbalanced group size as the ratios of the sample sizes in these groups: (1:2:27), (2:4:24), (3:6:21), (4:8:18), and (5:10:15). As in misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost ratios, these group size ratios represent relative scale of difference in sizes of three groups. For example, (1:2:27) means that the smallest group (hyperthyroid) is only half the size of the next smallest group (hypothyroid) while the largest group (normal) is 27 times as large as the smallest group. We keep the relative size of the two small group constants (ratio 1:2) to represent that ratio in the original data set. However, in a real application situation, since the size of the large group may vary, we consider different proportions of large group in the whole data set to examine its effect. Note that the sum of the group ratio sizes in each of these levels is equal to 30. Note also here that in the most unbalanced group situation $( { \mathrm { i . e . , r a t i o = } } 1 { : } 2 { : } 2 7 ) ,$ , the largest group has 90% (=27/ (1+2+27)) of the total observations. At the other extreme, the largest group contains only 50% $( = 1 5 / ( 5 + 1 0 + 1 5 ) )$ of the total observations, which is a more balanced group case as the large group size equals the sum of the two smaller group sizes. In cases of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient observations in the smaller groups in some of our experimental settings, the bootstrapping method (sampling with replacement) is used to augment the group size.

The last factor we considered is the sample size effect. Just like in most practical situations, we are limited to the available data where increasing sample size may not be feasible. Training sample size is varied at four levels: with sample size $N = 3 5 0 0 , N = 2 5 0 0 , N = 1 5 0 0$ and N=500. In each case, we randomly select training samples from the original training sample of 3500 observations.

Realizing that neural networks are sensitive to the training samples, we use a resampling scheme to draw multiple samples to examine the robustness of the neural classi<sup>fi</sup>ers. With this scheme, for each of the 120 cells in our experiment, we randomly draw 30 samples/replications for training. Then, neural networks trained on each of these replications are tested with the untouched test sample of 3700 observations.

## 2.3. Neural networks

We use the feed-forward type of neural networks in this study. All neural network structures are fully connected with one hidden layer. Twenty-one input nodes corresponding to 21 input attributes are used. Three binary output nodes are employed, corresponding to the three classes of thyroid conditions. We <sup>fi</sup>x the number of hidden nodes at three to investigate the main issues of unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>- cation costs and uneven group sizes. This number is chosen based on a pilot run with the original data set. The target value for each node is either zero or one depending on the true output class. The logistic activation function is used for both hidden and output nodes.

Training methodology is an important factor in designing feedforward neural networks. The purpose of neural network training is to estimate the node connection weights in order to minimize an error measure such as sum of squared errors or cross entropy. We use a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, faster converging training method called Rprop, for Resilient backpropagation [29]. Rprop is a local adaptive learning scheme, performing supervised batch learning. The use of Rprop eliminates the harmful effect of the size of the partial derivative on the weight step. As a consequence, only the sign of the derivative is considered to show the weight update. The method is very <sup>fl</sup>exible and can be used for any error function, as detailed in Riedmiller and Braun [29] and Riedmiller [28].

In order to have more robust result, cross entropy instead of sum of square error function is used as the objective function in the neural network training process. The entropy function is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
E = \sum_ {n} \sum_ {c} [ t _ {n, c} \log y _ {n, c} + (1 - t _ {n, c}) \log (1 - y _ {n, c}) ],
$$

where c denotes the class, n denotes pattern, y denotes the network output, and t denotes the target value for the network output. See

Kline and Berardi [11] for a discussion on the relative merits of the cross entropy function for ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>ers.

As outlined earlier, we have 30 random samples from the original training sample for each of the 120 experimental settings. Results will be summarized over the 30 training samples. Although detailed tables are available for all scenarios, graphical analyses are focused to facilitate the presentation of <sup>fi</sup>ndings and patterns.

Table 1 reports the three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) results for the overall effect of three factors as well as their interactions. Table 2 shows the ANOVA results for the effects of these factors on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance for each of the three test groups. One possible explanation of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results is that (1) our sample size is very large and (2) all factors contribute to the learning and prediction of the neural classi<sup>fi</sup>er jointly instead of independently.

The group size effect can be seen from Figs. 2–5 which summarize the results of overall classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rates with different misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost ratios and different sample sizes. Several observations can be made here. First, the patterns observed in Fig. 1 remain true for other group size ratios. That is, as the CR increases, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate decreases across all samples. In addition, larger sample sizes are relatively insensitive to the change of the cost ratio. But the smaller samples (especially with the sample size of 500) are quite sensitive to the change of CR. It is interesting to see that as the group size ratio becomes more balanced, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance becomes worse for all sample sizes and for larger CR values. This suggests that when the groups are more balanced, unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost consequences can have a dramatic effect on the neural classi<sup>fi</sup>er especially when the sample size is small.

Despite the high overall classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate, the group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance of neural networks is impeded by the problem of imbalance data sets. Table 5 is for the situation of highest imbalanced training set with the group size ratio of (1:2:27) while Table 6 is for the lowest imbalanced training set with group size ratio of (5:10:15). ## 4. Summary and conclusion

Neural networks have achieved remarkable successes as reported in numerous published studies in many classi<sup>fi</sup>cation related problems. However, most of these studies use the simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed assumption of equal consequence of a misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation to ease model development and selection of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation decision points. With this assumption, the total or overall classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance is the only objective to maximize. ANNs are often able to achieve high overall classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate, yet this does not suggest that the result is useful for many practical decision making problems where misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs are unequal and group size is uneven. As demonstrated in this study, when a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem is dominated by one large group or the groups are extremely unbalanced, high classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rate does not indicate the usefulness of the ANN model to classify members in often more important smaller groups. Thus, in building an ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model, it is important to consider both misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost consequence and the group imbalance issue in order to provide better decision making capability to reduce overall risk.

In this study, we show that both misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost and the group size have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance not only for the overall situation but also for the more important group classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Failing to consider these factors in designing a neural classi<sup>fi</sup>er will have serious effects on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation decision for many applications, including medical diagnosis. Through an application of thyroid disease diagnosis, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, depending on different decision makers' perspectives and situations, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance for small but important groups can be achieved. With the cost adjustment, ANNs are more capable of achieving an optimal classi<sup>fi</sup>cation decision to reduce the risk of medical maltreatments. In addition, the sample size is found to be important in developing ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models for unbalanced groups and unequal misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors.

When a database for a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem such as thyroid diagnosis is highly skewed toward one outcome, the predictive ability of the ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model is weakened. The use of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost information is obviously important for two reasons. First, many real-world classi<sup>fi</sup>cation applications such as bankruptcy prediction, quality control, and medical diagnosis naturally entail unequal consequences for different classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors. Ignoring this uneven cost information in decision making will cause considerable loss or risk to the businesses and/or society. Second, appropriate use of asymmetric cost information can help decision makers to achieve their goals of minimizing risks for particular mistakes made by the classi<sup>fi</sup>er. On the other hand, without the consideration of the imbalanced group size problem, ANN models can easily identify normal thyroid cases (the majority group), while the hyper- and hypothyroid patients (the minority groups) are poorly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Real-world data sets are often predominately composed of “normal” cases with only a small percentage of “abnormal” but “interesting” cases. It is often the case that the cost of misclassifying an abnormal (interesting) case as a normal case is much higher than the cost of the reverse misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation error. Both misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost and the size of the group imbalance are important considerations in improving ANN classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance for optimal decision making.

Our experimental results suggest the following strategies to counteract the problems of unbalanced data and misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation costs. First, increasing sample size can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the effect of both misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation cost and data imbalance. Therefore, if possible, researchers should seek to have a larger sample. Second, resampling is an effective approach to handle unequal error costs and unbalanced group distribution. It is found that when the misclassi-<sup>fi</sup>cation cost is highly uneven, increasing the portion of data in the smallest group can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the accuracy of the neural classi<sup>fi</sup>er.
