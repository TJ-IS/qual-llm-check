# Acceptance of electronic tax filing: A study of taxpayer intentions

Jen-Ruei Fu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Cheng-Kiang Farn <sup>a,1</sup>, Wen-Pin Chao <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Kaohsiung University of Applied Sciences, 415 Chien Kung Road, Kaohsiung 807, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>b</sup> National Tax Administration of Northern Taiwan Province, Ministry of Finance, No. 186 Fushing Road, Tau-Yuan City, Tao-Yuan County, Taiwan 330, ROC

Received 2 April 2004; received in revised form 6 February 2005; accepted 1 April 2005 Available online 29 August 2005

## Abstract

Government today has benefited from information technology in many ways. The importance of understanding and influencing citizens’ acceptance of e-government services is critical, given the investment in technology and the potential for cost saving. Using the filing of personal income tax in Taiwan as an example of e-government services, this study integrated two important theories (TAM and TPB), and discussed the factors affecting the taxpayers’ intention to adopt a particular tax-filing method (from manual, two-dimensional barcode, or Internet) based on empirical data gathered from a large-scale nationwide survey. The demographic characteristics and perceptions of the taxpayers were also explored in order to identify potential determinants. Results indicated that taxpayers tend to concentrate on the usefulness of a tax-filing method and may be fairly pragmatic in developing general attitudes towards using the method. Interestingly, the effects of perceived ease of use, subjective norms, and self-efficacy on behavioral intention were different for manual and electronic tax-filers. Understanding these factors can extend our knowledge of taxpayers’ decision making and lead to better planning and implementation of e-government services.

## 1. Introduction

According to the annual Global e-Government Study of Brown University in the United States, Taiwan’s ranking rose from the world’s number 2 position in 2001 to first in 2002, among the 198 countries surveyed [10]; e-government in Taiwan provides its citizens and organizations with convenient access to government information and services. Electronic Filing of Personal Income Tax (eTax) is an important application that automates tax related processes in an attempt to improve efficiency in assessing and collecting tax information. It has the potential to improve tax-filing service while at the same time reducing costs to both taxpayers and tax collecting agencies. However, despite the readily available tax preparation software and its promotion by the government, only forty percent of all taxpayers switched to eTax and most taxpayers were unwilling to give up their paper forms. Thus, the problem of underutilization still remained and plagued governments. Understanding why people accept or reject an IS has proven to be a challenging issues. The growing interest in e-government thus raises the question of how governments can increase citizens’ adoption of eTax.

User acceptance was defined by Swanson [30] to be a ‘‘potential user’s predisposition toward personally using a specific system.’’ A review of previous research suggests that many studies were anchored in behavioral intention and reported a strong causal link between behavioral intention and targeted behavior [8]. Agarwal and Prasad [2] also argued that, in a survey-based research design, analysis of intention is more appropriate than actual usage.

Building on a study of technology acceptance, we attempted to develop further understanding of taxpayers’ behavioral intention in the Taiwanese context. Specifically, a nation-wide survey of taxpayers supported by the National Tax Administration (NTA) of Northern Taiwan Province was conducted to examine the actions that participants were likely to take.

## 2. Personal tax filing in Taiwan

Starting in 1998, citizens in Taiwan were able to choose among three tax-filing methods: manual, Internet-based, and two-dimensional (2D) barcode. Manual filing is the traditional method either by hand or typewriter. Taxpayers usually perform complex calculations using mental arithmetic or calculator, and then the return is delivered to the tax agency through the postal service or in person. After receiving a return, the agency uses a data entry service to input the data. This process is cumbersome, time-consuming, and paper-intensive for both taxpayers and the tax agency. Error checking on paper is fault-intensive and was traditionally done by the tax agency staff.

The explosive growth in computing and use of the Internet provided a way for the tax agency to improve service quality while reducing cost. After a 2-year small-scale pilot study, in 1998 the agency introduced Internet-based and 2D barcode tax-filing methods in an attempt to reduce the costs of tax collection. Both of these electronic tax-filing methods use tax preparation software provided by the agency; the forms can be downloaded through the Internet or obtained from widely distributed compact disks (CD-ROM) free of charge. Calculation, error checking and suggestion for best return option for the taxpayer are carried out automatically by the software. Taxpayers can decide to either directly transmit the data to the tax agency through the Internet, or print a two- to three-page paper income tax return with a 2D barcode in the lower left-hand corner of the return and deliver it to the tax agency through the postal service. For the former, a taxpayer who possesses public key certification issued by a government agency can download his or her personal income tax withholding records via the Internet. Generally, a taxpayer only has to confirm the validity of these records without bothering to enter them. Thus, the Internet filing method may simplify and expedite the tax-filing process.

The primary purpose of a 2D barcode filing was to facilitate a smooth transition from pen-and-paper to Internet tax filing. Since most taxpayers had not obtained a government issued public key certificate, the 2D barcode provided taxpayers who had a computer a sound alternative to filing electronically. 2D barcode tax filing was similar to 1040 filing offered by the IRS in the U.S. A taxpayer used the tax preparation software to key in data and print a 2D barcode which encoded all the needed information in the return. The 2D barcode worked like a paper disk (see Fig. 1 for an example) which contained machinereadable information to help the agency quickly feed tax information forms into the agency’s IS through a scanner. The advantages to the government were significant. Elimination of manual entry errors by taxpayers provided the tax agency with a cleaner stream of income tax returns, requiring less error correction computations and adjustment notices.

## 3. Theoretical background and research model

Technology acceptance is important. We define this as an individual’s psychological state with regard to his or her voluntary, intended use of a technology [16]. A fundamental intention-based theory is the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [13]. According to this, external stimuli influence a person’s attitude toward a behavior indirectly by influencing his or her salient beliefs about the consequences of performing the behavior. Adapted from this, both the technology acceptance model (TAM) and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) are well established in the IT arena and appear to be widely accepted [23,26,31].

## 3.1. The technology acceptance model (TAM)

TAM theorizes that an individual’s intention towards using a system is jointly determined by perceived usefulness (PU), the user’s ‘‘subjective probability that using a specific application system will increase his or her job performance’’ [11], and perceived ease of use (PEOU), ‘‘the degree to which the . . . user expects the target system to be free of effort.’’ The effects of external variables (e.g., system design characteristics) on behavioral intention (BI) are mediated by these beliefs. According the Davis et al. [12], PEOU also has a direct effect on PU [35].

In predicting usage, TAM models might be useful within and across organizations for evaluating applications or technologies, or to make comparisons between user groups or applications, as suggested by Adams et al. [1] and Subramanian [29]. However, TAM has limitations in being applied beyond the workplace because its fundamental constructs do not fully reflect the variety of user task environment and constraints. Paul and John [25] suggested that TAM is a useful model but has to be integrated into a broader one which would include variables related to both human and social factors. The theory of planned behavior (TPB) takes these factors into account.

## 3.2. Theory of planned behavior (TPB)

TPB incorporates subjective norms (SN) and perceived behaviors control (PBC) as direct determinants of BI. Together with TAM, TPB was selected to provide a necessary theoretical premise for the research model examined in our study.

TPB asserts that BI is jointly determined by one’s attitude, which reflects positive feelings towards performing a behavior, SN, which reflects perceptions that other people desire the individual to perform a particular way; PBC, which reflects internal and external constraints on the performing of the action. In our context, TPB suggests that a taxpayer is more willing to file electronically if she or he has positive attitude towards using eTax, wants to comply with other important people’s opinions on the use of eTax, and has the requisite resources, skills, or opportunities.

## 3.3. Research model

Neither TAM nor TPB have been found to provide consistently superior explanations or predictions of behavior. A growing body of research has been focused on integrating them to predict usage intention from different viewpoints: SN has been included as an additional predictor of intention for mandatory settings [36], while Venkatesh and Davis proposed a theoretical framework that integrated TPB as the determinant of PEOU [33,34] and PU; also Riemenschneider et al. developed a series of models which involved various loose to tight combinations and employed a ‘‘combine and conquer’’ strategy to examine their overall fit and relative explanatory power. Finally, the decomposed theory of planned behavior, which decomposes attitude using core constructs of TAM, was proposed by Mathieson and has been further validated by Taylor and Todd and

Chau et al. The decomposed model allows researchers to pay attention to key antecedents to intention and has provided a richer understanding of how each of these antecedents explains variance across users. Given the focus on behavioral intention, we used the decomposed model in our research model.

Using TPB as a basic structure, attitude is decomposed by incorporating PU and PEOU. The approach provides several advantages. First, it seems unlikely that belief structures will be consistently related to intention; the relationships should become clearer and more readily understood. Second, by focusing on specific beliefs, the model becomes more managerially relevant, pointing to specific factors that may influence adoption and usage. The model is shown in Fig. 4.

Several studies have examined the role of attitude on IT usage behavior. The attitude variable was considered as a mediating variable that influences the BI and is affected by PU and PEOU. However, weak support of the relationship was found between the attitudes toward a specific IS and BI. Kay [21] suggested that it would be better to be as specific as possible about the content of the attitude object with respect to measuring attitudes toward IS usage. The omission of attitude allows researchers to investigate the influence of PEOU and PU on BI. Thus, the ‘‘attitudes’’ factor is removed to simplify the model. Additionally, a link between PEOU and BI is included because empirical studies have found a significant relationship between them [22].

The PBC construct refers to an individual’s perceptions of the presence or absence of resources or opportunities necessary for performing an action. It is decomposed into both the internal notion of individual ‘‘self-efficacy (SE)’’ [4] and to external resource constraints, similar to Triandis’s [32] facil itating conditions. SE is related to perceived ability. With respect to eTax usage, we anticipated that higher levels of SE would lead to higher levels of BI toward eTax. The facilitating conditions consist of two subdimensions: resource factors (RFC), such as computer equipment, and technology issues (TFC), that may restrict usage. The absence of facilitating resources results in barriers to usage which may inhibit usage. Thus, a taxpayer might be unwilling to accept eTax if little or no computer equipment is available, and if technical support is low.

As the literature in consumer behavior suggests, when engaging in an online transaction process, concerns of inherent risk have surfaced as salient to many potential adopters [18]. However, actual risk is difficult to measure objectively, literature has therefore primarily addressed the notion of perceived risk (PR), defined here as the taxpayer’s perception of the uncertainty and adverse consequence of a desired outcome. Perceived risk of online shopping has been shown to influence attitude towards online purchasing [17]. Taxpayers may hesitate to file their tax return electronically if they perceive a lack of security in the process.

Also included in our model is compatibility (CMP), which serves as an antecedent of both PU and PEOU. Compatibility, defined as the degree to which the technology fits the potential adopter’s previous experience, work practice, and needs, has been identified as an essential factor for innovation adoption [27]. Considerable prior research has reported significant effect of CMP on the user technology acceptance decision. More recently, Chau et al. examined the importance of CMP in physicians technology acceptance and found that CMP is a significant determinant of PU but not PEOU.

## 4. Research method and results

## 4.1. Data collection

Data to test the model were drawn from a crosssectional field study of Taiwanese taxpayers in 2003. A nation-wide questionnaire-based survey, supported by the tax agency, was conducted. Since taxpayers were able to adopt any one of the three filing methods (manual, 2D barcode, or Internet tax filing) at will, subjects were classified into one of three groups according to the method they adopted. Three variations of a questionnaire were developed and different data collection methods were employed to target the different groups.

Questionnaires were distributed via an online website, offline CD-ROM, and traditional mail for the convenience of respondents. First, the online survey was set up on the website of the tax agency (http://www.ntx.gov.tw). After completing the filing of an income tax return, the tax preparation program invited the taxpayer to participate in the survey. The respondent could choose to participate or not with a mouse click. The completed response was stored directly into a database. In order to access offline users, the survey program was bundled with the tax preparation software on the CD-ROM. To solicit responses, subjects were invited to fill out the questionnaire soon after completing their filing. The completed response was encoded and saved together with the income tax return in the 2D barcode for researchers. It should be noted that data collected using electronic means (e.g., WWW, offline program) have similar psychometric qualities to data collected through physical procedures [28], thus the methods used here can be justified. Finally, it was not easy for researchers to approach pen-and-paper taxpayers who did not use or have computer equipment, thus 1000 random respondents were selected from manual taxfiling taxpayers and questionnaires were sent by traditional mail to them with a stamped return envelope addressed to the tax agency.

The period of the data collection lasted from May to July of 2003 filing season. To stimulate responses, reminder letters were sent out after two weeks by mail or email and respondents were told that respondent names would be entered into a lottery for a gift. All respondents were guaranteed confidentiality. Completed questionnaires were received from 59,166 taxpayers (out of a population of 755,541 for Internet and 1,427,046 for 2D-barcode taxpayers) with 26,989 using Internet tax filing (a 3.51% response rate), 31,596 using 2D barcode tax filing (2.21%), and 582 using manual tax-filing method (58.2%). Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the respondents.

## 4.2. Measurement of constructs

Constructs were measured using multiple-item scales, drawn from pre-validated measures in previous studies. Items for PU, PEOU, and BI were adapted from Davis and Chau et al.; items for SN, SE, PBC, and CMP were from Taylor and Todd; PR was modified from Cases [7]. All items were measured using a seven-point Likert-type scale with anchors on ‘‘strongly agree’’ and ‘‘strongly disagree.’’ Since taxpayers are divided into three groups, appropriate modifications to make questionnaire items specifically relevant to the target populations were needed. For example, a sample item to assess Internet taxpayers’ behavioral intention: ‘‘I intend to use Internet filing method for my income tax return next year’’ was slightly modified for manual taxpayers to: ‘‘I intend to use manual filing method for my income tax return next year.’’ Since it is meaningless to assess manual taxpayers’ SE, TFC, and RFC for pen-and-paper technology, items for these constructs were consistent across the three groups in assessing taxpayers’ PBC for electronic tax-filing methods. Appendix A lists a final Internet-based version of questionnaire items used to measure each construct and its source.

A pretest was conducted to ensure that the instrument possessed acceptable reliability and validity. The subjects in the pretest sample were working students who had tax-filing experience in prior years. One hundred and seventy-eight subjects voluntarily completed the survey. All of the scales had reliabilities of 0.8 or more. The final questionnaire contained 31 questions to measure the constructs of interest, as well as some demographic and other related questions.

In analyzing the collected data, our study followed the two-step procedure recommended by Anderson and Gerbing [3]. We estimated and re-specified the measurement model prior to incorporating the structural restrictions. Convergent and discriminant validity of the remaining items and scales were tested with confirmatory factory analysis (CFA) using the EQS program [6]. Each item was modeled as a reflective indicator of its hypothesized latent construct. The nine constructs were allowed to covary in the CFA model. The measurement models were evaluated using maximum likelihood estimation. James et al. [20] recommended having three or four indicators for each latent construct to avoid model identification problems. Three constructs failed to meet this criterion. To avoid the problems associated with having only two indicators of three constructs (SN, TFC and RFC), we constrained the measurement errors of two items to be equal as suggested by James et al. Appendix B presents the results of the CFA analysis.

Since the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test is sensitive to sample size and the probability of rejecting any model increases as the sample size increases, even when the model is minimally false [5], it is suggested that other model fit indices should be used, and scores of 0.9 or higher on Bentler Bonett Normed Fit Index (NFI), Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI) and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) should be considered evidence of good fit. For the current CFA models, NFI, NNFI, and CFI were over 0.9 (see Table 2), suggesting adequate model fit.

Composite reliabilities $( \rho _ { \mathrm { c } } )$ of constructs ranged between 0.80 and 0.97 (see Appendix D). AVE, ranging from 0.67 to 0.92, was greater than the variance due to measurement error. Hence, all three conditions for convergent validity were met.

## 4.3. Data analysis and results

The research model presented earlier was tested using the structural equation modeling (SEM) approach. We estimated the model for all three groups. Overall, the goodness-of-fit of the structural model was comparable to that of the previous CFA models and provided evidence of adequate fit in the three groups.

Figs. 5–7 illustrate the path coefficients for the three groups, together with their significance. Two observations are worth noting. First, the path significance is not consistent across the three groups. This suggests that usage intentions of different taxfiling methods are associated with different antecedents. Second, several coefficient of significant paths are relatively small, especially for electronic taxpayers. The possible explanation for this is the statistical power of the test; the greater the power, the more often the path will be found to be ‘‘significant.’’ With a larger sample population of the 2D barcode and Internet groups, ‘‘non-significant’’ will be found less often. Thus, the paths reported statistically significant may not necessarily have real meaning.

Overall, PU was the strongest determinant and explained most of the variance of BI in all three groups, as shown in Table 3. For the TAM model, PEOU explained a significant amount of the variance in PU. This is consistent with most previous research. CMP was found to be a significant determinant of PU and PEOU.

The path coefficients of the other independent variables in TPB model (SN, SE, TFC, and RFC) were less than 0.1 for electronic taxpayers, which suggested that the four variables were not important predictors of BI for them. For manual taxpayers, the effects of PEOU, SN, and SE on BI were significant and stronger than the other two types of taxpayer. SE was also found to be significant, but to have a negative impact, suggesting that taxpayers with higher level of computer self-efficacy will tend to give up manual tax-filing method. Finally, PR, TFC, and RFC have little or no effect on BI.

## 5. Exploratory study

In our exploratory study we tried to identify potential differences among the three kinds of taxpayers. Both uni- and multivariate analysis of variance were used to assess the difference among the three groups. With these steps we were trying to provide a picture of the taxpayers in each group and give insight into the reasons for adoption of tax-filing methods.

## 5.1. Dependent and independent variables

The independent variable was derived by assigning a value of 1 to Internet taxpayers, 2 to 2D barcode taxpayers, and 3 to manual taxpayers. For the dependent variables, respondents were asked to give their demographics (age, gender, education, prior experience with computers and Internet, and computer and Internet resource conditions). These attributes were selected from a review of the literature [19], as well as discussion with staff of the tax agency. Table 1 shows the eight variables. Since the continuous and interval levels for dependent variables are required for MANOVA, gender was not included in the analysis. Finally, the perceptual variables were included to make all possible comparisons.

## 5.2. Sample selection and analysis

MANOVA and ANOVA were performed in an attempt to understand how the three kinds of taxpayers varied. MANOVA was employed to test if the centroids or the mean vectors of the three groups were significantly different, then a pairwise, univariate analysis was performed to determine which variables contributed to the difference between them. Since the sample sizes of three groups varied substantially, we randomly sampled from the larger groups, thereby reducing their size to a level comparable to the smallest group and thus worked with 582 samples in each group.

## 5.3. Results of exploratory study

The assumption of homoscedasticity was tested using Box’s M. Results indicated that M is significant $( M = 3 5 7 0 ; \ F = 1 0 . 3 0 ; \ \mathrm { s i g . } = . 0 0 0 )$ which suggested that the assumption was violated. Research has shown that for equal cell sizes, the significance level is not appreciably affected by unequal covariance matrices [24]. The three groups in this study are of the same size, thus a violation of this assumption has minimal impact. The F test and Wilks’ l show that the centroid of means of the dependent variables were not the same for the three groups which established that there were significant differences among them.

Along with the overall results, a post hoc, univariate test of group differences was conducted to determine which group means differed significantly from others. This helped specify the exact nature of the overall effect determined by the F test. Pairwise multiple comparison tests were employed to test each pair of groups to identify similarities and differences. Results, as shown in Table 4, indicated that 2D barcode and Internet taxpayers were quite similar across six demographic variables and five perceptual variables; significant differences between electronic and manual taxpayers were found in all variables in the study.

## 6. Discussion

Through the confirmatory and exploratory analyses, this study can help practitioners and researchers better understand taxpayers’ acceptance decision of eTax; this may shed light on interesting and subtle difference between different kinds of taxpayers. The study has the merits of conducting a technology acceptance investigation in a real-world tax-filing setting involving individual taxpayers and various taxfiling methods. Such a setting should increase the generalizability of the results.

While the exploratory study did not advance specific hypotheses, several interesting relationships were observed. First, Table 4 suggests that manual taxpayers, in comparison with electronic ones, had a lower education and were older, had less computer and Internet experience, had less IS resources, and were online less frequently. Also, all the responses of perceptions for manual taxpayers were significantly lower than those of their counterparts. Finally, inspection of the mean and standard deviation revealed the fact that the electronic taxpayers intended to continue using the method. However, manual taxpayers’ usage intentions were relatively diverse.

The confirmatory study represented a conceptual replication by re-examining theoretical models in taxfiling settings. Overall, we saw major differences in the path coefficients between manual and electronic taxpayers, and little differences between electronic taxpayers. The same observation can also be found in the exploratory study.

Across the three kinds of taxpayers it is important to recognize that BI was largely driven by PU, which, on its own explained, almost 40% of the variance in BI for manual taxpayers and 64% for the other two kinds of taxpayer. Furthermore, CMP represented the second largest determinant of behavioral intention. Thus, it seems that taxpayers tend to concentrate on the usefulness of a tax-filing method and may be fairly pragmatic in developing general attitudes towards using it, and the compatibility of a tax-filing method with taxpayers’ practice is a crucial antecedent to their behavioral intention. Given the dominant effect of PU and compatibility, our findings suggested that it is important for the agency to incorporate useful and valuable functions into the eTax software for the targeted taxpayers’ practices and service needs and also to demonstrate the value of eTax to taxpayers.

It is interesting to note that the effects of PEOU, SN, and SE on BI were inconsistent between manual and electronic taxpayers. Examining the minor role of PEOU for the electronic tax-filing systems shows that our results contradicted the findings reported by some prior research. Furthermore, insignificant effect of SN was also found for electronic taxpayers but not for manual taxpayers. The exploratory study might provide an explanation in that manual taxpayers may be less adaptable to new technologies, and have less mental and cognitive capacity. As a consequence, electronic taxpayers may not consider PEOU or SN an issue of particular importance. Moreover, they might be more confident about their own point of view and place less weight on other person’s opinions [9]. Conversely, a manual taxpayer’s decision to adopt taxfiling methods could be more likely to be influenced by PEOU and social pressures.

Also interesting was the role of SE, which had significant but negative effect on BI for manual taxpayers. In contrast, the effect of SE on electronic taxpayers’ BI was negligible. Table 4 indicates that self-efficacy perception of electronic taxpayers was significantly higher than that for manual taxpayers. It seems likely that individuals with higher self-efficacy in IT have more options available and feel free to choose whatever they want.

Another two sub-constructs of PBC (TFC and RFC) do not seem to contribute to taxpayers’ future choice. However, manual taxpayers perceived much less technology and resource support than the other two groups, and thus facilitating conditions did constrain manual taxpayers’ behavior. It seems likely that TFC and RFC play the role of threshold for taxpayers’ real behavior in choosing filing methods. This suggests that the electronic tax-filing method may not reach the entire population, and a ‘‘digital divide’’ may exist.

Finally, in spite of electronic taxpayers having perceived higher risk than manual taxpayers, contrary to our prediction, PR may not be an important factor that directly influences taxpayers’ choice of tax-filing method.

## 7. Conclusion

The study provided some preliminary evidence about the criteria that taxpayers used to evaluate their adoption of a taxation IS. This could be valuable for governments in designing and implementing their egovernment services. However, this study has a number of limitations. First, the effects of PBC may depend on the taxpayers’ past usage experience. In our study, instruments for PBC were consistent across taxpayers but electronic taxpayers had experience with eTax while manual might not have had. Venkatesh et al. suggested that perceptions become more specific after direct experience. Manual taxpayers may base their PBC perception on their general beliefs about computers and computer use while electronic taxpayers may adjust their system-specific perceptions to reflect their interaction with eTax. Second, this study used a cross-sectional design and correlational data which did not provide irrefutable evidence of causation. Also, all data are self-reported, giving rise to problems associated with commonmethod variance. Third, our model accounted for about 67.1%, 63.6% and 67.1% of the variance across three groups indicating that variables other than those examined in this study are needed to explain additional variance.

The findings might have policy implications for the implementation of electronic tax-filing and egovernment services. Understanding adoption factors can extend their knowledge of taxpayers’ decision making and lead to better strategies. Our research provides a starting point for government seeking ways to improve citizens’ acceptance of eTax services. Additional research is also needed to determine whether the results of this study can be replicated in other populations and e-government services.
