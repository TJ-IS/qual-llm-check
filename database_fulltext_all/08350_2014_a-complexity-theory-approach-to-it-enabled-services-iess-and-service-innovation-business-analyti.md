# A complexity theory approach to IT-enabled services (IESs) and service innovation: Business analytics as an illustration of IES

Bongsug (Kevin) Chae

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, Kansas State University, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 7 November 2012 Received in revised form 7 June 2013 Accepted 17 July 2013 Available online 24 July 2013

Keywords: Service innovation IT-enabled services Business analytics Service-oriented decision support systems Complexity theory

## a b s t r a c t

While <sup>fi</sup>rms view services as the main source of their revenue and competitive advantage, understanding of service and service innovation is limited. This lack of understanding is especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in IT-Enabled Services (IESs) and IES innovation. Much work is needed to understand the contemporary trend of integrating diverse material and social resources to address complex organizational and individual needs. This article proposes a novel framework for IES and IES innovation and develops propositions and implications for research and practice. This work draws upon the tenet of complexity theory and conceptualizes IES as complex adaptive systems (CAS), with such properties and behaviors as diverse adaptive elements, nonlinear interaction, selforganization, and adaptive learning, and IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process of variation, selection, and retention (VSR). The proposed framework is illustrated using business analytics (BA) as a new kind of decision support service (DSS) throughout this paper. Several propositions are developed. Finally, we present a discussion and implications.

## 1. Introduction

Firms in the business-to-business (B-to-B) market now increasingly view services, rather than traditional products, as the main source of their revenue and competitive advantage [18,20,54,67,77]. To this end, there are several streams of research and practice in what is now called “service innovation”, from economics [26], operations [49], marketing [54], strategy [18], and organization science [20]. Particularly, information systems (IS) literature discusses information technology (IT), along with services and service innovation, as a primary driver for economic growth and <sup>fi</sup>rms' competitive advantage [51,76,77]. Previous studies have suggested many roles for IT in service innovation [14,22,23,34]. What is most evident is that IT (e.g., the Internet) has profoundly changed the ways customers interact with service providers [25]. Many traditional services (e.g., healthcare) are now delivered through IT (e.g., IT-enabled healthcare) [22,74], often increasing operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, the role of IT does not stop here: certain new services, such as digitized services [76], remote diagnostic decision services [31] and cloud-based decision support services [19], are simply not possible without ubiquitous IT and big data management platforms. Thus, not only is IT (re)combined with traditional services and goods, but emerging technologies also enable the emergence of a new generation of services. Simply put, IT is integral to a growing number of services [33].

We call these services IT-enabled services (IESs). IESs differ from traditional IT products, such as word processors and email software, for which production (producer) and consumption (user) tend to be separable. These products often stand alone and support pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned requirements, such as storing texts and transmitting messages. IESs also differ from traditional business consulting services, through which a customer gets a master plan (e.g., strategic roadmaps) as the <sup>fi</sup>nal deliverable from a service provider. Instead, IESs emerge through combining IT and other organizational resources (e.g., processes, skills, work practices) [20,74], which are sourced from beyond organizational boundaries, including traditional providers, customers and even third-parties (e.g., developers). Related concepts are integrated solutions [18], servitization [66] and resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations [69]. An example is business analytics (BA) service, which combines various tangible and intangible resources, such as high-quality data, analytical talents, processes, analytical software, powerful hardware and data management platforms, and other organizational resources from providers, customers, and others [17]. There is considerable potential for researchers in IS to make contributions to the emerging debates and challenges in IES and service innovation [6,7,51].

In the literature, there are potentially useful perspectives and theories [67] for the research and practice of service and service innovation in general. Both research and practice are growing in these areas. Compared to product innovation, however, there is still a very limited understanding about service and service innovation in general: relevant theory and empirical work are quite limited [55,75]. This lack of understanding is especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in IES and their innovation [7,13]. Much work is needed to understand the contemporary trend of integrating diverse material and social resources to address complex organizational and individual needs.

This article aims to respond by developing a novel theoretical perspective on IES and IES innovation. The proposed perspective draws upon the tenet of complexity theory and conceptualizes IES as complex adaptive systems (CAS) and IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process. From this perspective, IESs are viewed as internally complex, dynamic, adaptive, and emergent. They are co-evolving with a wide network of technologies, organizational and individual needs, rules and regulations, institutional arrangements, etc., through variation, selection, and retention (VSR). Through this, this paper broadly covers two important topics in both IES research and practice: what IESs are; and, how such services emerge and evolve. How something (e.g., IT artifacts) is conceptualized in<sup>fl</sup>uences our way of studying, analyzing, designing, delivering, and changing it [47]. Thus, the inquiry into IES conceptualizations is important, since it reveals what constitutes an IES and the interaction of its internal elements, and thus affects the way “various research priorities for the science of service” [49] are examined. However, focusing on “entities” may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient: understanding process is also necessary [24]. Thus, studying IES-related service innovation is necessary, since it is the process by which IES comes to life and evolves. We use BA service, as an example of IES, to illustrate the proposed perspective throughout this paper.

The contribution of this paper is multi-faceted. First, there is a recognition that complexity theory can be useful for service science [69] and IS research [61]. This research offers complexity theory as the basis for explaining IES and service innovation. Second, there is not yet consensus on service, in general, and IES, in particular. Many existing studies tend to offer a product-oriented view of service, which undoubtedly leads to a static view of IES, or an intangible view in which IT is either absent or treated as passive. The proposed CAS framework offers a dynamic view that IES are the emergent structures from the interaction of diverse adaptive elements (tangible and intangible). This helps properly explain the properties and behaviors of IES in practice, and further formulate ways to take advantage of such properties and behaviors. Third, innovation in the CAS view is evolutionary. Thus, IES innovation is formulated as a co-evolutionary process of VSR, through which IES and the environment are co-evolving. This is a novel framework that can help develop strategies for designing and managing IES by taking advantage of the evolutionary nature of IES innovation.

In what follows, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review the literature on BA as an example of IES. The next section summarizes existing views of service and service innovation and suggests the need for a perspective to explain complex properties and dynamic behaviors inherent in BA service and other IESs, and the co-evolutionary process in IES innovation. Then, we introduce complexity theory and propose a novel framework for IES and IES innovation. Drawn from the proposed framework, theoretical propositions are developed and several implications for theory and practice are discussed. This discussion includes what strategies are effective for IES innovation.

## 2. Business analytics services: an example of IT-enabled services

BA service is neither a single IT product nor a set of non-IT resources. Rather, this emerging service is a con<sup>fi</sup>guration of organizational resources with diverse ITs, which aims to enable more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective decision-making by organizations and individuals. A typical BA service involves complex data and analytics technologies (e.g., in-memory analytics, parallel programming), enabling “the creation, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and manipulation of digital artifacts in the process of converting input into output” [36]. Also, there are common organizational resources (e.g., analytical talents, analytic groups or business units, processes, analytical leadership, high-quality data) mingled with those technologies [17]. There are many applications of BA, including marketing analytics, health analytics, public safety analytics, supply chain analytics, and big data analytics [12].

While BA is a relatively new service, having received more attention from industry and academia than any other business solutions [17,19], it has long evolved from traditional data management and decision support services in the 1970s [12]. Thus, BA emphasizes the extensive use of data, advanced analytics (e.g., data mining, optimization, simulations), and fact-based decision methodologies for decision-making [17]. Lately, many leading providers (e.g., IBM, SAP) have developed BA as their core service offering. In addition, there is a fast growing number of medium and small-sized providers, who are specialized in BA service for diverse industries and applications. Many of these services have embraced capabilities for supporting mobile work, social media and machine-generated data, service-oriented architecture, and decentralized decision-making [12,19]. Many customer organizations and business units are reported to have BA service in place [17].

## 3. Literature review on (IT-enabled) service and service innovation

This review will suggest the need for a perspective to explain dy namic properties and behaviors inherent in IES and IES innovation.

## 3.1. Conceptualization of (IT-enabled) service

Vargo and Lusch [67] proposed that “All economies are services economies”. Studies in marketing, operations/supply chain management, and economics have advanced service research and proposed various disciplinary views of services. The interest in service research has appeared in the IS [2,6,51,75,77], demonstrated in various topics, including service computing, service business models, service innovation readiness, and IT service management.

These views and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of services can be broadly categorized into two perspectives: divergent and convergent (Table 1). Divergent perspectives set services apart from products or physical goods and highlight the differences between them [26]. Services are viewed as primarily intangible. Intangibility, heterogeneity, perishability, and others [25] are often mentioned as unique characteristics separating services from tangible products [64]. In this perspective, service may be viewed as supplementary to products or physical goods [67].

However, since any IESs include speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT artifacts, as core or peripheral elements, and are operated on speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital platforms, the divergent perspective on service as intangible units of economic exchange, can be less useful to explaining the design and evolution of IES and their dynamic and interactive natures [13]. Our presumption is that IT is an integral part of services. Emerging IESs, such as cloud computing service and BA [19], carry material IT artifacts (or ITenabled resources), as well as other types of resources. For example, recent BA services co-created by actors rely on IT platforms or development architectures (e.g., Hadoop) and advanced computing devices.

Convergent perspectives view services as combining both tangible and intangible resources for customers [26], so the concern is beyond tangible products or intangible actions. Related concepts are integrated solutions [18], customer solutions [54], servitization [66], and value cocreation con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. An actor (e.g., organization, unit, individual) needs service for its needs, such as information management and data governance: whether it is tangible or intangible is not the actor's concern. This is an integrative and process view of service that “service is the fundamental basis of exchange” [68].

Recently, the convergent perspective on service has received increasing attention [26,39,75,77]. A closely related concept is service system [11,39], which is largely dependent on systems science (or systems thinking) [2,58]. This systems view of service can be more useful than the divergent perspective for research and practice regarding what IESs are and how they emerge and evolve. The systems view allows a framework for analyzing what constitutes an IES, and how such a system creates and is created by external environments. This has implications for the design of IES or IES innovation.

In this vein, IES as CAS can offer greater insights about IES and innovation, since complexity theory represents advancement in the tenet of systems theory. Complexity theory is considered the next wave of systems theory [44]. Some key characteristics of IES identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature are emergence (or the emergent nature of service requirements) [13,22], heterogeneity or diversity in service [39], and service elements [71]. Emergence, diversity, and self-organization are important characteristics of CAS, among others, in the literature [3,9]. Viewing IES as CAS does not treat IT as exogenous to service systems. Instead, this proposed view makes IT an integral part of the service system. IESs are viewed as “emerging properties” in an evolutionary process.

## 3.2. IES innovation

There are different streams of discussing service innovation (Table 2), but not for IES innovation. One approach tries to understand service innovation from the extant views of product innovation. This approach presumes that product and service are similar in many aspects. Much of the existing understanding of service innovation is based on the accumulated knowledge of product innovation [30]. Product innovation models (e.g., phased-view process) have tended to emphasize formal decision-making and highly structured processes with a series of distinct stages or phases [16]. They can be regarded as producer or supplier-driven, due to the large role of producers in the development process. This formal, structured, and producerdriven view of innovation has in<sup>fl</sup>uenced extant views of service innovation [57].

Another approach has focused on the differences between product innovation and service innovation, since this approach tends to view service as intangible, separated from physical products. In this vein, some observe that service innovation is nonlinear and highly iterative [30], “in some cases a convergence, of planning and execution” (p. 18). Other studies [23] found that service innovation is characterized by a less formal process and lack of R&D investments. Service innovation is “<sup>fl</sup>uid and continuous” [37].

A recent approach, a relational and institutional view of service innovation, focuses much attention on the interaction and dynamics in the development and use process. In this approach “co-creation” is considered a key distinction in service innovation. This relational concept emphasizes the role of customers and a network of actors as co-creators of service [67]. Related notions are service network, ecosystem, or ecology, which re<sup>fl</sup>ect institutional aspects of service innovation. These institutional notions expand the boundary of economic actors associated with a service system, going beyond immediate providers and customers. Service innovation occurs in broad economic and institutional environments, including those of various economic actors and their resources [11,20,69].

These three approaches have evolved, as the importance of service in business has grown over the years. They would offer complementary insights to understanding IES innovation. For example, those product-oriented models can provide a formal and structured framework for service innovation as a series of phases. More recent models add nonlinearity, continuity, co-creation, and ecology to our understanding of service innovation. Vargo and Lusch [69] have recently reviewed several theories or perspectives (e.g., structuration theory, service science, institutional theory) for service innovation research. They noted “almost all of these streams have ties to complexity theory (Arthur, Durlauf, & Lane, 1997), which lends itself to a whole host of systemic insights, including value as an emergent property, emergent structures, and fractal patterns” (p. 189). From complexity theory, service innovation can best be explained as a co-evolutionary process, which includes dynamic stages, nonlinear interaction, openness, and emergent properties in complex environments. A complexity perspective can be useful for understanding IES innovation and offering strategies to businesses that view service as the source of competitive advantage.

## 4. Complex adaptive systems (CAS), environment, and co-evolution

In this section, we present a brief overview of CAS. A CAS is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a system in which large networks of components with no central control and simple rules of operation give rise to complex collective behavior, sophisticated information processing, and adaptation via learning or evolution” [42]. There are many examples of CAS, from biological organisms to economies [9]. While they differ in details, in general there are three coherent dimensions to discuss CAS<sup>1</sup>: CAS display internal mechanisms or characteristics (e.g., agents, interaction) [29]; the environment of CAS is dynamic, characterized as a rugged and changing <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape [32]; and, a CAS co-evolves with its environment [3], which is depicted as “a co-evolutionary process” [5]. Below, we provide detailed descriptions on those dimensions (see Table 3 for summary).

## 4.1. Internal mechanism

## 4.1.1. Adaptive elements

The <sup>fi</sup>rst key element of any CAS is the concept of “adaptive elements” (or agents): that “CAS are, without exception, made up of large numbers of active elements that … are diverse in both form and capability” [29] (italics added). Organizations are considered as CAS, which are composed of such diverse elements as resources, policies, and activities [56]. Thus, nonhuman resources can be CAS elements. These elements are adaptive, through learning or evolution, for better <sup>fi</sup>t. Since the elements are adaptive, they are in constant <sup>fl</sup>ux. Thus, what constitutes a CAS is changing over time. That is, the boundary of a CAS is not <sup>fi</sup>xed but, rather, <sup>fl</sup>uid.

## 4.1.2. Nonlinear interaction and self-organization

Elements are distinct from others, but they also interact with each other. The interaction is non-linear. This nonlinear interaction is often spontaneous rather than pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and can lead to new forms or structures [29]. Thus, CAS are considered emergent structures of individual elements. Adaptive elements operate with local cues or information and respond to local environments. There is no central power or leader [29,42] controlling the behaviors of individual agents and the interactions among them. This interaction involves what is described as self-organization, containing “emergent properties, which are properties of the system that the separate parts do not have” [5].

## 4.2. Environment

## 4.2.1. Rugged

From complexity theory, the environment is understood as the emergent structure developed from the interaction of various CAS (e.g., providers, customers, regulators, technology trajectories, scienti<sup>fi</sup>c trends, market needs). The environment a given CAS faces is characterized as rugged landscape with many peaks and valleys [15]. Those peaks and valleys represent the success and failure of the company's strategic investment or outputs such as services [9]. The height of the peaks varies, representing different degrees of success.

## 4.2.2. Dancing

The environment is not only rugged but also dancing [61], meaning the rugged <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape continues to change in shape; thus, new topographies emerge as time passes [9,32]. For example, “what is a high <sup>fi</sup>tness peak today might not be a high peak tomorrow” [9]. This is due to the interaction of different <sup>fi</sup>tness landscapes. A change in one <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape (e.g., software market) affects other <sup>fi</sup>tness landscapes (e.g., hardware market).

## 4.3. Co-evolution

The life of CAS is well represented as a co-evolutionary process: CAS and the environment co-evolve with each other. Co-evolution involves three stages of change (variation, selection, and retention): “Variation generates the raw materials from which selection, by environmental or internal criteria, is made; retention mechanisms preserve the selected form” [1].

## 4.3.1. Variation

Adaptation is vital for CAS to survive in the rugged <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape, which continues to change. “Variation provides the raw material for adaptation” [5]. In the natural world, CAS create variations through operators such as mutation and recombination (or crossover) [5,29]. CAS, in organizational settings, uses incremental and radical variations that have resulted from managerial intentionality and improvisational activities. Managerial intentionality deliberately creates (or reduces) variation, through decision-making, planning, and other mechanisms (e.g., imitation) [50]. Improvisation, which is ordinary, informal and less structured, also leads to new variations [53]. The sources of variation exist inside and outside a focal CAS. For example, a new business unit emerges through either recombining existing members (or resources) or importing new people (or resources) from outside.

## 4.3.2. Selection

CAS face limited resources [3]. Therefore, variations are under pressure for higher performance in terms of customer satisfaction, revenue, or other success criteria [5]. Selection is a <sup>fi</sup>ltering process of evaluating the performance (or <sup>fi</sup>tness) of variations as successes or failures [5]. In nature, selection occurs at individual organisms, rather than genes, through the level of <sup>fi</sup>tness. In business, however, selection would occur at both levels [38]. Also, selection is performed internally and externally [28]. Both external markets and internal business decisions impact the selection of CAS. Also, selection is both planned and emergent [41]. For example, the elements of a CAS possess internal performance criteria for selection. Yet, external sources (e.g., new regulations, emergence of new industries) also can impact what will <sup>fl</sup>ourish or not.

## 4.3.3. Retention

Those selected are retained for the next generation of production. CAS with high <sup>fi</sup>tness “have on average a greater probability of replicating, and more variants are made of them than of less-<sup>fi</sup>t” CAS [9]. Those selected CAS and their individual elements are retained and passed on. For organizational CAS (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>rms), this is through routines, strategies, and business units. Organizational (and individual) memory, material artifacts (e.g., technologies), and potentially others are also important sources of retention [50]. The retention can potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uence the environment through mechanisms, such as diffusion and imitation. As a result, a population of similar CAS is likely to grow, leading to the diffusion of similar kinds in the population level.

## 5.1. Internal mechanisms: IESs as CAS

## 5.1.1. Adaptive elements

When an IES is viewed as a CAS, it consists of N number of diverse adaptive elements (e.g., n1, n2, n3). Consider BA as an IES. The key to an IES is the diversity of its elements. In general, BA service refers to a con<sup>fi</sup>guration of technological (e.g., data storage technologies, data processing platform) and organizational (e.g., work routines, competencies) resources, available with supply-side and customer-side actors [8], enabling the extensive use of data, analytical capabilities, and factbased methodologies [17]. There are different kinds of BA services (e.g., enterprise data management, enterprise performance management) in industries. Each of these services is made up of a similar set of those elements and considered a solution for one party's problem. In this sense, these elements are “building blocks or generators” [29] of IES.

Service elements are adaptive through learning or evolution. Being adaptive is the inherent capability of both social and material elements in a BA. Consider a software tool (R). R has become a popular material element in BA service. Ever since its inception, R has evolved and been extensible: there have been diverse contributors adding new functions and packages to the software. It performs multiple functionalities, from traditional statistical analysis, to data mining, and data visualization and reporting. R or other similar technologies are integral to any kind of BA service. It is also well known that social elements, such as work routines and organizational (and individual) capabilities, are adaptive through learning. Routines change over time [35,50]. Organizational (and individual) competencies are recon<sup>fi</sup>gured for evolving needs.

## 5.1.2. Nonlinear interaction and self-organization

While adaptive elements are analytically distinct and adapting for better performance, they are interacting. Two prominent actors in IES— supplier and customer—are inseparable, as some recent studies have recognized the two as co-creators of service [27]. Their resources, such as routines, technologies, and capabilities, are interacting. A change in one element is likely to lead to small or big changes in other elements. This nonlinear interaction, whether mundane or planned, continues with no central controller—self-organization—and results in the emergent structures, IES.

Consider two small-scale services: enterprise information management (EIM) and enterprise performance management (EPM). Each of these is made up of adaptive elements. While EIM is geared toward gathering, organizing, and storing enterprise data, EPM focuses on actualizing the value of information. Thus, they often work so closely together that EIM provides inputs (e.g., relevant information) to EPM. “Through these information <sup>fl</sup>ows, companies can ‘measure their activities more precisely and help motivate managers to implement strategies successfully’ (Porter and Millar 1985 pp. 159-160)” [43]. The interaction does not end here, yet relevant information is visualized, explored, and analyzed through EPM. During this process, feedback is created and offered back to EIM. This kind of interaction can lead to many combinatorial outcomes (e.g., marketing analytics, health analytics) and their performances (e.g., many local optima) [63].

## 5.2. Environment: rugged and dancing

## 5.2.1. Rugged

The business environment of a given service system is described as a rugged <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape or a large-scale emergent structure resulting from the interaction among various service systems. “What emerges is a picture of service systems with a complex internal service-system structure embedded in ecosystems with a complex external servicesystem structure” [58]. The environment is “rugged” in that there are many local optimal points (e.g., different degrees of success or “hills”), which represent IESs as various con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of social and material elements. For example, there are many similar BA services competing for success in industry. The issue is that the entire topography of the environment is not known to economic actors (or organizations).

## 5.2.2. Dancing

The <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape of IESs is not at equilibrium. It continues to change; thus, new topographies emerge as time passes [9]. For example, there are many similar services (e.g., supply chain analytics, marketing analytics) in the BA area. A rugged <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape can represent the market of these services where economic actors, diverse technologies, customer needs, and legal requirements are mingled. This landscape emerges through the interaction of environmental factors (e.g., markets, regulations, laws, science, and consumption), representing ecologies of complex innovation [20]. In reality this <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape is not stand-alone, but coupled with other <sup>fi</sup>tness landscapes, such as the markets or industries for other services. Due to this coupling, the <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape is not just rugged (with hills and valleys) but also constantly dancing. Thus, the environment of IESs is uncertain and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict [45].

## 5.3. Co-evolution: service innovation as co-evolution

## 5.3.1. Variation

Variation creates novelty in services. Likewise, JESs are improved from the past through “variations”. Not all variations are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for existing IESs. Nevertheless, variations are necessary for IES to remain <sup>fi</sup>t in a rugged and dancing <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape. Variations are “enactments” [72] toward changing business environments. For example, BA service, which may be perceived as brand new, is, in fact, a category of variations evolved (or recon<sup>fi</sup>gured) from its predecessors such as data and model-driven decision support services in the 70s and 80s, and intelligent decision support services in the 90s [12]. The recent BA service re<sup>fl</sup>ects economic actors' efforts at creating competitive value out of data deluge. This evolutionary process involves co-creators' deliberate planning and improvisational capabilities.

Two broad categories of variation in service innovation are mutation and crossover [11], corresponding to exploitation and exploration in organization science [40]. Mutational variation is based on existing agents, modifying their interaction, and pursuing incremental innovation. The evolution of EIM service in the corporate world over past decades demonstrates this. In early years, the service was to manage decentralized transactional data with less focus on reporting and analysis. However, the service has incrementally evolved into enterprise-wide data management with more focus on predictive analytics. There were technological and institutional contexts, such as advancement in data management technologies (e.g., data mart, OLAP) and an increasing need for data governance and regulatory compliance. Crossover variation is through signi<sup>fi</sup>cant recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations and often involves “importing energy” [3] from outside. Recent BA services, such as big data service and cloud-computing analytics [19], demonstrate examples of this kind, as previously unrelated service elements or separate IES (e.g., cloud computing service, decision support service, service-oriented architecture service) are combined and recon<sup>fi</sup>gured.

## 5.3.2. Selection

Selection is conducted internally and externally, while internal and external become blurred in practice. Internally, IES is involved in the on-going self-organization process—combining and recon<sup>fi</sup>guring various elements in such a way that the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of those elements may result in a useful solution to a problem [69]. As noted earlier, this self-organization is nonlinear and dynamic, without a centralized controller. Yet, this self-organization is not without elements' plans and internal performance criteria. Each element pursues high performance and interacts with other elements. Their performance and the performance of an IES as a whole are measured.

While internal selection may focus on the (re)con<sup>fi</sup>guration of elements, external selection tends to occur at the system or IES level. For example, business intelligence (BI) service had emerged in the 90s. The primary role of BI was to provide useful information to managers in easy-to-understand formats, such as charts, graphs, and tables. In the past decade there was the emergence of institutional and technological forces (e.g., data deluge, distributed decision making paradigm) favoring BA over BI. While the overall business environment became more competitive and uncertain, there was an increase in the need for prediction and optimization through IT infrastructure, algorithms, data governance, and business processes. Such predecessors of BI as information services for executives had been less popular. This selection process leads to strengthening some elements (e.g., data mining technologies, advanced visualization techniques) and systems (e.g., predictive analytics services), while weakening the rest. The emergence of small and large-scale BA services (and related elements) exempli<sup>fi</sup>es this.

## 5.3.3. Retention

As a result, those selected elements and IES are retained, and become the resources for new variations to emerge. They are copied or imitated. The retention is shown in various forms or replications, which could include computing platforms, organizational routines and competencies, material artifacts (e.g., data mining tools), system architecture, human talents, and others. This signals that the environment itself is changed and future variation is impacted: these retained elements become a part of the environment or the very reality itself for new generations of IES.

For example, the success of Harrah's and Marriott International's BA services [17] has been imitated or copied by other businesses. As a result, there are various kinds of revenue management services in the hospitality industry. Similar IT platforms, system architecture, materials artifacts, and organizational routines are copied into BA services, such as SCM analytics [63] and HR analytics [17] in other areas.

## 6. Theoretical propositions

The previous section presented a novel perspective on IES and IES innovation, using BA as an illustration. From this complexity perspective, this section develops theoretical propositions related to IES as CAS, and IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process.

## 6.1. Propositions for IES as CAS

One of the two main research questions was to address the conceptualization of IES. Regarding this, there is a tendency to reduce IES to either tangible products or intangible services, which was introduced earlier as the divergent approach to service. However, neither approach offers good explanatory power for real-world IESs. Each misses some important opportunities to study the dynamic and complex nature of IES in the dynamic service ecosystem, and to develop adaptive IES to target corporate needs through combining various resources.

A product-oriented approach largely ignores the “service-oriented” nature [51] of IES and is likely to lead to a static and transactional view of IES. In this view, a BA service would be regarded as a product created by one party for a clear problem(s) and sold or loaned to another party. After this transaction, there is some opportunity for the other party to modify that product. This view is limited in many situations: when what are traditionally called users (or customers) combine various resources available, inside and outside their boundary, to address their changing needs [46]; when such needs demand complex solutions, so that an IT or management technique alone is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient [20]; or, when production and consumption often occur simultaneously [13]. Another approach in the literature is viewing services as intangible actions by human actors. This view potentially sheds light on such aspects of IESs as contexts and interaction, which are often missing in the product-oriented approach. However, this intangible view ignores the very ontology of IES that IT is integral to IES, and that there are many tangible elements (e.g., technical standards, hardware platform) in IES. Consequently, this approach becomes less useful for the task of analyzing, designing, managing, and improving IES.

The CAS view of IES is based on different assumptions. One is that IES is made of diverse adaptive elements or building blocks. This diversity becomes the basis for the dynamic view of IES proposed in this paper. Unlike the previous approaches, focusing on either tangible or intangible resources, the CAS view posits that IESs are con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of social and material elements. For BA service, these elements include data storage and management systems, dashboards (or reporting documents), decision routines or patterns, analytical skills and techniques, people (e.g., data integrators, data analysts, developers, third-party people), and others. In fact, an IES is the emergent structure resulting from continual changes in the elements and their interaction.

Thus, unlike what the product-oriented view of service may imply, IESs are viewed as continually emerging, neither static nor fully predictable. It is the interaction among the elements that leads to the emergence of IES. Since the elements are not static, but adaptive to the changes in other elements (and the environment), their interactions evolve as well. For example, the software used in BA service is extensible: new software plugins (e.g., blog mining) are developed and added into the existing BA service. This can change decision routines and may demand new skills and competencies (and even new kinds of work and people, such as data science and data scientists). This results in a new way of interaction among the elements of the service. This implies that there is no such thing as “<sup>fi</sup>nished IES”: but, only “evolving” IES. What an IES is constituted of (or an IES' boundary) is in <sup>fl</sup>ux, over time, as elements are continuously changing: existing elements (e.g., people, skills, software) may be upgraded or dropped and new elements (e.g., remote sensors) added.

Proposition 1. Unlike the approach based on product development, which may offer a static view of IES, IES should be viewed as the emergent structures resulting from dynamically interacting adaptive elements over time.

In addition, unlike the intangible view of service, IESs contain material elements. These material elements matter [48]. It is not just that people's skills and competencies and work routines are recon<sup>fi</sup>gured for IES, but also that material elements, technologies in particular, are interweaving with other elements to form the emergent structures. Simply, there is no IES without IT. IT offers a strategic advantage in today's business environment [21]. For example, traditional data storage and data processing technologies (e.g., relational database) are not capable of BA service for large-scale or big data. Thus, new technologies (e.g., Hadoop, Mapreduce) become necessary elements [19]. These new platforms and techniques need to be integrated with work routines, actors, and new IT infrastructure. Distributed data processing demands new kinds of human competencies and IT platforms. Existing material elements emerge as enablers and constraints [35]. Not only this, but technologies “literally take actions” [50], as in the case of BA service (e.g., loan processing), where a software tool with AI algorithms can make decisions (e.g., approval, rejection, request for additional documents).

Proposition 2. Unlike the intangible approach to service, which neglects the material side of IES, material elements, technologies in particular, matter in such a way that they often become as essential for IES as work routines and people's competencies do.

## 6.2. Propositions from IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process

The second research question was about the nature of IES innovation. While the study on service innovation in general is growing, the understanding of IES innovation is still limited. A fundamental insight is that a guided VSR-based evolution can bene<sup>fi</sup>t IES innovation.

The CAS view recognizes that IES innovation occurs in the broader market and institutional networks [69], which are referred to as a “rugged and dancing” [61] environment. This environment is composed of adaptive elements and their emergent properties, CAS. IES as CAS exist in this environment like organisms in nature. Combinations of existing adaptive elements create diverse IES in the rugged IES <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape (or market). This IES <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape interacts with other types of <sup>fi</sup>tness landscapes or broader social and material environments. Thus, IES innovation is best characterized as a co-evolutionary process. Each IES in<sup>fl</sup>uences and is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by its environment, including other IES and markets. For example, ITs, which are the core of most BA services, “have generated and captured more data while also changing the nature of businesses” [33]. This data-intensive business environment has led to the emergence of more BA services. BA services and the data-intensive business environment have been co-creating each other.

Much of the existing understanding of service innovation relies on the approach geared for physical products, traditional ITs (e.g., software packages), or intangible services. From this understanding, service innovation is represented as supplier-driven, intra-organizational (or intra-departmental), and control-oriented. In developing and maintaining these physical goods or intangible services, the focus has been on suppliers as “developers”. The role of “customers” remains limited or passive. The innovation process was perceived as a set of intra-organizational activities, including problem identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, idea generation, and implementation. The emphases were on deliberation and on control of the innovation process and its outcome as planned. These views are traditional, narrow, and static with respect to what is being developed in IES innovation, who is participating in this process, and how the process should be managed, among other aspects. The nature of IES as CAS, discussed earlier, challenges these assumptions. IES innovation involves diverse social and material adaptive elements and their con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. It is evolutionary, since creating IES is similar to tinkering with or recombining existing social and material elements, as shown by the emergence of BA services from previous generations of services. This evolutionary process goes beyond the boundary of a single organization (or a single organizational unit) and its resources. There is no centralized designer, but many co-creators, even including customers, internal employees, and third-party people, in this process. This process is often nonlinear and dynamic: the outcomes are unpredictable. For this reason, the proposed CAS view of IES has argued that IES innovation is a co-evolutionary process.

Proposition 3. Borrowed from product innovation or intangible service innovation, there could be different ways to explain IES innovation in the literature. However, IES innovation is best viewed as a co-evolutionary process involving recombination, co-creation, and path-dependency.

In addition, three processes (variation, selection, and retention) are necessary to explain the underlying dynamics of IES innovation. Variation is novelty creation. Without variation, there is never IES innovation. Firms or units accomplish variations through recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations of heterogeneous resources. Resources are distributed within and outside their boundary. Rational planning (or managerial actions) and improvisational activities, in response to unplanned events (or surprises), are important. For example, variations of BA service are less likely to take place in the R&D department or at the management level, and are not entirely dependent on pre-determined planning activities (e.g., idea generation, service prototyping). Yet, they come from the implementation and use of BA service in practice. However, variations face internal and external selection. An internal selection process, through self-organization or nonlinear interaction among adaptive elements, selects “out” and “in” certain elements, based on internal performance criteria and goals [41]. External selection takes place at both element and system levels. For example, the increase in unstructured data in the environment leads to the pressure for more text and web analytics elements, but fewer legacy database-related elements, to be included in BA service. Finally, retention is critical for continuous IES innovation. Certain elements (e.g., routines, capabilities, software tools) are retained and reused for variations. Successful IESs (and their elements) are formalized as best practices or cases and copied by others. They become the source of variations. For example, certain data mining algorithms and analytics technologies [10] have strongly survived in competition, and are the sources of recombination with other social and material elements. ## 7. Theoretical and practical implications

The previous section presented a novel perspective on IES and IES innovation and developed theoretical propositions. This section offers several implications for the theory and practice of IES and IES innovation.

## 7.1. Implications from IES as CAS

Alive structures from emergent and bottom-up process: When IESs are understood as the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of adaptive social and material elements (Proposition 1), IESs are no longer static entities. Like products, material elements can be formalized through standardization and modularization. However, social elements, including people, are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict and control. Also, the interaction between and among social and material elements goes beyond any centralized control. Thus, IESs are adaptive or “alive” [50] structures. IESs are recon<sup>fi</sup>gurable through changing any of the social or material elements. Then, we need to think about IES from the emergent perspective. The product-oriented approach has a tendency to understand a system from top-down. That is, a system is decomposed into separable components, which are nested and <sup>fi</sup>xed. Thus, the design of such a system (e.g., car) requires a clear set of pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned requirements and a complete understanding of how those components behave with each other [76]. Predictability and separability are underlying assumptions of this approach. Studies of service using product modularity ideas are relevant to this approach [71]. On the other hand, a bottom-up view is suggested: the emergence of IES is viewed as a bottom-up process through the interaction of these social and material elements, none of which is the centralized controller in this interaction and emergent process. When there is no single controller and those elements are adaptive, then it can be expected that IES never “settle”. IES tend to be unpredictable and unintended and their boundary is <sup>fl</sup>uid. IESs as CAS are more like living species.

Technologies as active and complex: Proposition 2 suggests that the role of technologies in emerging services needs to better understood. In the service literature, there is a tendency for the role of technologies to be implicitly recognized or absent. In some cases, technologies are treated as passive mediums. They are the channel of delivering traditional services electronically [52]. They tend to be relegated to the background when discussing service, except in the IS-related service literature [6]. However, the CAS view suggests that technologies are active and even transformational in IES. Social and technical elements are closely intertwined in IES. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, then, to separate the contribution of social elements from that of technical elements [50]. The role of material can be as active as that of social elements. In addition, technologies are transformational [51], for example, in emerging IES (e.g., BA). Data mining and simulation tools not only collect, store, and maintain data, but also transform the data into actionable information for prediction [36]. The IES con<sup>fi</sup>gured with these technologies, rather than supporting people's traditional activities, can transform the nature of people's work (e.g., creating new work types). Sensory technologies for data collection in BA service “have the ability to produce and transform that can be acquired by no other means” [31]. Emerging technologies for data acquisition, transformation, and analysis have become the source of the opportunities for BA service to emerge.

Co-creation in practice: In the service literature, studies [18,65,71] offer ways of identifying services. For example, a service needs to contain systems integration, operational services, business consultancy, and vendor <sup>fi</sup>nancing [18]. The existing views are useful, in that IES can be decomposed into manageable components or modules, for example, a BA service as a set of smaller services or service modules (or service processes). This is the best practice with service providers (e.g., SAP, IBM) and customers in the industry. However, there are also limitations. The extant approaches are either supplier-oriented or customer-oriented: most studies look at service from a supplier's point of view, with few exceptions having a customer's point of view [65]. In addition, they offer an abstract representation of IES as <sup>fi</sup>xed, separable, and manageable, while not considering nonlinear interaction, emergence, and learning (or evolution) in IES. The CAS view complements the existing studies by (1) considering both providers and customers (and their resources) as adaptive elements for IES, and (2) offering greater insights about IES in practice. This highlights the critical role of providers and customers (and their internal employees) as co-creators of IES, which is related to the extensive discussion of co-creation in the literature. The CAS view also suggests that IES is dynamic, indeterminate and evolving in practice.

IES in abstract and concrete forms: The CAS view can re<sup>fl</sup>ect two complementary aspects of IES, abstract and concrete [50]. IES can be generalized and represented in certain forms in which potential adaptive elements are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the interactions between them are understood, and certain outcomes are expected. This abstract form of IES would be called IES-in-representation. There are various types of IES-in-representation (e.g., marketing analytics, EIM) in customer organizations and service markets. The value of IES-in-representation is actualized when they are instantiated in practice or action, which is referred to as IES-in-practice. These two aspects are interacting. IES-in-representation becomes the basis for IES-in-practice to live and IES-in-practice, through learning, to revise IES-in-representation. For example, an abstract form of text analytics can potentially generate diverse IES in practice or contexts, one of which could have led to the emergence of customer sentimental analytics such as IES-in-representation. Thus, IES-in-representation is a platform for many “alive” IES to emerge in practice.

## 7.2. Implications from IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process

## 7.2.1. Co-evolution

The CAS view of IES innovation, as a co-evolutionary process in the rugged and dancing <sup>fi</sup>tness landscape, suggests that IESs need to continually co-evolve with the environment in which the IESs are embedded. For this, IESs need to remain open to external changes and to be adaptable. Resources are abundant inside and outside organizational boundary [60]. Thus, companies should invest in dynamic capability, which is a capacity to sense, seize, and recon<sup>fi</sup>gure their tangible and intangible resources [62]. The key is to identify useful resources as the source of competitive advantage and to dynamically recombine those resources quickly for changing needs. The focus needs to be on continually accessing diverse resources, including technologies and people's skills, and recon<sup>fi</sup>guring them. Thus, renewal and recon<sup>fi</sup>guration of resources [61] or resource integration [69] become critical for IES innovation.

## 7.2.2. Strategic variation

In the rugged and dancing environment, strategic variation is a key for success [70]. Variation has to combine many mutations and occasional crossovers. Mutations are path-dependent searches, focusing on recombining existing resources. Average <sup>fi</sup>rms (and organizational units), in terms of service innovation, should take advantage of mutational variation. This can open the door for radical IES innovation to occur and, hence, competitive advantage. Crossover involves pathcreation searches, actively seeking new resources and recon<sup>fi</sup>guring them with existing resources. Strategic variations also take place in IES-in-practice through improvisation. A small change can have a large impact in IES. Both deliberate and improvisation variations need to be nurtured. Overall, this idea is captured by such concepts as dynamic capabilities [62], improvisation, self-renewal [61,70], and ambidexterity in strategy literature. For strategic variation, IESs need to contain loosecoupled structures and simple rules, so that diverse elements can quickly adapt themselves and emerge as an integrated solution.

## 7.2.3. Optimizing selective retention

IES innovation needs effective mechanisms for selection and retention. Variations need to be <sup>fi</sup>ltered through internal and external selection and successful ones must be retained for inheritance. Internal selection should go beyond top-down command and control. Instead, the bottom-up process, through the interaction of adaptive elements and localized decisions, needs to be nurtured. The internal rate of change is important for the survival of systems [70]. Against external selection, thus, the change of IESs needs to be equal to or faster than the rate of environmental change (e.g., IES improvement by competitors, market trends, institutional arrangements). IESs increase survival when adaptive elements provide distinct capabilities, yet can perform similar functions under certain contexts [73]. This can increase recon<sup>fi</sup>gurability of IES and their elements. For example, emerging BA application technologies can perform both data management and data analysis, not only one. More of these technologies are capable of handling not only traditional data, but also big data. This characteristic at the element level increases robustness and evolvability of IES (as well as the survival of those elements). This aspect is also found in the capabilities of individuals who are part of IES. For example, internal employees of a customer organization, such as data analysts, are often capable of performing other roles, such as data integrators and process engineers, when a supplier <sup>fi</sup>rm's employees (e.g., data integrators) are not available.

Retention should focus on retaining both successful IES and their elements and passing them on. Organizational memory is recognized as important for retention [50] and different strategies for managing can be employed for service innovation [59]. At the element level, “a shared system that individuals in groups and organizations develop to collectively encode, store, and retrieve information or knowledge” [4] can enable retention of successful individuals and groups for inheritance. This helps preserve organizational capabilities and routines, which can serve as repertoire. ITs and other formalization methods (e.g., methodologies, policies, formal procedures) have long played important roles in codi<sup>fi</sup>cation and transfer of this retained knowledge. At the IES or system level, investing in the abstract aspect of IES is necessary. Recall from Section 7.1 that IES-in-representation serve as a platform for IES-in-practice. They can formalize IES in terms of building blocks, relevant patterns of interaction, performance criteria, and expected outcomes. Thus, they become repertoire for future variations.

## 8. Conclusion

While the economies of many countries are shifting from manufacturing to service, understanding of service and service innovation is quite limited [75]. This lack of understanding is especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in IES and IES innovation, where IT plays important roles as core or peripheral elements. Drawn upon the tenet of complexity theory, this article has developed a novel framework for understanding IES as CAS and IES innovation as a co-evolutionary process of variation, selection, and retention. The proposed framework can be useful for explaining complex properties (e.g., diverse adaptive elements) and dynamic behaviors (e.g., nonlinear interaction) inherent in such IES as BA service, and the evolutionary (or combinatorial) process in IES innovation. Drawn from the framework, we developed theoretical propositions and offer several implications for the theory and practice of IES and IES innovation. Future work can focus on applying the proposed framework into real-world IES innovation and case studies of emerging decision support services.
