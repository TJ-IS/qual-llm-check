# Reach Out and Touch: Eliciting the Sense of Touch Through Gesture-Based Interaction

Yang (Alison) Liu<sup>1</sup>, Yi Shen<sup>2</sup>, Cheng Luo<sup>3</sup>, Hock Chuan Chan<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University, China, liuyang.alison@xjtu.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Research Center for Smarter Supply Chain, Dongwu Business School, Soochow University, China, sheny@suda.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>Corresponding author, College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China, cheng.luo@tju.edu.cn <sup>4</sup>Department of Information Systems and Analytics, National University of Singapore, Singapore, chanhc300@gmail.com

## Abstract

With the development of gesture-based interaction technologies (e.g., touchscreen devices and kinetic controllers), consumers can directly use their hands to interact with web interfaces, which may assist in creating a sense of touch for consumers. Drawing on feelings-as-information theory, this study investigates the impacts of two types of gesture-based interaction (i.e., touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction) on consumers’ sense of touch. Results from a laboratory experiment showed that touchscreen interaction elicited a higher sense of touch than mid-air interaction when the importance of product haptic information was high. However, touchscreen interaction did not differ from mid-air interaction in terms of eliciting consumers’ sense of touch when the importance of product haptic information was low. Furthermore, consumers’ sense of touch improved their shopping experience satisfaction by reducing uncertainty about products and fostering attachment to products. Theoretically, this study contributes to the existing literature by empirically investigating the effects of gesture-based interaction on consumers’ bodily sensations, elucidating the role of the sense of touch in affecting consumers’ virtual product experience, and highlighting the impact of the interaction method on consumer behavior. This study also provides practical insights into the application of gesture-based interaction technologies.

Keywords: Gesture-Based Interaction, Sense of Touch, Virtual Product Experience

## 1 Introduction

Even though mouse-based devices (e.g., desktops) are still an important interaction tool for human-computer interaction, such devices are increasingly giving way to gesture-based interaction technologies, such as touchscreen devices and kinetic controllers (Brasel & Gips, 2014; Steinmann et al., 2014). Market analysis reveals that 44.5% of US e-commerce sales in 2019 is expected to be driven by consumers using touchscreen devices, such as mobile phones and tablets (Droesch, 2019). Meanwhile, the popularity of kinetic controllers is underscored by ongoing developments in virtual reality (Liang, 2016). For instance, the innovative 3D virtual fitting technology (e.g., FXMirror<sup>1</sup> and N-show <sup>2</sup> ) has been widely adopted in shopping centers, communities, and airports. In the mobile context, both Samsung<sup>3</sup> and Huawei<sup>4</sup> have introduced mid-air gesture features that allow users to interact with smartphones via performing gestures in the air. Despite the emerging use of gesturebased interaction technologies, empirical research investigating their impacts on consumers’ perception and behavior still lags.

Unlike mouse-based interaction, which enables consumers to interact with interfaces by clicking, scrolling, and dragging the mouse, gesture-based interaction allows consumers to interact with interfaces via gestures. Notably, prior literature has underscored two popular types of gesture-based interaction, namely touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction (Saffer, 2008). Using touchscreen interaction, consumers tap, slide, swipe, and stretch the surface of a touchscreen device to perform tasks, whereas with mid-air interaction, consumers reach, point, and manipulate interfaces with fingers, hands, and even body movements in the air.

While both types of gesture-based interaction have become increasingly popular, they differ in two novel ways. First, touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction differ in the extent to which they support freeform gestures. Specifically, touchscreen interaction requires consumers to move their fingers on the surface of a touchscreen device. Thus, consumers’ hand movements would be constrained on a two-dimensional surface. Using mid-air interaction, however, consumers can freely move their arms and hands in a threedimensional space. Second, touchscreen interaction is distinctive from mid-air interaction in its ability to provide tactile feedback. In the case of touchscreen interaction, touching the tangible surface of a touchscreen device offers consumers tactile feedback provided by the texture of the surface. In contrast, tactile feedback is not available in the mid-air interaction because consumers do not physically touch anything during the interaction. Given the inherent differences between these two types of gesture-based interaction, it would thus be intriguing to examine whether and how mid-air interaction differs from touchscreen interaction in terms of affecting consumers’ perception and behavior.

Prior studies have suggested that consumers’ bodily movements (e.g., gestures) could elicit sensory experience, which could further influence their perception and behavior (e.g., Brasel & Gips, 2015). Among various types of sensory experience, haptic experience is particularly important for online consumers because online consumers normally cannot physically touch their desired products prior to purchase. The lack of haptic information about products greatly impedes online consumers’ ability to obtain a comprehensive evaluation of their desired products, which could further lead to an unpleasant shopping experience. To alleviate this concern, researchers have devoted great effort to studying how to elicit online consumers’ sense of touch, that is, the extent to which consumers feel they can touch a product (e.g., Jiang & Benbasat, 2004; Overmars & Poels, 2015a, 2015b). However, it remains unclear whether consumers’ sense of touch could act as real haptic input and influence consumers’ product evaluation and shopping experience.

In sum, driven by both practical and theoretical needs, our study aims to investigate the impacts of different types of gesture-based interaction (i.e., touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction) on consumers sense of touch. By adopting a cognitive-affective framework, we further explore how consumers’ sense of touch would affect their online shopping experience. Given that prior studies have mainly investigated the impacts of product presentation on consumers’ online shopping experience (e.g., Jiang & Benbasat, 2004; Overmars & Poels, 2015a, 2015b), this study aims to deepen knowledge about how to convey an enjoyable shopping experience through human-computer interaction, enrich the understanding of the impacts of consumers’ haptic experience on their shopping experience satisfaction, and provide clear practical guidance for online retailers on how to improve consumers’ shopping experience through the application of gesture-based interaction technologies.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we first review the theoretical foundation of this research, i.e., feelings-asinformation theory and prior work on simulated haptic sensations, followed by a brief introduction of gesturebased interaction. Then, we present our research model and develop our research hypotheses. We subsequently elaborate on the research methodology and present our research findings. The paper concludes by discussing the contributions of this study and proposing directions for future research.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Related Work

This study investigates the impacts of gesture-based interaction on consumers’ online shopping experience. In this section, we first review the theory of feelingsas-information, which emphasizes the role of consumers’ sensory experience in determining their perception and behavior. Among different types of sensory experience, we are particularly interested in consumers’ haptic sensations because they are critical for influencing online consumers’ product examination. More importantly, online consumers’ haptic sensations are typically difficult to elicit, as consumers are deprived of physically touching products before purchase. To understand the antecedents of consumers’ sense of touch, we review existing literature on simulated haptic sensations and discuss how gesture-based interaction could elicit consumers’ sense of touch.

## 2.1 Feeling-as-Information Theory

Unlike traditional information processing theories that highlight the role of people’s mind and cognition in affecting their feelings, judgment, and behavior, feelings-as-information theory stresses that subjective feelings (such as bodily sensations) can, in turn, influence people’s cognition and affection (Schwarz, 2011). For example, physical perceptions of coldness have been shown to increase the appeal of romance movies (Hong & Sun, 2011), incidental haptic experiences of roughness (vs. smoothness) make people evaluate social interaction scenarios as less cooperative (Ackerman et al., 2010; Lachman et al., 1979), and pleasant scents enhance people’s product evaluations and motivate variety-seeking behavior (Spangenberg et al., 2006). These and many other examples demonstrate that bodily sensations critically modulate people’s judgments, decisions, and behaviors.

Building on feelings-as-information theory, there is a surge of interest in sensory marketing suggesting that consumers often rely on bodily sensations to form judgments about products and make purchase decisions (Krishna, 2012; Krishna & Schwarz, 2013; Schwarz, 2011). Research has proposed that consumers’ bodily sensations, including vision, audition, haptics, smell, and taste, provide diagnostic product information for product examination and invoke consumers’ emotional responses. For instance, congruent scent (e.g., the smell of perfume in a retail store selling women’s clothes) makes consumers feel like a shopping environment is more informative and pleasant, thus enhancing consumers’ evaluation of retail stores (Bosmans, 2006; Mitchell et al., 1995). In contrast, the lack of sensory information may result in difficulty evaluating products and elicit negative emotions (Krishna et al., 2017; Laroche et al., 2005; Luo et al., 2012). For instance, Elder and Krishna (2010) show that advertisements may result in low product evaluation and purchase intention if they fail to stimulate people’s sensations (e.g., taste, touch, and smell).

Among different types of bodily sensations, touch (i.e., haptics) is a recent area of inquiry in consumer behavior research because touch has been recognized as a critical means of information acquisition (Klatzky & Peck, 2012; Lederman & Klatzky, 2009; Peck & Childers, 2003). The role of touch in product evaluation is twofold. First, touch provides haptic information about products, such as texture, weight, and rigidity, which facilitates consumers’ cognitive information processing. Research suggests that during product examination, touching a product enables consumers to acquire diagnostic information about the product (e.g., softness and texture), thus remarkably increasing consumers’ confidence in their judgment (Spence & Gallace, 2011; Stevens & Green, 1996).

Second, touching a product is likely to invoke consumers’ positive emotional responses by, for example, making people feel more affectionate and attached to products, which may enhance consumers purchase intentions and shopping satisfaction (Peck & Shu, 2009; Peck & Wiggins, 2006). In fact, barriers to touch prevent consumers from acquiring products haptic information and may consequently increase consumers’ frustration in information processing (Peck & Childers, 2003).

Despite its critical role in influencing people’s perception and behavior, touch is usually not feasible for online consumers. The mediated nature of ecommerce inhibits online consumers from directly touching their desired products prior to purchase, thereby raising the distance between products and consumers (Laroche et al., 2005). In most cases, online consumers judge the quality of products based on textual and visual information, such as textual descriptions, product images, and video demonstrations. The lack of haptic information greatly impedes online consumers’ ability to obtain a comprehensive evaluation of desired products, especially when the products contain attributes (e.g., texture) that consumers are accustomed to evaluating through physical interaction (Citrin et al., 2003). Therefore, it is important to understand how to simulate consumers’ haptic sensations in the online environment. Next, we review prior work on the antecedents of consumers’ sense of touch.

## 2.2 Simulated Haptic Sensations

People’s hands are the most primary source of haptic sensations. It is suggested that physical touch provides consumers with diagnostic product information, thus increasing their confidence in product evaluation (Peck & Childers, 2003). For example, Peck and Shu (2009) found that physical touch increases consumers perceived ownership as well as their valuation of a product. Liu et al. (2017) suggested that physical touch increases consumers’ willingness to pay for a product when consumers’ mental representation of the product is concrete. In fact, physical touch could affect consumers’ product evaluation even when haptic cues are nondiagnostic for the actual qualities of products (Ackerman et al., 2010; Barsalou, 2003).

Given the importance of haptic sensations in consumers’ product evaluation, researchers have explored how to simulate consumers’ haptic sensations absent physical examination of a product. Some researchers have focused on the impacts of product presentation. For example, Elder and Krishna (2012) suggested that consumers may construct mental imageries of touching, holding, and interacting with products when they are exposed to visual depictions of products oriented toward their dominant hand. Such mental imagery may activate brain regions related to haptic perception, even when people’s hands are not directly involved (Jeannerod, 2001; Lacey et al., 2007; Serino et al., 2008). Similarly, Overmars and Poels (2015a) employed interactive image technology, which enables product images to change in response to consumers’ actions, and found that such interactive product presentation significantly elicits consumers’ sense of touch.

Although prior studies have shed light on the relationship between product presentation and the sense of touch, the bodily and sensorimotor aspects of human-computer interaction have been entirely overlooked. Indeed, consumers’ bodily interactions could be important sensory inputs to form haptic perceptions. Prior work indicates that people’s haptic perceptions can be formed from two components: tactile feedback from the skin and kinesthetic experience from muscles (Lederman & Klatzky, 1987, 2009). Specifically, people’s sense of touch concerns not only receiving haptic information from physical contact with a product but also actions of reaching out, holding, and moving. Therefore, the actions that consumers perform to interact with products may also elicit their sense of touch. Gesture-based interaction enables consumers to interact with products using gestures and thereby has the potential to become a new approach to elicit consumers’ sense of touch.

## 2.3 Gesture-Based Interaction

Prior literature has identified two types of gesturebased interaction, namely, touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction (Saffer, 2008). There is initial evidence showing that touchscreen interaction could affect consumers’ perception and behavior (e.g., Brasel & Gips, 2014; Maher et al., 2014; Steinmann et al., 2014). For example, Brasel and Gips (2014) found that, compared to mouse-based interaction, touchscreen interaction leads to a higher sense of product ownership, which is typically established via physical touch. Steinmann et al. (2014) suggested that reaching out to touch a product on a touchscreen device makes the consumption experience more vivid in people’s minds, compared to using a mouse to click on the product. They argued that this is because directly contacting a product image via touchscreen provides the illusion of physical contact. Although these studies have not directly examined the effects of touchscreen interaction on consumers’ sense of touch, they provide preliminary evidence showing that mere hand movements could invoke perceptions of haptic experience. In fact, Brasel and Gips (2015) revealed that consumers’ decision-making (e.g., hotel choices) on touchscreen devices tend to rely more on tangible attributes (e.g., bed firmness) than other attributes (e.g., staff friendliness). Similarly, Shen et al. (2016) demonstrated that touchscreen interaction, compared to mouse-based interaction, leads consumers to prefer affect-laden choices over cognitively superior choices. This effect is driven by the enhanced mental simulation of product interaction with the more affective choice alternative on touch interfaces.

Mid-air interaction, a cutting-edge technology that involves touchless manipulations of digital content based on sensor-based tracking of body movements and gestures, has also gained interest from practitioners and researchers (Koutsabasis & Vogiatzidakis, 2019). Large e-commerce companies, such as Alibaba and Amazon, are investing heavily in virtual reality and augmented reality technologies, with mid-air gestures considered to be the most natural and appropriate interaction method (Herman, 2018). However, most of the existing studies on mid-air interaction have focused on gesture design (e.g., Ackad et al., 2014; Card, 2014). For example, Tversky et al. (2014) found that gestures could improve users’ task performance when designed to resemble the symbolic meanings they represent. Given the high development cost and the increasing popularity of mid-air interaction technologies, understanding the impacts of mid-air interaction on consumers’ online shopping experience is vital to researchers and practitioners.

In summary, feelings-as-information theory emphasizes the importance of consumers’ haptic sensations in affecting their perception and behavior. In the context of e-commerce, however, consumers cannot physically touch products prior to purchase. As a result, it is important to elicit online consumers’ sense of touch; otherwise, the lack of product haptic information may impair consumers’ ability to evaluate products and could decrease their online shopping satisfaction. Even though prior studies have examined various factors that may enhance consumers’ sense of touch, it is unclear how the sense of touch could be elicited by gesture-based interaction. To address these gaps, this study explores the impacts of gesture-based interaction on consumers’ sense of touch and the cognitive and affective impacts of consumers’ sense of touch on their shopping experience satisfaction.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

This study aims to investigate the impact of gesturebased interaction on consumers’ online shopping experience. Specifically, we first examine the effects of two popular types of gesture-based interaction (i.e., touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction) on consumers’ sense of touch. Touchscreen interaction enables users to directly touch the surface of a touchscreen device (e.g., smartphone, tablet, and kiosk) to interact with web interfaces. For example, consumers can move their fingers on the surface of smartphones and perform gestures (e.g., tap, slide, swipe, etc.) to browse e-commerce websites and evaluate products.

In contrast, mid-air interaction allows users to interact with digital interfaces by performing freeform gestures in the three-dimensional space without being in contact with any surface. For instance, FXMirror provides solutions for retailers to enable consumers to try on clothes displayed on a “virtual mirror” by performing gestures (e.g., waving, pointing, and grabbing) in front of the mirror. Similarly, on a Huawei Mate 30 Pro smartphone, consumers can browse web pages by waving their hands in the air without touching the screen.

Further, we investigate whether consumers’ sense of touch could act as real haptic input to affect their virtual product experience. We selected product uncertainty and product attachment to reflect the cognitive and affective dimensions of consumers’ virtual product experience, respectively. Product uncertainty refers to consumers’ difficulty in assessing a product’s characteristics and predicting how the product will perform in the future (Dimoka et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2014), whereas product attachment is defined as the emotional bond consumers experience with a product during product evaluation (Hadi & Valenzuela, 2014; Mugge et al., 2006, 2016). ## 3.1 Effects of Gesture-Based Interaction on the Sense of Touch

Peoples’ haptic perception can be formed from two types of experience, namely, kinesthetic experience and tactile experience (Klatzky & Peck, 2012; Lederman & Klatzky, 1987, 2009, 2014). Kinesthetic experience is the result of a combined awareness of the movements, position, and orientation of parts of the human body. It includes the operation of proprioceptors that are sensitive to forces in muscles, tendons, and joints. Evidence has shown that mere movements of the hand(s), even without direct contact with the real product, could facilitate the formation of people’s imagery about touching products, thus eliciting people’s sense of touch (Klatzky et al., 1991; Peck et al., 2013). Furthermore, haptic perception could also be obtained through tactile experience, which refers to the sensory feelings people gain from skin receptors (Montagu, 1971).

Using gesture-based interaction (i.e., mid-air interaction and touchscreen interaction), consumers directly use their hands to interact with web interfaces. Specifically, consumers move their hands and/or fingers to manipulate web pages and product images. In this case, the motion and position of consumers arms and fingers likely stimulate kinesthetic experience, eliciting consumers’ sense of touch.

Although both mid-air interaction and touchscreen interaction provide kinesthetic experience, they differ in their ability to support tactile experience. Specifically, when using touchscreen interaction to examine online products, consumers place their fingers directly on the tangible surface of a touchscreen device to manipulate product images. In this case, the tactile experience provided by the device surface likely enhances consumers’ sense of touch by simulating physical contact with the product. However, when consumers use mid-air interaction to evaluate online products, consumers move their hands and fingers in the air and do not obtain any tactile feedback from skin receptors. In this case, there is a distance between consumers and products, making it difficult for consumers to perceive simulated physical contact with the products. The absence of tactile experience may inhibit consumers from forming a vivid sense of touch. Based on the above justifications, we hypothesize that:

H1: Compared to mid-air interaction, touchscreen interaction leads to a higher sense of touch.

## 3.2 The Moderating Role of Product Haptic Information

Prior literature suggests that the importance of haptic information varies across products (Grohmann et al., 2007; McCabe & Nowlis, 2003). Haptic information is of particular importance in the evaluation of some products whose substance properties are predictive of their performance, such as clothing and toys (Klatzky & Peck, 2012). In contrast, the evaluation of other products such as laptops and digital cameras is mainly determined by their utility. Thus, the importance of haptic information for such products is relatively low.

When consumers are examining products for which the importance of haptic information is high (i.e., highhaptic products), they likely focus on the haptic attributes of the products because these attributes are critical in determining product quality. In this case, consumers who cannot directly touch the product may feel that important information about the product is missing and they would likely be highly sensitive to touch-related cues (e.g., kinesthetic and tactile experience). Moreover, with such products, consumers may seek to mentally simulate their interaction with high-haptic (as opposed to low-haptic) products by, for example, imagining trying on or using the products (Brasel & Gips, 2014). In this case, the impact of touch-related cues on consumers’ sense of touch would be more salient. Thus, when the importance of product haptic information is high, the advantage of touchscreen interaction over mid-air interaction in terms of eliciting consumers’ sense of touch is likely to be particularly prominent.

In contrast, when the importance of product haptic information is low, haptic information is less critical for product evaluation. In this case, consumers may concentrate on utilitarian aspects rather than the touchrelated cues of the products. Accordingly, compared to mid-air interaction, the positive impact of touchscreen interaction on consumers’ sense of touch would be attenuated. Based on the above, we hypothesize that:

H2: The impact of gesture-based interaction on the sense of touch is moderated by the importance of product haptic information, i.e., the difference between touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction in terms of eliciting consumers’ sense of touch is greater when the importance of product haptic information is higher.

## 3.3 The Impacts of the Sense of Touch

Online consumers often feel uncertain about products before purchase because of the mediated nature of ecommerce. Researchers have shown great interest in investigating the antecedents of product uncertainty (e.g., Bock et al., 2012; Grohmann et al., 2007; Hong & Pavlou, 2014; McCabe & Nowlis, 2003). For instance, Pavlou et al. (2007) suggested that consumers’ product uncertainty stems from the spatial and temporal separation between consumers and products. Given the capability of touch in increasing consumers’ trust and confidence in their judgment (Peck, 2010), we are particularly interested in whether consumers’ sense of touch induced by gesture-based interaction can influence consumers’ perceptions about products.

According to feelings-as-information theory, people’s bodily sensations can serve as informational input to affect their judgment, regardless of whether bodily sensations are an integral part of the judgment task or arise from incidental factors that are irrelevant to the judgment task (Bock et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Schwarz, 2011). As one type of bodily sensation, consumers’ haptic perceptions provide consumers with diagnostic information about the products’ substance properties, such as hardness and texture, which cannot be easily obtained through visual inspection (Klatzky et al., 1991). Such information improves consumers’ understanding of a product, thus likely reducing their product uncertainty (Peck, 2010; Peck & Childers, 2003; Peck & Wiggins, 2006).

More importantly, consumers’ haptic perceptions could impact their understanding of a product even when consumers do not have direct contact with the product. For instance, prior studies have shown that imaginary touch can have similar effects to physical touch in facilitating product understanding and evaluation (Peck et al., 2013; Schwarz, 2011). Using gesture-based interaction, consumers perform hand and finger movements to evaluate products. In this case, the motions of consumers’ hands and fingers lead consumers to mentally imagine interacting with the product (Peck & Childers, 2003; Schwarz, 2011). Based on vivid mental imagery about a product, consumers will perceive the product as having high telepresence, thus facilitating their understanding of the product (Schlosser, 2003). Thus, although real touch experience is normally unavailable for online consumers, the sense of touch elicited by interaction technologies may also reduce consumers’ uncertainty about a product (Laroche et al., 2005; Peck et al., 2013). Thus, we propose that:

## H3: The sense of touch is negatively associated with product uncertainty.

In addition to cognitive activities, consumers’ bodily sensations could also influence their attachment. For example, Hadi and Valenzuela (2014) find that hugs induce emotional attachment to others. In a similar vein, Peck et al. (2013) showed that touch increases perceived ownership of a product. Indeed, some researchers have argued that product attachment could be developed through consumers’ personal interaction with products (Ball & Tasaki, 1992). Even though online consumers are not able to physically touch products, their sense of touch likely stimulates mental interaction with the products, leading to enhanced product attachment.

Moreover, seen as synonymous with affection and warmth (Montagu, 1971), touch is usually considered to be a form of approach behavior that may induce personal liking and preference (Hornik, 1992, 2009). When consumers use various gestures to examine an online product, they may feel as if they were actually interacting with the product and infer that they like the product (Labroo & Nielsen, 2009). Based on the above, it is conceivable to expect that consumers’ sense of touch will lead them to develop an attachment to a product they are examining. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H4: The sense of touch is positively associated with product attachment.

## 3.4 Overall Effects on Shopping Experience Satisfaction

Prior literature suggests that product uncertainty reduces consumers’ confidence in their product judgment because consumers lack diagnostic information about the quality of products and thus have difficulty evaluating products’ characteristics (Dimoka et al., 2012). When consumers are uncertain about their product judgment, they may invest more time and effort in product-related information searching and processing (Chernev et al., 2015; Montagu, 1971). In this case, consumers’ shopping experience may become rather cognitively demanding and tiring. Moreover, product uncertainty is often associated with risk perceptions (Flanagin et al., 2014). As consumers are generally averse to risk, they may find the shopping experience involving uncertainty less satisfactory. Therefore, we predict that:

## H5: Product uncertainty is negatively associated with shopping experience satisfaction.

Previous studies have suggested that the establishment of affection with a product often predicts consumers’ behavior toward the product (Ball & Tasaki, 1992; Dimoka et al., 2012). For example, Miller (1997) found that people who are strongly attached to an object are generally committed to preserving their relationship with the object. Thus, it is conceivable that when consumers establish a tight bond and attachment with a product, they will have more favorable attitudes toward the product. Since prior literature suggests that product satisfaction and shopping process satisfaction reinforce each other (Tse et al., 1990), product attachment would be expected to enhance consumers’ satisfaction with their shopping experience as well. Further, product attachment likely evokes positive emotions such as pleasure. Accordingly, consumers are likely to feel more satisfied with their shopping experience if they develop an attachment to a product. Based on the above, we hypothesize that:

H6: Product attachment is positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Experimental Procedure and Manipulation

We conducted a laboratory experiment to test our research hypotheses. Specifically, this study adopted a 2 (interaction method: touchscreen interaction vs. mid-air interaction) × 2 (importance of product haptic information: high vs. low) between-subjects experimental design. We recruited participants from a large public university. Participants were informed that the purpose of this study was to investigate people’s online shopping behavior and were told that they would each receive a token of appreciation upon completion of the experimental task. In total, 134 participants registered for our study and were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions.

In the experiment, participants were required to conduct an online shopping task. Specifically, they were asked to carefully examine the products on our experimental website and choose one product as a gift for a friend. The design of our experimental website was similar to a typical e-commerce website, with an assortment of product images presented in the center of the home page followed by product names. Participants could click on the product image or name to go to the product page on which detailed information about the product (e.g., additional product images and comprehensive product descriptions) was provided. On the product page, participants could enlarge product images to explore the visual details of the product. We sourced both the product images and product descriptions from Amazon. The brand name for each product was removed to avoid any potential confounding effect of brand familiarity and preference.

We manipulated the interaction method by providing participants with different devices to conduct their shopping task. In the touchscreen interaction condition, the participants were asked to complete their shopping task using a touchscreen desktop. They could tap on the screen of the desktop to interact with our experimental website. In the mid-air interaction condition, the participants interacted with our experimental website via the Leap Motion controller. Leap Motion controller is a device that facilitates users’ interaction with computers via mid-air gestures. It creates a virtual “touch” surface in the air and recognizes users’ gestures when they move their hands over the device (see Figure 2). Before beginning the shopping task, participants were guided on how to interact with the website using the interaction device they were assigned to.

The importance of product haptic information was manipulated by creating different product assortments on our experimental website. In the high haptic importance condition, the products available on our experimental website were scarves. In the low haptic importance condition, we provided participants with laptops on our experimental website. According to a pretest using an independent pool of subjects (N = 100), we found that haptic information is more important for scarves (M = 4.21, SD = 0.47) than laptops (M = 3.93, SD = 0.76, F (1, 98) = 4.66, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.48).

After examining all products and making their decision, the participants were asked to complete a computerbased questionnaire. They were then paid ten dollars as reimbursement for their time and dismissed.

## 4.2 Measurement

Participants were first asked to indicate their product choice and justify their choice by offering important decision attributes (i.e., the attributes or aspects of their chosen product that led to their decision). To verify the effectiveness of manipulating the importance of product haptic information, we asked participants to indicate the extent to which haptic information was diagnostic for the product evaluation. Measurement items for the constructs of interest (i.e., sense of touch, product uncertainty, product attachment, and shopping experience satisfaction) were adapted from prior literature. We also measured the perceived novelty of the interaction method and participants’ need for touch as control variables. The detailed measurement items are presented in Table 1.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Participants’ Background Information

The 134 participants came from 8 academic faculties or schools within the university and represented highly diverse backgrounds. Among them, 87 (64.9%) were female and 47 (35.1%) were male. The age of the participants ranged from 18 to 29 (M = 22.2, SD = 2.17). In general, the participants were experienced in online shopping (M = 5.31/7, SD = 1.44). Table 2 summarizes the demographic information of the participants in each experimental condition.

## 5.2 Manipulation Check and Psychometric Properties of Key Constructs

The manipulation check revealed that the scarf (M = 6.17, SD = 0.94) and laptop (M = 5.27, SD = 1.4) differed in the importance of product haptic information (F (1, 132) = 18.63, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.75), suggesting that participants perceived that haptic information was more important when evaluating scarves as opposed to laptops.

## 5.4 Effects of the Sense of Touch

We used partial least squares (PLS, SmartPLS 3.0) structural equation modeling to investigate the effects of the sense of touch on product uncertainty, product attachment, and shopping experience satisfaction (Ringle et al., 2015). Bootstrapping resampling was performed on the structural model to examine path significance (see Figure 4). As predicted, a higher sense of touch reduced product uncertainty $( \beta = - 0 . 3 7$ t = 4.32) and fostered stronger product attachment (β $= 0 . 5 2 , t = 7 . 3 1 )$ . In addition, product uncertainty was negatively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta ~ = ~ - 0 . 3 9 , ~ t ~ = ~ 5 . 4 3 )$ , while product attachment was positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 , t = 3 . 4 9 )$ . Together, product uncertainty and product attachment explained 48% of the variance in shopping experience satisfaction. Therefore, H3, H4, H5, and H6 were all supported.

## 5.5 Content Analysis of Decision Attributes

To investigate participants’ decision-making process, especially their consideration of product haptic information, we conducted a content analysis of the important decision attributes provided by the participants.

We hired two research assistants who were blind to our research hypotheses to classify whether participants made their choices based on haptic attributes of the products. They received survey responses containing participants’ justifications for their product choice. If a participant made the choice based on haptic attributes (e.g., material, weight, texture, warmth, smoothness, etc.), “the mention of haptic information” was coded as “1”, and “0” otherwise. The research assistants coded each choice justification independently and the interrater reliability of their coding was high on all dimensions (all $\alpha ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } > 0 . 7 0 )$ .

The content analysis revealed that a greater proportion of participants (63.4%) in the touchscreen interaction condition, compared to those in the mid-air interaction condition (38.1%), mentioned haptic attributes (e.g., the texture of scarf, the weight of laptop, etc.) as instrumental in making their product choice $( \chi ^ { 2 } \ : ( 1 , N$ $= 1 3 4 ) = 8 . 5 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 , \varphi = 0 . 2 5 )$ . Furthermore, this effect was more salient for high-haptic products (i.e., scarves) than for low-haptic products (i.e., laptops). Specifically, when participants were evaluating scarves, the effect of the interaction method on participants’ mention of product haptic information was stronger (touchscreen interaction: 62.9%, mid-air interaction: $3 5 . 5 \% , \chi ^ { 2 } \left( 1 , N = 6 6 \right) = 4 . 9 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 , \varphi =$ 0.27). In contrast, when participants were evaluating laptops, even though the touchscreen interaction still outperformed mid-air interaction in terms of participants’ mention of product haptic attributes, the difference between the two interaction conditions was not significant (touchscreen interaction: 63.9%, midair interaction: 40.6%, $\chi ^ { 2 } \left( 1 , N = 6 8 \right) = 3 . 6 8 , p > 0 . 0 5 ,$ $\varphi = 0 . 2 3 )$ . The findings on users’ mention of haptic information thus further corroborate our results on users’ sense of touch.

## 5.6 Moderated Mediation Analysis

Our research model predicted that the strength of the hypothesized indirect effect of gesture-based interaction on shopping experience satisfaction through the sense of touch is contingent on the importance of haptic information. We followed the procedure outlined by Hair et al. (1998) and Preacher et al. (2007) for examining such moderated mediation effect. We utilized Hayes and Preacher’s (2014) macro (Model 7) to probe the significance of conditional indirect effects of the sense of touch at different values of product haptic importance, which provided results in multiple steps.

The first step regressed shopping experience satisfaction on the interaction method $( \beta = 0 . 7 0 , t =$ 3.11). Compared to mid-air interaction, touchscreen interaction stimulated greater shopping experience satisfaction. The second step regressed sense of touch on the interaction method, the importance of product haptic information, and their interaction, yielding a significant main effect of the interaction method $( \beta =$ $2 . 2 1 , t = 2 . 7 1 )$ and a significant two-way interaction $( \beta$ $= - 1 . 1 2 , \ t \ = \ - 2 . 1 7 )$ . This result indicated that the interaction method affected participants’ sense of touch, and the effect was stronger when haptic information was important for product evaluation. The third step examined the impact of the mediator (i.e., sense of touch) on shopping experience satisfaction, while controlling for interaction method, importance of product haptic information, and their interaction. When the sense of touch was included in the model, the effect of the interaction method on shopping experience satisfaction remained significant but decreased in significance $( \beta ~ = ~ 1 . 4 0 , ~ t ~ = ~ 2 . 1 3 )$ . In addition, the sense of touch was positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 3 8 , t =$ 5.53).

Finally, we tested the conditional indirect effect of the moderator, i.e., the importance of product haptic information. In the high-haptic condition (scarf), the conditional indirect effect was significant $( \beta = 0 . 4 2$ 95% bootstrap CI = 0.13 to 0.81). In the low-haptic condition (laptop), however, the conditional indirect effect was not significant $( \beta = - 0 . 0 1$ , 95% bootstrap CI $= - 0 . 2 5 \mathrm { t o } 0 . 2 6 )$ . The results confirmed the existence of the conditional indirect effects predicted by our research model (see Figure 5). Those findings remained meaningfully unchanged when participants demographic information, online shopping experience, need for touch, and perceived novelty of the interaction method were included. Therefore, we concluded that the sense of touch mediated the effect of the interaction method on shopping experience satisfaction and determined that the strength of the mediation effect was conditional on the importance of product haptic information.

## 5.7 Summary

Our study found that, compared to mid-air interaction, touchscreen interaction increased users’ sense of touch when haptic information was important for judging product quality. However, this advantage of touchscreen interaction disappeared when the importance of product haptic information was low. Further, we found that users’ sense of touch was able to influence users’ shopping experience satisfaction by reducing uncertainty about products and increasing users’ attachment to products.

While our study provides compelling evidence on the effects of gesture-based interaction on users’ online shopping experience, there are still some issues that still need to be addressed. First, some studies have suggested that visual presentations of products are critical in determining consumers’ product evaluation process (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007). In our experiment, we used static two-dimensional images to present visual details of products. It is plausible that such a product presentation format may affect the effectiveness of gesture-based interaction because the participants’ hand movements in the touchscreen interaction are constrained to a two-dimensional surface while participants using mid-air interaction can freely move their arms and hands in a three-dimensional space. To rule out this alternative explanation, we conducted a follow-up study (i.e., 3D experiment) in which threedimensional product images were provided. Second, the participants in the main study used the same desktop for the online shopping task.

## 5.8 Follow-up Study One: 3D Experiment

One hundred participants from a large public university were recruited and randomly assigned to our experimental conditions. The experimental task was identical to that in the main study, with three key modifications. First, on the product page, a threedimensional product image was provided. Second, an all-in-one computer with an 86-inch touch screen was provided to the participants for their shopping task. To browse the experimental website, the participants could either directly move their fingers on the screen (in the touchscreen interaction condition) or move their hands in the air using the Leap Motion controller (in the mid-air interaction condition). Third, for the participants in the high haptic importance condition, the products available on our experimental website were T-shirts. In the low haptic importance condition, we asked the participants to evaluate a set of digital cameras and make a choice.

An ANOVA test revealed a significant main effect of the interaction method on users’ sense of touch (F (1, 96) = 11.85, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.70). In addition, product uncertainty was negatively associated with shopping experience satisfaction (β = - 0.26, t = 2.80), while product attachment was positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction (β = 0.28, t = 2.76). Together, product uncertainty and product attachment explained 52% of the variance in shopping experience satisfaction.

The findings of the 3D experiment were consistent with the results of our main study, showing that the effects of gesture-based interaction are robust to the 3D product presentation format.

We recruited 120 participants from a large public university and randomly assigned them to one of our experimental conditions. ## 6 General Discussion

This study seeks to understand the impacts of gesturebased interaction on consumers’ sense of touch and the role of the sense of touch in influencing consumers online shopping experience. There are several important findings from our study. First, our experiment results show that, compared to mid-air interaction, touchscreen interaction elicits a higher sense of touch when haptic information is highly instrumental to product evaluation. The content analysis of participants’ justification for their product choices reveals that participants in the touchscreen interaction condition, compared to those in the mid-air interaction condition, were more likely to mention haptic-related attributes (e.g., texture, material, warmth, etc.) as reasons for their product choices, especially when haptic information was important for product evaluation.

Second, this study underscores the role of the sense of touch in stimulating a meaningful and enjoyable virtual product experience. The results suggest that the sense of touch enhances online shopping satisfaction by affecting both cognitive and affective dimensions of virtual product experience. Specifically, a higher sense of touch reduces product uncertainty and fosters a stronger attachment to the product.

## 6.1 Theoretical Contributions

Gesture-based interaction is becoming increasingly prevalent in the digital world. It changes the way that consumers interact with web interfaces, thus challenging how researchers understand consumers’ virtual product experience. This study contributes to the discourse on human-computer interaction and virtual product experience in several ways. First, it advances the understanding of virtual product experience by elucidating the impacts of gesture-based interaction on consumers’ product evaluation. In particular, we find that different gesture-based interactions can elicit different levels of the sense of touch and thereby influence product uncertainty, product attachment, and consequently consumers’ shopping experience satisfaction. Specifically, our findings suggest that touchscreen interaction leads to a higher sense of touch on products being evaluated, compared to mid-air interaction, if the haptic information is instrumental for product evaluation. Our study brings a new perspective on virtual product experience literature, arguing that consumers’ product evaluation is shaped by their gesture-based interactions with web interfaces. Although prior studies have provided initial evidence suggesting that how consumers interact with web interfaces may affect their sense of touch (Brasel & Gips, 2014; Cano et al., 2017), those studies did not tease the impact of the interaction methods apart from the inherent differences of various interaction devices. For example, people may use some devices (e.g., desktop) primarily for work but other devices (e.g., iPad) for entertainment. To account for such inherent differences across varying interaction devices, our experiment used the same device to enable either touchscreen interaction or mid-air interaction. Therefore, our study provides convincing evidence of the varying effect of gesture-based interaction on consumers’ sense of touch.

Second, this study calls for the incorporation of sensory experience into virtual product experience research. The predominant emphasis of prior virtual product experience research on product presentation may have unwittingly resulted in the misconception that visual and audio information is the only prevailing factor in shaping consumers’ perception and behavior online. By juxtaposing the role of sensory experience in a virtual product experience, we underscore the interplay between consumers’ bodily interactions with devices in the physical world and their product perceptions in the virtual environment. In particular, if the importance of a product’s haptic information is high, when people interact with touchscreen devices, they may perceive a higher sense of touch than when they interact with kinetic devices. Since sensory experience influences human judgment and behavior (Krishna, 2012; Krishna & Schwarz, 2013), understanding the relationship between sensory experience and information technology is critical for future human-computer interaction research.

Third, our research complements and enriches the research on sensory marketing by highlighting the effect of bodily sensations on both cognitive and affective dimensions of consumers’ product perceptions. By adopting the cognitive-affective framework, we provide a holistic model to understand how consumers’ haptic sensory influences product perceptions and shopping satisfaction. Our results indicate that, as a sensory input, consumers’ sense of touch provides a more convincing and emotionally connected product perception and consequently leads to a more satisfactory online shopping experience.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

The present study also has potential implications for online retailing practice. First, our study demonstrates the advantage of touchscreen interaction over mid-air interaction in eliciting online consumers’ sense of touch when the haptic information of the products under examination is of high importance. Thus, online retailers selling products with high haptic importance, such as apparel, should encourage consumers to adopt touchscreen-based technologies rather than seemingly fascinating and costly mid-air technologies.

Second, we show that consumers’ bodily sensations (i.e., sense of touch) play an important role in consumers virtual product experience. Even without direct contact with products, online consumers’ sense of touch, elicited by their bodily movements, has the potential to influence consumers’ cognitive and affective activities. Specifically, consumers’ sense of touch elicited by interaction technology can, to some extent, act as an actual touch experience, which reduces their uncertainty about products and enhances their attachment to products. This finding suggests that online retailers should leverage touchscreen interaction technology to create simulated haptic sensations and thereby enhance online consumers’ overall shopping satisfaction. Furthermore, retailers should take consumers’ bodily sensations into consideration and enrich consumers’ sensory experience when designing for virtual product experience.

## 6.3 Limitations and Directions for Future Research

We note three limitations of the current study. First, even though mid-air interaction technology is becoming increasingly prevalent (Lauren, 2020), it is still in a developing form that only supports simple gestures for interaction. Therefore, we only compared simple mid-air gestures and touchscreen gestures in our study. Further, we did not consider the specific gestures supported by different gesture-based interactions. Prior literature indicates that different gestures may have different metaphorical meanings, which could lead to variations in learnability, cognitive, and affective reactions (Beilock & Goldin-Meadow, 2010). Thus, a direction for future research would be to investigate the impacts of more advanced mid-air interaction technologies that support complex mid-air gestures.

Second, this study focused on the impacts of gesturebased interaction on people’s haptic sensations. The two interaction modes may also differ in other dimensions, making them fit for different types of tasks and scenarios. For example, a recent survey reveals that people’s perceptions of gesture-based interactions are rapidly changing in the post-COVID-19 era. In particular, touchless mid-air interaction is perceived as more hygienic, compared to touchscreen interaction (Ultraleap, 2020). Therefore, more effort is necessary to compare the two interaction modes from different perspectives other than from the perspective of the sense of touch. Furthermore, previous research suggests that multisensory information (such as visual and haptic) interacts to affect consumers’ perception and behavior (Krishna, 2006). Future studies could explore how the interplay between different haptic feedback afforded by gesture-based interaction and visual feedback enabled by visual product presentation technologies affects consumers’ online shopping experience.

Third, we only assessed two types of gesture-based interaction (i.e., touchscreen interaction and mid-air interaction) in our study because these two types of interaction are highly comparable, as they both involve natural hand movements. It would be interesting to provide a comprehensive comparison among different types of interaction methods, including mouse and other novel interaction modes (e.g., eye-gaze, voice, and head movements). In fact, an initiation comparison between mouse and gesture-based interaction revealed that touchscreen and mouse outperformed mid-air interaction in terms of enhancing shopping experience satisfaction. However, the effect of the interaction method was not mediated by participants’ sense of touch. This result suggests that the sense of touch does not explain the varying effects of mouse interaction, touchscreen interaction, and mid-air interaction on consumers’ virtual product experience. <sup>6</sup> Future research might investigate the mechanisms through which different interaction methods could affect consumers’ online shopping experience other than through the sense of touch. It would also be intriguing to explore how to leverage the power of different interaction modes with advanced visual presentation technologies, such as immersive virtual reality and augmented reality.

## Appendix A: 3D Experiment

## Experimental Procedure and Manipulation

This study adopted a 2 (interaction method: touchscreen interaction vs. mid-air interaction) × 2 (importance of product haptic information: high vs. low) between-subjects experimental design. We recruited participants from a large public university. Participants were informed that the purpose of this study was to investigate people’s online shopping behavior and they would each receive a token of appreciation upon completion of the experimental task. In total, 100 participants registered in our study, and they were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions.

In the experiment, participants were required to complete a shopping task on our experimental website. The design of our experimental website was similar to a typical e-commerce website. Participants could evaluate the products by reading the textual product description and viewing product images (including a three-dimensional product image and an assortment of images of product details).

The interaction method was manipulated by requiring the participants to use different interaction devices to complete the experimental task. In the touchscreen interaction condition, participants were asked to conduct their shopping task using an all-in-one computer. The all-in-one computer has an 86-inch touch screen, thus enabling the participants to interact with the experimental website by directing moving their fingers on the screen. In the mid-air interaction condition, the participants were provided with a Leap Motion controller for their shopping task. That is, the participants moved their hands in the air to browse the experimental website. Table A1 shows pictures of real settings of the two conditions. To avoid the potential influence of screen size on the participants’ behavior, we used the same screen in both conditions.

The importance of product haptic information was manipulated by varying the type of products presented on our experimental website. In the high haptic importance condition, the products available on our experimental website were T-shirts. In the low haptic importance condition, we provided participants with digital cameras on our experimental website. When the participants finished examining all products on the website and making their choice, they were asked to fill out a computer-based questionnaire. Upon the completion of the questionnaire, the participants were paid and dismissed.

## Measurement

Measurement items for the constructs of our interest (i.e., sense of touch, product uncertainty, product attachment, and shopping experience satisfaction) were adapted from prior literature. The detailed measurement items were the same as in the main study.

## Participants’ Background Information

Among the 100 participants, 54 (54%) were female and 46 (46%) were male. The age of the participants ranged from 19 to 31 (M = 22.7, SD = 2.26). There was no significant difference in terms of participants’ demographic information across the experimental conditions.

## Manipulation Check and Psychometric Properties of Key Constructs

The manipulation check revealed that the T-shirt (M = 5.08, SD = 1.45) and digital camera (M = 4.33, SD = 1.60) differed in the importance of product haptic information (F (1, 98) = 6.07, p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 0.49), suggesting that participants perceived haptic information to be more important when evaluating the T-shirt, as opposed to the digital camera.

The validity and reliability of our key constructs were assessed. Descriptive statistics and psychometric properties of key constructs are presented in Table A2, A3, and A4. Cronbach’s alphas were greater than 0.7 and all CRs exceeded the criterion of 0.7. Thus, measurement scales for all constructs showed good reliability. AVEs were above the threshold of 0.5, indicating that the constructs can account for at least 50% of the variance in the items. Additionally, all item loadings on their intended construct were higher than the recommended cutoff of 0.7 and were significant at the level of 0.05, demonstrating good convergent validity. Moreover, the square root of AVE for each construct was greater than the correlations involving the construct. Also, item-factor loadings were all greater than item-unintended factor loadings. Thus, discriminant validity was deemed acceptable.

## Effects of Gesture-Based Interaction on the Sense of Touch

Scores on the sense of touch were submitted to a 2 (interaction method: touchscreen interaction vs. mid-air interaction) × 2 (importance of haptic information: high vs. low) ANOVA. The results revealed a significant main effect of the interaction method $( F \left( 1 , 9 6 \right) = 1 1 . 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 , \mathrm { C o h e n } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } d = 0 . 7 0 )$ . Specifically, touchscreen interaction (M = 3.58, SD = 1.30) elicited a higher sense of touch, compared to mid-air interaction $( M = 2 . 6 7 , S D = 1 . 3 7 )$ . More importantly, the result yielded a marginally significant interaction effect between the interaction method and the importance of product haptic information $( F \left( 1 , 9 6 \right) = 3 . 0 0 , p < 0 . 1$ , see Figure A1). Planned contrasts showed that when evaluating T-shirts (i.e., the importance of product haptic information was high), touchscreen interaction induced a significantly higher sense of touch than mid-air interaction (M<sub>touchscreen</sub> = 3.87, M<sub>mid-air</sub> = 2.49, F (1, 96) = 13.39, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.02). However, when examining digital cameras (i.e., the importance of product haptic information was low), there was no significant difference between touchscreen and mid-air interaction $( M _ { \mathrm { t o u c h s c r e e n } } = 3 . 2 9 , M _ { \mathrm { m i d - a i r } } = 2 . 8 4 , F \left( 1 , 9 6 \right) = 1 . 4 6 ,$ p > 0.1, Cohen’s d = 0.35).

## Effects of the Sense of Touch

We used partial least squares (PLS, SmartPLS 3.0) structural equation modeling to investigate the effects of the sense of touch on product uncertainty, product attachment, and shopping experience satisfaction. Bootstrapping resampling was performed on the structural model to examine path significance (see Figure A2). As predicted, a higher sense of touch reduced product uncertainty $( \beta = - 0 . 2 6 , t = 2 . 6 6 )$ and fostered stronger product attachment $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 , t = 6 . 4 1 )$ In addition, product uncertainty was negatively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = - 0 . 2 6 , t = 2 . 8 0 )$ while product attachment was positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 , t = 2 . 7 6 )$ . Together, product uncertainty and product attachment explained 52% of the variance in shopping experience satisfaction. None of the control variables (i.e., age, gender, or participants’ need for touch) was found significant in affecting shopping experience satisfaction.

Among the 120 participants, 73 (60.8%) were female and 47 (39.2%) were male. The age of the participants ranged from 19 to 31 (M = 22.1, SD = 2.42). There was no significant difference in terms of participants’ demographic information across the experimental conditions.

## Manipulation Check and Psychometric Properties of Key Constructs

The manipulation check revealed that plush toy (M = 6.12, SD = 0.84) and toothpaste $( M = 4 . 4 9 , S D = 1 . 7 6 )$ differed in the importance of product haptic information (F (1, 118) = 41.57, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.18), suggesting that participants perceived haptic information was more important when evaluating the plush toy, as opposed to toothpaste.

Before testing our research model, we assessed the validity and reliability of our key constructs. Descriptive statistics and psychometric properties of key constructs are presented in Table B1, B2, and B3. Cronbach’s alphas were greater than 0.7 and all CRs exceeded the criterion of 0.7. Thus, measurement scales for all constructs showed good reliability. AVEs were above the threshold of 0.5, indicating that the constructs can account for at least 50% of the variance in the items. Additionally, all item loadings on their intended construct were higher than the recommended cutoff of 0.7 and were significant at the level of 0.05, demonstrating good convergent validity. Moreover, the square root of AVE for each construct was greater than the correlations involving the construct. Also, item-factor loadings were all greater than item-unintended factor loadings. Thus, we concluded that discriminant validity was acceptable.

## Effects of Gesture-Based Interaction on the Sense of Touch

Scores on the sense of touch were submitted to a 2 (interaction method: touchscreen interaction vs. mid-air interaction) × 2 (importance of haptic information: high vs. low) ANOVA. The results revealed a significant main effect of the interaction method $( F \left( 1 , 1 1 6 \right) = 4 . 6 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 , \mathrm { C o h e n } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } d = 0 . 4 0 )$ . Specifically, touchscreen interaction $( M = 4 . 0 3 , S D$ = 1.21) elicited a higher sense of touch, compared to mid-air interaction $( M = 3 . 5 3 , S D = 1 . 3 5 )$ . More importantly, the results yielded a significant interaction effect between the interaction method and the importance of product haptic information $\left( F \left( 1 , 1 1 6 \right) = 5 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 5 \right.$ , see Figure B2). Planned contrasts showed that when evaluating plush toys (i.e., the importance of product haptic information was high), touchscreen interaction induced a significantly higher sense of touch than mid-air interaction $( M _ { \mathrm { t o u c h s c r e e n } } = 4 . 3 7 , M _ { \mathrm { m i d - a i r } } = 3 . 3 4 , F \left( 1 , 1 1 6 \right) = 9 . 8 9 , p < 0 . 0 1$ , Cohen’s d = 0.83). However, when examining toothpaste (i.e., the importance of product haptic information was low), there was no significant difference between touchscreen and mid-air interaction $( M _ { \mathrm { t o u c h s c r e e n } } = 3 . 6 8 , M _ { \mathrm { m i d - a i r } } = 3 . 7 1 , F \left( 1 , 1 1 6 \right) = 0 . 0 1$ $p > 0 . 1 , \mathrm { C o h e n " s } \ d = 0 . 0 3 )$ ). Those findings remained meaningfully unchanged when control variables, such as age, gender, and participants’ need for touch, were included.

## Effects of the Sense of Touch

We used partial least squares (PLS, SmartPLS 3.0) structural equation modeling to investigate the effects of the sens of touch on product uncertainty, product attachment, and shopping experience satisfaction. Bootstrapping resampling was performed on the structural model to examine path significance (see Figure B3). As predicted, a higher sense of touch reduced product uncertainty $( \beta = - 0 . 2 5 , t = 2 . 5 0 )$ and fostered stronger product attachment $( \beta = 0 . 4 9 , t = 7 . 5 0 )$ In addition, product uncertainty was negatively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = - 0 . 2 0 , t = 1 . 8 3 )$ while product attachment was positively associated with shopping experience satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , t = 1 . 9 7 )$ . Together, product uncertainty and product attachment explained 23.2% of the variance in shopping experience satisfaction. None of the control variables (i.e., age, gender, or participants’ need for touch) was found significant in affecting shopping experience satisfaction.

## Appendix C: Experiment with a Mouse (Baseline) Condition

## Experimental Procedure and Manipulation

This study adopted a 3 (interaction method: mouse interaction vs. touchscreen interaction vs. mid-air interaction) × 2 (importance of product haptic information: high vs. low) between-subjects design. In the mouse interaction condition, participants were asked to complete an online shopping task via a desktop with a mouse. The experimental task and procedure remained the same as in our main study. One hundred and eighty participants were recruited and randomly assigned to one of the six conditions.

## Results

Among the 180 participants, 92 (51.1%) were female. The age of the participants ranged from 19 to 29 (M = 22.9, SD = 2.15). There was no significant difference in terms of participants’ demographic information across the experimental conditions.

We found that interaction method affected consumers’ shopping experience satisfaction $( M _ { \mathrm { m o u s e } } = 5 . 0 5 , M _ { \mathrm { t o u c h s c r e e n } } =$ 5.27, $M _ { \mathrm { m i d - a i r } } = 4 . 6 6 , F \left( 2 , 1 7 7 \right) = 3 . 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . However, the effect of the interaction method on participants shopping experience was not mediated by participants’ sense of touch (Omnibus indirect effect through the sense of touch = 0.02, 95% bootstrap CI = -0.001 to 0.04). Hence, the results of this study confirmed our conjecture that the sense of touch cannot explain the varying effects of mouse-based interaction and gesture-based interaction on consumers’ virtual product experience. Future research could investigate the mechanisms through which different interaction methods could affect consumers’ online shopping experience.
