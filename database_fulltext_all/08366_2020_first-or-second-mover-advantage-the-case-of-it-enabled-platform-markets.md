# FIRST- OR SECOND-MOVER ADVANTAGE? THE CASE OF IT-ENABLED PLATFORM MARKETS<sup>1</sup>

Haiyang Feng College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, CHINA {hyfeng@tju.edu.cn}

Zhengrui Jiang School of Business, Nanjing University, Nanjing 210093, CHINA {zjiang@nju.edu.cn}

Minqiang Li College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, CHINA {mqli@tju.edu.cn}

Nan Feng College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin 300072, CHINA {fengnan@tju.edu.cn}

Information technology-enabled platforms are playing an increasingly important role in the global economy. While some successful platforms entered their respective markets as pioneers, others were able to achieve market dominance despite starting as followers. This study investigates the conditions under which first-mover advantage (FMA) or second-mover advantage (SMA) occurs in a platform market, and derives their equili brium order of market entries and optimal market entry timing. The first three determinants of FMA and SMA we identify are (1) quality improvement rate of the second mover, (2) length of demand window, and (3) length of market growth phase of demand window. When the two companies’ order of market entries is predetermined, the second mover having a higher initial platform quality than the first mover guarantees SMA, whereas the first mover having a higher initial platform quality than the second mover does not ensure FMA. In the latter case, the three determinants are needed to determine FMA and SMA. When the two companies’ order of market entries is endogenous, if any of the three determinants is sufficiently small, preemptive entry, in which both companies prefer to move early, is the equilibrium; otherwise, sequential entry, in which one company prefers to move early and the other prefers to move late, becomes the equilibrium in most cases. We also find that strong (same-side) network effects can weaken SMA and increase the possibility that both companies prefer early market entry, whereas strong cross-side network effects may strengthen SMA and increase the possibility that both companies prefer late market entry.

Keywords: Platform economy, first-mover advantage, second-mover advantage, market entry timing, duopoly

## Introduction

We live in the age of the platform economy. Information technology-enabled platforms are profoundly affecting consumers and businesses alike. For instance, Internet-based platforms, such as eBay and Amazon, enable consumer-toconsumer and business-to-consumer sales transactions that are infeasible in traditional brick-and-mortar settings. Mobile app stores, such as App Store and Google Play, connect app developers with users and deliver apps to users at low prices. Social media sites, such as Facebook and Twitter, allow users to communicate with friends and followers in a way unconstrained by time and geographic boundary, thereby changing the ways they obtain and consume information.

By delivering value to its users, the platform market has also grown exponentially over the last two decades. eBay had amassed 165 million users and 25 million sellers as of November 2016, including not only individual eBay users and small businesses, but also large companies such as Best Buy (Smith 2016). Over two million apps exist in App Store and Google Play, which generated \$2.23 billion in revenue in the third quarter of 2016 alone (Statista 2016). Numerous platforms have profited from large customer bases. Successful platforms currently represent some of the most influential and profitable IT companies in the world. In 2016, the top 15 public platform companies, including Alibaba, Alphabet, Amazon, and Apple, represented \$2.6 trillion in market capitalization worldwide (Accenture 2016).

All new platforms introduced in the last two to three decades are enabled and empowered by IT. The advancement of IT, coupled with the tremendous business opportunity provided by prominent platforms, has helped attract considerable interest and investments into platform products, thereby leading to the emergence of numerous platform companies. The fast-paced and volatile nature of IT markets, however, also makes platform markets more volatile than most traditional markets. Many platforms struggle to gain traction in crowded markets. Some platforms, such as MySpace and Charles Stack Online Bookstore, were once market leaders but soon faded. Other platforms, such as eBay and Facebook, have achieved market dominance and are likely to maintain their market leadership in the foreseeable future.

## First-Mover Advantage (FMA) or Second-Mover Advantage (SMA)

Market entry timing is a critical decision for businesses. The economics and marketing literature, as well as practitioners outlets, have reported numerous cases that demonstrated the existence of either first/early-mover advantage or second/latemover advantage (e.g., Birger 2006; Suarez and Lanzolla 2005). Specifically, first-mover advantage (FMA) refers to the phenomenon in which the first (early) firm to enter a new market is more likely to achieve superior performance in market share or profitability than later entrants. Second/latemover advantage (SMA) occurs when the second (late) entrant to the market has a better chance of outperforming the first entrant despite its late start. In the platform market, wellknown examples with sustained FMA exist, including Twitter, eBay, and Uber. These examples demonstrate that once first movers have established a large installed base, it is very difficult for a follower to break their dominance. Nevertheless, real examples with the second mover overtaking the first in a platform market also exist (e.g., Facebook versus MySpace, Amazon versus Charles Stack Online Bookstore, and Taobao versus eBay China).

The high-profile case of Facebook and MySpace has received considerable attention from scholars and practitioners and revived the decade-old debate about FMA and SMA. In this widely recognized case, MySpace entered the market first but was soundly overtaken by the latecomer Facebook. The consensus reached by observers is that Facebook enjoyed SMA— it learned from MySpace’s mistakes and perfected the social media concept (e.g., Dredge 2016; Hartung 2011; McNeil 2012; Schenker 2015), thus defeating the first mover.

The CEOs of some platform companies also ponder whether to enter a market as a leader or a follower. Having noticed that FMA is not guaranteed, some firms strategically postpone their product releases to learn from pioneers and improve their own product quality. Chunk Cohn, CEO of Varsity Tutors, stated in an article entitled “When to Be a First Mover and When to Wait” (Cohn 2015) that the entry timing decision should depend on a firm’s resources, pace of technological innovation, and barriers to entry. Robert Dykes, CEO of TORA Trading, explained in an interview that his firm benefitted from SMA because the additional time allowed them to develop a superior product at a lower total cost of ownership to clients (D'Antona 2017).

Researchers have explored the sources of FMA and SMA and how FMA can be sustained (Boulding and Christen 2003; Lieberman and Montgomery 1988). The documented sources of FMA include technology leadership, preemptive control of scarce resources, network externality, and lock-in effect. The sources of SMA include free-rider effect and having less market uncertainty and more flexibility than has the first mover (Song et al. 2013).

These prior studies have provided numerous examples that document either FMA or SMA, but the examples are primarily from physical or packaged goods markets. We have yet to see any formal analysis on FMA and SMA in the context of the platform economy. The present study addresses this research gap.

## Characteristics of Platform Products

Platform products have unique characteristics that distinguish them from physical goods. Prior studies have shown that these characteristics can affect business decisions (Li and Agarwal 2017; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Parker et al. 2017). In this study, we consider three characteristics of platforms.

First, given that practically all platforms are enabled by IT, platforms carry some important characteristics of information goods, such as the presence of network effects and lock-in effects, and negligible marginal production cost.

Second, the revenue model of IT-enabled platforms typically differs from that of physical products. Instead of selling a product to consumers for one-time revenue, platforms tend to earn sustained revenue from the same user base over time. A unique feature of many present platforms is that they treat one side of the markets as a profit center and the other side as a consumer base (Rochet and Tirole 2003). Table 1 shows examples of such platforms. These platform companies offer their products for free to one side of the market, typically consumers, to maximize their user base and charge the other side of the market, typically businesses, to take advantage of two-sided network effects (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). For example, Google offers free search functions that help grow its user base, thus making itself valuable to advertisers. Consequently, the pricing strategy of such two-sided platforms differs from that of physical products. The amount of revenue generated from the profit side typically increases proportionately with the size of the platform’s consumer base. We focus on this type of two-sided platforms in this study.

Third, the platform market can expand at an exploding pace, and the supporting IT typically evolves rapidly. With the speed of technological innovation, the platform market tends to be highly volatile, thus potentially affecting the occurrences of FMA/SMA.

The first two characteristics are explicitly captured in our model setup. The third characteristic, although not explicitly modeled, exists in the background, and will be connected with our key findings.

## Summary of Findings

The primary objectives of this research are to identify the conditions that lead to FMA or SMA and derive the equilibrium order of market entries and the optimal entry timing for two competing platform companies.

Our analytical and numerical results lead to several important findings. When the order of market entries is exogenously given, the two companies’ initial platform quality (i.e., quality at the beginning of the time horizon) should be examined first to determine FMA or SMA. The second mover having a higher initial platform quality than does the first mover guarantees SMA, whereas the first mover having a higher initial platform quality than the second mover does not ensure FMA. In the latter case, three main determinants—namely, quality improvement rate of the second mover, length of demand window, and length of market growth phase of demand window—are needed to determine FMA and SMA. If the second mover does not have a higher initial quality than the first mover, then SMA occurs only when all three determinants are sufficiently large.

When the companies’ order of market entries is endogenously determined, we focus on three types of equilibrium order of market entries, namely, preemptive entry, under which both companies prefer to move early; waiting entry, in which both companies prefer to move late; and sequential entry, in which one company prefers to move early and the other prefers to move late. Under this more complex case, the same main determinants, namely, quality improvement rate of the second mover, length of demand window, and length of market growth phase of demand window, decide the occurrence of FMA and SMA and the equilibrium order of market entries. If any of these determinants is sufficiently small, preemptive entry is the equilibrium; otherwise, sequential entry becomes the equilibrium in most cases. Waiting entry is an equilibrium only under a set of restrictive conditions; even when such conditions are met, waiting entry is dominated by preemptive entry in terms of revenue for both companies, implying the presence of the prisoner’s dilemma.

We also analyze the impacts of network effects on the second mover’s market entry timing and the equilibrium order of market entries. The results show that, with stronger (sameside) network effects, SMA is less likely to happen, and the two platform companies are more likely to simultaneously choose to be the first mover (i.e., preemptive entry occurs).

We focus on a user-free/advertiser-pay platform model in this study. When advertisers incur a fixed operating cost, crossside network effects are significant; hence a larger number of platform users help attract more advertisers to join the platform. As a result, the platform’s revenue increases at an increasing rate with the number of users. ## Related Literature

The present research is related to several streams of literature in addition to the prior research on FMA and SMA (e.g., Boulding and Christen 2003; Lieberman and Montgomery 1988). The first stream is on platforms and two-sided markets. Parker and Van Alstyne (2005) explain that some platform companies are better off offering free products or services to one side of the market because of the two-sided network effects; the increased demand in a complementary market more than covers the cost of investment in the freeservice side of the market. Researchers have recently analyzed how optimal firm decisions in the platform economy depend on platform integration and demand spillovers (Li and Agarwal 2017), compatibility between two platforms (Adner et al. 2016), developer-to-developer competition, and platform-to-platform competition (Parker et al. 2017).

The second stream of research investigates the trade-off between time to market and new product quality for monopolist producers. Cohen et al. (1996) examine the quality and entry timing trade-off on the basis of a multistage model that includes product design, process, and market stages. They show that when product improvements in different stages are additive, a firm should allocate the maximum amount of time to the most productive stage. Jiang et al. (2012) find that the optimal time to release a software system can be shortened and the system reliability can be improved with the implementation of post-release testing.

In the third stream of research, analytical models have been proposed to study the market entry timing under duopoly settings with a predetermined order of market entries. Kopel and Löffler (2008) identify the sources of first-mover advantages and disadvantages using a Stackelberg (sequential) structure. The authors show that with a combination of technology choices and selection of internal organization (whether delegating production decision to a manager), the second mover can outperform the first mover and increase profits. Feng et al. (2018) study the market entry strategy for a new SaaS vendor. They find that when the initial product quality of the new entrant is significantly lower than that of the incumbent, it should release its product immediately; otherwise, the release should be delayed until its quality surpasses that of the existing product.

The fourth stream of related literature focuses on market entry timing in duopoly games, in which the order of market entries is endogenous. Hamilton and Slutsky (1990) build an extended game to study simultaneous versus sequential plays. The extended game includes a pre-play stage, in which players decide whether to take action at the first opportunity or to wait. Following the extended game with observable delay, Lambertini (1999) analyzes the endogenous choice of timing in a vertically differentiated duopoly, in which quality improvement requires a fixed convex cost. Amir and Stepanova (2006) investigate the issue of endogenous timing of moves in a differentiated-product Bertrand duopoly with asymmetric linear costs. Studies in this stream differ from the present research in the types of products considered and the key research questions addressed.

Table 2 compares this study with related prior works regarding problem context, research focus, and identified determinants of FMA and SMA. We highlight a few key differences. First, the prior studies have not examined market entry strategies for competing platform products, let alone the two-sidedness of a market. Second, we develop a continuoustime model, which can select optimal market entry timing from a continuous time horizon instead of more ambiguous discrete time periods. Consequently, our solutions and findings are more prescriptive and less qualitative than those derived using discrete-time models. Third, unlike some prior studies (e.g., Feng et al. 2018) that assume known sequences of market entries, we endogenize the order of market entries, thereby allowing the competition and market entry strategies in emerging (instead of established) markets to be studied. Fourth, among all studies that endogenize the order of market entries, this study is the only one that explicitly considers same- and cross-side network effects and allows product quality to improve after product release. Finally, we identify four key determinants of FMA or SMA, namely, quality improvement rate of the second mover, length of demand window, length of market growth phase of demand window, and intensity of network effects. On the contrary, no prior study has identified more than one of the four determinants.

## Continuous-Time Revenue Model

Prior studies on market entry timing have typically employed either discrete-time models (e.g., Moorthy and Png 1992) or continuous-time models (e.g., Jiang et al. 2019). Relatively speaking, discrete-time models tend to produce business in sights that are more qualitative in nature, whereas continuoustime models are more likely to generate not only detailed insights but also actionable solutions. The cost of employing a continuous-time model, however, is considerable complexity and consequent difficulty in deriving analytical solutions. Given the objective of this study, a discrete-time model is inadequate. Hence, we develop a continuous-time model to address our research questions. We assume that a platform can be launched at any time during a continuous demand window, and its quality can be improved even after market launch.

As previously noted, this study focuses on the types of platforms that offer free access to the consumer-base side and charge the profit-center side (see Table 1). For ease of exposition, we consider the most common type of such platforms, in which platform users are on the free side and advertisers are on the paid side (henceforth called user-free–advertiserpay).

## Two-Stage Model

The platform market we model includes two platform companies. Their platforms compete for the same potential consumers and are considered by these consumers to be substitutable. The two companies face the trade-offs among quality, time to market, and competitive (dis)advantages as a first or second mover, and play a non-cooperative game when making their production and marketing decisions. Following previous studies (e.g., Amir and Stepanova 2006; Savin and Terwiesch 2005; Tran et al. 2012), FMA/SMA is determined by comparing the two companies’ revenues gained over the entire demand window.

Without loss of generality, we let the first mover’s market entry date be time 0 and denote the second mover’s entry time by $\tau _ { 2 } .$ As shown in Figure 1, the duration of the monopoly stage is $\tau _ { 2 } ,$ and that of the duopoly stage is $( T - \tau _ { 2 } )$ , where $T$ denotes the length of the demand window. Under the userfree–advertiser-pay model, users’ and advertisers’ utilitymaximizing behaviors must be considered in the derivation of optimal market entry decisions for the platforms.

## Advertisers’ Decisions

We first examine advertisers’ decisions. In the type of market we consider, advertisers commonly pay a platform according to the number of times their advertisements (ads) are clicked by platform users. This mechanism is known as pay-per-click (PPC) pricing (e.g., employed by Google AdWords and Facebook Ads). In this study, we assume that advertisers are multi-homing, and an advertiser’s decision to join one platform is unaffected by its decision to join other platforms.

For broader application, we assume that the average profit margin gained from a consumer, denoted by $\rho ,$ , varies across advertisers. $\rho$ is uniformly distributed in [0, b]. If an advertiser joins the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ mover’s platform $( i \in \{ 1 , 2 \} )$ at time t, its profit per unit time assumes the following form:

$$
\pi_ {A d} ^ {i} (t) = \rho \varphi \omega Y _ {i} (t) - p _ {i} (t) \omega Y _ {i} (t)\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i } ( t )$ denotes the number of users of platform i at time $t ; \ \omega$ is the users’ average click rate; $\boldsymbol { \Phi }$ is their average conversion rate, which measures the percentage of ad-clickers who purchase the advertisers’ products; and $p _ { i } ( t )$ is the flatrate PPC price charged by platform i at time t.

From $\pi _ { A d } ^ { i } \left( t \right) \geq 0$ and the distribution of $\rho ,$ we obtain the number of advertisers who join the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ mover’s platform at time t as

$$
D _ {A d} ^ {i} (t) = b - \frac {p _ {i} (t)}{\varphi}\tag{2}
$$

Hence, platform i’s revenue per unit time at time t equals

$$
r _ {i} (t) = \left(b - \frac {p _ {i} (t)}{\varphi}\right) p _ {i} (t) \omega Y _ {i} (t)\tag{3}
$$

The platform’s revenue at time t is optimized, and we obtain platform $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ optimal PPC price, number of advertisers who join the platform, and optimal revenue per unit time as

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c} p _ {i} ^ {*} (t) = \frac {b \varphi}{2} \\ D _ {A d} ^ {i *} (t) = \frac {b}{2} \\ r _ {i} ^ {*} (t) = \frac {b ^ {2} \varphi}{4} \omega Y _ {i} (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

Solution (4) indicates that both platforms’ optimal PPC price increases with conversion rate but is independent of click rate. The platforms’ optimal revenue per unit time increases with conversion and click rates.

## Platform Users’ Decisions

We evaluate platform users’ decision making and its impact on the two platforms’ market share and profitability. After launching its platform at time 0, the first mover attracts all the platform users in the market until the entrance of the second mover. Thus, during the monopoly stage, the first mover accumulates users, and the second mover continues developing its platform product. Due to network effects (Katz and Shapiro 1985), consumers’ adoption decisions depend on not only platform quality but also network size or the number of users of each platform. Analogous to previous studies, this work assumes product quality to increase linearly with time (Cohen et al. 1996). Thus, upon the second mover’s market entry, the value of the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ mover’s platform is given by

$$
V _ {i} = q _ {i 0} \left(1 + \alpha Q _ {i} + \lambda_ {i} \tau_ {2}\right)\tag{5}
$$

## Platforms’ Revenue Functions

After substituting Y (t) in the optimal unit-time revenue function in Equation (4) with Equations (8) and (9), we can calculate the two companies’ total revenues gained during the entire demand window by

## Exogenous Order of Market Entries

In this section, we assume that the two companies’ sequence of market entries is given, and the first mover launches its platform at time 0. ## Endogenous Order of Market Entries

In the previous section, as well as in most previous studies (Feng et al. 2018; Kopel and Löffler 2008), the order of market entry is assumed to be exogenous. In reality, entering a market early or late itself is an important decision for businesses, especially when the order of market entry has substantial financial (or even market survival) implications. We endogenize the order of market entries in a two-player game to gain broad business insights into market entry timing in a competitive platform market.

Following the framework proposed by Hamilton and Slutsky (1990) for an extended game with observable delay, we construct an extended game with two stages.

Pre-play game: The two companies simultaneously decide whether to move early or late in a basic game.

Basic game: Given their choices in the pre-play game, the two companies decide their market entry timing.

Consider two platform companies, A and B, offering platforms A and B, respectively. Without loss of generality, we assume that Platform A has a higher initial quality than does Platform B $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , q _ { A } ( 0 ) > q _ { B } ( 0 ) )$ . Both companies can choose to enter the market late and improve their product qualities $( q _ { A } ( \tau _ { A } )$ and $( q _ { B } ( \tau _ { B } ) )$ ).

In the pre-play game, {E, L} is the set of actions from which the two players (platform companies) can select; E and L denote moving early and moving late, respectively. As shown in Figure 7, the possible outcomes of the pre-play game are {(E, E), (E, L), (L, E), (L, L)}. We refer to the outcome in which both companies prefer to move early, that is, (E, E), as preemptive entry; the outcome in which both companies prefer to move late, (L, L), as waiting entry; and the two remaining outcomes, (E, L) and (L, E), as sequential entry.

It is widely acknowledged that deriving the optimal move time from a continuous time horizon for competing players is a challenging problem, and no perfect means of treating simul taneous moves has been developed, especially when both players may decide to wait for the other player to move first (Dutta et al. 1995). To address this problem, we assume the following. If the two platform companies simultaneously decide to move early in the pre-play game, both will launch their respective platforms at time 0. In case both companies decide to move late, delaying the launch of their platforms essentially shortens the remaining demand window; therefore, they cannot delay their market entry forever. Eventually, the companies will reach a point in the demand window where, suppose one company releases its platform at this point, SMA disappears for the other company. This is the point where both parties will launch their platforms.

Proposition 3 confirms that network effects remain an important determinant of the optimal market entry strategy when the order of market entries is endogenous. As network effects strengthen, the preemptive entry $( E , E )$ is more likely, whereas sequential entries $( E , L )$ and $( L , E )$ are less likely, to become the equilibrium. The primary reason for this result is that strong network effects benefit the first mover at the expense of the second mover. Specifically, when the second mover enters the market, the first mover has already accumulated a certain number of users. Given stronger network effects, the first mover’s platform is more attractive to new users. Therefore, as the intensity of network effects increases, the benefit of being the second mover is reduced, hence preemptive entry becomes more likely to occur.

## Taking into Consideration of Advertisers’Fixed Operating Cost

So far, we have assumed that advertisers incur no fixed operating cost after joining a platform (Equation (1)). In reality, however, fixed operating cost typically cannot be avoided. For example, computing resources and staff hours are required to maintain up-to-date product information on a platform, manage payment and revenue accounts, and maintain telecommunication channels between the advertiser and platform’s servers. In this section, we investigate a general scenario. After joining platform i, an advertiser incurs a fixed operating cost per unit time, denoted by $c .$ Then, the advertiser’s profit per unit time at platform i becomes

$$
\pi_ {A d} ^ {i} (t) = \rho \varphi \omega Y _ {i} (t) - p _ {i} (t) \omega Y _ {i} (t) - c\tag{20}
$$

The change in the profit function from Equation (1) to Equation (20) is minor but presents an important implication. With the large network size on the user side, an advertiser’s fixed operating cost can be divided by a large number of customers. Hence, its profit per customer increases. In other words, the value of an additional platform user to an advertiser increases with the size of users’ network size, that is, cross-side network effects exist. Subsequently, we examine how equilibrium outcomes change after considering the fixed operating cost.

## Exogenous Order of Market Entries

Assuming that the order of market entries is known, we are able to obtain several analytical properties with regard to the second mover’s optimal market entry timing.

Proposition 4. When $\lambda _ { 2 } > \lambda _ { 1 } + \alpha \beta ,$ as the fixed operating cost for advertisers increases, it is optimal for the second mover to further postpone its market entry, but its launch time should be no laterthan $\begin{array} { r } { t = \frac { \sqrt { \lambda _ { 2 } k ( \lambda _ { 2 } - \lambda _ { 1 } - \alpha \beta ) \left( \bar { \tau } ( \lambda _ { 2 } + \lambda _ { 1 } k + \alpha \beta k ) + 1 + k \right) } - \lambda _ { 2 } ( 1 + k ) } { \lambda _ { 2 } \left( \lambda _ { 2 } + \lambda _ { 1 } k + \alpha \beta k \right) } } \end{array}$

According to Lemma 4, with a higher operating cost for advertisers, the number of platform users plays more important role in influencing advertisers’ participation decisions. In other words, cross-side network effect is strengthened with increasing operating cost. In this case, because of the stronger cross-side network effects, attracting additional platform users becomes relatively more important than pursuing a long duration of market existence. Therefore, when the rate of quality improvement of the second mover is sufficiently high $( \lambda _ { 2 } > \lambda _ { 1 } + \alpha \beta )$ , as the advertisers’ fixed operating cost increases, the second mover is motivated to further postpone its platform launch. This is because a delayed launch results in higher platform quality, thereby attracting a larger number of users.

It is important to note that although prior studies (e.g., Feng et al. 2018) have shown the impacts of same-side network effects on the companies’ optimal market entry timing, no prior study have investigated how cross-side network effects affect platform companies’ market entry timing. Proposition 4 shows that, contrary to the finding that stronger same-side network effects make it beneficial for the second mover to enter the market earlier, cross-side network effects can have an opposite impact on the second mover’s decision. With same-side network effects considered, the second mover faces the tradeoff between the quality of its own platform and the network value of the first mover’s platform. Thus, with stronger same-side network effects, the second mover could choose an earlier market entry to limit the first mover’s benefit derived from network effects, although doing so may sacrifice its own platform quality. When considering crossside network effects, the tradeoff faced by the second mover is different. If the second mover enters the market later, it could attract more users when its quality improvement rate is sufficiently high $( \lambda _ { 2 } > \lambda _ { 1 } + \alpha \beta )$ ; the cost of a later market entry is a short window of market existence. Then, with stronger cross-side network effects, because the advertisers value the platform users more, the second mover could be better off postponing its market entry if it can attract a sufficient number of additional users.

Figures 13 and 14 numerically show how the second mover’s optimal entry timing and the revenue reductions of the two platform companies change with advertiser’ fixed operating costs. In line with our analytical finding, Figure 13 shows that the optimal market entry time of the second mover is further delayed if the advertisers’ fixed operating cost increases, or if the second mover’s rate of quality improvement becomes higher. Figure 14 reveals that although both platforms’ revenues decrease with the advertisers’ fixed operating cost, the second mover’s revenue loss is less than that of the first mover. As a result, SMA is more likely to occur when the advertisers’ fixed operating cost becomes higher. There are two reasons behind this finding. First, in scenarios where the second mover gains more users but earns less revenue, i.e., no SMA, a larger operating cost c is more likely to reduce the second mover’s revenue less than the first mover’s, because the second mover has a larger user base in the majority of the demand window (as illustrated in Figure 15) to share the higher operating cost. Second, the second mover can postpone its platform launch to partially offset the negative effect of high operating cost for advertisers, whereas the first mover cannot.

## Extension Models and Summary of Findings

We also develop extension models to examine the effect of several important assumptions, namely, (1) no market expansion after the entry of the second mover, (2) lock-in effects, (3) sequence of development of the two companies, (4) correct estimate of demand window, and (5) all the users are single-homing, on the occurrences of FMA and SMA. On the basis of the first extension model, we find that SMA has a higher possibility to occur when the market expands upon the entry of the second mover. From the second extension model, we conclude that in the absence of lock-in effects, the second mover is better off postponing the launch of its platform, while still gaining a larger share of the market revenue. Hence, SMA has a higher possibility to be present. In the third extension model, the second mover is assumed to start its platform development after the first mover’s platform launch. We find that SMA has lower probability of occurring. The forth extension assumes that the two platform companies may overestimate or underestimate the length of demand window. Our analysis shows that if the two companies overestimate the length of the demand window, (E, L) or (L, E) is more likely to happen; if the two companies underestimate the length of the demand window, (E, E) and $( L , L )$ are more likely to occur. The fifth extension proves that taking into consideration of multi-homing users does not affect our main findings. Further details about these extension models are provided in Appendix B.

## Conclusion

Both first-mover advantages (FMA) and second-mover advantages (SMA) have been observed in the platform market, resulting in debates on whether a platform company should enter a market as a pioneer or a follower. Previous studies have analyzed the tradeoff between the quality and time to market of competing companies. However, no prior studies have been conducted in the context of the platform economy or have considered the unique characteristics of platform companies. In this study, we develop a two-stage gametheoretic model to evaluate the incentives of two competing platform companies regarding whether to move early or late and derive their optimal market entry timing.

## Theoretical Contributions

This study contributes to the existing literature on market entry strategy in general and market entry timing in a duopoly setting in particular. We would like to highlight three important theoretical contributions. First, we find four determinants of FMA or SMA, namely, quality improvement rate of the second mover, length of demand window, length of market growth phase of the demand window, and intensity of network effects, and derive the conditions under which FMA or SMA occurs. No prior study has identified more than one of these determinants.

Second, we analyze a complicated case in which two competing companies’ order of market entries is endogenous and show that their equilibrium order of market entries depends on the same four determinants. Moreover, we obtain conditions in which preemptive entry (i.e., both companies prefer to enter a market early), sequential entry (i.e., a company prefers to enter early, but the other prefers to enter late), or waiting entry (i.e., both companies opt to enter late) is the equilibrium. Similar results have not been found in prior literature. Hence, this work is an important contribution to the literature on market entry timing.

Third, this study focuses on the user-free-advertiser-pay platform model, which allows us to identity factors unique to platform products (such as the two sidedness of the market and the existence of cross-side network effects), and their impact on the occurrences of FMA/SMA and the equilibrium order of market entries of two competing companies. Given that previous studies have not considered such unique market features, this work helps improve our understanding of market entry strategy for the increasingly important platform market.

## Managerial Implications

Our findings provide critical guidelines to help platform companies assess the risk and benefit of entering a market as a pioneer or as a follower. First, our findings suggest that a platform company should evaluate the quality advantage it enjoys compared with its competitor at the start of a time horizon. Entering a market as a pioneer is safe only if its quality advantage is remarkable and sustainable.

Second, it is important for a platform company to evaluate its own technical capabilities regarding how fast it can improve the quality of its platform. All else being equal, a company with high technical capabilities can afford a late platform launch because it can match the performance of the first mover in a short period and outperform the leader. Generally, our findings suggest that being a second mover is a less risky option for companies without substantial advantages in platform quality or technical capabilities. Although similar observations and guidelines have been obtained from practitioners works (D'Antona 2017; Klingebiel and Joseph 2015; Seave 2014; Worstall 2012), the present study provides theoretical validation for these practical guidelines.

Third, our findings also suggest that platform companies should consider the lengths of the demand window and the market growth phase. Specifically, a short demand window or short market growth phase generally favors the first mover. Hence, risking to enter a market as a pioneer is advantageous when the demand window for a platform product is unlikely to be long or the market size will not expand extensively. Conversely, a long demand window and long market growth phase favor the second mover because of its long time frame to match or overtake the first mover with regard to platform quality and market share. This finding implies that a company can wait long in a more stable and predictable market.

Fourth, our results indicate that both same-side and cross-side network effects likely exist in a two-sided platform market, and that strong same-side network effects favor the first mover, while strong cross-side network effects favor the second mover. Hence, the relative significance of the two types of effects should be thoroughly evaluated. We believe that for platform companies, same-side network effects are typically more significant, hence increasing the lead time over the late mover can result in substantial financial returns and sustainable competitive advantage. When cross-side network effects are more significant, a company should avoid rushing into a market and instead invest in quality improvement before market entry.

Finally, by adopting the continuous-time model, we are able to derive not only the equilibrium order of market entries, but also the optimal market entry timing for a platform company under various conditions. This result provides platform companies an actionable solution for production and marketing planning.

## Future Research Directions

The present study suggests several directions for future research. First, we focus on the type of platforms with free and paid sides. A future study can examine the market entry strategy for other types of hybrid platforms, such as Sony’s Blu-Ray player and the Kindle e-reader. Second, this study uses a stylized market growth model with market growth and market maturity phases. In another possible direction, a diffusion model can be used to represent the market growth process. Finally, other economic factors, such as platform compatibility and pricing models, are not considered in the present study but can be analyzed in future work.
