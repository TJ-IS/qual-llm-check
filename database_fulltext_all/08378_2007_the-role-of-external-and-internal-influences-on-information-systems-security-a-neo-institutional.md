# The role of external and internal influences on information systems security – a neo-institutional perspective q

Qing Hu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Paul Hart <sup>a</sup>, Donna Cooke <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Technology and Operations Management, College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, Florida, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Management, International Business and Entrepreneurship, College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, Florida, United States

Available online 25 June 2007

## Abstract

This research is an attempt to better understand how external and internal organizational influ ences shape organizational actions for improving information systems security. A case study of a multi-national company is presented and then analyzed from the perspective of neo-institutional the ory. The analysis indicates that coercive, normative, and mimetic isomorphic processes were evident, although it was dificult to distinguish normative from mimetic influences. Two internal force related to work practices were identified representing resistance to initiatives to improve security: the institutionalization of work mobility and the institutionalization of eficiency outcomes expected with the adoption of company initiatives, especially those involving information technology. The interweaving of top–down and bottom–up influences resulted in an efort to reinforce, and perhaps reinstitutionalize the systems component of information security. The success of this efort appeared to hinge on top management championing information system security initiatives and propagating an awareness of the importance of information security among employees at all levels of the company. The case shows that while regulatory forces, such as the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, are powerful drivers for change, other institutional influences play significant roles in shaping the synthesis of organizational change.

## 1. Introduction

The significant advances in networking technologies, epitomized by the explosive growth of the Internet, have exacerbated the complexity and vulnerability of networks used by individuals and organizations throughout the world. The high level of connectivity, the availability of sophisticated hacking tools, the enormous growth of electronic commerce, and other factors have created unprecedented opportunities for the dark side of the technological advancement to emerge and prosper. Attacks by computer viruses and spyware and security breaches in computer systems are almost daily occurrences. These attacks have resulted in financial losses amounting to at least hundreds of millions of dollars each year to U.S. companies and other organizations including government agencies (Gorden et al., 2004, 2005; Power, 2002; Richardson, 2003), and possibly in the trillions worldwide (Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Mercuri, 2003). The rampant spread of computer viruses from one organization to another and the denial-of-service attacks often launched from thousands of computers on unsuspecting organizations highlight the challenges faced by security managers and IT professionals today.

At the same time, the recent anecdotal evidence of data theft in recent months at the U.S. Energy Department (Stout, 2006) and Ofice of Veterans Afairs (Files, 2006) have highlighted the critical role of human and organizational factors for ensuring security. These factors have also been the focus of a number of studies on information systems security (Goodhue and Straub, 1991; Hu and Dinev, 2005; Straub and Welke, 1998; Von Solms and Von Solms, 2004; Vroom and von Solms, 2004). Noting the dominance of technical and functional issues in information security research, Dhillon and Backhouse (2001) call for the use of a socio-organizational perspective for understanding information systems security issues. Socio-organizational factors are important for ensuring information systems security because information systems comprise technologies that are designed, maintained, and used by human agents in organizations to facilitate collaboration, to support information sharing and work processes, and to conduct business transactions. While considerable resources have been devoted to developing increasingly sophisticated technologies to combat threats to information systems security, it is often the organizational factors, including employees, company policies, and organizational culture, rather than or in addition to technological weaknesses that create the most significant threats to the security of the organization. On the other hand, information systems security based on an understanding of organizational factors provide a significant defense against these threats.

The above discussion leads to the following research questions: (1) what are the external and internal institutional factors salient to information systems security, and (2) how do these institutional factors shape the cognition of managers and employees about information systems security and inform their actions? Given the limited literature on the socioorganizational perspective of information systems security, we attempt to explore these questions using a case study methodology. Instead of attempting to develop a comprehensive list of the influential factors, our focus is to understand those that are evident and how they function and interact within a single organization. The case study presented provides a useful exploratory opportunity for highlighting the salient socio-organizational issues in a company attempting to strengthen its information systems security. We obtained data through interviews with business managers, security experts, IT professionals, and other employees of the organization. The next section contains a description of the theoretical perspective that provided a framework for our exploration. We then explain the case research design used and how the interview data were obtained. The final discussion sec tion of the paper is an attempt to integrate the concepts provided in the theoretical framework with the case and to assess how well the concepts explain the observations presented in the case data.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. Research background

Early studies of information systems security primarily focused on the issue of information systems user perceptions of security from a technology acceptance perspective. Good hue and Straub (1991), for instance, argue that since protective measures require significant managerial vigilance, an appropriate level of user awareness of and concern for security may be a prerequisite for adequate systems and information security in organizations. They further propose three main factors that influence user concern about security: industry susceptibility to system misuse, organizational actions against security violations, and individual awareness of potential problems. In a later study, Straub and Welke (1998) reiterate the argument that security breaches are far more frequent and damaging than is necessary because managers are not concerned with information systems security issues and are not versed in the nature of systems risk.

While information systems security concerns may have been on the backburner of business managers, they have garnered increased attention from researchers over the last decade. Numerous studies have been published that ofer both prescriptive and normative guidelines and methodologies for design, implementing, and managing secure information systems (e.g., Baskerville, 1988; Rees et al., 2003; Straub and Welke, 1998). Dhillon and Backhouse (2001) present a comprehensive review of information security research. Based on a socio-philosophical framework, security research is classified into four paradigms: functionalist, interpretive, radical humanist, and radical structuralist. Dhillon and Backhouse (2001) find that while security research was dominated by the functionalist paradigm that emphasizes formalized rule structures in designing and managing security, an increasing number of researchers have begun to explore alternative perspectives related to the interpretive, radical humanist and radical structuralist paradigms. The latter are based on various sociological and philosophical theories, including structuration theory, phenomenology, hermeneutics, and critical theory. At the same time, however, empirical investigations of information systems security are often not based on strong theoretical frameworks. A more coherent socio-organizational framework is required to explain why managers and users behave in certain ways and how their beliefs and attitudes toward information systems security are developed and, perhaps more importantly, how they can be influenced and changed.

Bjo¨rck (2004) calls for the use of neo-institutional theory in studying IT security issues in organizations. He argues that neo-institutional theory, as outlined in Meyer and Rowan (1977) and DiMaggio and Powell (1983), can be used to explain why formal security structures and actual security behavior difer and why organizations often create formal security structures without implementing them fully. Drawing from Bjo¨rck (2004) and Dhillon and Backhouse (2001), we submit that neo-institutional theory is well-suited to explain how external institutional factors influence the behavior of organization actors and thus the behavior of the organization. Institutional theory is especially salient for explaining the change of behavior at the organizational level. In the next section, we briefly describe neo-institutional theory and develop our research propositions accordingly.

## 2.2. Neo-institutional theory

In contrast to the traditional functionalist view that organizations are rational entities with structures, rules, and procedures designed to perform certain tasks eficiently, institutional theorists argue that organizations are social constructions in the sense that organizational structures are adaptive vehicles ‘‘shaped in reaction to the characteristics and commitments of participants as well as to the influences and constraints from the external environment’’ (Scott, 1987, p. 494). However, institutionalists diverge on the mechanisms and consequences of the interaction between organizations and their external environment. The works of Selznick (1949, 1957) marked the beginning of a branch of institutionalism in organizational analysis, commonly referred to as the ‘‘old institutional theory’’ (DiMaggio and Powell, 1991; Selznick, 1996), as opposed to the ‘‘new institutional theory’’ or neo-institutionalism articulated by Meyer and Rowan (1977) and DiMaggio and Powell (1983). The neo-institutional theory emerged because ‘‘Selznick’s conception remains largely definitional rather than explanatory. He defined and described the process but largely did not explicitly account for it. His treatment of institutionalization informs us that values are instilled, not how this occurs’’ (Scott, 1987, p. 495).

As a consequence, the school of neo-institutionalism emerged in the field of organizational studies marked by the publication of the seminal work of Meyer and Rowan (1977). A distinguishing aspect of neo-institutional theory is the argument that organizations are structured by phenomena in their institutional environment and gradually become isomorphic with them (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). This isomorphic process, in turn, promotes the success and survival of organizations. By incorporating externally legitimated formal structures and organizational practices, an organization may increase the commitment of internal participants and external constituents. This is because ‘‘[b]y designing formal structures and implementing organizational practices that adhere to the prescriptions of myths in the institutional environment, an organization demonstrates that it is acting on collectively valued purposes in a proper and adequate manner’’ (Meyer and Rowan, 1977, p. 349).

Two of the most important components of the neo-institutional theory are the institutionalization process and the isomorphism process. Tolbert and Zucker (1983) define ‘‘institutionalization’’ as the process through which components of formal structure become widely accepted, as both appropriate and necessary, and serve to legitimate organizations. They argue that the initial decision to adopt an innovation in a formal structure depends, to a large degree, on how the adoption will improve its internal process. In contrast, once historical continuity establishes the importance of the innovation adoption, changes in the formal structure are adopted by other members of the community because of its societal legitimacy, regardless of its value for the internal functioning of their orga nizations. When some organizational elements become institutionalized, that is, when they are widely understood to be appropriate and necessary components of eficient, rational organizations, an organization is under considerable pressure to incorporate these ele ments into its formal structures in order to maintain its legitimacy. By doing so, an organization demonstrates that it is acting on collectively valued purposes in a proper and adequate manner (Tolbert and Zucker, 1983).

If ‘‘institutionalization’’ explains how external structures and practices get traction in organizations, then, how do those institutionalized structures and practices propagate among organizations within and across industries and organizational fields? Or in other words, why do organizations tend to become more similar over time by adopting similar formal structures and organizational practices? DiMaggio and Powell (1983) argue that the concept that best captures the process of homogenization is ‘‘isomorphism’’ and tha there are three isomorphic processes. Coercive isomorphism occurs as a result of the formal and informal pressures exerted on organizations and decision makers to follow or adopt certain institutionalized rules and practices by other organizations upon which they are dependent and by cultural expectations from the society within which organizations function. Mimetic isomorphism occurs as a result of organizations imitating other organizations and is especially evident in uncertain environments because it minimizes risk. Mimetic behavior also has considerable economic benefit because it reduces the cost of finding a viable solution when organizations are faced with similar problems with ambig uous causes or unclear solutions. Normative isomorphism is the result of professionalization of the organizational actors, such as managers and administrators. When organizational actors are professionalized (i.e., they have similar formal education and training and participate in professional networks), they tend to occupy similar positions across a range of organizations and possess similar orientations and dispositions in their professional activities to such degree that they are almost interchangeable (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983).

Over the last three decades, neo-institutional theory has been widely adopted and become the dominant theoretical framework in organizational studies (Haveman, 1993; Kraatz and Zajac, 1996; Mezias, 1990; Mizruchi and Fein, 1999). In recent years, neoinstitutional theory has become one of the major theoretical frameworks for studying organizational reactions to technological innovations. For example, Tingling and Parent (2002) demonstrate that mimetic forces play an important role in the selection of IT prod ucts by managers. Teo et al. (2003) show that all three types of institutional forces are significant drivers of an interorganizational linkage technology adoption by organizations, thus providing strong support for the presence of institutional influences in the context of information technology adoption. In a more recent study, Liang et al. (2007) extended the institutional model of Teo et al. (2003) by examining the mediating role of top management between the institutional forces and enterprise system assimilation in organizations. Empirical evidence of institutional forces in other aspects of organization– technology interactions are also found in other studies (Hu and Huang, 2006; Hu and Quan, 2006).

However, some organizational scholars have been critical of the neo-institutionalists’ focus on external influence on organizational change and stability. Their critique might be summarized in the view that if external forces are such powerful influences on organizations causing homogenization, how is it that they are so diferent in many aspects? The search for variation leads proponents of this perspective to better understand the internal influences that afect change. For example, Hasselbladh and Kallinikos (2000) argue that ‘‘[n]eo-institutionalism ofers no account of the means through which a domain of action is conceived, rules of conduct, performance principles and devices of control are developed and forms of actorhood constituted. It instead concentrates, by and large, on investigating the patterns of difusion’’ (p. 701). To account for variations in organizational forms and practices in organizational fields that are normally considered as ‘‘highly instititutionalized,’’ Kraatz and Zajac (1996) test the propositions of neo-institutional theory using private liberal arts colleges facing significant pressure for ‘‘professionalization of curricula’’ during the 1970–1980s in order to survive demographic and labor market changes. The authors find that none of the seven neo-institutional theory based hypotheses are supported by the empirical evidence of organizational changes and that technical environments could have impelled many of these colleges to make significant, legitimacydecreasing changes in their structures and practices. Kraatz and Zajac (1996) conclude that ‘‘maintaining or establishing legitimacy should be viewed as only one of the several possible paths toward organizational viability, and that alignment with global and local technical environments (even at the risk of institutional illegitimacy) represents an alternative path that provides an escape from the ‘iron cage’ of a strong institutional environment’’ (p. 832).

Notwithstanding these criticisms and limitations, neo-institutional theory can provide valuable insight on the forces that drive organizational change or inertia, as has been shown in numerous studies. Furthermore, Scott (2001) recently has provided a more comprehensive perspective accounting for both external and internal processes. External forces influence difusion into the organization and internal forces influence creation from within. In his model, social institutions impose difusion from the top–down while actors from the bottom–up engage in invention and negotiation. There are a range of possible processes in both directions including socialization, sanctions, inducement, and imposition from the top–down direction and sense-making, invention, reproduction of patterns, error, defiance, and manipulation from the bottom–up direction. The evolution of institutional change can involve the interweaving of top–down and bottom–up processes. Moreover, these processes are recursive and iterative. Scott’s more comprehensive perspective of institutional forces is attractive because it accounts for the important role of disparate interests and values and at the same time the inexorable momentum to search for synthesis. In the analysis and discussion section of this paper, we rely heavily on Scott’s perspective by attempting to account for both the external forces identified by neo-institutional theory as well as the internal forces we observed in the case company.

It is our belief that the theoretical framework provided by neo-institutional theory, especially the comprehensive perspective of Scott (2001), is particularly relevant for understanding of organizational behavior in the highly turbulent and uncertain organizational environment created by significant market and technological changes in the recent years. This environment is fueled epitomized by the extensive globalization of the economy and the rapid growth of the Internet and other information technologies. In the next section, we present a narrative of the case study. Following the case study, we provide analyses and discussions of the case in which we rely heavily on Scott’s (2001) perspective by attempting to account for both the external forces identified by neo-institutional theory as well as the internal forces we observed in the case company and the consequences of the inter-play between the forces from these two sources. From this analyses, we hope to enrich neoinstitutional theory and better inform systems security practices.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Case study

The case study research design is considered most appropriate when ‘‘how’’ or ‘‘why’’ questions are being asked about a contemporary set of events, over which the investigator has little or no control’’ (Yin, 2003). Further, case studies are regarded as appropriate for studying state-of-the-art IS questions in a natural setting and when investigating an area where little or no previous research has been performed (Benbasat et al., 1987; Dube´ and Pare´, 2003; Pare´, 2004). These features of case study research design fit well with our motivation to understand how and why institutional forces might influence the behavior of mangers toward information systems security issues.

We adopted an a priori theoretical framework based on neo-institutional theory to guide the formulation of initial constructs, drafting the initial interview questions, and the actual interview processes. However, the focus is not on testing hypotheses of neoinstitutional theory but on building, adding to, and elaborating the theory in an inductive fashion. We were mindful of the recommendation that ‘‘although early identification of possible constructs or factors allows them to be explicitly measured in interviews, it is equally important to recognize that the identification of constructs is tentative in theory-building research’’ (Pare´, 2004, p. 239). For this purpose, we followed a theory-based case sampling strategy, attempting to select an example of the theoretical constructs and thereby elaborating and examining them (Pare´, 2004). The objectives are to discover and address the research questions regarding the relationships between the institutional and technical environments and organizational attitude and behavior towards information systems security as presented in Section 2. However, we were also mindful that such exploratory case studies must be conducted with an open mind for additional organizational factors that might be revealed during process that could augment the original theoretical framework in unexpected ways. As a result almost all of our interview questions were semi-structured and whenever opportunities presented themselves, open-ended follow-up questions were asked for interviewees to elaborate on more details.

## 3.2. The case company

Our case company, ABC International<sup>1</sup>, is a publicly traded company. ABC has experienced phenomenal growth over the last decade due to favorable economic conditions for its industry. In 2004 it had over 6000 employees worldwide and a market capitalization over \$2 billion. While its principal executive ofices are located outside the U.S. mainland, the company also has ofices and properties in over a dozen countries, with its data pro cessing and computer and communications network management center located in a large city in the southeastern U.S.

ABC International’s IT department consists primarily of network engineers, programmers, application specialists, and help desk support representatives. The IT staf works closely with the IT staf at various ABC International locations, creating and maintaining technologies required for the corporate ofices. ABC International houses most of the hardware and software required to run operations on site at the individual company locations. The data processing center houses the hardware that is required to network all of the individual properties, as well as perform centralized functions such as those required to operate the central reservation system that supports all locations.

## 3.3. The interviews

Interviews were conducted with selected managers and employees in various positions at ABC International. We selected subjects who were employed in various levels within the IT department, and those who had responsibility for setting IT security policies at corporate and divisional levels. Interviews were conducted between March and August in 2005 at the work sites. All interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed with the permission of the interviewees. Each interviewee was given a typed transcript of the interview to review, edit as needed, and approve. We also collected public data about ABC from the Internet and the company’s SEC filings. ABC’s top management granted us exceptional access to its managers, security oficers, IT mangers, and IT professionals. We conducted a total of seven interviews each lasting about one hour at the southeast U.S. ofice location and three more interviews in one of the largest company-owned resort located outside the U.S. mainland.

The interviews were conducted in a semi-structured format. A specific set of interview questions for each particular interviewee was prepared before the interview as a guideline. However, during the interview, if an interesting statement was made about a viewpoint or an event, the interviewee was asked to elaborate on that statement and provide more relevant information. Interview questions were developed from the research roadmap and theories as discussed in Section 2 of this paper. The objective of the interviews was to identify the primary socio-organizational factors that impacted the security of information and networks in the focal organization and to analyze the interactions among these factors using the roadmap of neo-institutional theory framework.

One of the major challenges in conducting research on information systems security is acquiring access to organizations and individuals who are willing to discuss information which is understandably sensitive. In general organizations and individuals are reluctant to talk about security issues for fear of being negatively impacted. An organization usually wants to project a positive and secure image to the outside world, regardless of its actual state of information systems security. At the same time, successful research is contingent upon acquiring accurate interview data. One condition for allowing us access to the interviewees was that we not ask questions about specific technical or managerial initiatives related to information systems security by the company. Therefore in the following case description, we only refer to these initiatives in general terms. This is an important limitation of our study. Elaboration on specific initiatives would have allowed us to undertake a deeper level of the analysis described in the discussion section for this paper. However, it is also a condition that we had to accept in order to acquire access to the interviewees.

## 4. Information systems security at ABC international

## 4.1. Sarbanes-Oxley audit and top management response

Information systems security was always the responsibility of particular individuals in the IT department at ABC International. Top level executives in the company, business managers at various levels, and even IT personnel who were not directly responsible for security all had the similar expectation that the IT personnel were doing all the right things to put failsafe security technologies and procedures into place. Thus, security issues were not among the chief concerns of other top managers of the company. This is evident in the following statements by the Chief Operating Oficer (COO):

[COO] As an operator, certainly we acknowledge that there must be security, but we get busy with other things. We hear that other people are getting certified on appli cations, and we hear that the passwords are getting changed regularly – every 90 days. Sort of it’s just out there. So embarrassingly, we realized when we went through a very sobering assessment of our compliance, and to keep the comments just to security, we are nowhere. . . . We didn’t have a [security] strategy, and therefore we didn’t have processes or procedures or policies or checklists or measurements or consequences – all of the teeth that go with it.

However, just prior to the time we began conducting the interviews, the top managers were in the middle of responding to a compliance audit required by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act enacted by the US Congress in 2002. Sarbanes-Oxley required that companies implement appropriate controls over critical business processes. The outside consulting firm hired to conduct the audit identified numerous areas that needed change. To facilitate the implementation of the recommendations of the audit, one of the consultants, who was responsible for IT compliance, was hired as the interim CIO. He had extensive experience in change management and Sarbanes-Oxley compliance. When the case study was initiated, top management had recently authorized sweeping changes in all divisions of the company, including significant information systems security initiatives.

The Sarbanes-Oxley audit was viewed by the company’s top managers as both a wake up call and an important opportunity to initiate change management. As a wake up call the audit provided compelling evidence that the company had no security strategy. The Chief Operating Oficer indicated that the company’s assessment was ‘‘13 out 100 on compliance to Sarbanes-Oxley broadly. But when it came to security, we just weren’t there. There was just no compliance whatsoever’’. Moreover, managers and employees of the company could no longer defend the view that responsibility for information security could be relegated to a few individuals in IT department. A diferent perspective would have to begin to permeate every business area of the company and every level of the com pany hierarchy. All individuals in the company would need to be regarded as important elements in a system whose involvement in ensuring information system security would be critical.

As an important opportunity to initiate change management, the company executives used the results of the Sarbanes-Oxley audit to justify organizational changes, including initiatives related to security. The audit provided the executives with leverage in the form of external pressure. The Chief Operating Oficer (COO) compared Sarbanes-Oxley to the

Y2K initiative: it is a tool or force that can be used to make organizational actors move without having to sell them on the importance of change.

[COO] This is not something we created on a wall map and said, ‘‘This is our vision.’’ We’re saying this is our company; this is critical to our company. We’re saying ‘‘SEC’’ and ‘‘external audit’’ every second breath, so don’t think there is wiggle room here because there isn’t, and everybody gets it. So that’s why I say we’re hiding behind it. We’re using it; we’re leveraging it for all of the right reasons because we will be a better business. Operating in this secure, miserable, controlled environment we’ll be more responsive. There’s no question, but we’ve got to go through this in order to get there.

The direction articulated by the COO and the initiatives executed by the top management team of the company represented a top–down approach to implementing information systems security reforms. The VP for Global Technologies indicated that the bottom–up approach of the past had been a long struggle. Procedures related to information systems security had been only informally established. But with the top–down initiatives, procedures had become formalized and there was acceptance across the entire organization; the procedures were becoming a company-wide policy not just a policy of one area of the company. The IT Operations Manager corroborated: ‘‘I would say we probably would have gotten there sooner or later, but Sarbanes-Oxley made us get there sooner’’. If the company had been hacked or if there had been an incident in which credit card numbers had been stolen, that may have been a catalyst for mandating and formalizing the security related procedures put into place. In efect, as the IT Security and Compliance Manager put it: ‘‘Sarbanes-Oxley comes along and it lit a fire’’.

## 4.2. Challenges to implementing security initiatives

While the company had clearly initiated an important set of reforms, other data were obtained during the interviews reflecting important challenges that had to be overcome for the reforms to be successful. In general these challenges had to do with making individuals at all hierarchical levels and in all business areas aware of the importance of information systems security so that they could accept the necessary consequences for how they access information and execute other information-related work routines.

One striking example of the challenge was evident in a comment by the Chief Information Oficer (CIO) about requests from executives in the company. The following description indicates how certain IT requests posed insurmountable obstacles to maintaining credible information system security.

[CIO] Business folks want access to everything all of the time; and in case that didn’t cover it, they want to leave a caveat to be able to do whatever they want wherever they want whenever they want it. So that pretty much covers anything, right? So you’ve got that pressure because that’s what they see needs to enable business. Then you’ve got the pressure – classic example – where I get a request from a senior vice president saying, ‘‘I want to have a place where we can put documents that’s available from anyplace in the world without us going through a VPN or a secure connection because we work deals all around the world and I need to have our partners get in.’’ So I’m sitting there going, ‘‘Okay, on the one side I’ve got a vector that says this is the most incredibly sensitive document there is, and on the other vector I’ve got him saying he wants to share it with anybody around the world at any time.’’ I’m going, ‘‘This is a real problem!’’

The executive’s request suggests the perception that eforts to ensure information security can be handled by IT personnel without afecting the use and availability of information. This perception reflects a lack of awareness about security software and protocols as important elements of a system in which they interact with other types of information software and protocols.

A similar perception underlined comments of other interviewees who indicated either mixed or negative attitudes toward security protocols because the protocols afected work routines. The importance of information system security seemed not to deter negative attitudes toward implementing security procedures. For example, the IT Security and Compliance Manager indicated that the overall perception of security procedures was one of burden. His eforts to introduce security procedures were viewed as ‘‘the mundane or the boring pieces or the things that might impede your progress or your eficiency.’’

While these observations reflect a lack of awareness of the importance of information systems security, conversations with other interviewees indicated that this lack of awareness was evident in more passive forms. For example, the Chief Operating Ofice (COO) said that she had not thought much about security in the past.

[COO] I’ve assumed too much. I’ve assumed that people who are on an application have the right to be there, need to be there, and that they have been certified in how to be there, and that behind it all is this mysterious way that these securities are in place and that somebody is making sure that if an employee transfers from one department to another and therefore the need for one application to another that the right security enablement or disconnect is happening, and we discovered that that’s not the case all.

Similar comments came from unexpected sources. For example, the IT Operations Manager indicated that her responsibilities were in the area of applications. Responsibility for the network infrastructure belonged to a diferent area and manager. As a consequence she and her staf really knew little about information systems security: ‘‘. . .[U]ntil I really got involved in this, change management, I was like that’s their area. That’s typically the thought process. ‘Oh, security goes over on the other side of the room.’’’

The lack of awareness demonstrated in these examples can be attributed to the division of labor in typical medium to large organizations. However, the IT Security and Compliance Manager strongly argued that internal controls were necessary to compensate for the lack of awareness about information systems security protocols. Systems administrators with the ability to make unauthorized changes in applications programs or developers attempting to fix non-routine software problems could unintentionally create opportuni ties for breaches in information systems. Without appropriate controls, the company could unknowingly become vulnerable because of well-intentioned eforts to maintain IT support.

## 4.3. Important ideas about security from external sources

Lack of awareness was an important issue at ABC International. However, at the same time, a number of those interviewed including the Chief Information Oficer, the Vice President for Global Technologies, and the Security and Compliance Manager were all participating in conferences and professional organizations and interacting with peers regarding information systems security issues. These individuals and their interactions were an important conduit for the procedures and policies related to security that were adopted by the company. The Vice President for Global Technologies indicated that the SysAdmin, Audit, Network Security Institute, or SANS, was an important source of ideas. Since he ‘‘hadn’t been handed a set of policies by our company for IT infrastructure, we always tried to develop it – not only to secure it but to stabilize it in a manner of speaking’’. The CIO was especially influenced by the IT Infrastructure Library, or ITIL, developed in the United Kingdom. Both he and the Security and Compliance manager stated that they were constantly using professional organizations and publications as their sources for ideas and practices. Moreover, they were also active participants in and contributed to professional repositories of ideas and best practices.

These external sources had an influence in shaping the information system security practices and policies of ABC International. Similarly other executives who were interviewed also participated in conferences and interacted with their professional peers in other companies. These professional conferences served to socialize the executives by exposing them to knowledge in their functional specializations (Nambisian et al., 1999). At ABC International we observed that conventional divisions of labor had historically excluded information systems security issues from being addressed by anyone other than individuals with dedicated responsibilities in that area. The specialization of the IT function at ABC International could explain why the executives in various business areas (e.g., operations management) who attended business conferences would not necessarily have had interactions about information systems security because IT was not their primary responsibility. The extent of the influence of external sources on information systems security at ABC International was selective; those managers who attended conferences in their respective functional areas were influenced by external sources addressing the issues related to their main functional concerns.

The fact that these external sources provided ideas about information systems security meant that they shaped what might be the practices and policies at ABC International. The issue of whether and how successfully they would be implemented was determined to a large extent by the willingness of the company’s top management to enforce them. This, in turn, further suggests the importance of the eforts taken by ABC International’s top managers to adopt significant security related initiatives in the wake of the Sarbanes-Oxley audit. The critical role of top management involvement in implementing security initiatives is reflected in the following statement by the IT Security and Compliance Manager when he described how the changes got started:

[ISC] It didn’t appear that we had the subject matter experts in house to deal with these things. . . . So basically, we went and used consultants, and one of those consultants now is firmly imbedded in our organization and is speaking with top management all of the time. We have what is called an IT Governance Board. He goes to the Governance Board meetings, sits on that committee. So his job and others in the team that were brought in to re-engineer IT is to communicate with upper management, and then next-level management is communicated to by them as well – vice presidents – and then management below that.

## 5. Analysis and discussion of case observations

The efects of regulatory pressure in the form of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act and the neg ative results of the audit conducted were significant. Each of the top managers interviewed highlighted the pivotal influence of this external pressure as a catalyst for instituting information systems security reforms. This pressure constitutes a coercive force in the context of the neo-institutional theory described earlier. The mandate to comply with the Sarbanes-Oxley Act was a significant formal pressure that motivated the top managers at ABC International to initiate change, as was noted by one manager: ‘‘Sarbanes-Oxley comes along and it lit a fire’’.

Other sources of external influence came from the communications and interactions with professional associations and publications. For example, the IT Security and Compli ance Manager stated that he monitors about twenty trade publications related to security, including the Gartner Group and Meta Group reports, and closely follow the activities and publications of the SANS Institute (www.sans.org) and the COBIT tool set from the Information Systems Audit and Control Association (ISACA) (www.isaca.org). The VP for Global Technologies mentioned that SANS Institute as a main source of information for industry practices. The CIO was actively involved with the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) at Carnegie Mellon University through personal connections and professional interests. He was also an active participant in a network of IT professionals who communicate and share information through a grass root organization called the IT Process Institute (www.itpi.org). These activities constitute a normative influence for those individuals most closely responsible for ensuring security at ABC International: the Chief Information Oficer, the Vice-President for Global Technologies, and the IT Security and Compliance Manager.

Our assessment is that these two sources of influence, or isomorphic forces in the context of the neo-institutional theory, had two diferent efects. The normative influences provided an important source of ideas about what security policies, practices, and technologies to adopt. Professional associations are a useful structure for introducing, selecting, and disseminating information about managerial practices. They are a forum for sharing and, to some extent, a repository for shared experience across organizational boundaries. The participation of the individuals at ABC International in professional associations (e.g., SANS, ISACA, ITPI, and SEI) in which security practices were discussed provided the opportunity for these individuals to glean from the experience of other professionals with similar responsibilities. This became an important source for framing the security practices introduced at ABC International. However, comments of the interviewees indicated that these sources of external influence were not suficient for successfully implementing information systems security throughout the company. If they had been, it is likely that the results of the audit would have been more positive with respect to security issues.

On the other hand, the coercive force of Sarbanes-Oxley had a diferent efect than the normative influence. It motivated the top managers to mandate top–down information security related changes across the entire company. The universal mandate formalized initiatives that were encouraged by other individuals whose eforts at promulgating security practices and protocols had previously been less efective. The universal mandate infused a new legitimacy to the previously advocated security practices and protocols.

While the role of coercive and normative institutional forces in influencing security initiatives at ABC International was clear, the role of mimetic influence was ambiguous. According to neo-institutional theory, mimetic influence comes from other organizations whose rules and practices are perceived as an efective response in uncertain environments or best practices. Evidence of mimetic influence would come in the form of replicating the practices and protocols of other companies. When we asked interviewees in general about whether their behavior was influenced by the actions of other companies, the general response was ‘‘not really.’’ However, the efect of mimetic forces cannot be dismissed simply based on the negative responses. Given the strong evidence of normative influences at ABC International, it may be dificult to extricate the mimetic efect from the normative one. For example, it is entirely possible that the CIO got some idea about the implementation of ITIL in another company through the connections at the ITPI network, and did not believe this was mimicking. In fact, studies that use neo-institutional theory in other areas have also encountered dificulties in diferentiating mimetic and normative influences (Mizruchi and Fein, 1999).

On the one hand, we might explain the lack of mimetic attribution to the nature of information system security issues. Mimetic behavior occurs when there are peer organizations perceived to be successful and the success is generally attributed to certain actions or behavior, resulting in the so-called ‘‘bandwagon efect’’ (Hu and Huang, 2006; Staw and Epstein, 2000; Tingling and Parent, 2002). While the popular media has been flooded with reports of security breaches, rarely is a company reported for its success in ensuring security. The very definition of ‘‘success’’ in this context means that nothing has happened!<sup>2</sup> This lack of ‘‘success stories’’ limits the bandwagon efect in the context of security.

On the other hand, would not the opportunity to hear about the security practices and protocols adopted by other companies at professional conferences and through professional associations be an important conduit for learning that could be copied by participating individuals in various companies? Since we were unable to discuss specific information systems security practices initiated during the interviews, it is dificult to provide a deeper analysis on this issue. However, based on the information we were able to obtain, we can at least conclude that the information obtained through professional conferences and associations were ‘‘in play’’ to some degree in the initiatives championed by top managers at ABC International.

Overall, the case suggests that coercive and normative external influences played important but diferent roles in information systems security initiatives undertaken in the company. While there was no reported mimicking of security practices and procedures of particular companies, the exchange of ideas through professional conferences and associations for some managers strongly suggests that useful ideas from external sources were to some degree implemented in the security initiatives at ABC. All three isomorphic processes appear evident in the company, however it is dificult to clearly distinguish normative from mimetic influences.

In addition to external institutional influences, there were internal forces that were an important part of the dynamics to introduce information systems security initiatives. Both of the forces were related to work patterns. One was represented in the process of institutionalizing mobility as a means of working and the other might be referred to as the institutionalization of eficiency outcomes expected with the adoption of company initiatives, especially initiatives that included information technology. Both of these influences were forms of resistance to change in the context of an efort to implement security practices and protocols. The institutionalization of mobility was evident in the top management’s expectation that corporate data be accessible to ‘‘anybody around the world at any time’’. This expectation is a representation of an institutionalized view that values the ability to work flexibly and conveniently enabled by information technology. However, it is an expectation that was dificult for the CIO to universally satisfy given the concurrent expectation that the information systems of the company be secure. The institutionalization of eficiency outcomes can be viewed as a consequence of both the power of technology to automate and streamline information related activities and the justification for significant investments in information technology. The evidence that this institutionalized influence had a constraining efect on eforts to improve security was seen in the comment of the IT Security and Compliance Manager that security ini tiatives were viewed as a burden. Also, the IT Security and Compliance Manager’s insis tence that security controls were necessary for application developers represented a cost to work related eficiency.

These internal institutional forces were held by managers and employees of the company. According to Scott’s (2001) model of top–down and bottom–up institutional processes, they were held by organizational actors representing a bottom–up influence. They also represent forms of resistance to implementing security initiatives. Scott (2001) has also noted that ‘‘recent and interesting work has begun to surface that emphasizes the interweaving of top–down and bottom–up processes as they combine to influence institutional phenomena’’ (p. 196). The ABC International case suggests that such an interweaving process was underway during the time we conducted the interviews. The case also suggests that there was a reinforcement and clarification of an intuitional force under way as a consequence of the interweaving.

The interweaving was evident in the attempts by the individuals who participated in professional associations to introduce security practices and protocols acquired from external sources into the company. These attempts were made on an informal, rather than formal or company-wide, basis. While the source of the ideas for the practices and protocols they introduced was external (top–down), the specific initiatives undertaken and the efort to introduce them were the agency of actors in the organization (bottom–up). Also, it should be remembered that the individual ABC International hired to be their interim CIO had extensive experience in change management and Sarbanes-Oxley compliance. He was also an advocate of the IT Infrastructure Library, or ITIL, that prescribes security practices and protocols should be pervasively integrated throughout all business functions of the organization. If he had been brought into the company at a lower level, his influence might have been directed from the bottom upwards. However, as a high level manager, he had more leverage to influence top–down initiatives, particularly those related to security.

The interaction, or dual influence, of both coercive force (i.e., the Sarbanes-Oxley audit) and the normative influences (i.e., recommended practices and protocols from external sources) in addition to the roles of various managers responsible for security, especially the interim CIO, resulted in what we view to be a reinforcement and institutionalization of information systems security. Whereas information systems security was an important concern for the company in an earlier period, the recent confluence of institutional forces appeared to have the consequence of a building efort to reinforce the systems component of information security in the following ways.

During the time we conducted the interviews, the efort to institute significant organizational change was underway. Information security no longer meant relegating security related responsibilities to certain individuals. Information systems security now meant that all employees of the company were to have certain levels of responsibility for ensuring information security; all of the employees were now viewed as elements of a system. While this shift was evident in the comments of all of the interviewees, another perspective was also advocated in the remarks made by others, especially the CIO. That is, information systems security now means that adopting certain practices, protocols, and protective software could serendipitously afect the use of other software applications and the security of entire system. For example, executives had to understand that allowing external parties to have access to company data without going through secure VPNs was not possible. Nor was it feasible for developers to change application programs without conforming to security-related controls.

In addition to the findings that in general support the propositions of neo-institutional theory, we also found that other organizational factors could interact with institutional forces in organizational change in the context of information systems security. We found that success in reinforcing the importance of the systems dimension of information security appeared to hinge on two mechanisms, the role of top management in championing the security initiatives and the ability to instill an awareness of the importance of information systems security throughout the company.

In analyzing the interviewee data, the role undertaken by the management team at ABC International was critical. Sarbanes-Oxley provided the impetus but the management team used the external pressure to leverage internal security changes throughout the entire organization. By prioritizing information systems security, the management team’s actions were consistent with the well-established prescription for ‘‘top management championship’’ (Howell and Higgins, 1990; Purvis et al., 2001). By selecting information systems security as a top priority among many of the competing issues on the agenda of their daily activities, they demonstrated ownership of the idea and vigorously promoted it within the organization (Howell and Higgins, 1990). The important role played by the management team at ABC International is consistent with the MIS literature on successful information technology implementation (Fichman, 2000). Managerial advocacy is a resilient predictor of successful information technology implementation.

This case study further suggests that the interaction between coercive external influence and internal managerial championship could be an even stronger factor and may be especially important with respect to information systems because of resistant internal institutional forces. As we noted earlier, among them are the acceptance of mobilization in work and the expected work related eficiencies to be gained by initiatives involving information technology. These are powerful institutional forces whose influence may only be tempered by a combination of external institutional force and internal managerial eforts.

Lastly, in conducting the interviews we were struck by the number of times individuals spoke of the awareness of the importance of security. For top level managers, awareness was triggered by the Sarbanes-Oxley audit. For a select number of individuals, awareness was informed in a more gradual manner through professional interactions. However, for most employees of ABC International, awareness of the importance of security would have to be informed through management championing of the information systems security initiatives. Our interviews with two employees (one IT and one business) clearly convey the attitude that ‘‘it’s not my job to care about information security.’’ The case suggests that awareness is a salient factor in security initiative acceptance. This observation is consistent with the findings of a number of researchers (Goodhue and Straub, 1991; Hu and Dinev, 2005; Straub and Welke, 1998) who have reported the significant role of awareness of security issues in influencing and shaping the attitudes and behavior of individuals. Awareness is also an important counter to the powerful influences resistant to adopting security initiatives.

## 6. Conclusions

Using a case study research design, we conducted interviews among managers and employees at a multinational company focusing on initiatives related to implementing information systems security practices and protocols. Neo-institutional theory was used as a framework for analyzing the case. We found that coercive and normative external influences played important but diferent roles in information systems security initiatives undertaken in the company. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act passed by the US Congress in 2002 was a significant coercive force that was pivotal in motivating top managers to important security initiatives throughout the company. Also, the interactions of managers in professional conferences and associations were an important external source of normative influence that informed the nature of the practices and protocols implemented. While there was no reported copying of security practices and procedures of particular companies, the distinction between normative and mimetic processes was ambiguous since the interactions with peer professionals in other organizations was a useful conduit for bringing security related ideas into the company. Thus, all three isomorphic processes appear evident in the case, however, it is dificult to clearly distinguish normative from mimetic influences.

In addition to external institutional influences, there were internal forces evident as well. These forces were related to work patterns. One was represented in the process of institutionalizing mobility as a means of working and the other was the institutionalization of eficiency outcomes expected with the adoption of company initiatives, especially initiatives that included information technology. Both these influences were forms of resistance to change in the context of an efort to implement security practices and protocols.

In our analysis of the case, we noted an interweaving of top–down and bottom–up insti tutional forces. We further found evidence that the recent confluence of institutional forces appeared to have the consequence of a building efort to reinforce the systems component of information security. All employees of the company were now to be viewed as elements of a system with shared responsibilities for ensuring security. In addition, all software and information tools were to be viewed as elements of a secure system whose access and avail ability would be subject to security controls. We found that success in reinforcing the insti tutionalization of information systems security is contingent on two factors, the role of top management in championing the security initiatives and propagating awareness about the importance of information security. From an institutional perspective the interaction, or dual influence, of external coercive force and internal management championing can be viewed as an important counter to the institutionalized influences of resistance to implementing security initiatives.

Several possibilities exist for future studies. One natural extension of this study is to use the findings of the case analysis to test an empirical model based on a survey research design. This would provide further support and greater understanding of organizational behavior towards information security and prescriptive guidance to enhancing information security. Another area would be to investigate the relationship between organizational security climate – as defined by management priority and awareness – and the behavior of individual employees. This investigation should attempt to understand what are the most significant determinants of employee behavior and attitude towards information security? Overall, research focusing on socio-organizational factors can make an important contribution to supporting eforts to improve organizational security since so much attention is given to technical solutions for problems that are often rooted in human, rather than machine - based, factors. Finally, the fact that the case was based on a company which processes a large amount of consumer financial and private data on a daily basis must be considered when attempting to generalize the findings. A natural extension will be to conduct comparative case studies in organizations in multiple industries. Such comparative studies could yield much richer insights and deeper understanding of the information systems security issues in organizations.
