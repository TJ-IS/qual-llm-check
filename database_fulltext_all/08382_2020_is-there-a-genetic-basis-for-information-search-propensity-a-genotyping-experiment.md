# IS THERE A GENETIC BASIS FOR INFORMATION SEARCH PROPENSITY? A GENOTYPING EXPERIMENT<sup>1</sup>

Glenn J. Browne and Eric A. Walden Information Systems & Quantitative Sciences, Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409 U.S.A. {glenn.browne@ttu.edu} {eric.walden@ttu.edu}

In this work we explore the question of whether there is a genetic basis for people’s different propensities to search for information. Specifically, we test whether the rs1044396 polymorphism—one variation in the cholinergic receptor nicotinic alpha 4 (CHRNA4) gene—is a reliable predictor of propensity to search for information. A sample of 205 subjects performed an information search task and then donated genetic material, which was analyzed. Using these data we found that the rs1044396 polymorphism is associated with both search duration and the amount of information searched, contributing a genetic explanation for a portion of propensity to search for information. People with the C:C version of rs1044396 gathered 25% more information but spent 13% less time on each piece of information than people with T-alleles before making a decision. Thus, the amount of information that people gather and the temporal cost of gathering that informa tion have a strong genetic component, which has implications for internet search strategies, information use, and information systems design.

Keywords: NeuroIS, information search, information systems, genetics, systems design, decision making, human genetic markers, rs1044396 polymorphism, cholinergic receptor nicotinic alpha 4, internet addiction

## Introduction

Information systems are built to facilitate decision makers search for information, either by performing the search or by displaying a set of information or both (Davis 1982). Thus, research on information search in the IS field has understandably been significant (e.g., Abrahams and Barkhi 2013; Browne et al. 2007; Davis 1982; Ghose et al. 2012; Ho et al. 2011; Hong et al. 2004). The world wide web and the vast infrastructure and supporting tools that have developed around it have provided methods for information search that were unimaginable even a generation ago. These methods have led to an increasing demand for personalized search capabilities by consumers around the world and the devolution of decision-making power and responsibility to individual people in an increasing number of countries. Hundreds of millions of people search for information billions of times a day using the world wide web.<sup>2</sup> The web has become the most fundamental and ubiquitous information technology (IT) artifact and search is the most common activity in which users of this artifact engage (Chriss 2014; Cottrill Research 2013).

With this vast amount of search it is worthwhile to recognize that there are individual differences in the ways in which people search for information online. Information systems (IS) managers and designers strive to design systems to help users search for, assimilate, and use information to make deci sions. However, users are not uniform in their willingness and ability to perform these activities, which is why the study of individual differences in IS behaviors is one of the largest and most enduring research streams in the IS literature (Agarwal and Prasad 1999; Benbasat and Schroeder 1977;

Compeau and Higgins 1995; Couger 1986; Culnan 1983; Gefen and Straub 1997; McElroy et al. 2007; Riedl, Hubert, and Kenning 2010; Thatcher and Perrewe 2002; Thong et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 2017; Zmud 1979). Understanding individual differences in information search, assimilation, and use can facilitate the management and design of information systems by informing options for customization and personalization or intelligent default choices for individuals or groups.

One promising new tool for understanding IT artifact usage is NeuroIS (Dimoka et al. 2011; Dimoka et al. 2012; Riedl et al. 2010). While NeuroIS includes many different methods, ranging from fMRI (Walden et al. 2018) to EEG (Léger, Riedl, and vom Brocke 2014) to eye tracking (Léger et al. 2014), one that has not been widely used, but has great potential, is genetic methods. Recent research (e.g., Plomin 2018a) indicates that “about 50% of [individual] differences in such psychological traits as personality, mental health and illness, and cognitive ability and disability” (Plomin 2018b, p. C1) are determined by DNA rather than the environment. This research has put a new perspective on the centuries-old “nature vs. nurture” debate (the influence of genetics is greater than generally believed) and emphasizes the importance of studying genetic factors in all areas of human behavior.

## As Riedl and Léger (2016) observe,

We foresee that IS scholars will begin using tools from genetics to reveal genetic foundations of human interaction with information and communication technologies. To investigate whether humans are endowed with genetic variations that could account for individual differences in IS behaviors, research could apply a quantitative method based on an experimental task. Moreover, we even foresee molecular genetic studies in IS research, in which DNA sequences are specified and related to specific IS constructs. In particular, we believe that in future studies it will be possible to develop insight into the relationship between single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and IS behaviors (p. 68).

This statement reflects the goal of this paper, to link a SNP to an IS behavior (information search). This is now a viable type of NeuroIS research because of the dramatic progress that has been made in genetic tools in the past few decades.

We first provide important background on information search and then build a theoretical argument for why a particular genetic polymorphism (termed rs1044396, and discussed below) has the potential to cause individual differences in information search behavior. Our specific research question is: Does the rs1044396 polymorphism have an impact on the quantity of online information search?

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We begin with background material on information search and genetics and on using genotyping for IS-relevant behaviors. We then provide our theoretical argument linking search to the rs1044396 polymorphism. We follow with background on individual differences and then present our hypotheses. We then describe our method and the results of our experiment. We conclude with a discussion of our results and directions for future research.

## Background

## Information Search

Search is a fundamental human activity and therefore has received widespread attention in the behavioral, social, and biological sciences. Information technology is now the primary enabler of information search, and thus there has been considerable research on information search in the IS field (e.g., Abrahams and Barkhi 2013; Browne et al. 2007; Davis 1982; Ghose et al. 2012; Ho et al. 2011; Hong et al. 2004). The internet and the world wide web have provided access to nearly limitless information for most important decisions; the world wide web is the most widely used information system on earth, and its primary functionality is to facilitate search by users.<sup>3</sup> In addition, advances in data science and business analytics techniques are producing data in enormous quantities that can be utilized if the appropriate (search) tools are available. As noted by Pirolli (2007, p. 3), “Modern mankind forages in a world awash in information, of our own creation.” Various studies and industry surveys have shown that knowledge workers spend 20% to 30% of their time searching for information (Bughin et al. 2012; bwdit.com 2017). CIO Magazine reported in 2017 that, for the purpose of making knowledge workers more efficient, “search is everything,” and reported a study that found that “88% of effective employee work begins with searching for information” (Bourque 2017). This search is often fruitless, however. According to a 2018 industry study, much of the time spent searching is ineffective and wasteful (Di Noi 2018). The fact that information search is so fundamental to human existence and yet so often unproductive motivates research that provides an improved understanding of the phenomenon.

Information search in information systems research has been pursued in various contexts, including both offline and online contexts, such as information requirements determination, decision support systems, and the world wide web (Abrahams and Barkhi 2013; Browne et al. 2007; Ho et al. 2011). In the context of online information search, for example, Hong et al. (2004) investigated the impact of flash animation, finding that response time, recall of brand names, and focused attention were all enhanced by the use of flash. Kuruzovich et al. (2008) investigated online information search for price and product information in auto retailing. Ho et al. (2011) investigated online consumer information search in the context of adaptive personalized recommendation agents and the timing of those recommendations in sequential search problems. Numerous other search contexts in IT have also been investigated, such as the economics of search.

## Theory Development <sup>4</sup>

As noted, the internet has dramatically changed people’s access to information, and the ease of access and (for practical purposes) the unlimited amount of information available has increased the amount of search performed (Sparrow et al.

2011; Wang et al. 2016). Although in many (and arguably most) ways the access to information has been highly beneficial to consumers and other types of users, in some people online search has become an uncontrollable behavior. For such individuals, this behavior in its extreme form can be characterized as a type of internet addiction (Panksepp 2004; Wang et al. 2016; see also Yadava 2016; Yoffe 2009; for internet addiction generally, see, Montag and Reuter 2017; Young 2017), sometimes viewed more broadly as technology addiction (Turel et al. 2011).<sup>5</sup> Internet addiction has been defined as “a maladaptive use of the internet” (Ko et al. 2010, p. 121) and consists of various types of heavy internet engagement. Such engagement can include online gaming (Dong and Potenza 2014) or gambling disorder, but also encompasses a variety of search behaviors such as pathological search (Davis 2001) for pornography, social media, news, or other information search such as home remodeling, cooking, or pictures of cats. Thus, while internet addiction is an ill-defined and multifaceted disorder, one far-reaching type is pathological information search (Davis 2001; Young 1999). That internet addiction is a problem is difficult to dispute; depending on the exact definition used, as much as 27% of the world population may be afflicted (Vink et al. 2016). Contexts such as online gambling, online gaming, and participation in online auctions have been studied (Jeong et al. 2017; Ko et al. 2010; Turel et al. 2011), as well as emailing/texting (Young 2009) and Facebook use (Andreassen et al. 2012; Biolcati et al. 2018). In the context of internet search, Wang et al. (2016, p. 2) have noted,

Once the Internet is out of reach and when they cannot immediately find out what they want, people would show withdrawal-like symptoms, such as irritability, tension, and depression [Block 2008]. Those symptoms are largely overlapping with withdrawal symptoms of substance addictions and pathological Internet use [Davis 2001; Dong and Potenza 2014; Young 1999].

Panksepp (2004) notes that “seeking” (i.e., search) behavior is one of the primary forces of living creatures and causes the release of dopamine in humans and animals. With seeking behavior, “each stimulation evoke(s) a reinvigorated search strategy” (Panksepp 2004, p. 151), leading to more search. Our seeking circuits cause us to search through the marketplace of ideas and motivate us to navigate through the world around us (Yoffe 2009). As noted by Yoffe,

All our electronic communication devices—e-mail, Facebook feeds, texts, Twitter—are feeding the same drive as our searches. Since we’re restless, easily bored creatures, our gadgets give us in abundance qualities the seeking/wanting system finds particularly exciting. Novelty is one. Panksepp says the dopamine system is activated by finding something unexpected or by the anticipation of something new. If the rewards come unpredictably—as email, texts, updates do—we get even more carried away. … “The dopamine system does not have satiety built into it,” [University of Michigan psychologist Kent] Berridge explains. “And under certain conditions it can lead us to irrational wants, excessive wants we’d be better off without.” So we find ourselves letting one Google search lead to another, while often feeling the information is not vital and knowing we should stop. “As long as you sit there, the consumption renews the appetite,” he explains (2009, pp. 2-3).

As these researchers and commentators cogently argue, extreme and uncontrolled information search is one type of internet addiction. The rs1044396 polymorphism has been shown to be associated with internet addiction (Montag et al. 2012) and internet gaming disorder (Jeong et al. 2017) and the CHRNA4 gene is associated with pathological search behavior (Jeong et al. 2017; Montag et al. 2012). Additionally, in the Jeong et al. paper noted above, 618 different SNPs were examined and only the rs1044396 SNP was significantly related to internet gaming disorder. Because researchers have investigated internet addiction in connection with the rs1044396 polymorphism, we study the same polymorphism to investigate information search. No research has investigated this previously. We look at information search specifically, rather than other forms of internet addiction such as online gaming, because, as noted earlier, search is the most common activity in which people engage using the web.

An additional line of evidence suggests that the rs1044396 polymorphism might affect information search behavior. There is important research on the effects of the rs1044396 polymorphism on attention. Attention is an important part of the information search process because it is attention that must be allocated to direct the process and to evaluate the information found (Simon 1996). It is known that the cholinergic system (i.e., the system that uses acetylcholine as a signaling neurotransmitter, and the type of receptor that the CHRNA4 gene contains the instructions to build) has impacts on attention (Bentley et al. 2011; Demeter and Sarter 2013). Specifically, the rs1044396 polymorphism has been shown to have effects on attention. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging, it has been shown that rs1044396 T-allele (that is, T:T or T:C) carriers have significantly greater activation in the anterior cingulate cortex and supplementary motor area, two brain regions associated with attention (Winterer et al. 2007). Further research found that C:C carriers reacted more strongly to stimuli outside the focus of attention in brain regions involved with detecting violations of expectations, while T:T carriers reacted to similar stimuli more strongly in areas responsible for reorienting attention (Gießing et al. 2012). Attention is an important variable because the ability to sustain attention in a search task is likely to have effects on the amount of search a user performs. Thus, the evidence concerning the effects of rs1044396 on attention seems to suggest that it is a candidate for affecting the quantity of information a user seeks before making a decision.

In sum, in the present research we are interested in finding a genetic correlate for the propensity to search for information. Based on the past research discussed above, an extreme version of information search is a type of internet addiction, and the rs1044396 polymorphism is related to internet addiction and its gene is related to pathological search behavior. Thus, this polymorphism represents an important starting point for our investigation.

## Individual Differences

Mason and Mitroff famously described an information system as

a PERSON of a certain PSYCHOLOGICAL TYPE who faces a PROBLEM within some ORGANIZA-TIONAL CONTEXT for which he needs EVI-DENCE to arrive at a solution, where the evidence is made available through some MODE OF PRE-SENTATION (1973, p. 475).

The explicit recognition of a person of a certain psychological type by early IS researchers was the basis of a stream of research on individual differences in the use of information systems (Bariff and Lusk 1977; Benbasat and Taylor 1978; Dickson et al. 1977; Henneman and Rouse 1984; Lusk and Kersnick 1979; Zmud 1979) that has continued into the present (Agarwal and Prasad 1999; Liu et al. 2011; Luse et al. 2013; Thatcher and Perrewe 2002; Thong et al. 2002). This stream of research has been so prodigious and enduring because “the empirical literature regarding the influence of individual differences upon MIS success indicates rather clearly that individual differences do exert a major force in determining MIS success” (Zmud 1979, p. 975).

There are individual differences in the propensity to search for information. For a given task the amount of information sought will depend not only on task characteristics but on characteristics of the individual performing the task (Browne et al. 2007). We further conjecture that at least some of this propensity will result from genetic traits, in the same way that some of the propensity to consume food results from genetic traits (Garlapow et al. 2017).

This work is an addition to the individual differences literature. Following Riedl and Léger (2016) who, as noted earlier, argue that genetics can be used to identify individual differences in IS behaviors, we propose that individual genetic differences lead to differences in the use of those IT artifacts that are focused on providing information to users. Specifically, we focus on IT artifacts that provide information in the form of a list of attributes. We theorize that genetic differences lead to a change in the amount of information users seek from such IT artifacts. This has potentially far reaching implications for information systems success because the amount of information users seek is a primary determinant of both the cost and quality of a decision. Consuming information requires time and cognitive resources (Simon 1960, 1996); thus, the cost of using a system is largely measured in terms of time and effort utilized (Jarvenpaa 1989; Todd and Benbasat 1992). The quantity of information sought also determines the systematic upper bound on the quality of a decision. The systematic upper bound is controllable through good decision-making practices. One could accidently make a good decision based on very little information, but to make systematically good decisions requires an appropriate amount of information.

The argument we put forth is that the rs1044396 polymorphism affects information search behavior. Thus, it constitutes an individual difference that has an effect on an ISrelevant behavior. It is important to make clear that we do not theorize about the biochemical pathways that lead to this effect. Science has not progressed to that level of theorizing any more than it is ready to explain the biochemical pathways that lead from perceptions of usefulness to intentions to use. Moreover, we do not theorize about the cognitive mechanisms involved. Again, science is not to the point at which the feelings, attitudes, or perceptions arising from having different versions of the rs1044396 polymorphism lead to intentions, beliefs, or other behaviors.

## Hypotheses

As prior literature has found that the C:C genotype is more likely to occur in individuals identified as having internet addiction (of which extreme search behavior is one type), all of our hypotheses are written based on C:C genotypes. The literature reviewed above has identified a SNP that has been implicated in internet addiction, which is closely related to pathological search behavior. Specifically, C:C genotypes have been found to be more prone to internet addiction. As the internet is the largest repository of information in the world, some aspects of internet addiction result from too much search. If too much search is associated with the C:C genotype then the total quantity of search might also be associated with the C:C genotype.

Prior literature has also implicated this SNP in attention reorienting, which is a necessary condition for ending search. Specifically C:C genotypes have relative deficits in attention reorienting. If C:C genotypes show deficits in the condition needed for ending search, it is reasonable to hypothesize that they will require more effort to end search and switch attention to the next task. Thus, we theorize that this particular SNP could have important impacts on information search. To measure the potential impact, we propose to measure the quantity of information search in terms of both time spent and number of pieces of information consumed.

We offer three different operationalizations of the same hypothesis, as we have three measures of information search. One measure is the number of product attributes for which users search. Another measure is the amount of time spent on each attribute. The third is a combination of these two, which is the total time spent searching. These are three different measures, each with its own implications. In theory, the number of product attributes searched is a pure measure of the quantity of information a decision maker possesses for making a decision. However, in practice, a decision maker may ignore, forget, or otherwise incompletely process each product attribute, so the number of attributes forms an upper bound on the amount of information available to the decision maker. The amount of time spent on each attribute is a measure of how much effort is exerted to process each piece of information. Finally, the total amount of time spent in search is a measure of the overall cost of information gathering. Time is a finite resource, and the more of it that is used in information gathering, the less of it is available for all other activities. Thus, while these are all measures of the same construct, they measure different aspects of the construct and have different implications. All hypotheses are stated in the alternative form.

H1a: C:C homozygotes of the rs1044396 polymorphism will search through more product attributes than Tallele carriers.

H1b: C:C homozygotes of the rs1044396 polymorphism will spend more total time searching product attributes than T-allele carriers.

H1c: C:C homozygotes of the rs1044396 polymorphism will spend more time on each attribute than T-allele carriers.

## Method

## Subjects

Subjects were 205 business school students from a large research university in the United States. They received course credit for participating in the research. Males made up 61.5% of the sample. Among our subjects, 47 were C:C homozygotes (57.4% male), 101 were T:C heterozygotes (66.3% male), and the remaining 57 were T:T homozygotes (56.1% male). We tested for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using a standard $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test; the p-value was 0.86, and thus we failed to reject the null hypothesis that our sample was in Hardy– Weinberg equilibrium (see Appendix F for more details). We did not collect ethnic origin data. However, the demographic makeup of the student body is predominantly of European descent (62% white) with a large Hispanic population (19%). We compared our genotype distribution to the 100Genome project’s measured distribution of rs1044396. We used both the EUR (European) sample and the AMR (American) sample. Neither was significantly different from our observed population. These tests are shown in Appendix F. As this was a student population, 187 were 18–24 years old, 13 were 25–30 years old, and 5 were over 30 years old.

## Task

One of the most common ways for information systems to display information to users is as a list of attributes of a class. For example, an online display for a product such as a refrigerator might list interior capacity, number of bins, number of shelves, number of cooling zones, colors available, etc. The user is expected to read each attribute and the value and then use that information. The specific information system we examine here is the product attribute page from BestBuy.com. Figure 1 presents a screenshot of such a page for a television.

Subjects in our study were given sets of three products in four product categories and asked to search for information from the BestBuy website. Product attributes were presented one at a time to simulate reading down the list. A local script was used to manage the timing on the local computer to carefully control for internet latency. As detailed below, subjects spent, on average, about 2 seconds reading each attribute, and they read an average of 18 attributes. The information was presented to them in the same order BestBuy presented it with the exception of the price, which was presented later rather than first. There are three reasons we did not present the information in a random order. First, BestBuy presented the information in the same order for each product, and that is true of most attribute listings. In fact, we are aware of no information system that presents product attributes in a random order. Second, we wanted different products to have the same information so that subjects were not making decisions based on some information about product 1 and a different set of information about product 2. Third, we wanted to be able to compare the information content between subjects. If subject A requested 16 pieces of information before stopping, and subject B collected the exact same 16 pieces of information but continued to collect further pieces, then we know subject B requested more information than subject A; however, we could not make that comparison if subjects A and B were given differently ordered information sets.

After each attribute the subjects had the choice to read the next attribute or to indicate that they had enough information about this product. When a subject decided he had enough information about the product to make a (subsequent) choice, he was given the list of product attributes of the second product within the class, and finally the third product, and then chose which of the three products he thought would be best for him. The product categories were single lens reflex cameras, televisions, portable GPS systems, and refrigerators. The products, in addition to their attributes, were shown to each subject in the same sequential order. For each product, attributes (e.g., price) were presented one at a time and it was not possible to go back and reexamine the attributes. The subjects had unlimited time to read each single piece of information. With each piece of information, they were offered two choices: they could choose to receive additional pieces of information or they could choose to stop receiving information. If they chose to gather more information, the next (different) attribute was shown to them. As noted, the product attributes were downloaded from the product detail list at BestBuy.com for these specific products. So, in essence, the subjects were searching for information about three products in each of four product categories from BestBuy.com, using the same information they would have received had they used the BestBuy comparison feature. Nonetheless, we do note that this is not precisely identical to searching on the BestBuy.com website, which is a limitation of this work.

As an example, we can consider a hypothetical trial for cameras. The first piece of information was: “Brand: Nikon.” The subjects had the choice of seeing more information or stopping. If they selected “More,” the next attribute presented was “Vista Compatible: Yes,” as shown in Figure 2. The next piece of information was “Price: \$699.99.” Subjects continued to view attributes until they signaled that they had enough information about that product to make their subsequent decision by selecting “Stop.” When they chose to stop, the next camera was shown to them with the same characteristics in the same order (i.e., the first attribute was “Brand,” which was “Canon” for camera #2). After looking at the three cameras they were asked which they would choose. This series of steps proceeded for all the products in all the categories.

## Summary of SNP Analysis

The details of the genetic analysis are included in Appendix D. However, we provide a simple basic summary here. First, we collected a cheek swab from each subject. We then sent the swabs in a cooler of dry ice to a DNA testing company. The DNA testing company charged \$10 per person to process the DNA and sent us a spreadsheet with each subject’s genotype. ## Results

In general, research on rs1044396 has compared T-allele carriers to the C:C homozygotes. It is generally considered that a T-allele, whether as part of a T:T homozygote or as part of a T:C heterozygote, has protection or immunity from addiction or depression that C:C homozygotes do not have (Markett et al. 2011). Thus, since analyses tend to look at Tallele carriers compared to C:C homozygotes, we follow the prior literature in using this comparison in our analysis.

The three dependent variables we examine are the average number of screens searched, the total amount of time spent searching screens, and the average time spent searching per screen. For our analysis we use log transformations because all of the distributions are skewed and logarithmic transformations convert them to normality. However, we present the summary statistics for each variable below in whole units, as they are more comprehensible. The statistics are shown in Table 1.

The results suggest, as we hypothesized above, that the C:C homozygotes consumed more information as measured by the number of information screens they viewed before abandoning search. These results support H1a. Specifically, they searched almost four more screens (3.97), which is 24.31% more information searched. The total time the C:C homozygotes spent searching was also significant (14.31% greater). These results provide support for H1b. However, the surprising outcome is that C:C homozygotes spent 13.14% less time on each screen than the T-allele carriers and the effect was significant. Thus, H1c is not supported and the directionality of the difference is not as predicted. This finding has important implications that will be discussed at length below. The table also shows that the same pattern of results holds when using logarithmic scales.

Next, we tested the robustness of these findings using a general linear model to control for potential confounds. The pattern of results was the same. The first factor we controlled for is the sex of the subject. Sex is an important control variable because it is a major genetic factor. It is estimated that nearly 500 genes show sex differences in brain expression (Trabzuni et al. 2013). Thus, sex represents an important potential confound. We controlled for sex by adding a dummy variable for sex and an interaction between sex and genotype. As the interaction term is not significant, we also present a model with no interaction.

As the Table 2 shows, the same basic pattern holds again. The effects of genotype on number of screens searched and on average time spent on each screen are significant after controlling for sex and a genotype-sex interaction, as well as when controlling for sex, age, years of internet experience, and frequency of internet use. The effect of genotype on total search time is also significant after controlling for sex and is marginally significant when including an interaction and when controlling for sex, age, years of internet experience, and frequency of internet use. As an additional robustness check, we analyzed results using all three levels of genotype,

C:C, C:T, and T:T, after controlling for sex, and found the same pattern of results.

Overall, the results are consistent across multiple model specifications. T-allele carriers search fewer screens but take longer to examine each screen. The net effect is shorter search times for T-allele carriers. Importantly, the genotype effects are clear and present even after controlling for sex and age and other variables. The results clearly show there is a genetic component to how people use information systems to search for information.

## Discussion

## Implications for Theory

Our results show that the rs1044396 polymorphism on the CHRNA4 gene predicts both the number of information items subjects view and the time subjects spend searching for information before making a decision. This suggests there is a genetic basis for propensity to search for information online. The magnitude of the differences in search behavior between the two gene variants is profound. People with C:C genes searched 25% more than people with T-alleles, and they required 14% more time to make decisions. In other words, C:C homozygotes, if given the opportunity, request 25% more information before making a decision. To put the results into perspective, if people were 25% taller, the average U.S. adult man would be just over 7 feet tall! This is an exceptionally large difference in the propensity to search for information.<sup>6</sup>

One surprising finding was that while C:C homozygotes searched more screens, they spent less time on each screen, thereby consuming information at a greater rate than T-allele carriers. There are three possible explanations for this finding. First, C:C homozygotes may spend less time on each screen because they are more likely to react to stimuli outside the focus of attention, as noted in our theory development section above (Gießing et al. 2012). This could cause their attention to shift more quickly or to drift. Second, it is known that the CHRNA4 gene modulates response times (Schneider et al. 2015), so it could be the case that this gene somehow makes the process of evaluating information faster. The third explanation comes from studies on food appetite, in which obese people tend to consume more calories in a shorter period of time (Andrade et al. 2008; Drabman et al. 1977; Gaul et al. 1975). Thus, it is possible that the rs1044396 polymorphism leads to a greater appetite for information that is satisfied by faster consumption of information. Researchers have already used food foraging theory to explain information foraging behavior (Pirolli and Card 1999; Stephens and Krebs 1986). Perhaps the literature on obesity and food consumption in general can provide important theoretical insights into people’s information search behavior (for the analogy between food appetite and information appetite, see Brown 2012; Johnson 2012).

The answer to our specific research question, “Does the rs1044396 polymorphism have an impact on the quantity of online information search?” seems to be “yes.” The implication from this work is that there is a group of people who search for more pieces of information but spend less time on each piece, and another group that spends more time on each individual piece of information but prefers fewer pieces of information overall. As far as we know this is a unique discovery and worthy of further research. However, equally important are the implications for our overall research question, “How might advances in genetics offer additional insights into individual differences in people’s willingness and ability to search for, assimilate, and use online information?” By finding that one specific polymorphism has one specific effect we have offered a proof of concept for future work. At a bare minimum SNP analysis seems to be a promising venue for study. But there are additional possibilities in RNA analysis, epigenetics, and full genome sequencing. We have long recognized that there are individual differences in the way people use technology, and genetic analysis can potentially provide more reliable data than race, culture, or sex data.

Our findings contribute to theory concerning information search and the CHRNA4 gene and the rs1044396 polymorphism. As noted, the gene and polymorphism have been found to be associated with internet addiction and pathological search behavior. However, to this point no work has investigated more general internet search behavior such as product search in connection with this gene or polymorphism. Our results point to a more generalized effect of the polymorphism on search behavior. This is an important finding and opens new avenues for research concerning the rs1044396 polymorphism and search as a fundamental aspect of the human experience, including consumer and social activities involving IT artifacts during internet search.

The rs1044396 polymorphism is part of a particular chemical pathway. For information search, understanding the chemical pathway can be quite useful for theory and practice. First, it gives us a base to start theorizing about non-chemical interventions. For example, CHRNA4 is associated with both attention and addiction, so an important question is which is more influential in the case of information search. More importantly, as genotyping becomes more accessible, the amount we know about a particular genotype grows and thus our basis for theorizing grows. For instance, in the time since we submitted the first draft of this work, it was discovered that C:T heterozygotes of the rs1044396 polymorphism performed better on a variety of cognitive tasks than either the T:T or C:C homozygotes (Smucny 2018). This immediately leads us to theorize that there may be an inverted U-shaped relation between rs1044396 and optimal search behavior. Whether that is true is an empirical question, but it is a natural theory to test given this new information.

Moreover, as we have estimates of the relative distribution of the rs1044396 polymorphism in different populations, we can begin to theorize about how many people might over- or under-search and the implications of such search behavior in terms of time, costs, and decision-making accuracy and effectiveness (Browne and Pitts 2004; Connolly and Thorn 1987). We can also hypothesize about whether it is worthwhile to build systems to address the potential issues involved in search behavior generally and pathological search behavior specifically. These are theories that one might propose and test. We use them as examples of how knowing the effects of a genotype on an IS behavior, in this case information search, could help to develop theory and practice. Thus, a major contribution of this work is in the linking of the genotype to the IS relevant behavior because it gives researchers an entirely new basis from which to develop theories of information search.

Another theoretical contribution is related to attention. We found that T-allele carriers spent more time on each screen than C:C homozygotes, implying that they were able to sustain attention on the stimulus material for a longer period of time. These results are consistent with the greater activation in attention-related areas of the brain in fMRI studies for rs1044396 T-allele carriers (Winterer et al. 2007) and thus provide converging evidence for an attention advantage for Tallele carriers.

## Implications for Practice

These findings have important implications for IT managers and users. For IT managers, one consideration is the people they manage; all of these people have some genotype and are probably wired differently. This is one of the main points of Dalio’s (2017) best-selling work, and something that managers often fail to address and that science is often reluctant to discuss. As Dalio puts it,

I discovered that though it is obvious to all of us that we are born with different strengths and weaknesses in areas such as common sense, creativity, memory, synthesis, attention to detail, and so forth, examining these differences objectively makes even most scientists uncomfortable. But that doesn’t make it any less necessary (p. 205).

Understanding these differences is also especially important for IT managers who are specifically charged with designing artifacts to help users search for and process information effectively. While IT managers will probably not genotype users, there are millions of users already genotyped and the data are available.<sup>7</sup> Thus, IT managers can often access reliable genetic information on the populations for which they are designing systems. It would be valuable for an IT manager to know what percent of a target population is likely to desire 25% more information.

There are at least two ways that the designers of customized interfaces can make use of the rapidly growing stores of genomic data that are being collected. The first and most straightforward is simply to link genomic data to a person. This is the business model of Vinome, Startline, Slumbertype, Fitness Diet Pro, Muscle Builder, and others who collect customer DNA and then make recommendations on wine, nutrition, and exercise. These firms partner with Helix to perform DNA testing and store DNA data. This method of using genomic data is in its infancy, but as the cost of genotyping drops and the number of people who have been genotyped climbs into the hundreds of millions and billions, this business proposition will become much more viable.

A second method is possible because the genomic data that are currently available represent one, if not the largest, store of potentially actionable psychological data in the world. By linking genomic data to geographical areas companies can predict the relative frequency of people with specific behavioral dispositions in an area. It need not be the case that every person with genotype A exhibits behavior B. It is sufficient that genotype A contributes to behavior B in an appreciable manner. Websites currently can use the type of device with which a person logs on (e.g., cell phone or laptop) to customize the content and interface. This is not because one type of device user automatically wants a specific piece of content or a feature. Rather, it is because designers can forecast that the users of a specific type of device are more likely to want a certain type of content than other types of users. Designers can accomplish the same type of customization using general genotype data (that is, data in populations or societies). Just as IS designers might believe that users in Arizona are more likely to want sunblock advertisements than users in Minnesota, they might believe that populations with a larger proportion of colorblind people will prefer system interfaces that are blue rather than red or green.

Users of information technology have a vested interest in understanding the genetic components of information search. As we have mentioned, millions of users have already been genotyped and that number is expected to reach billions in less than a decade. Most famously, Sergey Brin, co-founder of Google, used 23andMe to discover a mutation in his LRRK2 gene that predisposes him toward developing Parkinson’s disease. Brin notes on his blog,<sup>8</sup> “I know early in my life something I am substantially predisposed to. I now have the opportunity to adjust my life to reduce those odds (e.g., there is evidence that exercise may be protective against Parkinson’s).” Thus, users will know features of their genotype and can make decisions based on this information. It is already the case that companies like Helix and 23andMe send users research updates based on their genotypes (see, for example, https://blog.23andme.com/health-traits/23andmeadds-new-genetic-health-risk-reports/). This manuscript’s findings could be included in a report by 23andMe to help users better understand their own information search propensities. T-allele carriers might need to consider collecting more information before making a decision and C:C homozygotes might need to spend more time ruminating before making a decision. Both of these types are likely to find that their internet search behaviors are explained in some part by genetic propensities.

One important consideration for the use of genetic traits is ethics. In principle, information providers can use genetic information to provide better services to information users. However, in practice, better could mean better for the provider rather than better for the user. A direct implication from this work is that providers could potentially target people who are particularly vulnerable to over-consumption of information. This also raises some privacy concerns as curators of genetic data can potentially sell the information they possess to vendors who also might use it for their own ends rather than to benefit the user or customer.

## Limitations

As with most research, replication of the present study would be useful to rule out alternative explanations for our findings. We controlled for sex, age, years of internet experience, and frequency of internet use, but other variables such as computer self-efficacy, IQ, or online shopping experience could potentially affect search behaviors. Future research should consider potential alternative explanations.

One limitation of the study is the laboratory nature of the task. While the information for the product search came from a real consumer information system and was presented in (mostly) the same order the system presented it, we only allowed subjects to view one piece of information at a time. Effectively, that is all they could process at one time with the real system, but with the real system they could move between pieces of information with eye movements rather than clicks. In a real world setting people could potentially move through the list in a non-sequential order. While for experimental purposes it was necessary to have subjects move through the same information in the same order, that is not a constraint most systems would apply, and thus research on unconstrained search should also be undertaken to assess the effects of rs1044396.

We should also note that in some contexts people can look back at information, whereas in our study we did not permit them to go backward and reexamine information. The reason for this constraint in our study is that it would have obscured what we were attempting to measure. We gave people one piece of information at a time to measure how many pieces they consumed and how long they took to consume them. Moving between an attribute in a forward then backward fashion would have added a confound as subjects tried to remember where in the list the target information was and then remember where they were in the list. Moreover, it would be unclear whether they were consuming all of the intervening information for decision making about the product or consuming it as a guide to where they were in the list. Perhaps eye tracking could help with such an issue, but it could be problematic if we accept that people could potentially skip around and not read both the attribute name and the attribute value. That is, in Figure 1, someone could read “Screen Size” and then not read “48.5 Inches” or vice versa.

We note also a few caveats concerning genetic research. The first is that genetic impacts generally have a long causal chain to phonotypical (i.e., observed) behavior. Perhaps the most well-known gene currently is the BRCA1 gene, which is associated with breast cancer. The BRCA1 gene makes a protein that helps repair damage to DNA, but mutations in BRCA1 can have a faulty copy so they are less able to repair damage to DNA. One of the possible outcomes of damaged DNA is cancer. Not all people with harmful BRCA1 mutations will develop breast cancer and not all people who develop breast cancer have harmful BRCA1 mutations. According to the National Cancer Institute, the probability of a woman without the mutation developing breast cancer in her lifetime is around 12%, while the probability of a woman with the mutation developing cancer is between 55% and 65%.<sup>9</sup> Thus, this gene does not cause breast cancer, it simply increases one’s predisposition toward cancer; however, many other conditions must also occur. A second caveat is that environment plays a role. A woman who is exposed to radiation or drinks alcohol can increase the risk of breast cancer, while a woman who maintains a healthy body weight, has a full term preg nancy at a young age, and breast feeds can reduce the risk. Other caveats also apply. Genetic factors have long causal chains on behavior and should be viewed as contributory factors rather than causes.<sup>10</sup> However, despite these caveats, genes have meaningful effects, and it is in that spirit that we conduct this research.

## Directions for Future Research

There are many opportunities for IS researchers arising from genetic information. One of the primary concerns of the IS discipline is to design systems that enhance and support users decision making, and genetic information can contribute to such systems. For example, IS researchers can use genetic data to investigate questions of sex and cultural differences in IS use and adoption. By making use of genetic data researchers can disentangle genetic vs. societal influences on sex differences in the use of technology. Similarly, distinct ethnic groups will tend to share similar genes but often be part of different societies. Genetic data can help develop a nuanced understanding of the effects of culture and genetics on IS relevant behaviors. For example, consider that there may be systematic differences in the processing of a single “tweet” based on genotype. The C-allele is common in the Yoruba people (\~97%) and Han Chinese people (\~80%) but is found in only around 40% of Europeans (Espeseth et al. 2010). This suggests that Twitter may be a very different information system in Nigeria and China than it is in France or the United Kingdom. While adoption is a very complex phenomenon, this might be a contributory factor to the adoption of Twitter in various countries. This principle applies to other technologies as well. As another example, social networking preferences show a strong genetic component (Ayorech et al. 2017). There is also emerging evidence that economic variables are affected by genetic factors (Cronqvist et al. 2014; Cronqvist and Siegel 2015). Moreover, genetic data will be a central issue in the study of health informatics for the foreseeable future. Whether an IS researcher studies economic, psychological, or social variables, there is the potential to use genetic data to develop richer and more nuanced theories of IS-relevant behaviors.

Additional opportunities for IS researchers also exist. Before genetic testing became easily accessible most genetic research was conducted with twins. Using a wide range of common economic games, including trust games, dictator games, risk taking games, and others, twin studies have consistently shown there to be a large and important genetic component to behavior. Cesarini et al. (2009) conclude “as is usually found in twin studies, genetic influences seem to be a more important source of variation than shared environmental influences” (p. 71). Twin studies have also found that heritable factors can explain up to 66% of the variance in problematic internet use (Deryakulu and Ursavaº 2014; Li et al. 2014). Twin studies of mobile phone usage have led to the following conclusion: “The clear implication of these heritability results is that genes matter more than family environments in predicting how often people use their mobile phones to talk and text” (Miller et al. 2012, p. 101). These are just a few examples of IS-related behaviors that may be impacted by genetic factors and that are potentially important areas for research.

While our specific research question in this work was focused on information search, we also expect that genetic methods can be applied to learn about the assimilation and use of the information provided. For example, we found that while some people consume more single pieces of information, they spent less time on each individual piece. This suggests that there are differences in assimilation of information. Due to the reduced time spent, one group’s members either assimilate faster or they assimilate more shallowly and thus less effectively. People may have different genetic predispositions to various information processing strategies. Many aspects of assimilation and use of information present opportunities for research.

There is also an interesting potential for epigenetics to inform IS theory and practice. While the genome contains a list of all possible gene products that a body can make, not all genes are expressed all the time in all cells. Epigenetics studies the expression of genes. For example, ultraviolet light increases the expression of genes, which in this case creates melanin, which results in a tan. Environmental factors can cause genes to turn on or off, as can internal genetic factors (e.g., the differentiation of stem cells into specific tissues). Thus, there is a combination of genetic structure and environment that influences which genes are expressed and how much impact they have on a person. As an example, it is hypothesized that the use of social media leads to a variety of long-term neural effects. Long-term neural effects are the result of gene expression, often in the form of building additional receptors in specific neurons. Sometimes this will be in the expression of the CHRNA4 gene. The point is that environmental factors, including information systems, can lead to changes in the expression of genes that can lead to changes in the structure of the organism, usually in the brain. This, in turn, leads the organism to react in different ways, and in principle inexpensive genetic testing can allow us to observe the gene expression that leads to these different behavioral reactions in humans.

## Conclusion

According to Moore’s Law the price of computing power fell by half every 18-24 months for many decades, driving an explosion in computing technology and use. “But as an exemplar of rapid change, [Moore’s Law] is the target of playful abuse from genome researchers” (Hayden 2014, p. 294). In genetics, what took 10 years and cost \$3 billion only 15 years ago can now be done for about \$100 in a few hours. Thus, a whole new technology space has opened for IS researchers and the world in general. Because of the ubiquity and importance of technology IS researchers seem uniquely positioned to use advances in genotyping to add value to literally hundreds of millions of consumers’ lives through improved IS design.

In this early foray into this new research space we found that one genetic marker, the rs1044396 polymorphism, was associated with differences in the quantity of information consumed and in the amount of time spent on each piece of information in an online search task. We note that the measured difference between groups in quantity of information consumed was 25%, which is the difference in height between an average man and someone who is seven feet tall. This suggests that IT managers and designers need to create systems that take into account the differing propensities for information search.

Perhaps more than anything, our findings suggest a rich possible future for combining genetics and information systems research. Online information search is the most common activity in information systems usage today. And with millions of people already having their DNA sequenced and as many as a billion more anticipated to be sequenced in the next decade, there is truly an enormous opportunity to understand IT user behavior, including information search and many other IT-relevant activities, in a way never before possible. We hope this work helps guide researchers and practitioners in this direction because genetic science has the potential to impact IS theory and practice in fundamentally profound and novel ways.

## Appendix A

## A Short Primer on Genetics

We have proposed that individual genetic differences will lead to different individual propensities to search for information from IT artifacts. We now provide an explanation of some of the nomenclature of genetics.

The human genome is made up of strands of DNA as shown in Figure A1. DNA is a molecule made of repeating units, which are called nucleotides. Each nucleotide has a phosphate and sugar backbone and a base. The four bases are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T). One strand of DNA is joined to another complementary strand of DNA through hydrogen bonds in the bases—A joins with T and C joins with G. From an information standpoint, DNA is a long sequence of bases (A, C, G, and T) that represent information in base 4. The information in DNA is converted into chains of amino acids.

![](/api/attachments/7PRXZD2H/fulltext/images/d303b4d8cc69474d03566f60f4e427a48af4660ec74868e723c82a156dfeee3e.jpg)

Figure A1. DNA Strand Adapted from Wikipedia Commons Image Difference DNA RNA-DE.svg by Sponk

Each amino acid is specified by a codon. A codon is a group of three nucleotides that codes for one of the 20 amino acids in the human body. Because a codon contains three positions, each of which can be occupied by one of four nucleotides, there are 64 different combinations of possible codons that only make 20 different amino acids; thus, more than one codon combination can code for the same amino acid. A gene is a series of codons that specifies a sequence of amino acids that fold into three-dimensional shapes to carry out the functions of the body.

A given gene may have multiple variants that result from the substitution of one base for another. This may make the gene defective or it may alter the function of the gene. Some of the variants are caused by single substitutions of a base. Such a variant is called a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP—pronounced snip). A SNP may result in a difference because it causes a codon to code for a different amino acid or because it changes the speed of gene expression or because it changes the nature of the three dimensional folding of the final gene product.

SNP analysis can be quite useful for several reasons. First, there are millions of SNPs, and though many of these are nonfunctional, there are many SNPs that might affect behavior. So while a psychological test may offer a few high level constructs as predictors of behavior, SNP analysis can offer a more nuanced set of explanatory factors. Second, SNPs are parts of genes, of which there are about 20,000 in a human. The functions of many of these genes are known, so that helps to narrow down the range of possible SNPs for a given behavior. That also allows one to understand more easily the mechanisms that lead to given behaviors. Third, SNP analysis is easy and inexpensive. Data collection requires only that a person rub the inside of his or her cheek for a few seconds (or just spit). The cost to analyze a SNP is low. For this work, our cost was around \$10 per person, but in an industrial setting in which many SNPs are analyzed at a time, the cost is less than a penny per SNP. So it takes a few seconds of someone’s time and a few dollars to analyze many SNPs on a single individual. By comparison, the Myers-Briggs type indicator costs \$49.95 and takes 15 minutes of a person’s time <https://www.mbtionline.com/TaketheMBTI> to be classified into one of sixteen types. Finally, SNP analysis is generally more precise than psychological testing because SNPs do not depend on the mood of the subject, the comprehension of the subject, or even the veracity of the subject. Classifications based on personality testing may be less intimidating to individuals without training in the physical sciences, but there is a great deal to recommend SNP analysis.

## Appendix B

## The CHRNA4 Gene and rs1044396 SNP

Humans have about 20,000 genes. A tiny proportion of those may be related to information search behavior. Based on the research cited concerning pathological information search (Jeong et al. 2017; Montag et al. 2012), we chose the CHRNA4 gene (a welcome acronym for “cholinergic receptor nicotinic alpha 4”), which we theorized would be important in explaining internet search behavior. We now describe this gene in detail.

We begin with a layperson’s description of the gene and then progress to further scientific detail. Neurons signal each other by releasing neurotransmitters into the space between them. The neurotransmitter we are concerned with in this paper is acetylcholine. Some neurons have pores in their surfaces that are sealed with a set of five complex proteins that act as gates (see Figure B1). Acetylcholine can bind to these gates and cause them to open. The pore with the gates is called an ion channel because, when the gates open, ions from outside the neuron flow in, changing the electrical potential of the inside of the neuron. Specifically, the interior of the neuron has a negative charge and positive ions fill the area around it. This change in charge propagates along the neuron and is what is responsible for the electrical signals in the brain. The CHRNA4 gene contains instructions for how to make a portion of those gates, called the á4 subunit. The entire CHRNA4 gene contains many instructions encoded in DNA base pairs. The rs1044396 polymorphism is a change in one of those base pairs, which causes the subsection of the gate to be built slightly differently, which in turn causes the neurons to signal each other slightly differently (we discuss this in more detai below). Put in a single sentence, the rs1044396 polymorphism seems to change the way some neurons signal each other

In the mammalian brain, the most common type of nicotinic acetylcholine receptor contains two á4 subunits and three â2 subunits (denoted 26 (á4) (â2) (Dani 2015)). The particular single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) on which we focus is rs1044396, which codes for part of the á4 subunit. Rs1044396 is a substitution of the base pair thymine (T) for the base pair cytosine (C). This particular change is a synonymous substitution, which means there is no change in amino acid coding. Both variants (AGC and AGT) code for the amino acid Serine. However, a synonymous substitution can affect transcription, translation, splicing, or folding of the resulting molecule. While the exact mechanism of the substitution is unknown, rs1044396 has been shown to have meaningful effects on important cognitive traits and performance including attention (Greenwood et al. 2012), internet gaming addiction (Jeong et al. 2017), working memory (Markett et al. 2011; Markett et al. 2013), internet addiction (Montag et al. 2012), and response speed (Schneider et al. 2015). Thus, through a process that is not entirely understood at this time, the substitution of thymine for cytosine seems to change the way the á4 subunits function, which in turn changes the way the (á4) (â2) nicotinic acetylcholine receptor—the most common nicotinic acetylcholine receptor in the brain—functions.

Figure B1. Ligand gated channel acetylcholine is the ligand in both the image and in this work (http://philschatz.com/anatomy-book/contents/m46526.html#fig-ch12\_04\_02 licensed under creative commons 4.0 https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

Nicotine bears enough chemical similarity to acetylcholine to allow it to bind to neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors<sup>11</sup>, thereby causing the gate to open and allowing ions into the neuron. This signaling mechanism has been widely studied in relation to nicotine, which is why the word “nicotinic” appears in the name (Dale 1914). Because nicotine also stimulates the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, a great deal of research focusing on the rs1044396 polymorphism in the CHRNA4 gene has examined smoking. For example, researchers have found that having a T-allele of the polymorphism significantly deceases the score on several nicotine addiction instruments among Chinese men (Feng et al. 2004).<sup>12</sup> A similar finding also holds for German men (Breitling et al. 2009). Further research suggests that the rs1044396 polymorphism is not only associated with nicotine addiction, but also with smoking initiation (Chu et al. 2011). In addition, T-allele carriers experience less severe nicotine withdrawal symptoms after 24 hours of smoking abstinence (Lazary et al. 2014).

The rs1044396 polymorphism has also been implicated in non-smoking related behaviors. Carriers of a T-allele report being less lonely and depressed than C:C carriers (Tsai et al. 2012). Moreover, carriers of a T-allele report being less anxious and emotionally unstable relative to C:C carriers (Markett et al. 2011).

## Additional Information on Synonymous Substitutions in Genetics

As described above, there are 64 combinations of A, C, G, and T nucleotides coding for 20 amino acids, and some different combinations code for the same amino aci. This is true in the present research. Whether one has a C or a T does not change the amino acid for which the codon codes because it is a synonymous substitution. Serine is the result in any case (and there are four other ways Serine can result).

However, while the codon may code for the same amino acid, it does not necessary do so in the same way. Some combinations are more efficient in transcription or translation. This can affect the timing and folding of the protein. This, in turn, can affect the function of the gene product (Kimchi-Sarfaty et al. 2007; Nackley et al. 2006). In other words, while the same material is being used in construction of the gate, it is being used differently, just as two houses may be built out of bricks but built differently.

To be even more specific, it is important to understand that genes do not make gene products; rather, they only store the code. A molecule of RNA, called messenger RNA (mRNA), binds to the DNA to copy the information (in a process called transcription) and then travels out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm of the cell. The mRNA travels to a ribosome where it interacts with transport RNA (tRNA) in a process called translation. tRNA has an anti-codon on one end and the appropriate amino acid on the other end. The first codon on the mRNA matches with the anti-codon on the tRNA. Then the ribosome advances the mRNA strand one codon and a second tRNA matches with the second codon on the mRNA strand. There are now two tRNAs attached with two amino acids. The amino acids form a peptide bond and the ribosome advances the mRNA and releases the first tRNA. A new tRNA binds with the next codon on the mRNA and that amino acid joins the chain being formed. This process continues until the entire gene is expressed as a string of amino acids. Synonymous substitutions can cause this process to proceed up to twenty-five times slower (Shabalina et al. 2013). After translation, the protein must fold through another complex process. What is now understood is that synonymous substitutions can have an important effect on this whole process (for a more thorough review, see Shabalina et al. 2013).

While synonymous substitutions clearly have an effect on gene expression, there is also the possibility that rs1044396 is a linkage disequilibrium with some other functional gene. Linkage disequilibrium is like correlation; it occurs when the probability of gene variant 1 occurring times the probability of gene variant 2 occurring is not equal to the probability of both genes occurring together. It could be the case that rs1044396 is in linkage disequilibrium with another functional gene variant and it is the other gene variant that causes the observed cognitive effects attributed to rs1044396. This does not change our argument that there is a genetic basis for propensity to search for information, but it does change one of the gene variants that might contribute to that basis. However, recent research suggests that synonymous substitutions in the gene have a functional effect through mRNA stability and receptor sensitivity (Eggert et al. 2015). This research looked at haplotypes (i.e., combinations of SNPs) that included rs1044396 but was not exclusive to rs1044396.

The bottom line from this long explanation is that the current best scientific understanding of rs1044396 is that while it is a synonymous substitution, it is a non-silent substitution (i.e., it has functional effects). This understanding may change as scientific understanding progresses. Thus, we suggest that rs1044396 is the source of the measured genetic effect on propensity to search for information.

## Genotyping and Human Behavior

In the paper we cite a great deal of literature that examines the use of genotyping to explain human behavior. In this section of the appendix we provide additional detail. We cannot hope to cover all of the disciplines of genetics, neuroscience, and psychology, but we can provide an overview. DNA contains a set of instructions for building an organism, including the organism’s brain, if it is that sort of organism. Within DNA are substrings, called genes, which make a specific chemical product. Some of these products are physical building blocks, some are chemical transmitters, and some are intermediate products. All behavior is a result of some chemical interaction in the nervous sy stem, and those chemicals and the structures they interact with are specified by genes. There is flexibility in the structures that can be modified by the environment, and behavior is the result of many complex interacting processes.

Some steps in the process are critical and can result in clear behavioral effects. For example, the PAH gene specifies the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which converts the amino acid phenylalanine to compounds the body needs. Certain mutations in the PAH gene mean that the body cannot produce phenylalanine hydroxylase, which in turn means that phenylalanine is not converted to other things in the body and causes it to build up to a toxic level in the system instead. The brain is particularly susceptible and the person with the mutation ends up with permanent brain damage and microcephaly. This then leads to behavioral issues such as problems in speech.

On the other hand, some steps in the process are more subtle. The SNP rs53576 is part of the oxytocin receptor gene and seems to lead to more oxytocin receptors in certain neurons (Li et al. 2015). Oxytocin is known as the “love hormone,” and appears to be something the body produces in response to prosocial situations. Increased oxytocin receptors may lead to increased oxytocin uptake, which leads to more prosocial behavior as those people with more oxytocin receptors are more highly stimulated from prosocial behavior. We must caution that this is just current conjecture in scientific circles and a simplified version at that, but it is a good exemplar of how genes can affect behavior.

Another example comes from the gene variant we study. The rs1044396 polymorphism is associated with nicotine addiction. rs1044396 specifies part of the instructions to build a nicotinic receptor, which reacts to nicotine. It has been proposed that people with one version of the polymorphism are more attracted to nicotine because it regulates these receptors to an optimal level that people without the polymorphism experience naturally. Essentially, the idea is that the polymorphism causes nicotine addiction because it makes people feel better.

## How Genotyping Works

To understand how genotyping works, it is first important to note that DNA has been replicating for billions of years, since the first organism appeared on earth almost four billion years ago. Unfortunately for scientific study, DNA is sequestered inside cells. The challenge for geneticists was to develop a way to replicate DNA outside of cells. The first step in the process is to separate the double helix of DNA into two strands. In a cell enzymes perform the separation, but it can also be accomplished physically by heating the DNA, which is easy and straightforward. A remaining problem, however, was the temperatures needed destroy the enzyme, called a polymerase, that copies the strands In the 1970s Chien et al. (1976) isolated a polymerase from a bacterium discovered in hot springs in Yellowstone National Park, which can remain functional at high heats. In the 1980s Mullins (1998) added this polymerase to DNA while heating it to high temperatures. As the polymerase was specifically designed for replicating DNA at high temperatures, it successfully accomplished that task.

DNA takes the form of a double helix, which can be duplicated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The first step in this process is to separate each side of the strand of DNA, which can be done by heating it to a specific temperature in a machine like that shown in Figure D1. The DNA is mixed in a solution of nucleotides with a polymerase, which is an enzyme that connects to half of a DNA strand and makes a copy of it. The main polymerase used is derived from thermus aquaticus, a type of bacteria. Replicating DNA is then just a series of heating and cooling steps.

Figure D1. Rad S1000 Thermal Cycler (image from http://www.bio-rad.com/en-cn/product/s1000-thermal-cycler)

Included in the chemical mixture that is heated and cooled are primers. These primers are short sequences of DNA that match one of the two SNPs being measured. These short strands of DNA have fluorescent markers on the ends that cancel each other out. For example, one end of the primer may have a green marker and the other end will have a dye that absorbs green photons. As these two markers are close togethe on a short strand of DNA, most of the green photons are absorbed. However, when the polymerase links the primer to the half of the DNA strand that it matches, the fluorescent marker is cleaved or broken off and floats freely away. Now that the green fluorescent marker is far from the green absorbing end, less of the green light is absorbed. The important factor is that the polymerase will only join the correct primer into the DNA strand. That means one version of the SNP primers will be incorporated into DNA and fluorescent markers will be allowed to fluoresce, but for the other non-fitting primer this will not happen. So after the process is finished the chemical solution has a measurable colo and one can determine which SNPs were present by observing the color.

## Procedure

The genotyping for our study was performed by ACGT Inc. (http://www.acgtinc.com). Genomic DNA was extracted from provided swabs containing buccal cells.<sup>13</sup> Automated purification of genomic DNA was conducted on the Maxwell 16 platform by means of Maxwell 16 Bucca Swab LEV DNA Purification Kit (Promega).<sup>14</sup> Genotyping was performed by competitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) analysis, with PCR reactions run on the Bio-Rad S1000 thermocyclers (see Figure D1). The KASP Master mix (from LGC Genomics) contains the universal FRET cassettes, ROX passive reference dye, Taq polymerase, free nucleotides, and MgCl in an optimized buffer solution. The primers (from IDT) for the KASP assays were as follows:

Primer\_AlleleFAM 5'-CTTCGGTGTCCCCGAGC-3'

Primer\_AlleleHEX 5'-CCTCTTCGGTGTCCCCGAGT-3'

Primer\_Common 3'-CGCTTTGGTGCTGCGGGTCTT-5'

The assay-specific KASP Primer mix and the universal KASP Master mix were added to DNA samples (10 ng per reaction), and a thermal cycling reaction was then performed, followed by an end-point fluorescent read.

The PCR parameters include:

1. 94 °C for 15 minutes Hot start enzyme activation

2. 94 °C for 20 seconds Touchdown over 61 55°C for 60 seconds 10 cycles (dropping 0.6°C per cycle)

3. 94°C for 20 seconds 55°C for 60 seconds 26 cycles

Following completion of the initial 36 cycles of PCR, the fluorescence signals from all genotyping reactions were read on a BMG PHERAStar plate reader. Additional recycle steps (three cycles per recycle step) were performed and read after each recycle step. Once reactions were confirmed to have reached endpoint, Kraken software automatically called the genotypes. All automatic calls were evaluated by qualified personnel, and some of the calls were corrected manually.
