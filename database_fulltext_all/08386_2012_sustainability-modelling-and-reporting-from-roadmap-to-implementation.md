## a b s t r a c t

Sustainable business management aspires towards balancing and integrating social, economic and environmental dimensions. Existing roadmaps, frameworks and systems do not comprehensively support sustainable business transformation nor do they allow decision makers to explore interrelationships and in<sup>fl</sup>uences between the sustainability dimensions. This leads to silo-based decision making where vision and strategies are not mapped to execution, and sustainability modelling and reporting processes are uncoordinated. This research proposes and implements a generic sustainable business transformation roadmap, which is supported by a framework and architecture for integrated sustainability modelling and reporting. The implementation leverages system dynamics, work<sup>fl</sup>ow modelling and adaptable system concepts.

## 1. Introduction

Sustainability is an approach for managing businesses that draws on an integrated and balanced performance of the business's economic, environmental and social aspects [12], referred to as sustainability dimensions or the Triple Bottom Lines (TBL) of business management [18]. In Our Common Future: the World Commission on Environment and Development, Bruntland de<sup>fi</sup>nes sustainable development as an approach to development that meets current needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs [53]. Sustainable businesses seek the delivery of balanced and integrated performances in the three sustainability dimensions: social, economic and environmental. Irrespective of whether they are in government or the private or public sector, organisations are under increasing pressure to meet inter-generational equity. Strong mobilisation of stakeholders forces organisations to rethink whether or not their operations need to be sustainability-driven, and whether they will be party to increasing the problem's intensity or providing solutions to it. Many organisations are committed to transforming their business and have taken initiatives for sustainability modelling and reporting, but they have still failed to achieve the anticipated goal; most of them still manage their businesses in a traditional way with the preparation of sustainability reports being the only action taken.

A business model that addresses win–win–win strategies for these sustainability dimensions is the means to successful assimilation and application of the sustainability concept into business management. Decision makers need to be guided by a coherent roadmap and a modelling and reporting system that realises that roadmap, in order to manage a sustainable business lifecycle. A coherent roadmap is an overall view of the sustainable business management process, known as a macro-level lifecycle, and contains several high-level phases, each of which is a micro-level lifecycle, comprised in turn of many low-level steps.

Numerous roadmaps, frameworks and systems have already been proposed for facilitating sustainable business process management and sustainability reporting management [18,34,52]. They, however, do not comprehensively support a business management lifecycle, from providing an understanding of the relevance of the sustainability concept for business, to development of sustainability vision and strategies, to business process engineering, to implementation, to monitoring, controlling, reporting and continuous improvement. These roadmaps and frameworks are not supported by robust information systems; they do not incorporate useful models for the integration process and requirements of the sustainability dimensions; they do not prescribe how to identify inter-relationships and in<sup>fl</sup>uences between modelling and reporting processes. Therefore, current sustainability decision making is silo-based; vision and strategies are not mapped to implementation activities, and sustainability modelling and reporting processes are uncoordinated. Currently available systems that claim to support the roadmap steps for formulating, communicating, executing and monitoring the sustainability concept overlook harmonisation of sustainability modelling and reporting, and lack an over-arching principle for integrating models from diverse paradigms and managing multi-dimensional data. These systems do not encompass the required level of support for adaptability, integrability, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, extensibility and versatility for macro-level and micro-level decision making lifecycles throughout the sustainable business transformation.

In order to address these problems, this paper proposes a generic sustainable business transformation roadmap (Section 4), supported by a framework (Section 5) and architecture (Section 6) of sustainability modelling and a reporting system which leverages characteristics of existing roadmaps, frameworks and architectures. The proposed roadmap, comprised of discover and learn, strategise, design, transform, and monitor and control phases, guides decision makers on to a sustainable business management journey. The modelling and reporting framework and architecture are modular, versatile, <sup>fl</sup>exible, scalable, adaptable, customisable, and extensible and will support implementation by leveraging system dynamics, work<sup>fl</sup>ow modelling, object orientation, and componentisation concepts. They support modelling of diverse paradigms and their integration, and dynamically manage multi-dimensional data. They support decision making requirements for each phase and step of the sustainable business transformation roadmap.

## 2. Problems and issues

There are many organisations of high repute, ranging from small to large, practising sustainable business management, and these are supported by a good number of global and regional sustainability bodies (such as WBCSD [52], NZBCSD [34], GEMI [14], GRI [18], and United Nations [49]). However, because the sustainability concept continues to be applied unsystematically, these practising organisations experience huge dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in realising their goals of full sustainability status. This is due to a lack of understanding and support for the design, development and implementation process, and lack of proper procedural and technological support for decision making for sustainability management.

Decision making is a daunting task in the sustainable business transformation process as it involves various sustainability dimension paradigms (social, economic and environment), decision-making paradigms (strategic, tactical and operational) and sustainability modelling paradigms. Current decision-making processes are ad-hoc; decisions are very task-oriented and uncoordinated; the tasks are discrete silos and support only one or two of the three sustainability dimensions. In current practice, top management conceives and develops the sustainability vision and strategies, without aligning these with organisational vision and strategies. They then initiate tasks, such as reduction of transportation, tree planting, recycling and waste reduction, fund raising for community activities, stakeholder participation, etc., as showcase applications of sustainability practice. Many other businesses have taken initiatives such as life cycle analysis of products and environmental modelling but they do not map the conversion and realisation of the vision and strategies with these operational activities. Decision makers can not develop a blueprint that displays end-to-end connection from sustainability vision to strategic planning, to operational planning, to implementation, to performance measurement and monitoring, to reporting.

Organisations are practising sustainability modelling and sustainability reporting as separate activities. The model builders develop various models to support the decision makers for developing a sustainable business model. Sustainability modelling in<sup>fl</sup>uences how to prepare the sustainability report, what needs to be included in it, and how to present the sustainability status of the organisation. On the other hand, sustainability reporting exposes the organisational practices and norms to internal and external stakeholders, identi<sup>fi</sup>es unsustainable patterns and argues for improvement. Therefore, sustainability reporting also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the modelling processes. Several normative frameworks do draw attention to the importance of coordination between the modelling and reporting processes, but due to a lack of comprehensive procedural and technological support, sustainability modelling and reporting processes are not properly harmonised.

The sustainable business management process deals with a variety of models (such as sustainability, business process, scenario, domain, organisation, function, document, data, and report models). Representation of these models is managed using diverse modelling methodologies (such as system dynamics, balanced scorecard, work<sup>fl</sup>ow, report, scenario, object, document and data modelling). Furthermore, these modelling activities are undertaken with the support of a number of purpose-built modelling tools. Many of these models are very complex as to their domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c and sustainability dimension requirements. Existing procedural and technological roadmaps, frameworks and architectures do not provide the overarching methodologies for representation and integration of models from diverse paradigms. They do not provide support for analysis, design, development, simulation, storage and retrieval, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation and integration of all these models.

Existing information systems of an organisation capture a huge amount of quantitative transaction data that are related to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of the organisation. Current IS systems are not designed to capture social and environmental related data, nor are they designed to support the management of data of all the sustainability dimensions. At the same time, a traditional business model does not capture enough of the DSS data, transaction data and benchmarking data required for a sustainable business model. Many pieces of these data could be qualitative and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to process and analyse for decision support and reporting. Furthermore, a DSS that claims to support sustainability decision making needs to access all the sustainability dimensional data seamlessly, to import them within a single platform, and to analyse them in an integrated fashion for investigating the sustainability decision-making elements. Current systems also lack the capability to apply the intelligence density concept for exploring sustainability drivers and performance indicators. DSS literature lacks overarching principles for managing such multidimensional qualitative and quantitative data.

Currently available frameworks, architectures and systems do not support sustainability modelling and reporting in an integrated manner. None of the available systems supports the full requirements of sustainable business transformation. Only a few aim to support modelling requirements, but they do not support sustainability reporting. Similarly, there are frameworks and systems that aim to support sustainability reporting but not its modelling. These systems do not support sustainability modelling and reporting in a harmonised fashion.

None of the available information systems supports the formulation, communication, execution, monitoring and controlling of the sustainable business management strategies in an integrated manner. A system that claims to support sustainable development, communication and realisation, is required to be interactive with a wide variety of modelling methodologies, diverse models and databases. The system needs to support decision-making work<sup>fl</sup>ow management. These features are neither present in the currently available sustainability management-related information systems nor in typical enterprise systems. Moreover, organisations these days are characterised predominantly by structures, processes and systems that are brittle, slow, in<sup>fl</sup>exible, and not agile, leading to stagnancy and an inability to adapt quickly. Sustainable business transformation requires framing and reframing of the business system over a period. Currently available decision making systems do not support these features within a single environment.

## 3. Research objectives and methodology

This research followed and ful<sup>fi</sup>lled Nunamaker et al.'s [33] <sup>fi</sup>ve criteria and Hevner et al.'s [21] seven guidelines of design science research for developing objectives. The major objectives of this research were: a) Proposal of a sustainable business transformation roadmap; b) Design and development of a sustainability modelling and reporting framework and architecture; c) Implementation of the sustainability modelling and reporting framework and architecture through a concrete prototype; d) Realisation of the sustainable business transformation roadmap in the context of a business case with the support of the sustainability modelling and reporting system; and e) Evaluation of the relevance of the sustainable business transformation roadmap phases and steps, support provided by the sustainability modelling and reporting framework, architecture and system, and supportability, performance, usability and reliability of the prototype.

Information systems research encompasses modelling requirements to better facilitate developing, implementing, using, and maintaining valuable information [51]. This research followed Wand and Weber's [51] research agenda by introducing a variety of methodologies, models, framework and architecture for business and information systems. It also supports Hevner et al. [21] and Zmud [56] as it targets the enhancement of organisational capabilities by providing computational tools. The adopted research methodology is similar to the information systems research framework proposed by Hevner et al. [21]. Overall, this research could be divided into two distinct, yet interwoven phases: artefacts creation and artefacts evaluation. The artefacts creation phase corresponds to the Develop/Build phase of Hevner et al. [21] and the artefacts evaluation phase corresponds to their Justify/Evaluate phase.

This research programme was carried out through four interrelated steps, namely observation, theory building, system development, and evaluation, and was adapted from the multi-methodological research approach [33]. The paper proposes the sustainable business transformation (SBT) roadmap, and the sustainability modelling and reporting (SMART) frameworks and architectures. These artefacts have been validated and tested through the development of a SMART prototype and application of the prototype using real-life business scenarios.

## 4. Sustainable business transformation roadmap

An organisation is composed of business systems, structures, people and technologies [5,45] and a sustainable business system integrates them to achieve sustainability. The decision making process for such a system involves: 1) development of a sustainability vision and strategies for designing organisational infrastructure, 2) design and establishment of an information systems infrastructure that is aligned with business strategies, and 3) alignment of the organisational infrastructure with that of the information systems [20,21]. A roadmap follows a common format, ensures the setting of clear objectives, de<sup>fi</sup>nes action plans for achieving these objectives, and helps users to analyse critical decision points. This research synthesises key concepts from currently available roadmaps and frameworks such as the Integrated Modelling Frameworks [34,52], SIGMA Management Framework [42], Sustainability Journey Roadmap [14], SD Planner Architecture [14], Business Process Gap Detection Mechanism [22], Business Lifecycle Management Process [38], Sociotechnical Approach to Business Process Simulation [17], The Natural Step Framework [9,37], SAP Strategic Enterprise Management [32], Model Driven Business Transformation Framework [26], MIT90s Framework [41], Zachman Framework [54], ARIS Business Process Excellence [19,39], and Life Cycle Assessments [1,35,43,47].

Based on the outcomes of this synthesising process, this research proposes the sustainable business transformation (SBT) roadmap (Fig. 1), which is primarily a procedural guideline for helping decision makers in the fusion of the sustainability concept into a traditional business and developing a new sustainable business model. Interactions of business strategies, IT strategies, organisational and information systems infrastructures [20,21] have been taken as a philosophical guideline for proposing the sustainable business transformation roadmap.

Fig. 1. Sustainable business transformation roadmap

The roadmap entails a cyclic journey from discovery and learning, to strategising, to designing, to transforming, to monitoring and controlling, with the stages repeated. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the journey is to discover and learn about unsustainability in the context of the business. Once the decision maker understands and learns from this, they can then envision how sustainability can be achieved. This vision needs to be realised through one or more strategies, exploring various sustainability scenarios and deciding on the strategy that best suits the organisation. The next logical step in the journey is the design of process, the organisational and technological elements that would enable the business to execute the strategy. This design would then be implemented by the transformation of the organisation in terms of processes, organisational structures and information systems. This transformation is not a one-off activity but is envisaged as a continuous process of improvement that allows the organisation to become more and more sustainable. For this to be possible, top management needs to be able to monitor and control the organisation as a whole. This monitoring and controlling enable continued discovery and learning about the organisation and its surroundings which could in turn lead the decision makers to reformulate sustainability strategies, redesign, and continue to transform the organisation into a sustainable one.

## 5. Sustainability modelling and reporting framework

A framework is a design pattern that organises a complex subject in abstract form, provides methodologies and guidelines for application development, and de<sup>fi</sup>nes relationships among the components [25]. In order to support decision making requirements throughout the sustainable business transformation roadmap steps, the decision makers need a sustainability modelling and reporting system. The system must be developed based on a framework that supports various modelling processes such as scenario-based decision support [2], systems dynamics, simulation, business process, data, and information modelling. Currently available frameworks and systems that have been developed and implemented for modelling and reporting various dimensions of sustainability are neither integrated nor exhaustively backed by tools and technologies to support sustainable business requirements; nor do they support the complete life cycle of a business transformation process.

This research proposes a generic domain-independent Sustainability Modelling and Reporting (SMART) framework that is aligned with the sustainable business transformation roadmap and supports integration of various sustainability models [28], and modelling and reporting processes for developing sustainable business models and reports. The SMART framework is modular, versatile, and extensible, and leverages systems thinking, object orientation, and componentisation concepts.

First, a high-level conceptual view is introduced in Section 5.1. To address the requirements of business analysts and modellers, a highlevel process and model-oriented view is provided in Section 5.2. To address the requirements of architects, designers and implementers, a model and component interaction view is presented in Section 5.3. The integration of models and components of the SMART framework are discussed in Section 5.4.

## 5.1. The SMART framework: conceptual view

Since most of the decision making activities as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the SBT roadmap are to be supported by various types of business and decision models, model representation is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant function of the SMART system. Representation and integration of these models involves many modelling processes and systems. Similarly, models are also used in the sustainability reporting processes for managing and processing multi-dimensional data. Modelling processes and systems for sustainable business development, and those for reporting, mutually in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other's activities. A sustainable business scenario is the key ingredient of the conceptual SMART framework. On an abstract level, the SMART framework needs to deal with representation and integration of diverse models, modelling methodologies and paradigms, and management of multi-dimensional data.

## 5.2. SMART framework: process- and model-oriented view

An enterprise system framework acts as a collaborative force between various aspects of business planning, organisation structures, processes and data [40]. The process- and model-oriented view of the SMART framework identi<sup>fi</sup>es models that are relevant to particular phases of the SBT roadmap, as shown in Fig. 2. The main focus of decision makers' activities in the discovery and learning phase of the roadmap is dealing with knowledge management which primarily involves document and data models. The decision makers should be able to use previously developed sustainability simulation modelling games, sustainable business processes models, sustainability reports and other related documents during the discovery and learning process.

In the strategise phase, the decision makers develop the business strategies that mainly involve sustainability, data and document modelling. The sustainability model, which is an integrated model of economic, environmental and social dimensions of the business scenario, is proposed to develop within the system dynamics environment. The SMART framework should support integration of this model with other models through importation and conversion, instantiation, simulation, analysis and management of the model outputs within the SMART environment. During the design phase, decision makers conceive, design, develop and simulate the TO-BE business process model and compare it with the AS-IS counterpart. The decision makers also prepare a future business blueprint, as well as models of organisation, information systems' function, data and control, comparing them with the existing models. They prepare a gap analysis report. The decision makers require a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant input of scenario analysis during this phase; for decision support, the design phase involves models of business process, function, organisation, data, document, domain object, and scenario. During the transformation phase of the roadmap, they prepare a transformation plan, making extensive use of the process model, the organisation model, function model, and document and data models. In the monitor and control phase, the decision makers continuously monitor the system, using the dashboard, and preparing sustainability reports. Thus, this phase involves the report model, the control model, the document model and the data model.

The various models required in the different phases of the roadmap come from such diverse paradigms that the modelling methodologies, techniques and tools used to model them are for the most part discrete. A key challenge is the seamless integration of these diverse models conceptually, methodologically and technologically. The process- and model-oriented view of the SMART framework enables the designer to address this conceptual and methodological challenge. The model and component interaction view discussed in the following section attempts to address the technological challenge.

## 5.3. SMART framework: model and component interaction view

A component-based framework represents collaboration of the participating components [11,27] that provide fundamental elements, relationships, and structural and dynamic integrity. It uses static models to illustrate the component structure and dynamic models to demonstrate the component collaboration. The design of the model and the component-oriented view of the SMART framework are supported by a number of DSS frameworks [2,3,24,36,46] and object-oriented frameworks [7,13,30].

The SMART framework deals with different types of models, namely sustainability, process, scenario, document, report, domain object, organisation, function, control, and data models during the de-<sup>fi</sup>ning of the process-oriented and model-oriented view of the SMART framework (Section 5.2). A variety of modelling methodologies need to be used for representing these models. The SMART framework is an integrative one that facilitates combining these diverse models for supporting the sustainable business management process. The framework organises these models in such a way that they are loosely coupled; they can work in an integrated and seamless manner; and the relationships among them can be established dynamically at runtime. Fig. 3 shows the constituent models and components of the framework and their relationships and interactions. This model and component interaction view of the SMART framework consists of three main parts. These are: 1) Models: sustainability model, process model, data model, scenario model, and various object models; 2) Persistence: service library, component library, object repository and data storage; and 3) Model life cycle management: creation, integration, instantiation, execution, storage, retrieval, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation and deletion of various types of models. The core model types and the way they function together and interact with the supporting models are brie<sup>fl</sup>y discussed below.

While the sustainability model supports sustainable business strategic planning, it also needs to support sustainable business scenario analysis, to communicate decision support information using the report model, and to prepare sustainable business strategies using the document models. The SMART framework needs to facilitate <sup>fl</sup>exible integration of the sustainability, scenario, report, document and data models.

A process model needs to be supported by the domain, data, report, organisation, and scenario and service models. The domain models serve as an entity, service or conceptual object for participating in service or work<sup>fl</sup>ow processes; the data models manage instantiation and outputs of the process model; the report models generate reports; the organisation model supports organisational resources (especially the actors who participate in the processes), and the scenario model supports analysis of decision scenarios. Process models are used to generate the business blueprint document that contains information about processes, organisations, services and tasks, performance indicators, and critical success factors. The SMART framework needs to facilitate <sup>fl</sup>exible integration of scenario, report, organisation, and document and data models with the business process model.

A report model is a specialised object model or a document model that supports the processing of data and presentation using a special template. It is supported by the scenario models, and used by the sustainability and process models. A scenario model integrates domain model, data, solver, and visualisations for analysing decision scenarios. It supports sustainability, process and report models. A document model is supported by the data and object models and supports management of scenario, sustainability, process, and report models.

The model- and component-oriented view of the SMART framework encapsulates both the core and supporting models, de<sup>fi</sup>ning the dynamic relationships among them, and providing integration and communication support for their interaction towards analysing a business problem for supporting decision making requirements. Their kernel and integration components provide an infrastructure for the integration and interaction of the models, and manage persistence services dynamically. These models, integration components and persistence features of the SMART framework are discussed below.

## 5.4. SMART framework: model and component integration

Frameworks or systems that support integration of models and data sources enhance decision support [10,50]. Sustainable business management is supported by a number of models namely, sustainability, business process, scenario, object, data, document and report. However, one modelling methodology or an environment does not provide support for lifecycle management of these models in an integrated fashion. Generally, these modelling environments are not mutually supportive. Sustainable business development processes need support from a wide range of modelling methodologies and models. Model integration issues can be categorised as: 1) similar model types and modelling methodologies and environments; 2) similar model types and modelling methodologies but different environments; and 3) different model types. Model integration methodologies such as ad-hoc or permanent models, structural integration, instance level integration, and integration of a model output as an input to another model for scenario modelling, and service level integration can be useful for the model integration. Models can be integrated using one or more of methodologies: 1) Scenario Level Integration; 2) Model Level Integration; 3) Document Level Integration; and 4) Data Level Integration.

The SMART framework provides an infrastructure for the integration of the models required for sustainable business management. The kernel and integration components include application programme interfaces (API) to interact among the models developed using diverse modelling methodologies and environments. Thus, the kernel, with the support of APIs, enables integration of the models. The SMART framework also supports the scenario management process for facilitating integration of scenarios and models using a mapping system. The SMART framework incorporates APIs for the integration of report, document and data models. The kernel manages communication among the models and components if they can not directly establish the relationship.

The SMART framework is a high-level design pattern that needs to be realised through an architecture. The following section presents the process of extending the SMART framework for the design and development of the layered and tiered SMART architecture.

## 6. Sustainability modelling and reporting architecture

The proposed sustainable business transformation (SBT) roadmap prescribes non-technological procedural guidelines for sustainable business modelling and management. The proposed Sustainability Modelling and Reporting (SMART) framework, as an abstract level design pattern, acts as an interface between the business system and the information systems, explores models and components for each of the SBT roadmap phases, and displays inter-relationships and interactions among the models, framework components, and objects. The SMART architecture is a high-level blueprint that aims to realise the proposed SMART framework and the SBT roadmap, and guide the system developer to comprehend and build the SMART system. It articulates the structure of the SMART system and incorporates fundamental mechanisms for interaction and integration of the architectural components.

This paper presents two different views of the SMART architecture: a layered and component view, and a module and sub-system view. The layered and component view supports the system architects for the design and development of the architectural components and their placement in logical layers for building the SMART system. The SMART architecture provides the infrastructure that allows components to co-exist, and establishes communication mechanisms and error processing capabilities. The module and sub-system view is an implementation level view that packages various models and components as modules and/or sub systems for implementing as multitiered architecture.

The SMART architecture is a domain-independent high-level diagram that organises the architectural components in layers. The components are loosely coupled and they support <sup>fl</sup>exible scenario modelling and their integration, <sup>fl</sup>exible mapping between different components, <sup>fl</sup>exible integration of components, and scenario analysis. This mapping component facilitates dynamic communication between model-data, model-solver, and model-visualisation. The SMART architecture is modular, <sup>fl</sup>exible and extensible.

The SMART architecture is designed using a number of layers: component, object, data, services, access services, primitive component, runtime component generation, scenario management and integration support. These are grouped as the scenario management layer, core component layer, and four support layers, namely those for integration, persistence, connectivity and extensibility. The modelling part of the SMART framework is realised through the component and scenario management layer. The data, object, service, and access service layers of the architecture provide persistence management services, and the integration layer acts as glue for creating infrastructure for integration and the interaction of models and components. The SMART architecture contains other layers for providing <sup>fl</sup>exibility, adaptability, versatility, con<sup>fi</sup>gurability and connectivity.

The component layer contains all types of models, such as sustainability, process, report, document, scenario, data and domain models that were previously de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the SMART framework modelling section. In addition, the component layer houses control, mapping, solver, and visualisation components. The object layer manages different types of objects such as transaction and DSS database schemas, scenario instances, business blueprint schema, documents and reports. The data layer manages transaction data, DSS data, scenario data and the SMART system data. The services layer establishes communications and interactions between integration, and data and object layers. The access services layer contains various middleware components that provide services for communicating to and from the external environment and the SMART environment. The primitive component layer contains the basic component structure for developing model, solver, data, visualisation and report components at runtime with the support of the runtime component generation layer. The scenario management layer integrates the various models and components which represent problem scenarios. The integration layer contains integration components such as kernel, mapping and validation components, and provides infrastructures for integration of models and components, and supports development of the DSS. The layers and components view of the SMART architecture is displayed in Fig. 4 below.

The SMART architecture supports the integration of different models and modelling environments; it supports runtime component and control generation, runtime object generation, dynamic GUI generation, dynamic visualisation, data and document modelling, and scenario lifecycle management; it enables the capturing of input and output data from a scenario and importing or exporting them to a different type of scenario. With the support of the kernel, the document model of the component layer would provide the services of a variety of application packages using a number of third party application-programming interfaces (APIs) and manage a sustainability knowledge base to support the discovery and learning phase of the SBT roadmap. The data and report models of the component and object layers can be used to support the decision makers during this discovery and learning phase for simulating and displaying previously developed sustainability and sustainable business process models and reports. The documentation model would support documentation of newly discovered knowledge. The sustainability model of the component layer with the support of the access services layer, Kernel and the SMART framework provides the services for the strategise phase of the roadmap.

Similarly, the process, data and service models, access service layer, and kernel support the process design activities, and the data and document models support data, function, control and organisation modelling for support of the design phase. The document and data models are used to support sustainability project planning, and project progress monitoring and controlling for realisation of the transform phase. The documents, reports and data models support realisation of the monitoring and controlling phase. The report model contains several types of dashboard support, monitoring activities and also supports preparation of the sustainability models at different levels of abstraction. The SMART architecture is <sup>fl</sup>exible, scalable, extendable and customisable, and can be implemented as a range of modules and sub systems. It is also generic and pattern-oriented and is suitable for implementation as an n-tiered system.

Fig. 4. SMART architecture: layered and component view.

Object-oriented methodologies and technologies support independent development of the SMART architectural components. Each component is compiled as a dynamic link library (DLL) or as an executable sub-system. The integration component combines the DLLs and executables at runtime. Though the SMART environment is designed using mainly object-oriented methodology, the SMART architecture supports development of any components provided from separate environments. For example, the sustainability model, process model, domain model, and data model are developed, stored, and implemented using system dynamics modelling, work<sup>fl</sup>ow modelling, object modelling, and data modelling environments respectively. On the other hand, multiple environments are used for modelling a component. For instance, sustainability models have been developed using a system dynamics modelling environment (iThink), object-oriented languages (such as VB.NET), and database management systems (such as SQL Server 2005); process models have been developed using a work<sup>fl</sup>ow modelling environment (such as work<sup>fl</sup>ow foundation), .NET Framework, VB.NET, SQL server, web services, etc.

The SMART architecture provides an information system infrastructure that extends the proposed SMART framework, and supports analysis and decision making in executing the SBT roadmap steps in the process of developing a sustainable business model. It manages various types of models such as sustainability, business process, scenario, domain, organisation, document, report and data models, and a host of components that provide system functions, integration, persistence, and connectivity services. The architecture is represented using a layer and component view that facilitates design, development, layering and integration mechanisms of the models and components, and a module and sub system view that supports implementation of the architecture as an n-tiered SMART system. The SMART architecture supports management of model lifecycles and multi-dimensional data, is domain and paradigm independent, and is <sup>fl</sup>exible, adaptable, extensible and customisable.

In the next section, we discuss implementation of the SMART architecture and analyse how it realises each phase of the SBT roadmap. The discussion also includes how the SMART framework, architecture and system support functionality, usability, reliability, performance and supportability features.

## 7. Sustainability modelling and reporting system

As part of the proof of concept, this research developed an evolutionary research prototype, termed the SMART system, using an iterative prototyping methodology for demonstrating and clarifying ideas [23] put forward as the SMART framework and architecture (Sections 5 and 6). The SMART system is a generic information systems infrastructure that supports con<sup>fi</sup>gurability, scalability, adaptability, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, extendibility, communicability, integrability and interactivity of the models and components, and supports management of the diverse models and modelling paradigms, and multi-dimensional data for addressing the research problems and issues. All these features are useful for facilitating the realisation of the SBT roadmap for managing sustainable businesses.

The SBT roadmap's <sup>fi</sup>ve high-level phases have been directly mapped as <sup>fi</sup>ve main menu items for user interaction as shown in the top part of Fig. 5. Each of these menu items contains a number of sub-menus (Fig. 6 displays Strategise sub-menus) for representing various micro-level steps for each of the SBT roadmap phases. As can be seen in Fig. 6 the SMART system was instantiated, con<sup>fi</sup>gured, tested and evaluated in the context of a tertiary education provider to explore various transportation scenarios. The education provider's operations, programme and course delivery, campus facilities, students and staff services are coordinated using traditional unsustainable approaches. The SMART system was used in this context to enable the education provider to become:

• <sup>fi</sup>nancially solvent in terms of operational expenses and capital in vestment in the short and medium term;

• environmentally friendly, with, for instance, low per capita ecological footprint and e-waste, and net zero carbon emission; and

• socially responsible, in that it caters for the skills requirements of local and regional industries, supports socio-economic background of the local and regional communities, takes care of health and safety issues, looks after students' well-being, retains students and employees, provides continuous staff training, and conducts research that enhances learning and supports industry.

In the following sections, we explore the SMART system and how it supports the sustainable business transformation roadmap.

## 7.1. The SBT roadmap: Discover and Learn

The Discover and Learn menu is linked to the Directed Knowledge Discovery and Learning, and Undirected Knowledge Discovery and Learning sub-systems. This connects to sustainability knowledge bases that contain only highly relevant useful resources on sustainability concepts and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of sustainability performance indicators, roadmap and frameworks, sustainable business modelling and management processes and plans, related business and industry documents, sustainability reports, example business cases, and previous experiences. The knowledge bases also contain various sustainability, business processes and scenario models, benchmarking documents and data. The SMART system dynamically generates a user interface at runtime based on the knowledge base contents. Documents and models can always be added, removed or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the knowledge base. The SMART system displays a selected document within its own environment irrespective of the creation software packages. It uses various APIs and document models to display them; it also retrieves data from the databases for displaying in a document; and it can also open a model from a third party environment such as sustainability simulation games for the decision makers to engage in playing and developing a sustainability vision and understanding how to develop sustainability strategies and to deal with business process models. It also supports interaction with third party programmes for running specialised models and documents. The user can then prepare a report using the SMART system on discover and learn steps of the roadmap before proceeding to the strategise phase.

## 7.2. The SBT roadmap: Strategise

The Strategise menu is linked to four sub-systems for interacting with both internal and external sustainability modelling systems and environments. It facilitates importing, generating a dynamic graphical user interface, instantiating, and simulating sustainability models, and supports sustainability scenario analysis and strategy documentation. The sustainability models are associated with the SMART system using a con<sup>fi</sup>guration <sup>fi</sup>le, and a drop down menu displays the sustainability models. Fig. 6 shows an example of a transportation sustainability model that has been developed in a system dynamics modelling environment, and the SMART system has imported, instantiated, simulated (three times), analysed and visualised the output.

The SMART system presents simulated outputs in both tabular and graphical forms, which can be output in a single canvas through drag and drop for scenario comparison, evaluation and analysis purposes. This facilitates comparison of the outputs and inputs of various sustainability models within one interface. This scenario analysis integrates the social, environmental and economic dimensions, and supports the decision makers in identifying the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats of the current, most likely, best case and worst case scenarios and thus helps in developing sustainability strategies for the organisation. It also helps identify change requirements, critical success factors and key performance indicators. The SMART system supports annotations and storage of the graphs and tables as graphic objects or SMART objects to be made available to other decision makers or for further analysis in the future. The system also has the ability to save, update and retrieve the simulated output data to an existing database or to a new database. The SMART system automatically provides an intelligent wizard service for creating a new database or a new table to an existing database if the desired table is not available in the selected database. These input and output data and simulated graphs can be used in other phases of the SBT roadmap, especially in the design and reporting environment. This leads us to a discussion of the design phase activities.

## 7.3. The SBT roadmap: Design

The Design phase of the SBT roadmap is realised through the Design menu which is linked to eight sub-systems: Process Model, Scenario Model, Object Model, Data Model, Organisation Model, Function Model, Business Blueprint, and Design Documentation. The Design menu supports <sup>fl</sup>ow-through of the roadmap processes as well as integration of business process models and their simulation and analysis for the business process design. This design phase includes development and analysis of the scenario model (Fig. 6 shows an example scenario model), development of domain object, data models, organisation model and functional model, design documentation and preparation of a business blueprint.

The Business Process Modelling sub-system supports business process reengineering using activities, event/state <sup>fl</sup>ow or service oriented process modelling. It supports the development of TO-BE (prescriptive) process models as well as comparison with the AS-IS (descriptive) process model of the selected sustainable business scenario within Visio and Windows Work<sup>fl</sup>ow Foundation process modelling environments. The SMART system supports importing, instantiating, simulating and analysing the process models developed outside the SMART environment. It supports the creation of GUI at runtime and the use of transaction and DSS data for the model instantiation and output management within the SMART environment. It can add new activities/events to the process models at runtime for simulation, testing and process evaluation. During the simulation and analysis process, it identi<sup>fi</sup>es requirements and suitability of various steps, events, activities, services, tasks and other organisational resources and organisational units.

Scenario and Object Modelling sub-systems facilitate the management of decision scenarios through scenario generation, instantiation, execution and what-if analysis. It creates new scenarios using a combination of entity and service models, solvers, data and visualisations as shown in Fig. 7. It also incorporates an infrastructure that supports development of the object and service models and solvers at runtime. This object modelling system supports code generation, compilation into a dynamic link library, and object creation at runtime. These objects can be of model or solver type and they can be used for scenario development at runtime. The current data and visualisation objects are <sup>fl</sup>exible and they are suitable for data management and visualisations. This scenario analysis provides decision making support for redesigning business process and developing a business blueprint.

The Business Blueprint sub-system supports presentation of the business using various views, such as Sustainability View, Organisation View, Process View, CSF View, KPI View, and Objective Function View. For example, an Organisation View illustrates hierarchical relationships from organisation to department to strategies to CSF to KPI to Business Process to operations to tasks and rules, etc. It helps to identify any gaps or unintended relationships (either missing or unnecessary elements) within the organisational structure, strategies, processes, CSFs, KPIs, activities, tasks, rules, conditions and parameters.

The Data Modelling sub-system supports creation of a new database and also modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of existing databases at runtime. The user can add a new table to an existing database or add, modify and delete columns in an existing table. It facilitates dynamic modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a database, when a user needs to save records but the selected database does not contain the necessary table(s) and/or column(s).

The Organisation and Functional Modelling sub-systems: The Organisation Model sub-system provides an environment (Visio, MS Excel and Ms Word) for designing and developing a new organisation structure which supports the newly designed business processes. The Functional Model sub-system provides an environment for requirements analysis and system design of the newly designed processes and services. This includes, among others, use case design, class design, sequence diagram and collaboration diagram that support the development of organisational information systems.

The Design Documentation sub-system connects to the design related knowledge base that deals with process, scenario, domain object, data, organisation, and function models. It also presents the design reports and business blueprints. The design documents created in this phase are used in the transform phase as discussed below.

## 7.4. The SBT roadmap: transform

The Transform phase of the SBT roadmap is implemented using six sub-systems: Key Documents, Project Scheduling, Organisation Transformation, Process Transformation, IT Transformation, and Transformation Status Report. The SMART system brings all the planning and design documents in one knowledge-base as the Key Documents, for example sustainable business scenarios, strategic planning documents, TO-BE process models, data models, information systems models, control model, and organisation model so that the user can access them from a single environment. The user then prepares the transformation project plans and schedules the activities, and undertakes the transformation activities. As the execution activities move on, the change manager prepares the transformation project progress management and monitoring reports in terms of the transformation of Business Processes, IT, and Organisation. The system supports the preparation of the Transformation Status Report. Once the transformation is complete, we need to monitor and control the execution of the processes that lead towards sustainability and transformation of the organisation.

## 7.5. The SBT roadmap: monitor and control

The SMART system supports the monitoring and controlling of the organisational, sustainability KPI, and process performances using various dynamic dashboards available through a number of reporting sub-systems such as, Ad-hoc Reports, Organisation Benchmark Report, Organisation Performance Indicators, Strategic Performance Indicators, Process Benchmark Report, Ad-hoc Report Documents, Strategy Documents, Design Documents, Transform Documents, KPI Report, Monitor and Control Report and All Saved Reports. The dashboards are easy to use monitoring and controlling devices which have rich dynamic runtime features, as shown in Fig. 8. For example, they can be dynamically connected to various transaction and DSS databases at runtime. The dashboards allow <sup>fi</sup>ltering using any or all of the attributes, namely organisation, KPI, and business process. It displays a list of databases and datasets associated through con<sup>fi</sup>guration.

The system has several built-in dashboards and reports but new reports and dashboards can be added to the library at any time. It supports modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the existing dashboards and is capable of third party dashboards which are executable in the .NET environment. It also supports creation of ad-hoc dashboards and reports. The <sup>fi</sup>ltering process supports generation of a selected dashboard; for example KPI <sup>fi</sup>ltering displays the dashboard for a selected KPI and its constituent process dashboard. Similarly, a process <sup>fi</sup>lter presents the process KPI dashboards for the selected process irrespective of the organisation.

Each monitoring dashboard can be stored as a PDF document for non-expert users and as a knowledge base resource reference. An instance of a dashboard is useful for future reference, sustainability reporting and discovery and learning processes. The system also supports creation of additional analysis reports based on the monitoring and controlling experiences.

## 7.6. Supportability features of the SMART system

Many supportability features have been built-in into the SMART system: those of con<sup>fi</sup>gurability, connectivity, work<sup>fl</sup>ow support, compatibility, extensibility, maintainability, data versatility and integration, persistence, reporting versatility, model versatility and integration, modelling versatility, and adaptability. Several tools, such as con<sup>fi</sup>guration manager, scenario assessment, DB-Excel-DB conversion, third party system integration, objects' property management, and various modelling tools, as shown in Fig. 9, have been included for enhancing supportability features. These tools support the features, in particular versatility of models and modelling diversity of sustainability modelling, process modelling, scenario modelling, object modelling, and data modelling. The system provides dynamic support for database and model connectivity, integration of third party models and systems, extension of databases, con<sup>fi</sup>guration management, work<sup>fl</sup>ow, reporting versatility and adaptability.

Due to these customisability and adaptability features, the SMART system can be used in a variety of business scenarios and contexts. However, although we have incorporated a number of built-in models for each of the SBT roadmap phases these may need customisation. They may also not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for an unknown business context. Therefore, a modeller needs to build various business speci<sup>fi</sup>c contextual models such as sustainability, business process, scenario, domain, data and report, and a SMART system administrator needs to integrate these models with the SMART system using the con<sup>fi</sup>guration sub-system. Many of these models can also be developed within the SMART environment such as data, object, scenario, business process, report models. In addition, the SMART system can dynamically integrate third party models developed using many other platforms such as iThink, .NET Framework, any database environment, etc. and use them for sustainability scenario analysis.

## 8.1. Critical review of the research artefacts

The SBT Roadmap is a procedural guideline that represents a macro-level sustainable business transformation life cycle that is developed by integrating various concepts of the currently available procedural roadmaps, frameworks and methodologies as described in Section 4. The SBT roadmap is realised using the SMART system artefacts which are deeply rooted to the concepts of established IS and DSS frameworks, architectures and systems. The SMART framework provides transitional methodologies for stepping towards the technological framework from the procedural framework. The process- and model-oriented view of the framework presents requirements of the information systems and operations management models for sustainable business management, and the model and component interaction view provides interaction of various models and components. This framework is a high-level design pattern that supports a variety of information systems and DSS frameworks. The SMART architectures have been designed and developed based on the SMART frameworks. The proposed architecture realises all the features of the framework and provides a useful technological infrastructure for the end user. It provides detailed design of the models, components, modules, and subsystems, and organises them in various layers that support multi-tiered implementation. Each of the architectural models and components is supported by various modelling and system design methodologies; these have been separately designed and developed but implemented as an integrated system. They include, among others, system dynamics, balanced scorecard, process modelling, report modelling, object modelling, etc. The system also supports the model life cycle, namely representation, storage, retrieval, modi<sup>fi</sup>cation, integration, instantiation, simulation, validation, visualisation and deletion of the model. The SMART system is highly con<sup>fi</sup>gurable and supports runtime extension, runtime dynamic scenario modelling, and document and data modelling, usage of third party developed system dynamics and business process models. It also supports runtime generation of sustainability reports. The system can be connected with various types of data sources and databases. The GUI is simple, and directly mapped to the SBT roadmap, and this decreases memory load. A naïve user with basic computer knowledge will be able to use the system but more complicated activities such as scenario modelling, dynamic data modelling, object modelling, and creation of new and ad-hoc reports need to be undertaken by the model builders and advanced users. It also supports importing the system dynamics model, process model and thirdparty systems. Thus, the SMART system can be used as a modelling tool and a DSS that is highly <sup>fl</sup>exible, extensible and adaptable.

## 9. Conclusion

Sustainable business development is a lengthy journey that needs to be guided by a procedural framework and supported by a technological infrastructure which caters to decision makers' needs for smooth transition from a traditional business to an innovative sustainable business through the successful infusion of sustainability concepts. This research proposes a SBT Roadmap as a procedural framework, and a generic domain-independent SMART framework and architecture as a technological infrastructure for realising the SBT roadmap phases and steps. The roadmap prescribes diverse modelling paradigms (economic, social, and environmental) and methodologies (such as sustainability, balanced scorecard, business process, scenario, domain object, report, data and document modelling). Representation and integration of these models are complex processes, as the modellers need different modelling methodologies as well as different technological environments for developing useful models. The SMART framework is the <sup>fi</sup>rst step towards conversion and realisation of the SBT roadmap using information systems. The conceptual view of the framework exhibits highly abstracted relationships among the major business and information systems issues, namely model representation, modelling processes and systems, and reporting processes and systems. The process and model oriented view describes a process for business analysts and modellers for identifying the models required for each phase of the SBT roadmap, model description and modelling processes. The model and component interaction view of the SMART framework presents the interaction and integration process of the models, supporting and integrating infrastructure and persistence components. The SMART framework and architecture encapsulate various models, modelling and reporting methodologies, and their integration processes for developing sustainable business model and sustainability reports. The SMART framework and its associated architecture are modular, versatile, and extensible leveraging of systems thinking, object-orientation and componentisation concepts.

The SMART system supports the roadmap phases and steps, macro-level and micro-level decision-making lifecycles, representation of models and their integration, and management of multidimensional data. It is adaptable, <sup>fl</sup>exible and con<sup>fi</sup>gurable. The SMART system provides high quality support for the roadmap phases and steps, and the SMART framework, architecture and system have addressed the need for con<sup>fi</sup>gurability, connectivity, and data, model and report versatility. The SMART infrastructure supports runtime con<sup>fi</sup>guration, access to various services including third party systems, and characterises the management features of multidimensional data and modelling diversity. The experts observed that the SMART system performed impressively with regards usability, performance and reliability requirements. The SMART system is simple, easy to learn and suitable for use by decision makers.

The key research artefacts, namely the SBT roadmap, and the SMART framework, architecture and system have been evaluated at various development life cycle stages during each iteration of the prototyping. The underlying concepts of these artefacts are supported by theories of various roadmaps, frameworks, architectures and systems already available in the sustainability, information systems and DSS domains. Overall, the evaluation con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the problems, issues and requirements identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Section 2 have been respectively solved, addressed and ful<sup>fi</sup>lled.

## 10. Future research directions

Many of the current enterprise resources planning (ERP) systems support a number of the steps of the SBT roadmap and the integration of a limited number of modelling methodologies incorporated in the SMART framework. Future research might investigate how easily this roadmap, framework and architecture can be incorporated into the widely used ERP systems. For that, we would need to investigate how the entire body of SMART artefacts can be added as a component of those existing information systems that take advantage of current transaction processing systems, and enable them to adopt a sustainable business management process. Rigorous evaluation through realisation of the design artefacts in various types of business environments is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult in design science research [55,48]; this may limit generalisation to other environments [29]. The roadmap, and the SMART framework and architecture have been tested using a business scenario but they need to be tested within the entire business.

The prototype has been implemented in the transportation, printing and supply chain domains in the context of educational institutes, and warehousing and distribution companies. While we have conducted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant expert evaluation, there is potential for the enhancement of the SBT roadmap and SMART system artefacts through in-depth <sup>fi</sup>eld studies and laboratory experimentations. We look forward to their being tested in wider domains such as construction, manufacturing, global trades, government departments and city councils.
