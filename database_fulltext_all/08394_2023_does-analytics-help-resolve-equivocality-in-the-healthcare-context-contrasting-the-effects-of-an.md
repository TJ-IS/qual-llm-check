# Does Analytics Help Resolve Equivocality in the Healthcare Context? Contrasting the Effects of Analyzability and Differentiation

Idris Adjerid,<sup>1</sup> Corey M. Angst,<sup>2</sup> Sarv Devaraj,<sup>3</sup> Nicholas Berente<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Information Technology, Virginia Tech, USA, iadjerid@vt.edu <sup>2</sup>IT, Analytics, and Operations Department, University of Notre Dame, USA, cangst@nd.edu <sup>3</sup>IT, Analytics, and Operations Department, University of Notre Dame, USA, sdevaraj@nd.edu <sup>4</sup>IT, Analytics, and Operations Department, University of Notre Dame, USA, nberente@nd.edu

## Abstract

Organizations are increasingly using data analytics to help make decisions and drive positive outcomes. But organizational scholarship has warned us that the sort of information processing associated with analytic capabilities, while effective for uncertainty reduction, may be less effective in equivocal contexts. Equivocality is evident when tasks are not easily analyzable (task analyzability) or when organizational departments are highly differentiated (differentiation). We hypothesize that analytics will be less effective in driving positive outcomes when equivocality is high because of low task analyzability. However, when an organization is more differentiated, resulting in high equivocality, we anticipate that analytics will be more effective in driving positive outcomes. To test this theory, we studied how clinical healthcare analytics influenced experientia quality (akin to patient satisfaction) in over 3,000 hospitals across nine years. Our results show that analytics capabilities, on average, do improve outcomes in terms of patient experiential quality, suggesting that analytics can reduce uncertainty, but we also found evidence for the moderating role of equivocality. Specifically, as task analyzability decreases (i.e., increasing equivocality), clinical healthcare analytics becomes less effective in improving experiential quality. However, when equivocality is high because of differentiation, there is a positive relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and experiential quality but only in larger hospitals. From a managerial perspective, this study has implications for boundary conditions of data analytics in organizations.

Keywords: Data Analytics, Clinical Healthcare Analytics, Experiential quality, Patient-Centric Performance, Healthcare technology, Longitudinal, Information Processing, OIPT, Uncertainty, Equivocality, Analyzability, Differentiation, Variety

## 1 Introduction

Over the last two decades, hospitals have generated massive amounts of data that can be analyzed to inform healthcare professionals, solve patient problems, and provide better-quality care (e.g., Agarwal et al., 2010; Kc et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019). However, it is important to avoid thinking of analytics as the answer in every situation. Healthcare professionals draw on years of training and experience to make tough decisions in complex situations—analytics is no replacement for human judgment and human care (Chan, 2016; Mesko, 2015).

In this paper, we attempt to address a fundamental question central to healthcare information systems research: In what organizational contexts are analytics more effective? Finding answers to this question will certainly benefit practitioners as well. If data analytics is about aiding decision makers by processing widespread and ever-growing sources of information and providing codified, quantified guidance, what are the limits to this sort of quantified guidance? What theoretical lens might provide a window to examine the boundary conditions of the effectiveness of analytics? We address these and other questions in what follows, but first offer some preliminary definitions and background information.

“Clinical” analytics in healthcare refers to the technology and organizational capabilities that enable the analysis of historical clinical data to directly support patient care in terms of clinical issues such as diagnosis, prognosis, and treatments (Bates et al., 2014; Wang & Krishnan, 2014). Although there are a variety of ways that hospitals might use analytics to improve operations<sup>1</sup> as well as patient diagnosis and outcomes, <sup>2</sup> clinical healthcare analytics directly supports clinician decisions in their interactions with patients (Guha & Kumar, 2018).

As noted, some caution is necessary regarding the use of analytics for patient care (Chan, 2016; Mesko, 2015). However, there is general agreement that arming clinicians with better predictions, reducing uncertainty, and providing evidence-based protocols will lead to improved patient outcomes, including how positively patients view their experience (Kc et al., 2020). Improving patient experiential quality (sometimes called patient satisfaction) is an important goal for hospitals (Institute of Medicine, 2001; Betts et al., 2016; Tsai et al., 2015), in part because perceptions of lowquality care are notoriously difficult to overcome (Rau, 2015; Siegrist, 2013) and even minor improvements in the patient experience can lead to dramatic reputational effects for healthcare providers (Elliott et al., 2015). Thus, patient experiential quality has emerged as a critical outcome variable for the study of healthcare in a host of domains, including information systems, operations management, and medical informatics research (e.g., Chandrasekaran et al., 2012; Li et al., 2020; Meyerhoefer et al., 2018; Romanow et al., 2018; Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016; Wowak et al., in press). In general, the value created by analytics capabilities and the conditions under which this value accrues remain unclear (Jernigan et al., 2016; Ransbotham et al., 2016; Strong et al., 2014; Trieu, 2017); this is certainly the case for the impact of clinical healthcare analytics on patient experiential quality.

We draw on organizational information processing theory (OIPT) (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Galbraith, 1977; Goodhue et al., 1992; Te’eni, 2001) to understand the influence of clinical healthcare analytics on patient experiential quality. Organizational information processing is a useful framework for evaluating the impact of clinical healthcare analytics on the patient experience since it points out that reductions in uncertainty or equivocality will improve performance in different ways (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft and Macintosh, 1981; Te’eni, 2001). Uncertainty is the difference between the amount of information required to perform a task and the amount of information already possessed (Galbraith, 1977). Equivocality, on the other hand, involves ambiguity in the information available (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Equivocality occurs due to a lack of understanding, indeterminate outcomes, minimal problem structures, multiple perspectives, and few precedents to a situation (Daft et al., 1987).

We theorize that clinical healthcare analytics fundamentally impacts patient experiential quality by processing information in order to reduce uncertainty. However, different contexts vary in their level of equivocality and equivocal contexts do not necessarily benefit from more information processing because these situations generally require more human experience, judgment, and collaboration (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Daft & Weick, 1984; Dong, 2021). As a result, equivocal situations should, in theory, see less value from the types of analyses and predictions that are foundational to clinical healthcare analytics. However, there is little understanding of whether clinical healthcare analytics has a greater or lesser impact in equivocal contexts and situations. Importantly, equivocality is not a discrete, easily bounded thing— although it is often treated as such. Equivocality is typically associated with tasks that are not easily analyzable <sup>3</sup> and with contexts that generate multiple, often conflicting interpretations of information about those tasks (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Goodhue et al.,

1992; Haußmann et al., 2012). In a healthcare context, the analyzability of clinical tasks directly relates to the severity and multitude of ailments (comorbidities) affecting patients—complex, difficult cases with many interactions are difficult to analyze.

Relatedly, in the healthcare context, situations that are subject to multiple potential interpretations can result from high levels of context differentiation within the hospital (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005). Hospitals that specialize in clinical areas such as pediatric cardiology or cancer will be less differentiated, whereas those that are more general and treat a greater variety of patients will be more differentiated. Cooper and Wolfe (2005) point out how the differentiation among departments can contribute to equivocality because differentiated departments “have distinct specialties,<sup>4</sup> time horizons, goals, frames of reference, and jargon” (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005, p. 34). Thus, we expect that task analyzability and context differentiation generate equivocality in healthcare contexts. We distinguish between these two forms of equivocality and theorize that equivocality arising from low task analyzability diminishes the value of clinical analytics but clinical analytics can effectively reduce equivocality associated with context differentiation. We discuss this in greater detail below.

In this study, we leveraged a nine-year panel dataset (2007-2015) of 18,082 observations across 3,138 hospitals to explore the relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and the patient experience, as well as the impact of task equivocality on this relationship. We analyzed between-hospital and across-time variation through a difference-in-differences (DID) estimation approach with propensity score matching. We found that clinical healthcare analytics can positively impact the patient’s clinical care experience but that this effect is nuanced and has heterogeneous effects in different care settings. In particular, we found support for our hypotheses that the benefits of clinical healthcare analytics are diminished when contexts are equivocal because of reduced task analyzability but the benefits of clinical healthcare analytics persist when equivocality is generated from context differentiation—albeit only in larger hospitals.

This study contributes to existing research in a number of ways. First, by drawing on OIPT we identify uncertainty reduction as the main theoretical mechanism for the positive relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and patient experiential quality; we also point out that the equivocality generated by the complexity of the healthcare environment influences this relationship. Further, by distinguishing between task analyzability and context differentiation, we go beyond typical studies of equivocality to show how different characteristics of complex situations contribute to equivocality in different ways and demonstrate that these different forms of equivocality impact the relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and the patient experience. Given the widespread adoption and diffusion of analytical tools in the healthcare domain, it is increasingly important for research to delve into how organizational context influences the value of analytics. Further, evidence appears to be converging that healthcare technologies drive improvements in quality and efficiency but in nuanced ways (Baird et al., 2018). We contribute to the current frontier of this research, which examines how analytics based on data from earlier generations of information technologies (e.g., electronic health records, radiology information systems) can improve the quality and efficiency of healthcare (Murdoch & Detsky, 2013). During the time frame of our study, clinical healthcare analytics likely were not using artificial intelligence (AI) technologies extensively; however, like analytics tools, AI is fundamentally a prediction technology that conducts fast and efficient information processing (Agrawal et al., 2019). Thus, our results can inform how we think about the potential benefits of using AI in clinical healthcare contexts.

Overall, the results of this research provide a perspective for hospital administrators and clinicians to understand the conditions under which clinical healthcare analytics can provide value and to distinguish between different forms of equivocality in clinical contexts. It may not be surprising that analytics can improve conformance to care guidelines and offer specific treatment options but it is likely less obvious that the benefits of analytics are apparent to patients as well. With the tremendous importance that hospital administrators place on the patient experience, our findings that analytics can play a role in improving the patient experience is highly significant for administrators and clinicians.

## 2 Clinical Healthcare Analytics and Information Processing

Clinical healthcare analytics involves the technologies and capabilities that support the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of patients and, as such, can be distinguished from other forms of analytics, such as those that support hospital operations (Guha & Kumar, 2018). One reason that clinical healthcare analytics has been attracting more attention in recent years is that information technologies have matured, are now widely diffused, and currently generate large stores of clinical data that support analytics (Angst et al., 2010). Government actions such as the HITECH Act have contributed to this diffusion of technology—for example, the requirement that electronic health records (EHRs) be used by all healthcare providers (Blumenthal, 2010). Although there are a variety of EHR vendors, they all involve collecting and storing standardized patient data that can be readily analyzed. With these rich repositories of data emerging from the use of EHRs<sup>5</sup> and other health information technologies, analytics can be used in a variety of ways, including improving clinical decision-making. In what follows, we describe specific examples of the role that analytics can play in improving care delivery and, ultimately, the patient experience, but it is important to recognize that EHRs can contribute to the reduction of uncertainty as well (Lanham et al., 2013). Further, we point out how the contribution of clinical healthcare analytics to the reduction of uncertainty goes beyond what EHRs provide.

Specifically, we argue that analytics—in particular, clinical healthcare analytics—is different from other forms of health IT such as EHRs. Much of the work in healthcare focuses on EHRs as the IT artifact of interest (e.g., Angst et al., 2017; Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016; Strong et al., 2014). Fully functional EHRs are very broad technologies that provide numerous services, including but not limited to, data repositories, decision support, order entry, and notes (Adler-Milstein et al., 2014; Dranove et al., 2014). In fact, clinical analytics’ functions can be delivered through EHR modules or interfaces, meaning that studies of general EHR use could miss or blur the effects of individual functionalities like analytics. We believe it is important to move beyond EHRs as a black box and consider how clinical analytics contributes value. To that end, the promise of clinical healthcare analytics is considerable for a variety of applications. Take, for instance, oncology. Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide and there are a variety of different cancers, many of which have different treatment paths at various stages. Some diseases have well-established treatment paths for particular diagnoses, such as early-stage prostate cancer or certain forms of leukemia. In these cases, clinical analytics could inform clinicians of treatment options and evidence of effectiveness (El Naqa, 2016). In other cases, there is more ambiguity regarding courses of action. Thus, although it is clear that these investments can generate significant value for healthcare, several indications suggest that value may be greater in some contexts and less salient in others. Chan (2016) argues that human intuition and clinical decision-making should take priority over computer-based methods, especially in complex situations that can benefit from the training and experience of healthcare professionals. Along similar lines, Mesko (2015) makes the case that there is nuance and uncertainty in all types of tasks related to caring for other humans. Further, humans appear to generally prefer human decision makers over algorithms, even when humans are less accurate (Dietvorst et al., 2015). People generally lose confidence in algorithms more quickly than humans when mistakes are detected.

However, there are potential limitations of analytics. For example, advanced stages of cancer require aggressive treatments such as chemoradiotherapy. While analytics could help address some of the issues surrounding information uncertainty by aggregating and correlating relevant information for patient care (diagnostic and patient information, physiological metrics, etc.), advanced cases are often idiosyncratic and ambiguous and include a variety of confounding issues. According to El Naqa (2016), advanced oncology cases are often subject to “the classical statistical inference problem of large number of variables with relatively small number of samples” (p. 33). Even when drawing on “big data” across healthcare organizations, there are a variety of problematic issues that limit clear inferences from the data, including spurious correlations, selection bias, Yule-Simpson effects, and “ghost analytics” that result from arbitrary threshold values (see El Naqa, 2016). Relatedly, prior work has shown that analytics should be configured with other organizational processes to produce positive effects (Park and Mithas, 2020). Thus, certain forms of complex diseases and advanced stages of cancer may stand to glean less value from clinical analytics than other kinds of ailments.

Thus, although there is general agreement that more data and evidence-based insight should be valuable in clinical applications (Straus et al., 2019), clinical healthcare analytics may influence the patient experience differently in varied contexts. This question regarding the value that clinical healthcare analytics brings to different contexts directs our theorizing on how clinical healthcare analytics impacts the experiential quality of patients and the factors that moderate this impact. Experiential quality has been identified in a literature review (Cleary & McNeil, 1988) as a key indicator of quality care. In recent years, experiential quality has been used quite extensively in the healthcare, operations, and IS literature (in part because it is a standardized and validated means for direct comparison across hospitals; Chandrasekaran et al., 2012; Giordano et al., 2009; Jha et al., 2008; Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016). Our basic premise is that clinical healthcare analytics reduces uncertainty in healthcare service provisioning by processing information to complement the activities of providers but that the value of this information processing varies in different contexts. Our contention generally aligns with Lyytinen and Grover’s (2017) premise that data analytics can offer new ways to learn and improve decision processing. Therefore, we draw on OIPT to build out this argument and conceptualize these different contexts.

## 2.1 Organizational Information Processing Theory

The premise of OIPT is that organizations are continually faced with uncertainty and leverage a variety of different approaches to reduce this uncertainty (Galbraith, 1977). Uncertainty is defined as the difference between the information required to perform a particular task and the information possessed by the organization (Galbraith, 1977). For low-uncertainty environments, organizational hierarchies and standardized procedures are suitable solutions; however, high-uncertainty environments, which can emerge when there is high task variety,<sup>6</sup> require more flexible mechanisms such as lateral relations and computerized information systems.

Building on this foundational view of information and uncertainty, OIPT was extended to include the notion of equivocality. Organizations process information to reduce equivocality (Daft & Lengel, 1986) but, in contrast to uncertainty, which involves insufficient information, equivocality is driven by ambiguity and more information does not necessarily help and may even be a source of equivocality (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Te’eni, 2001). Equivocality results from a lack of understanding caused by ambiguity, few task precedents, and poorly structured problems (Daft et al., 1987; Te’eni, 2001). It can also be caused by diverse and pluralistic contexts that lead to multiple interpretations of tasks (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Goodhue et al., 1992; Haußmann et al., 2012).

In the context of healthcare delivery, the OIPT perspective highlights how healthcare providers can improve patient outcomes by processing clinical information in ways that reduce uncertainty as well as equivocality (Flynn & Flynn, 1999; Galbraith, 1977). However, this perspective also suggests that this is not an easy feat. Reducing equivocality in clinical settings may require contextually rich information processing aided by human experience and more meaningful use of that information, which is much different from the sort of information processing required for reducing uncertainty (Beynon-Davies & Wang, 2019; Daft & Lengel, 1986; Goodhue et al., 1992). Indeed, “certain types of information processing mechanisms (such as computerized IS and, by implication, systems with integrated data) provide large amounts of information and can thus help reduce uncertainty. However, they are not as rich a source as other information processing mechanisms (such as face-to-face meetings) and thus are not as effective in reducing equivocality” (Goodhue et al., 1992, p. 298). Current capabilities of clinical healthcare analytics focusing on the aggregation and synthesis of large quantities of patient-centered information are likely well-suited for reducing uncertainty in clinical settings (Wang & Hajli, 2017) but perhaps less likely to reduce equivocality. Going back to our oncology example, if it is just a matter of uncertainty—i.e., the patient has clear genetic markers, a singular diagnosis, and a wellestablished treatment path—then processing this information and providing it to the provider and the patient will reduce uncertainty, increase confidence in the treatment, and thus will result in a more positive patient experience. On the other hand, if the situation involves greater equivocality—i.e., the patient has a variety of interacting ailments, there multiple treatment paths with unspecified differences, and there is little conclusive data available—then processing information may do little to improve the patient experience.

Overall, we expect that clinical healthcare analytics can positively impact the patient experience by reducing uncertainty (Balsa et al., 2003; Galbraith, 1974; Wang & Hajli, 2017). However, uncertainty reductions from clinical analytics may be less valuable in settings marked by equivocality. Scholars substantiate this notion and suggest that “when equivocality is present, the precise information needed to resolve the situation is not clear” and those technologies and capabilities which reduce uncertainty are less valuable in contexts where “cause and effect relationships are ambiguous and critical success factors are unclear and constantly shifting” (Goodhue et al., 1992, p. 298).

As noted, equivocality can come from two different sources: task analyzability and context differentiation (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Te’eni, 2001). Tasks are more analyzable when clearly correct paths can be identified through standards, organizational routines, and computational procedures (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Reus et al., 2009; Te’eni, 2001). In the clinical healthcare context, task analyzability is driven by patient-specific factors. The extent to which a patient is analyzable exists on a continuum from high to low. For example, a highly analyzable patient is one for whom near complete medical information is available, and the analytics system can thus provide clear evidence and information about diagnoses and treatments. In their description of business processes, Mani et al. (2010) would describe such a patient treatment process as “transactional.” Patients with low analyzability, in contrast, have conditions involving many unknowns in terms of either diagnosis or treatment—perhaps because there are multiple diseases and conditions or incomplete or erroneous medical records, making it difficult to offer definitive advice. These arguments suggest that the value of clinical healthcare analytics would be diminished with such patients.

Another important factor that can contribute to equivocality is the task context—some contexts are diverse and pluralistic and thus subject to multiple diverse interpretations. Building upon the original work in OIPT, subsequent scholars have refined it to include nuances related to the departmental structure of organizations (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Haußmann et al., 2012), indicating that context differentiation is also a source of equivocality. In the healthcare context, we explore this construct—context differentiation— and its role in the impact of healthcare analytics on experiential quality. Some hospitals choose to specialize in a few specific treatment areas, attracting patients seeking specialists for treatment (Lewis et al., 2000), which can result in limited offerings focusing in specific areas at the expense of other areas. Greater differentiation is associated with greater equivocality in terms of what clinicians and analytics tools are expected to do at any particular moment. It is unclear whether the value of clinical healthcare analytics is diminished in contexts characterized by greater equivocality. Next, we unpack this argument—first by generating a baseline hypothesis around the relationship between clinical healthcare analytics on patient experience through uncertainty reduction, then by hypothesizing potential moderating effects emerging from different sources of equivocality.

## 2.2 Uncertainty: The Impact of Clinical Healthcare Analytics on Experiential Quality

We propose that clinical healthcare analytics will have a positive main effect on care quality due to reductions in uncertainty. Organizations can deal with high levels of uncertainty by increasing their ability to process information by adopting information technologies (Goodhue et al., 1992) and aligning IT strategies with their business strategy (Bergeron et al., 2004; Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Sabherwal et al., 2019). Because analytics tools are known to provide information at the point of care where it is needed most (Wang & Hajli, 2017), a robust healthcare analytics system that is integrated into the workflow of the clinicians, will lead to better case management.

Healthcare analytics systems are a type of information technology that is well-suited to uncertainty reduction because of their data management and analytics capabilities (Wang & Hajli, 2017). Given many of the processes and activities necessary for the provision of effective clinical healthcare involve a good deal of uncertainty in decision-making (Balsa et al., 2003; Gittell, 2002; Wang & Hajli, 2017; Wennberg, 1985), this reduced uncertainty will ultimately be reflected in improved patient outcomes. Uncertainty is perhaps the single most important factor influencing physician behavior (Balsa et al., 2003; Galbraith, 1974). The more information a physician can access regarding the diagnosis and treatment of similar cases and the more guidance offered by analytics tools, the more likely the physician will be to provide superior care. For example, sepsis is a potentially life-threatening complication of an infection. <sup>7</sup> Clinical standards for sepsis care require early and accurate identification followed by the timely administration of antibiotics and fluids. But sepsis is not always easily identifiable—there is often uncertainty regarding both diagnosis and treatment that can be solved by processing patient information quickly and comparing it to existing models and datasets. Clinical healthcare analytics capabilities that monitor and analyze patient vitals can provide early identification of sepsis and help hospitals provide reliable care conforming to clinical standards (Taylor et al., 2016). Clinical healthcare analytics can also improve reliability in care outcomes by helping reduce adverse drug events (ADEs). <sup>8</sup> Specifically, clinical healthcare analytics allows for processes that automatically assess the ADE risk and flag high-risk situations (Harpaz et al., 2012; Savage, 2012).

Further, clinical healthcare analytics can enable physicians to navigate the uncertainty of patient-topatient differences and process information for better treatment customization. This is akin to what Cooper and Wolfe (2005) describe as task variety. In the face of this type of uncertainty, decision makers tend to rely on routines, habits, and heuristics that have worked in the past, which may undermine custom treatment paths that could potentially improve the patient experience. Clinical healthcare analytics can automatically reduce some of this uncertainty by segmenting patients into meaningful categories, allowing hospitals to more easily tailor patient care (Press Ganey, 2016). Since applying the same care processes to all patients is less effective and inefficient, the segmentation afforded by data analytics can allow hospitals to cater to the specific needs of subpopulations (Press Ganey, 2016).

It is important to note that clinical healthcare analytics does not need to process all of the relevant information to reduce uncertainty—as noted earlier, uncertainty reduction is a collaborative effort between the treating clinician and analytics tools. Physicians with more information about a diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment are likely to draw on that information to complement other sources of information. Physicians do not blindly follow the system’s prescriptions but use a variety of information to complement their judgment (Straus et al., 2019). For example, if a patient needs a knee replacement, providers can draw on various clinical healthcare analytics data sources to explore alternative approaches and procedures in terms of cost and outcomes for similar patients. Such evidence-based decisions have repeatedly been found to be superior to physicians relying on training, experience, and their own judgment alone (i.e., without processed information; see Straus et al., 2019).

Further, the availability of processed information will likely improve the patient experience in other ways as well. Additional information can improve the confidence of providers in their decisions, and this confidence can help to instill trust in the competence of the provider, which will improve the overall patient experience. For example, an interventional radiologist receiving analytics reports on specific cases prior to patient meetings can look for certain medical characteristics in advance to be better prepared when meeting new patients. Even if such customized treatment does not impact care outcomes, it can certainly improve the patient experience by increasing patients’ trust in their providers. More importantly, the presence of clinical healthcare analytics information about a situation can make situations seem less complex (Daft & Weick, 1984), which can reduce perceptions of uncertainty among both patients and providers.

Finally, off-loading some information processing tasks to analytics systems may increase providers’ abilities to engage in the high-touch, human-to-human interactions that are critical to positive healthcare experiences. Overall, clinical healthcare analytics can reduce uncertainty and improve outcomes by, for example, improving the coordination of care, reducing medical errors, informing decision-making, strengthening communication, instilling more confidence in providers, and freeing up time for fulfilling interpersonal interactions with patients. Thus, consistent with fundamental OIPT insights, we propose that by reducing uncertainty, clinical hospital analytics can improve patient experiential quality, and hypothesize:

H1: Clinical healthcare analytics will positively influence experiential quality.

## 2.3 Equivocality

Another insight from OIPT is that, although information processing can reduce uncertainty, in situations involving equivocality, the effect of analytics tools is less certain. Equivocality can involve contradictory information (“mixed signals”) that may be subject to a variety of interpretations or simply lack clarity or familiarity (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Te’eni, 2001). Equivocal tasks result from complex contexts that require richer and more varied approaches to information processing and resist quantification and codifiable solutions (Daft & Lengel, 1986). More recent work expanding OIPT states that equivocality can also emerge because of departmental differentiation within organizations (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Haußmann et al., 2012). The challenge of communicating between groups is exacerbated by differentiation and often involves situations that are ambiguous and difficult to interpret (Allen & Cohen, 1969). Cooper and Wolfe (2005) point out that when differentiation is high, there is often a lot of ambiguity, conflict, and misunderstanding in information processing, leading to equivocality. Thus, decision makers in uncertain contexts seek to acquire data to solve problems (Daft et al., 1987), whereas in equivocal contexts, decision makers may not even know that information exists within the organization or the barriers between departments might be difficult to overcome without a concerted effort to improve communication and information sharing (Cooper and Wolfe, 2005; Haußmann et al., 2012).

Information systems and management research traditions have long understood that complex tasks drive equivocality (e.g., Daft et al., 1987; Dennis & Kinney, 1998; Koufteros et al., 2002; Lim & Benbasat, 2000; Te’eni, 2001) but, as noted, all equivocality is not the same. Building on prior literature (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Perrow, 1967) and drawing on Cooper and Wolfe (2005) and Haußmann et al. (2012), we argue that equivocality can result from task analyzability and context differentiation. Task analyzability refers to the clarity and unambiguity of a task—i.e., the more understandable and predictable the context, the more analyzable the task (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Te’eni, 2001). Context differentiation is an organizational measure that assesses the extent to which departments are unique (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005). In hospitals, highly differentiated departments emerge when the hospital’s focus is low. Both task analyzability and differentiation result from dynamism, change, and plurality but they have different implications for outcomes associated with different forms of information processing (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft & Weick, 1984; Te’eni, 2001).

Specifically, we posit that the value of clinical healthcare analytics is diminished in contexts that are high in equivocality due to low task analyzability because the advanced information processing provided by analytics does not typically address lack of structure in terms of what needs to be done and how to do it. Conversely, we expect that clinical healthcare analytics yields additional value in contexts that are high in equivocality due to high differentiation because equivocality from differentiation typically results from human limitations, hubris, interdepartmental politics, and conflicts (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005) between clinicians and administrators rather than ambiguity in terms of what needs to be done. As a result, we conjecture that this type of equivocality can be aided by the advanced information processing that analytics provides because clinical analytics does not recognize departmental barriers. Next, we explain this logic in the development of two opposing hypotheses about the moderating influences of equivocality.

## 2.3.1 Equivocality and Task Analyzability

One source of equivocality in healthcare involves the characteristics of the patient population. Some patient populations are more complex than others, in terms of case severity or the diversity of clinical needs (Fetter et al., 1980). This complexity can vary: At the low end of the spectrum are hospitals that tend to treat lowcomplexity patients characterized by fewer comorbidities and less-severe medical conditions. Care for such patients generally involves less equivocality because providers are likely to be familiar with the ailments they are treating and thus more confident about their diagnoses and treatments. In these contexts, the decisions impacting patient outcomes are likely to be more predictable and the care task more analyzable. At the high end of the spectrum are hospitals that treat a greater variety of patients in terms of comorbidities and the range and severity of ailments, meaning that diagnoses and treatment decisions are more complex and require more contextual information, making the outcomes less predictable and the care task less analyzable.

While in hospitals with low complexity, uncertainty often still exists, (e.g., which antibiotic would be most appropriate based on the patient’s history), this uncertainty can be readily resolved by processing additional information. Thus, in such cases, significant value may be derived from the reduction of uncertainty. In contrast, hospitals with high complexity often deal with more comorbidities, rarer diseases, and conditions that are more difficult to treat, meaning that providers are more focused on reducing equivocality that is specifically related to task ambiguity in order to deliver quality care and improve patient experiential quality.

For example, consider a scenario where a provider is faced with a patient with symptoms that do not clearly indicate a particular condition. This leaves the provider unsure about the diagnosis and treatment. Equivocality in such a case cannot be readily addressed by the standardized, codifiable information processing capabilities associated with analytics systems (Goodhue et al., 1992; Te’eni, 2001). Thus, as the complexity of the characteristics of the patient pool increases, equivocality increases, and the value of clinical analytics is diminished (as compared to tasks with high analyzability). Hospitals that treat patients with less complexity stand to benefit most from clinical healthcare analytics because the cases they treat are simply more analyzable and involve less equivocality.

H2: The influence of clinical healthcare analytics on experiential quality will be greater in hospitals where task analyzability is higher.

## 2.3.2 Equivocality and Context Differentiation

Another source of equivocality in hospitals results from differentiation—i.e., how many specializations a particular hospital has. Hospital specialization (e.g., oncology, cardiology, pediatrics, etc.) directly concentrates on specific sets of service offerings (Ding, 2014; Huckman & Pisano, 2006; Kc & Terwiesch, 2011; Skinner, 1974). Highly focused, specialized hospitals will generally be less differentiated because they see more consistency in the types of patients treated, allowing them to develop expertise in specific clinical areas and thus reducing the overall number of functions performed in a given hospital (Ding, 2014). Specialized hospitals typically have well-established institutional knowledge about the diagnoses and treatments indicated for their particular area(s) of focus. Clinicians in specialized hospitals are more likely to be experts in a particular clinical context. They have likely developed high levels of trust in their own judgment rather than clinical healthcare analytics tools, and they may perceive such tools to be too simplistic to be useful. Importantly, hospitals with low context differentiation do not necessarily have simpler care cases (i.e., high task analyzability). Specialist hospitals may have patients with unique needs and may focus on highly complex cases. The task analyzability in less differentiated hospitals may be higher, lower, or equal to that of more differentiated hospitals.

In contrast, generalist hospitals do not specialize in particular conditions and typically treat a broader range of patients. By definition, these hospitals will have more differentiation and diversity among their departments than specialized hospitals. This differentiation requires a greater degree of nonroutine work across differentiated departments, resulting in an increased diversity of perspectives and interpretations (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Cooper & Wolfe, 2005) and requiring substantial integration across units to ensure high performance (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). Differentiated hospitals must divide their attention across a diversity of patients and care areas and are less likely to develop deep institutional knowledge in any particular area. Due to the multiple interpretations that often need to be reconciled across clinical areas, it is more likely that clinicians will face more ambiguity about the mix of daily provisioning of care. Physicians at differentiated, nonspecialized hospitals may have less experience with a specific type of patient, fewer colleagues with intensive knowledge to draw insights from, and less focused training on the particular clinical needs of certain types of patients.

In highly differentiated hospitals, we would expect clinical healthcare analytics to be quite impactful. In such cases, the information processing potential of the analytics system can also help to reduce uncertainty as well as equivocality by providing information across departmental boundaries, providing a common ground or standard through which different units can begin to reconcile their diverse interpretations of a situation. Units or departments in differentiated hospitals may have different interpretations of a patient situation but the necessary information to process associated tasks may be accessible elsewhere in the hospital or in historical data. In such instances, information processing can not only reduce the uncertainty of the tasks themselves but can also attend to the wider scope of the range of issues (Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Te’eni, 2001).

Thus, in contexts with high differentiation, the more information that can be processed, the better. On the other hand, when hospitals are specialized in a limited number of domains, situations will generally be more consistent and familiar and will therefore involve fewer different interpretations by clinicians. Therefore, given the same level of uncertainty, we anticipate that specialized, more homogeneous healthcare contexts will benefit less from information processing than those marked by more diversity and differentiation across units (Daft & Macintosh, 1981). Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: The influence of clinical healthcare analytics on experiential quality will be greater in more differentiated hospitals.

## 3 Data

We leveraged nine years of panel data (2007-2015) to assess the impact of clinical analytics on patient experiential quality (Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016). Our central outcome measure is derived from the Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS) survey (see Table 1 for a summary of constructs). This data was collected annually for the majority of hospitals in the U.S. We measured experiential quality using six measures capturing patient perspectives on care across several topics including clinician communication, hospital staff responsiveness, pain management, information about medicines, and discharge information, <sup>9</sup> <sup>,</sup> <sup>10</sup> an approach to measuring patient experiential quality that is consistent with prior work (Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016). Dimensions of patient experiential quality were measured on a scale of 0 to 100. We took the average of these six measures (P<sub>i</sub>) and applied a logit transformation to the average score for each hospital in a given year. The measure of experiential quality for hospital i with a compliance percentage P<sub>i</sub> is:

$$
\mathrm{ExperientialQuality} _ {\mathrm{i}} = L n \left(\frac {P _ {i}}{1 - P _ {i}}\right)
$$

To measure clinical analytics for hospitals, we drew on data from the annual HIMSS Analytics Database (HADB), which captures data on hospital capabilities associated with a variety of technologies. We focused on hospital adoption of “business intelligence” or “data mining” and designated a hospital as having clinical healthcare analytics (HAClinical) capabilities if it reported adopting clinically focused business intelligence and data mining.<sup>11</sup> We note that in the later years of the HIMSS data, information became available on other analytics capabilities as well. For example, in 2014, HIMSS started collecting data on analytics capabilities centered on population health management. Because the adoption of these capabilities was rare in the time period of study (e.g., 2.8% of hospitals had adopted analytics-focused population health management), these tools were not considered in our main measure of analytics capabilities but were used in robustness checks, which measured analytics efforts in a more continuous manner.

We used multiple datasets to measure the two forms of equivocality that, we argue, moderate the influence of clinical analytics on experiential quality: task analyzability and differentiation. We measured task analyzability using annual data from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) on a given hospital’s case mix index (CMI). The CMI reflects the mix of patients in high- or low-severity diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) (Fetter et al., 1980). Higher values of CMI indicate more complex and resource-intensive caseloads, which we argue have lower task analyzability. This is useful for normalizing patient care-related measures, which may vary across hospitals as a function of the complexity of the cases (Fetter et al., 1980).

We measured differentiation by calculating the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) for a given hospital (this approach is consistent with related research defining this construct as “focus” (Ding, 2014; Ding et al., 2020). This index captures whether a hospital has a narrow or wide clinical focus, in terms of the range of conditions that it typically treats. To develop the index, we leveraged data from CMS Hospital Compare to identify the number of medical cases in the hospital across 10 major diagnostic categories (MDC), based on the 70 most common DRGs as designated by CMS. We first tallied the total number of cases in these MDCs for each hospital i (TotalCases<sub>i</sub>) and then calculated the proportion that any one MDC j accounted for relative to the total number of cases. These values were then squared and summed to generate a value between 0 and 1. Hospitals with higher values represent those focusing on fewer clinical areas (i.e., they are more specialized and less differentiated), whereas hospitals with lower values treat a diverse set of clinical areas and are more differentiated.<sup>12</sup>

Finally, we used the HADB for multiple relevant control variables. Specifically, we measured hospitals’ general technology capabilities by capturing their adoption of a suite of electronic medical record technologies (six in total) and remote access portals (two in total; one for clinicians and one for patients). We also included a number of additional hospital controls for time-varying hospital features, including the number of staffed beds, number of ER visits, number of outpatient visits, number of operating rooms, number of inpatient days, market competition, and whether a hospital had adopted financial analytics technology (a hospital investing in both financial data mining and financial business intelligence). Market competition (MarketComp) has also been theorized as a potential antecedent to a number of outcomes, including patient satisfaction (Rivers & Glover, 2008). Thus, we leveraged data on hospitalstaffed beds to compute the HHI (Herfindahl, 1950) for a given hospital’s healthcare market.<sup>13</sup> The final dataset included 18,082 observations across 3,138 hospitals. By 2015, 35% of hospitals in our analysis reported adopting clinical analytics. ## 4 Estimation Approach

We evaluated the relationships between healthcare analytics and experiential quality using a DID estimation approach and an OLS, panel fixed effects model that included hospital and time fixed effects and robust standard errors. Our main specification is as follows:

## ExperientialQuality<sub>it</sub> = <sub>1</sub> \*HAClinical<sub>it</sub> + γ\*CONTROLS<sub>it</sub> + θ<sub>i</sub> + λ <sub>t</sub>+ <sub>it</sub>

ExperientialQuality<sub>it</sub> is the logit transformation of the average patient satisfaction for hospital j at time t.

HAClinical<sub>it</sub> is a dichotomous variable indicating whether a hospital i at time t reported clinical analytics adoption. We treated this adoption decision as binary but our results were also robust when we relaxed this assumption. We also included controls to account for other relevant variables (see Table 2a) potentially related to experiential quality and the hospital’s adoption of clinical analytics. We included time and hospital fixed effects $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { t } }$ and $\theta _ { \mathrm { i } } ,$ respectively; $\mu _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is the error term). Time fixed effects controlled for time trends and shocks that impacted all hospitals in a given year. Hospital fixed effects controlled for timeinvariant factors that could have impacted both clinical analytics and patient experiential quality. Hospital fixed effects are key to identification in our estimated model because healthcare markets do not generally change rapidly over time. Fixed effects may be particularly useful in reducing bias and endogeneity concerns from unobserved heterogeneity in the context of healthcare where hospitals have historically been slow to change and innovate.

To address endogeneity concerns further, we also utilized a propensity-matching approach to estimate our model on a subsample of our data that more closely mimicked randomized assignment (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). Using a combination of DID with propensity matching helped further reduce bias and improve the consistency of our estimates (Girma & Görg, 2007). This method leverages relevant observables to identify a control group of nontreated observations that would have been most likely to have been treated (i.e., to have invested in analytics). Specifically, we considered the hospitals that adopted analytics as “treated” and matched each of these hospitals to a hospital that did not adopt but was very similar in observable characteristics preadoption. The hope from this matching exercise is that hospitals that adopted and did not adopt in our sample of hospitals followed similar trends prior to the introduction of the analytics (including factors we could not observe). In theory, this matched sample should help us to more robustly identify the impact that analytics makes in patient experience. We performed a nearest neighbor match with replacement using a Probit model. We matched each treated hospital to only one untreated hospital to create a control group for hospitals that invested in clinical analytics (we used a caliper level of 0.001 to ensure that only hospitals with sufficiently close matches were used in our analysis). We utilized all available hospital features to conduct our matching and achieved balance on these variables (see Appendix C for details). Based on the combination of controls, hospital and year fixed effects, and propensity matching, we are confident that we produced unbiased effects of analytics capabilities.

We then estimated the influence of our moderators (task analyzability and differentiation) through interactions with our measure of clinical analytics. We measured task analyzability by the case mix index of hospital i at time t (TaskAnalyzability<sub>it</sub>). We measured context differentiation using the hospital-specific HHI derived from its mix of clinical activities (Differentiation<sub>it</sub>). This extended model allowed us to tease out any differential effects of analytics under different levels of task analyzability and differentiation.

## 6 Robustness

While these initial results are encouraging and suggest some support for a main effect of clinical analytics adoption on patients’ experiential quality, several concerns arise with analyses using secondary data. In what follows, we address concerns about biased estimates due to endogenous adoption of analytics, spurious correlation, data measurement issues, and the use of adoption data rather than actual use.

## 6.1 Parallel Trends Assumption

An important concern with the DID estimation approach is the potential for hospitals’ adoption of clinical analytics to be endogenous. This could have materialized in several ways. First, there may be simultaneity or reverse causality concerns in our data: hospitals that are already improving in terms of patient experience may also be those that tend toward greater adoption of analytics capabilities (i.e., improvements in patient experience are driving adoption and not vice versa). Second, because hospitals self-select into clinical analytics adoption, there is a potential for omitted or unobserved variable bias due to systematic differences between hospitals that adopt and those that do not. Our main estimated model addressed these concerns through a set of controls to capture observable hospital characteristics and, perhaps more centrally, through hospital and time fixed effects and a propensity-matched sample that accounted for time trends and relevant differences between hospitals. Nonetheless, these concerns may persist if factors not captured by our model impact both experiential quality and the adoption of healthcare analytics.

Related to this point, a key requirement for the causal integration of DID estimation approaches is that treated and nontreated groups have parallel trends prior to treatment (i.e., the adoption of clinical healthcare analytics). We evaluated these concerns by estimating a relative time model proposed by Autor (2003) to investigate whether those who adopt analytics follow parallel trends prior to adoption (Column 1). Specifically, this model includes indicators for the initial year of adoption (HAClin 0), as well as two years prior to adoption (HAClin -2, -1) and two years after adoption (HAClin 1, 2).

We found small and insignificant effects for the two years preceding adoption but significant increasing benefits from analytics in the years following adoption (Table 3, Column 1 and Figure 1). These results substantiate the parallel trends assumption for effective DID estimation and suggest that reverse causality concerns did not drive our results.

In addition to this estimation approach, we employed a falsification test to evaluate the potential of unobserved factors or spurious correlations driving our results. Although our interest was in the effect of clinical healthcare analytics on experiential quality, hospitals can also adopt financial analytics technologies focused on hospital financial outcomes. The availability of financial analytics adoption data provided an intriguing opportunity to verify the validity of our results. If some unobserved difference in quality or management between hospitals positively correlated with overall analytics adoption and this drove our results, we would expect positive effects for both financial and clinical analytics. Thus, we generated a similar measure of analytics adoption related to financial (rather than clinical) analytics tools (HAFinancial) and estimated the effect of these analytics capabilities on experiential quality. As expected, we found no effect of financial analytics adoption, which suggests that our results were not driven by unobserved factors related to general analytics adoption (Table 4, Column 2).

## 6.2 Measurement of Clinical Healthcare Analytics and Use

To generate our main measure of clinical healthcare analytics capabilities, we initially focused on technology efforts that had data available for the majority of our study period. We also measured these capabilities in a binary fashion in order to more easily utilize propensity matching approaches on our sample (Rubin, 1978) and to enable estimation of a relative time model of adoption (see next paragraph). However, using a binary measure may have impacted our results if the measure was too coarse to identify a more nuanced effect (e.g., that those who made a partial investment in analytics could see value). As a result, we also considered the effect on our analysis of relaxing the binary approach toward measuring analytics capabilities. In particular, we used data available in the later years of our study period on additional analytics capabilities adopted by hospitals to generate a continuous measure of adoption (HAClinCont). This measure ranged from 0 if no analytics technologies were adopted, to 4 if all analytics technologies were adopted. Using this alternate measure, we continued to find a consistent and positive and stronger relationship between analytics and experiential quality (Table 4, Column 3). These results indicate that hospitals that adopted analytics capabilities had, on average, a \~4% higher ratio of patients with high experiential quality if they adopt all clinical healthcare analytics capabilities.

Related to the prior challenge, we also considered how variation in the use of analytics may have impacted our results. While studies of analytics use are not prevalent (Bhatt et al., 2019; Khalifa & Househ, 2021; Park & Mithas, 2020), parallels can be drawn to the relationship between health IT use and individual and organizational outcomes. It is safe to say that the majority of the literature has found a positive effect of IT use on performance, i.e., more use (quantity and/or quality) translates to better outcomes (e.g, Adjerid et al., 2018; Denton et al., 2018; Dexheimer et al., 2008), yet some research has shown inverse relationships (e.g., Marmor et al., 2018; Welch et al., 2007; Zhou et al., 2009). Some studies have suggested that the rigor associated with the study is a factor in this relationship, with randomized controlled trials demonstrating positive effects and cross-sectional studies showing negative results (Zhou et al., 2009). Unfortunately, to our knowledge, there is no available data on the hospital use of technologies related to analytics capabilities on a national scale. That said, our relative time model (Table 4, Column 1) helped to address issues around the use of analytics, as prior literature has predicted lagged effects of technology adoption since clinicians may be acclimating to the new technology and not fully or appropriately using it in the initial period of adoption (Reardon & Davidson, 2007; Vishwanath et al., 2010). In time, however, it is expected that use will increase as the IT is assimilated into routines (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). This suggests that more benefits would accrue in subsequent years following adoption. We found evidence consistent with this prediction and actually identified an insignificant effect on experiential quality in the initial year of adoption and a significant and increasing impact on experiential quality in subsequent years (Table 4, Column 1).

We attempted to alleviate these concerns further through both quantitative and qualitative approaches. From a qualitative perspective, we posted questions on an informatics society’s listserv asking how hospitals are using clinical healthcare analytics and the types of results they are witnessing. We received responses from several practitioners and administrators and matched those responses to the hospitals in our dataset. We found that all respondents were affiliated with hospitals that reported having clinical healthcare analytics in service. While this does not provide information on the extent to which they are using analytics, it does suggest that, at least in the case of these hospitals, those that have adopted analytics are using it.

We also considered a quantitative approach by leveraging alternative estimation approaches that help approximate variation in hospitals’ likelihood of using analytics capabilities effectively. First, we collected data on meaningful use (MU) attestation from CMS. This data identifies the extent to which each hospital is achieving MU criteria <sup>15</sup> over time. In particular, we differentiated between hospitals that reported Stage 1 versus Stage 2 MU attestation. Hospitals at greater than Stage 1 attestation needed to show both broad use of IT in treating patients as well as more advanced uses of IT for achieving clinical outcomes. As a result, our contention is that those hospitals achieving more advanced stages of MU should also be more adept at using analytics appropriately to gain benefits. Using data on MU, we created a composite measure of clinical healthcare analytics capabilities (HAContUse) that combined the continuous measure of analytics capabilities (i.e., Column 3) with the stage of meaningful use reported by the hospital.<sup>16</sup> Using this composite measure, we found a similar effect of analytics on experiential quality (Table 4, Column 4).

## 7 Heterogeneous Effects

A related empirical concern relates to the robustness of the moderating effects in the analysis. Notably, evaluating heterogeneous effects based on task analyzability (clinical complexity of patients) or differentiation (the degree of focus of the hospital) can introduce some additional concerns about unobserved or spurious correlations with technology adoption, patient experience, and variation in hospital characteristics. Some of these concerns were mitigated by the empirical approach we took. For example, the differentiation in hospitals changes very slowly in healthcare settings and highly focused hospitals generally do not transition to highly diverse hospitals in short timespans. As a result, the hospital fixed effects in our analysis addressed many of these concerns because they are generally relatively stable over time. Also, our matching approach included task analyzability (clinical complexity) as a variable of interest and thus resulted in adopting and nonadopting hospitals with similar levels of complexity. Finally, it is worth noting that task analyzability is positively correlated with technology adoption but negatively correlated with patient experience. Thus, regarding a potential selection concern with the interaction term, it is probable that these selection concerns made our results more conservative.

Nonetheless, we performed additional analyses to help rule out spurious correlation or other factors related to patient selection on our moderators. Specifically, we considered whether other technologies adopted by the hospital had similar interaction effects. If so, then we would be concerned that the coefficients on our interaction terms could be identifying some unobserved factors that are simultaneously related to both technology adoption, our moderators, and patient satisfaction. We examined these effects first for electronic health records and found small and insignificant interaction terms on our moderators for these other healthcare technologies (Table 5, Columns 1 and 2). Similarly, we found small and insignificant effects on concentration when we parsed by large and small hospitals (Columns 3 and 4). We repeated this analysis for patient portals and found similarly insignificant effects for these technologies (Columns 5 and 6) and similar noneffects when parsing between large and small hospitals (Columns 7 and 8). These results helped alleviate concerns around the potential selection, for example, of more satisfied patients into hospitals with more advanced technology adoption. These results also highlight the distinct nature of analytics technologies from other related technology efforts that are not moderated in the same way by complexity and concentration.

## 8 Discussion

Research across a number of disciplines has shown that investments in healthcare technologies can drive a variety of beneficial outcomes that are, however, not universally realized in the same way (Agha, 2014; Buntin et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2013; McCullough et al., 2010; McCullough et al., 2013; Queenan et al., 2011). We contribute to what scholars (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Agarwal et al., 2010; Guha & Kumar, 2018) have argued is the next frontier for this research—i.e., examining how the quality and efficiency of healthcare delivery can be improved by data analyzed from early investments in healthcare technologies such as electronic health records and radiology information systems (Murdoch & Detsky, 2013). We also demonstrate the nuanced value of clinical healthcare analytics adoption and reveal heterogeneous benefits driven by the degree of equivocality in clinical settings (see Table 6).

Guided by foundational thinking in OIPT, we assessed the effect of clinical analytics on patient experiential quality and found a significant main effect and evidence of theoretically informed moderators. For our main effect, we found that clinical healthcare analytics can improve patient experiential quality by \~2%, on average. While this effect might not seem particularly large, the effect is not trivial—industry observers have noted that low patient satisfaction (i.e., experiential quality) scores result in negative perceptions of patient care that are extremely difficult to overcome (Rau, 2015; Siegrist, 2013). It has been shown that a 1% increase in patient experience translates into about a 12% improvement in the overall rank of the hospital (Elliott et al., 2015). Given that hospital reimbursements such as CMS are now based on these scores, the patient experience is now an even more critical strategic issue for hospitals (Siegrist, 2013). A recent study showed that 60% of the variation in the financial margin between high- and low-performing hospitals is accounted for by patient experience (Betts et al., 2016). Further, it is important to note that in our study, the effect size increased significantly (up to \~8%) when considering the moderating effects.

With respect to our second hypothesis addressing how contexts of high inpatient complexity impact the relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and experiential quality, our results support the hypothesis that hospitals with low task analyzability tend to see reduced gains from clinical analytics in terms of experiential quality. These hospitals generally treat highly complex cases with high levels of equivocality. This would explain why hospitals with less complex patient populations have more analyzable tasks and therefore greater gains from clinical healthcare analytics. In addition, while the moderating effect of differentiation was found to be nonsignificant in the full model, a post hoc analysis revealed that the interaction is significant and negative in larger hospitals. The findings related to moderators highlight how, as clinical analytics becomes ubiquitous, it is important to understand contexts where they are more or less likely to drive value in organizations. As analytics capabilities continue to evolve and become more powerful, it will be important to investigate whether the moderating effects associated with different forms of equivocality will hold in the future.

Further, it is important for future research to understand the mechanisms through which clinical healthcare analytics impact patient experience. In our analysis, we observed that (1) clinical healthcare analytics capabilities are also positively related to clinical care quality in hospitals, and (2) clinical quality measures are positively related to experiential quality. These results, though not part of our main estimation models, do suggest a mechanism—via clinical quality—through which clinical healthcare analytics capabilities impact experiential quality. It is important to understand how different contexts influence this mechanism as well. Analytics is less likely to impact the patient experience in equivocal contexts where task ambiguity is high but may improve objective health outcomes, for example, through some sort of scope reduction. Understanding the mechanisms for how analytics drive different outcomes in contexts with different forms of equivocality is critical for understanding the value of analytics in organizations. This research is a step in this direction.

However, this study is not without limitations. The measure of clinical analytics is indeed coarse but the results are robust over a variety of conditions and the sample is actually quite large relative to the overall hospital population. Further, this problem of course measures is not unique to clinical analytics. The same issue is also present in studies of EHR adoption where a rather small number of technologies made up a suite (Angst et al., 2010; Bower, 2005). Clinical analytics is still relatively new to most healthcare contexts, and actual use data remains difficult to attain. Until this data becomes available, it is incumbent upon researchers to take some initial steps in understanding the impact of clinical analytics.

Another limitation is that while we argue that the information processing capabilities based in OIPT provide the theoretical mechanism, we do not directly measure information processing. Clearly, OIPT does not describe the only mechanism that could apply to this context. However, this lens does apply and we believe it is a solid foundation upon which to conduct further research into the impacts of clinical analytics. In addition, some limitations are largely due to data constraints, which we hope to address in future work by, for example, collecting primary data on actual information processing capacities in a specific clinical setting.

## 8.1 Contributions

The management of analytics assets in hospitals has become a very important research topic from a variety of disciplines, ranging from information systems to operations management (OM) to health informatics. Ward et al. (2014) outlined the challenges and barriers to realizing value from healthcare analytics. We argue in this paper that researchers need to delve deeper into the value proposition of analytics efforts (Fink et al., 2017; Trieu, 2017). This point is further reinforced in recent commentaries in the New England Journal of Medicine (Chen & Asch, 2017) and JAMA (Cabitza et al., 2017) that highlight the unique value dynamics of analytics and prediction in healthcare. It is important to acknowledge that for an organization to use analytics capabilities effectively, it must apply its knowledge assets successfully to address challenges and/or capitalize on opportunities (Trieu, 2017; Trkman et al., 2010). Yet little research has explored how analytics fits into information systems or operations management research or how analytics can drive value in organizations (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Chen et al., 2012; Guha & Kumar, 2018; Trieu, 2017; Trkman et al., 2010). With this study, we take a step toward addressing this gap in the healthcare context, where technology investments in recent decades have led hospitals to generate massive amounts of clinical data that can potentially yield significant insights and help solve problems.

There are several managerial contributions that stem from our research. First, this is one of the first studies to examine the performance implications of hospitals investing in clinical analytics. The use of clinical healthcare analytics is clearly linked to experiential quality, which is a metric of increasing interest to hospitals. Second, while clinical healthcare analytics impact experiential quality, it is important to understand how different contexts influence this relationship. We found that the equivocality arising from the operational environment is an important determinant of the return on investment in clinical healthcare analytics. But it is important to note that the equivocality arising from complex tasks is different, depending on the type of complexity of the task and departmental differentiation. This insight contributes to the practitioner debate on the trade-offs involved in focused or specialized hospitals versus generalist hospitals. One important and highly consequential challenge that hospitals face is providing high-quality experiences for their key constituents— their patients (Tsai et al., 2015). We point out how the complexity of the context matters, an underexplored phenomenon that is worthy of further research (Wang & Hajli, 2017).

## 8.2 Theoretical Contributions

We use OIPT to explore whether the adoption of clinical analytics (which is still in its early stages) can improve experiential quality, and what factors moderate this effect. Experiential quality (Sharma et al., 2016), is a measure of the level of interaction between providers and patients, as assessed by the patient while receiving care in a hospital (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012). OIPT is a relevant perspective for exploring the impact of clinical healthcare analytics on the quality of the patient experience since it posits that reductions in uncertainty or equivocality will improve performance. Healthcare contexts are replete with problem uncertainty (Balsa et al., 2003; Wennberg, 1985). By resolving some of this uncertainty, clinical analytics will generally improve a patient’s experiential quality. However, as OIPT indicates, these outcomes will not always be evident, because clinical analytics does not necessarily reduce equivocality. It has long been understood that most information systems are less useful for resolving equivocality than uncertainty (Goodhue et al., 1992). Current clinical healthcare analytics tools are still evolving and provide basic analytics capabilities (Murdoch & Detsky, 2013), and more advanced clinical analytics such as the real-time generation of diagnoses and care paths are still rare. Further, the types of analytics that are being used are often limited by a lack of clinician trust (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Hosanagar & Cronk, 2016).

Another key contribution of this paper involves distinguishing between different forms of equivocality stemming from task analyzability and differentiation, and pointing out why they matter. Empirical research generally does not consider different forms of equivocality. For example, multiple studies operationalize equivocality in terms of the complexity arising from multiple task components (e.g., Vickery et al., 2016). Other studies operationalize equivocality through a lack of clarity and diverse interpretations (e.g., Dennis & Kinney, 1998; Peng et al., 2014), which emphasizes the analyzability dimension of equivocality. Koufteros et al. (2002) conceptualized equivocality in terms of “confusion and lack of understanding” (p.338, a focus on analyzability) and then operationalized equivocality in terms of the complexity of products. The purpose of citing these studies is not to suggest that they have shortcomings, but rather, to point out that since Daft’s seminal work (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft & Macintosh, 1981), researchers have only begun to make distinctions between the dimensions of equivocality (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Haußmann et al., 2012). In this research we not only conceptualize and operationalize this distinction, but we show how it has differential effects. This contribution is particularly important in the age of widespread analytics and the emergence of artificial intelligence applications (Agrawal et al., 2019).

Indeed, when the Daft and Macintosh (1981) article was written, artificial intelligence (AI) was in its infancy, and there was no explicit attention paid to the interplay of human-machine combinations in dealing with uncertainty and equivocality. In our paper, we recognize that the treating clinician is the final decision maker and that cognitive human processes still hold; however, we also acknowledge that analytics tools are increasingly guiding the decision maker. Importantly, we demonstrate that equivocality continues to be a key factor involved in decisions that are made in conjunction with analytics tools. As such, our work extends prior work (Cooper & Wolfe, 2005; Daft & Lengel, 1986; Daft & Macintosh, 1981; Haußmann et al., 2012) and can be used as a starting point for exploring the collaborative decision processes involving humans and AI.

As in other industries (Trkman et al., 2010), the potential benefits of healthcare analytics are substantial, but the link between the capabilities of clinical healthcare analytics and hospital outcomes has not yet been theorized or validated empirically. Trieu (2017) referred to this process as converting analytics investments into analytics assets, which are then transformed into impacts and firm value. Fink et al. (2017) proposed something similar by suggesting that an analytics infrastructure produces analytics capabilities that lead to value creation. Our paper contributes to the large body of work on the business value of IT in an important way by shifting the focus to the means by which analytics contributes value via its uncertainty reduction capabilities. We hope this study will spur further research examining the underlying dimensions of uncertainty and equivocality while addressing the effectiveness of computerized information systems.

Finally, our post hoc analysis sheds light on the effect of the scale and scope of healthcare operations on performance. While hospital concentration is a dimension of scope, hospital size is a dimension of scale, and our ex post analysis shows evidence of an interaction effect between scale and scope on the relationship between clinical analytics and experiential quality. This is relevant since widespread gains in hospital performance from clinical analytics are not automatic. A common theme across decades of prior research highlights how technology investments are not silver bullets. Simply adopting a technology is not sufficient for realizing value. Healthcare contexts, in particular, are notorious for heterogeneous and nuanced effects of technology adoption across a variety of outcomes (e.g., Angst et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2018; Menon et al., 2009; Wani & Malhotra, 2018). This work suggests that value from clinical analytics is likely to be heterogeneously distributed across hospitals.

In sum, this research leveraged the OIPT tradition to examine how clinical analytics can help with the resolution of uncertainty and equivocality (Flynn & Flynn, 1999; Galbraith, 1974; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2004; Goodhue et al., 1992). We found that analytics can positively impact a patient’s clinical care experience but that this effect is nuanced and has heterogeneous effects between care settings. In particular, we found support for our hypotheses that clinical healthcare analytics benefits are greater in less equivocal environments that have more analyzable tasks and also greater benefits in more equivocal environments in large, highly diversified, hospitals. Although there is much variation among hospitals in terms of complexity and their diversity of patients (Steinwald & Dummit, 1989), no prior study has addressed how equivocality stemming from both task analyzability and differentiation relates to the use of clinical analytics.

## 8.3 Conclusion

Despite the wide-ranging discussion of big data and analytics in the popular and research press, surprisingly few studies have investigated the means by which analytics produces value. This study is an initial attempt to theorize how heterogeneous types of hospitals can benefit from clinical analytics. OIPT provides the basis for the argument that clinical healthcare analytics is an uncertainty-reducing mechanism that impacts experiential quality. We further propose the moderating role of equivocality stemming from either the task analyzability of patient cases or departmental differentiation in hospitals. The overall effect of clinical healthcare analytics on experiential quality is decidedly positive, suggesting that clinical analytics does reduce uncertainty in healthcare decisions. Similarly, the conditional effect of equivocality from less analyzable tasks is negative, while the conditional effect of equivocality from differentiation is less clear-cut but appears to be influenced by the size of the hospital. These findings should provide a springboard for other researchers to investigate how data analytics can drive organizational value across contexts.

## Appendix B: Conformance Quality

## Clinical Healthcare Analytics, Conformance Quality, and Experiential Quality

Although clinical healthcare analytics seem to have a positive relationship with experiential quality in hospitals, clinicians (and not patients) are the primary users of these capabilities. As a result, the effects we observed likely materialized because clinicians were utilizing these capabilities in ways that improved the patient experience. Taking this consideration into account, we evaluated the relationship between clinical healthcare analytics and clinical quality. Specifically, we supplemented our experiential quality measure with additional data from the CMS Hospital Compare database, focusing on measures related to a hospital’s performance in meeting evidence-based standards. These measures capture elements of the reliability and responsiveness of the services provided to patients during their hospita stays. Given that clinicians are the primary users of analytics capabilities, any impact of analytics on experiential quality is likely to be accompanied by analytics impacts on conformance to care standards (CMS refers to these as measures of timely and effective care). Again, we leaned on prior literature to generate our measure of conformance quality (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012; Chassin et al., 2010; Senot et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016). Similar to these prior works, we focused on heart attack (AMI), heart failure (HF), pneumonia (PN), and outpatient surgical care (OP) to generate our measure (see Table B1). Like our measure of experiential quality, we applied a logit transformation to the weighted average (C<sub>i</sub>) of compliance along these four dimensions.
