# Using Personal Communication Technologies for Commercial Communications: A Cross-Country Investigation of Email and SMS

Chuan-Hoo Tan

Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong SAR, ch.tan@cityu.edu.hk

Juliana Sutanto

Department of Management, Technology, and Economics, ETH Zürich, 8092 Zürich, Switzerland, jsutanto@ethz.ch

Chee Wei Phang

Department of Information Management and Information Systems, Fudan University, Shanghai 200433, China, phangcw@fudan.edu.cn

Anar Gasimov Jinbox Sàrl, 8005 Zürich, Switzerland, gasimov@jinbox.com

he widespread use of personal communication technologies (PCTs) for commercial message dissemination necessitates understanding that PCTs might lead to better commercial performance in different situations. Building primarily on apparatgeist and social construction theories, this research proposes that consumer responses to PCT-disseminated commercial messages are jointly influenced by the PCT (i.e., technology) that carries general symbolic meanings about its nature and purpose (its “spirit”), and the context culture (i.e., the cultural milieu) in which it is used. We began with focus groups’ assessments of two commonly utilized PCTs—email and short message service—which revealed their comparative symbolic meanings in terms of intimacy or formality of communication—to be in line with extant literature. Then, in a commercial setting where retailers leverage PCTs to disseminate product discount coupons, we examined the difference between two distinct environments that differed in their context-cultural dimensions (their cultural milieus of social interaction and communication)—i.e., China (an environment of high context-cultural dimension) and Switzerland (an environment of low context-cultural dimension). To do so, we first validated the context-cultural differences through a survey (study 1) and conducted two matching field experiments in the two countries involving more than one thousand consumers (study 2). Results support our propositions, demonstrating favorable commercial performance for SMS use in the high context-cultural environment and for email use in the low context-cultural environment. Follow-up surveys (study 3) corroborated the results and provided deeper insights into how both PCTs’ general meanings and pertinent values in the cultural milieus we studied led to consumer responses. Besides presenting empirical evidence to inform the selection of appropriate PCTs for commercial communications, this research contributes to the theoretical development of apparatgeist and social construction theories via its joint examination of technologies and consumers’ environments.

Keywords: personal communication technology; apparatgeist theory; social construction; context-cultural dimension; multimethod investigation; field experiment

## 1. Introduction

Use of personal communication technologies (PCTs) to reach consumers individually and quickly, relative to mass media (Heinonen and Strandvik 2007, Okazaki and Taylor 2008), has developed significantly in recent decades. Two PCTs—short message service (SMS) and electronic mail (email)—have become especially deeprooted in consumers’ daily lives, albeit in different ways that derive from their different characteristics.

SMS is designed primarily for dyadic exchanges of short messages (Taylor and Vincent 2005), as its typical 160-character restriction enforces brevity. Thus, Longmate and Baber (2002) found people tended to use SMS for sending short, expressive messages and arranging social activities. In contrast, email supports exchanges of longer messages on a one-to-one or broadcast basis (Longmate and Baber 2002); email use tends to be associated with impersonal interactions involving instrumental content and SMS with informal, situationspecific interactions (Heinonen and Strandvik 2007, Kim et al. 2007, Longmate and Baber 2002, Miyata 2006).

Notwithstanding their differences, both PCTs are widely used by marketers and retailers to disseminate product sales information, including product discount coupons. A Google search of “SMS marketing” and “email marketing” yielded about 28 million entries, most relating to marketers and vendors offering such services to retailers.<sup>1</sup> For example, SAP offers a Sybase 365 service that enabled retailers to reach 4.5 billion consumers across six continents via SMS;<sup>2</sup> the digital marketing firm Aprimo offers email marketing software that enables retailers to send email product messages to targeted consumers across four continents.<sup>3</sup> Retailers also have shown considerable interest in PCT use: Juniper Research predicted that business use of SMS to reach consumers will overtake person-to-person texting by 2016;<sup>4</sup> the 2012 Marketing Channel and Engagement Benchmark Survey found 63% of businesses surveyed cited email as the channel offering the best returns on investment.<sup>5</sup> These observations suggest that PCTs afford great potential for reaching out to consumers across the globe. Which PCT should retailers use though and should retailers adopt a single approach to target consumers across the world or is it worthwhile to use different PCTs, i.e., email versus SMS, in different countries?

One field of thought argues that, although complex technologies (such as enterprise-wide systems) may require different types of information technology (IT) skills, which lead to diverse usage patterns (Park et al. 2007), usage behaviors tend to converge for less complex, universal consumer technologies such as PCTs (Cleveland and Laroche 2007, Friedman 2005, Lahiri 2010). This view aligns with apparatgeist theory, which suggests the “spirit” of a technology—or its functional characteristics—constrain users’ range of possibilities in interacting with that technology and shapes the significance they accord it, leading to convergence in usage behaviors (Cleveland and Laroche 2007, Friedman 2005, Lahiri 2010, Katz and Aakhus 2002). An alternate perspective, rooted in social construction theory, suggests that user responses to the choice of a particular PCT for message dissemination will differ across social settings (Fulk 1993, Nord 1986). Conflicting findings from extant research seem to favor the social construction perspective: for example, whereas Koo et al. (2011) reported instant messaging technology had a positive effect on performance, Cutrell et al. (2001) found that it had a disruptive effect. The contrasting behavioral responses to SMS can be explained by variations in users’ social constructions of technologies in the different social settings in which the studies were conducted. We therefore find it useful to complement apparatgeist theory with a social construction perspective, to enhance understanding and prediction of user responses to retailers’ use of different PCTs for communicating commercial messages.

Our conceptual approach differs from that of most existing literature on technology-aided communication in two respects. First, many prior studies have emphasized either technology or culture, but have not considered both. For instance, Sahay and Robey (1996) noted that different social interpretations of a technology in different organizational contexts influenced how that technology was implemented, and thus the ensuing consequences, but did not consider explicitly the role of the technology’s characteristics in shaping those outcomes. In general, technology comparison studies have considered how PCTs influence the construction of message meanings in ways that subsequently affect communication outcomes, but have not explicitly accounted for how variations in social settings might impact those outcomes. For instance, Miranda and Saunders (2003) posited that electronic media lowered perceptions of social presence relative to face-to-face communications, which impeded the construction of meaning and reduced information sharing. But whether this effect is robust to different social settings is not clear, so investigating this area is pertinent in view of the increasingly prevalent use of PCTs for global commercial communication. Second, even studies that considered both technological and cultural elements (e.g., earlier works on group support systems) typically operationalized culture on such dimensions as individualism versus collectivism (e.g., Tan et al. 1998, Mejias et al. 1997); context-culture, a dimension that is more closely related to communication, has yet to receive the attention it deserves (Leidner and Kayworth 2006).

We examine consumer responses to retailers’ communication with different PCTs across culturally different social settings. Our central theses are that different PCTs encompass different meanings about their nature and purpose, and that users in different social settings will respond differently to PCT use for commercial message dissemination based on that PCT’s general meanings 4the spirit of a technology as advocated by the apparatgeist theory5 and their culture of communication 4as advocated by the social construction theory5.

To validate these theses, we conducted three related empirical studies to compare two PCTs—email and SMS—and two social settings representing different context cultures. Email and SMS possess distinct characteristics (Ducheneaut 2002, Eldridge and Grinter 2001) and are widely employed in global marketing campaigns (Heinonen and Strandvik 2007, Okazaki and Taylor 2008). The context-culture dimension of culture characterizes how people in a culture communicate and relate to one another (Kim et al. 1998, Hall 1976).

This research affords contributions to research and practice. Theoretically, our research shows that simple communication technologies such as PCTs do indeed carry a set of meanings about their nature and purpose that are general across social settings (consistent with the apparatgeist theory). Yet, consumers in different social settings responded differently to the use of a PCT based on whether its general meanings fit with their context-cultural dimension (consistent with the social construction theory). Practically, our multistudy, multimethod research produced valuable empirical insights into why and how different PCTs, i.e., SMS and email, were more or less suitable for different environments. In study 1, surveys and focus groups enabled us to surface consumers’ values and perceptions of PCTs across different context-cultural environments. In study 2, a field experiment validated consumers’ responses to messages received via the focal PCTs. In study 3, a follow-up survey provided deeper insights into consumer responses observed in the field experiment.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations and Empirical Investigation Overview

This research integrates apparatgeist and social construction theories to account for both PCTs’ characteristics and consumers’ social settings in understanding consumer responses to commercial messages. Apparatgeist theory focuses on the influence of technologies’ functional characteristics, but differs from a technology deterministic view in proposing that users accord meaning to a technology based on the options it affords them for communication, i.e., the spirit of a technology. As Katz and Aakhus (2002, p. 307) put it,

0 0 0 technology does not determine what an individual can do; rather, it serves as a constraint upon possibilities. Much as a cafeteria menu will not offer infinite meal choices, but rather presents a finite selection of meal choices, so too historically bound technology offers us a flexible menu of extensive, but not infinite, choices.

In other words, users assign a technology a general set of symbolic meanings based on the functions it was designed to facilitate (its spirit). Although the gist of apparatgeist theory is that users’ attitudes toward technologies can be understood from their spirit, this theoretical lens cannot be applied in a social vacuum.

Social construction theory highlights the idea that a recipient’s interpretation of a message might not align with its sender’s intent and different recipients may interpret the same message differently (Larsson 1997, MacKenzie and Wajcman 1999, Fulk 1993, Nord 1986). Miranda and Saunders (2003) demonstrated empirically that the outcomes of communication depend on a message recipient’s social construction of its meaning, which depends in turn on the social presence of the communication media employed. Their research supports social construction theory, but also suggests the importance of considering communication technology characteristics in understanding the social construction of meaning in message communication.

## 2.1. PCT: General Symbolic Meanings

Social construction research cautions us not to treat technology as a “black-box,” but to appreciate it as a collection of symbolic meanings conceived by a cohort of people (Williams and Edge 1996). This viewpoint coincides with that of the IS literature, which suggests that technology is intricately involved in the completion of tasks and activities (Orlikowski et al. 1995), so that neglecting its features’ influence on society and markets precludes our understanding of how it is adapted to our uses (Majchrzak et al. 2000). A comprehensive understanding of a technology requires us to consider both its functional characteristics and the set of general symbolic meanings users attach to its nature and purpose.

The notion of symbolic meanings was coined by Fulk to denote how technologies are characterized socially, i.e., through users attributing sense and connotations to them, which arise and are sustained over time by the “ongoing processes of joint sensemaking within social systems” (Fulk 1993, p. 922). The concept can be observed in recent efforts to synthesize two primary theories of communication technology—media richness and social influence theories (Webster and Trevino 1995). The former focuses on the message content, e.g., “message equivocality” (Daft and Lengel 1986), and the latter emphasizes the social context (e.g., peer and/or group influences) that determine users’ media choice (Fulk et al. 1990). But despite the difference in their focus, these two theories highlight the need to consider the general symbolic meaning of a communication technology (Webster and Trevino 1995). Trevino et al. (2000) illustrated this concept by suggesting that such technologies as video conferencing symbolize participation and collaboration (i.e., personal contact), whereas email tends to signify a relatively higher level of formality (i.e., impersonal contact): such understandings introduce the notion of how communication technologies are symbolically perceived and how they are used for fostering relationships (Hian et al. 2004, Kiesler et al. 1984, Katz and Aakhus 2002).

Although the two PCTs we consider in this study— SMS and email—are similar in that both support written peer-to-peer and peer-to-group communication (Frehner 2008), they differ in the levels of personal intimacy they permit in communication. SMS—a technology typically embedded in mobile phones—is designed to support short dyadic message exchanges (Longmate and Baber 2002, Taylor and Vincent 2005) and offers fast, direct, and convenient peer-to-peer communication (Eldridge and Grinter 2001). Email, on the other hand, is designed to allow exchanges of longer messages (one-to-one or broadcast); it is often used for instrumental and less intrusive communication (Ducheneaut 2002) and is known for its utility as a workplace technology (Longmate and Baber 2002). These differences are somewhat “fixed,” as both PCTs are relatively mature, i.e., their feature sets are stable and no major disruptive changes are expected in their designs or functions in the near future (Le Bodic 2003). These characteristics allow us to theoretically contrast the two technologies, distinguishing between their key symbolic meanings (Orlikowski 2008, MacKenzie and Wajcman 1999).

## 2.2. Cultural Milieu: Context-Cultural Dimensions of Communication

Culture is the coherent set of ideologies, core values, and beliefs shared by groups of people (Sackmann 1992). A cultural milieu is the social setting in which users with a shared set of ideologies, values, and beliefs deploy a communication technology and where their interpretation of a communication is constructed (Klein and Kleinman 2002). Reviewing work by Schutz (1967), Miranda and Saunders (2003, p. 88) note that “the very social setting in which information is encountered contributes to its meanings.” Essentially, interpretations are rooted in the collective perspectives of many people, a point that is well exemplified in earlier work by Pinch and Bijker (1987), who highlight how “all members of a certain social group share the same set of meanings attached to a specific artifact” (p. 30). Klein and Kleinman (2002) contextualize this argument by indicating that established cultural milieus constrain and characterize how people construct meanings, and Fulk (1993) demonstrates empirically how the interpretations and behaviors of people in well-defined cultural milieus converge in terms of how they respond to, and construct meaning from, communication technologies.

In their seminal review article on culture in IS research, Leidner and Kayworth (2006) differentiated people at societal/national levels across eight dimensions of culture: uncertainty avoidance, power distance, masculinity/femininity, individualism/collectivism, time orientation, monochronism/polychronism, locus of control, and their context-cultural dimensions (refer to Table A1 in the online appendix for the definitions of the cultural dimensions and supplementary explanation box A1 in the online appendix for the discussion of each of the cultural dimensions and their relevance to our study, except the focal context-cultural dimension that we will discuss here (available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.2014.0519).

In this research, we focus on the context-cultural dimension when evaluating PCT for three reasons. First, context-cultural dimension is closely related to information processing communication as suggested by Leidner and Kayworth (2006) and exemplified in the investigation of communication media choice by Richardson and Smith (2007), which both suggest a direct relevance of the dimension to our focus on PCT use. Second, as we will elaborate in our hypothesis development, we expect a PCT, depending on its level of intimacy/intrusiveness, could yield vastly different results when deployed in different context-cultural environments, which is in accordance to the thesis of social construction (Larsson 1997, MacKenzie and Wajcman 1999, Fulk 1993, Nord 1986). Third, as Leidner and Kayworth (2006) have rightfully pointed out, context-cultural dimension has been understudied, and given its relevance to PCT investigation, this research seeks to fill in this research gap.

The context-cultural dimension refers to the fact that different cultural milieus (specifically countries) can be ranked on a continuum with high contexts and low contexts at each end (Kim et al. 1998, Onkvisit and Shaw 1993). At one end are those countries with a relatively high context-cultural dimension—such as China, South Korea, and Japan—where people value their intimate interrelationships, and where information is typically communicated through messages that may be simple but entail deep, implicit meanings, making communication more implicit and personal. A high context-cultural dimension is defined as an environment in which there is little explicit coding in such message communications, because most of the relevant information is in the “physical context or internalized in the person” (Hall 1976, p. 91). In this type of environment, close personal relationships are crucial, and extensive informational networks play major roles in the meanings of messages. At the other end of the continuum are countries with a relatively low context-cultural dimension, such as Switzerland, Norway, and Sweden, where—as Hall (1976) finds— consumers typically have little involvement with each other, and so where interpersonal communication is more explicit and nonpersonal. An environment with a low context-cultural dimension is one in which message communication is typified by explicit coding (Hall 1976).

Indeed the context-cultural dimension has been widely employed in empirical investigations of communication media choice (Richardson and Smith 2007).

For instance, Li et al. (2010) illustrated the difference between these context-cultural dimensions with their observations that Chinese students, from a high context-cultural environment, paid more attention to their enjoyment in using instant messaging, whereas American students (from a lower context-cultural environment) placed more emphasis on the usefulness of the technology. Another study by Guo et al. (2008) asserted that Chinese users value feedback and social cues in communication—and so prefer instant messaging or phone communications to email—whereas Australians (from an environment of lower contextcultural dimension) prefer email, because they feel more comfortable with its arm’s-length formalism and fewer nonverbal cues.

## 3. Conceptual Model and Hypothesis Development

As already noted, our theses—which integrate apparatgeist and social construction theories—are that different PCTs have general meanings about their nature and purpose, and that users in different social settings respond differently to PCT use for commercial message dissemination based on the PCT’s general meanings 4the spirit of a technology as advocated by the apparatgeist theory5 and their culture of communication 4as advocated by the social construction theory5. Building on the notion of task-technology fit (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), we propose a technology spirit-communication culture fit such that consumer responses to a message delivered by a PCT are contingent on the match between the symbolic meanings of the PCT and the communication culture of the social setting where the consumer resides. In other words, we treat communication in different cultural contexts as different tasks. Our approach is consistent with previous studies that applied the notion of fit in different settings involving different constructs, e.g., fit between an enterprise system and organization characteristics has been shown to influence organizational performance (Sia and Soh 2007, Strong and Volkoff 2010).

Figure 1 depicts a simplified view of our conceptual model. We argue that the impact of a product discount coupon sent via email—which is symbolically less intimate and intrusive—will garner more favorable consumer responses in a low context-cultural environment, because of a fit of email characteristics with consumer preferences for maintaining personal space in this social setting. Conversely, a similar coupon sent via SMS— which is symbolically more intimate and personal—will engender more favorable consumer responses in a high context-cultural environment, because of a fit of SMS characteristics with consumers’ inclination to engage (and being comfortable) with close interpersonal communications in this social setting.

The message content chosen to test this proposition was a discount coupon, which offered price discounts to encourage purchases (Anand et al. 1999, Chiou-Wei and Inman 2008, Fortin 2000). We considered consumers’ perceptions and responses to these coupons disseminated via the two focal PCTs in two ways: their perceptual assessment of the value of the coupon offer, which reflects their essential psychological judgment (Aurier and Siadou-Martin 2007, Zeithaml 1988) of the coupon; their objective reactions manifest in redeeming the coupons (i.e., using it to make a purchase) or forwarding them (i.e., sharing it with others).

## 3.1. Consumer Perception of Coupon Value and Coupon Redemption Propensity

The notion of “value” denotes a consumer’s assessment of the utility they expect to gain from a product or service (Zeithaml 1988): in terms of a discount coupon, therefore, it reflects a consumer’s subjective opinion of the net benefit they will gain from redeeming the coupon for a product purchase, after accounting for the costs incurred, such as the physical and psychological effort required (Aurier and Siadou-Martin 2007). Thus, a positive perception of a coupon’s value would imply it was sufficiently attractive to warrant redeeming it (Leone and Srinivasan 1996). Recent studies on understanding consumer responses to digital product discount coupons have focused on understanding their responses to coupons sent via specific communication technologies (Kumar and Swaminathan 2005). For instance, using secondary data from a fast-food chain campaign that sent coupons to customers’ mobile phones, Banerjee and Yancey (2010) demonstrated that favorable responses could be expected when the coupons explicitly targeted consumers based on their needs and desires; similar observations have been made in studies of delivering such coupons via the Internet (e.g., Cheng and Dogan 2008, Chiou-Wei and Inman 2008). Although they are informative, these studies do not shed light on which communication technology should be deployed to induce the most favorable consumer responses in different markets.

Extant studies on communication technology and value perception have predominantly focused on using the technology to build customer relationships (e.g., Grönroos 2004, Larson and Kulchitsky 2006, Page and Lepkowska-White 2002, Steenkamp and Geyskens 2006). Beyond the value of using it as the communication medium, users construe the technology itself as having certain meanings (Wagner et al. 2002); indeed Trevino et al. (1987) suggest that a communication technology may even be deployed simply because of the symbolic meanings it bears, whose value could be seen as exceeding that of the message itself. This view accords with the apparatgeist theory’s notion that the spirit of a technology can induce specific interpretations. As discussed, SMS, designed for the fast and dyadic exchange of short messages are in general interpreted by consumers as more intimate but also more intrusive than email, which is designed more for instrumental and deliberate message communication (Eldridge and Grinter 2001, Longmate and Baber 2002, Taylor and Vincent 2005).

However, although a PCT carries certain general symbolic meanings (its spirit), social construction theory suggests that people’s perceptions of its appropriateness may still differ based on their social settings. Thus, whether people in different social settings react favorably to a coupon received via SMS or email will also depend on their local communication-oriented context-cultural dimension. The high value put on intimate relationships by consumers in high contextcultural environments is often used to explain the rapid penetration of mobile phones in South Korea, where arranging informal gatherings on short notice is the norm (Kim 2002, Ling and Yttri 2006). Comfortable with being intimately engaged, consumers in such environments may be less concerned with the intrusiveness of SMS, and so may evaluate coupons received via SMS more favorably than those received via email. The latter PCT signifies formal, arms-length communication, which fits comparatively less well with the communication styles in such environments than SMS. This should lead consumers in environments of high context-cultural dimension to perceive a coupon received via SMS as having higher value, making them more likely to redeem it than one received via email.

In contrast, consumers in low context-cultural environments—such as the United States—have been found to be more sensitive toward the intrusion of mobile phones in their personal lives, and their obtrusiveness in public settings, and prefer only to engage in mobile communication when it is really convenient to them (Cormode and Krishnamurthy 2008, Mante 2002). Their perceptions of SMS as being highly intrusive are likely to undermine their evaluation of the use of

SMS for disseminating commercial messages (such as coupons). In comparison, email affords people a greater degree of freedom as to whether and when to read their messages (Hovick et al. 2003), and so represents a less intrusive communication mode—fits better with a low context-cultural environment that emphasizes personal space—hence, we posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> In communication environments of high (low) context-cultural dimension, consumers will

(a) perceive a product discount coupon sent via SMS as having a greater (lower) value, and

(b) be more (less) likely to redeem the coupon than one sent via email.

## 3.2. Coupon Forwarding Propensity

Consumers’ inclination to forward coupons reflects their tendency to share product information with acquaintances, colleagues, family members, and friends. Assessing this inclination has gained importance with the growth of viral marketing (Bampo et al. 2008), which relies on consumers transmitting messages to other potential consumers in their social sphere (Wiedemann et al. 2008). Indeed, receiving messages from social contacts rather than from a marketer is believed to enhance recipients’ trust in product messages (Wiedermann et al. 2008). Further, people are more likely to be influenced by those with whom they share common sets of ties (e.g., friendship), given that they tend to share common understandings of the same signals (Borgatti and Everett 1992). However, previous research has also indicated that consumers’ fears of being negatively evaluated by their social contacts for forwarding product messages dissuade them from doing so (Phelps et al. 2004).

Integrating the perspectives of apparatgeist and social construction theories, we argue that using a PCT whose general symbolic meanings, i.e., their spirit, fit (do not fit) with the local communication-oriented context-cultural environment may amplify (diminish) consumer inclination to forward such product messages. In high context-cultural environments, consumers are more deeply involved in people-to-people relationships, forming closer and more intimate bonds with each other, and are more likely to suppress their own feelings to maintain social harmony (Hall 1976)—so they are less likely to perceive using SMS (relative to email) as a barrier in their decision to share a coupon with others. Rather, as SMS (as opposed to email) prompts more intimate and personal communication, SMS use for disseminating product messages will be a better fit for the interpersonal sharing behavior preferred within this culture. However, in low context-cultural environments, people establish bonds with each other that are more fragile, and from which they feel they can withdraw when they want to; so they will tend to communicate in more explicit and nonpersonal ways (Hall 1976). In such environments, consumers may particularly fear earning the disapproval of recipients to whom they forward product messages. Email, which generally carries less intimate and intrusive symbolic meanings than SMS, may lower their risk of earning such disapproval. This may then lead consumers in low context-cultural environments to more likely forward a product discount coupon they received via email than one received via SMS.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> In communication environments of high (low) context-cultural dimension, consumers are more (less) likely to forward a product discount coupon sent via SMS compared to one sent via email.

## 5. Study 1—Short Survey and Focus-Group Discussions

Study 1 consisted of two sequential empirical investigations—a short survey and focus group discussions. The survey (§5.1) involved consumers from the two different cultural environments, China and Switzerland, that we had chosen for our field experiment, to verify that their consumer markets reflected, respectively, Hall’s descriptions of high and low contextcultural dimensions (Hall 1976). Our focus group discussions (§5.2) with consumers in these two different context-cultural environments aimed to confirm our expectations about their perceptions of the two focal PCTs—SMS and email. In each country, all participants in the surveys, focus group discussions, and field experiments were recruited from the same populations (see details in supplementary explanation box A2 in the online appendix).

5.1. Validating the Context-Cultural Environment Previous cross-cultural studies have often attributed divergent outcomes observed to differences in cultural factors without verifying whether the cultural differences believed to underlie the sampled populations really exist (e.g., Kim et al. 1998, Würtz 2005). To ensure our results were grounded in convincing context-cultural differences, we first verified that the two countries we chose did fit Hall’s descriptions of environments of high and low context-cultural dimensions. We conducted surveys in China and Switzerland (involving 92 and 94 respondents, respectively) using questions adapted from previous studies on contextcultural dimensions (Hall 1976, Kim et al. 1998), and translated into the local languages. Table A2 in the online appendix documents t-tests, which show that Chinese respondents had significantly higher ratings on all five context-cultural dimensions than their Swiss counterparts. These findings validated our expectation that the two countries indeed differed along the context-cultural continuum, and thus confirmed our rationale for studying them.

## 5.2. Validating the PCTs Selected for Research

Our objectives here were twofold: (1) to identify the general symbolic meanings users attach to the focal PCTs (in terms of their general perceptions in everyday life), and (2) to understand the differences in how consumers in environments of high and low contextcultural dimensions interpreted their use for delivering commercial messages. To achieve our two objectives, we needed first to address a pertinent problem: that culture is a macro-level generalization of a cohort of consumers, and so often lacks sufficient explanatory power to help understand consumers’ behaviors at the individual level (Straub et al. 2002). Scholars such as Srite and Karahanna (2006) have proposed addressing this problem of examining a consumer from the cultural perspective by treating culture as an individual difference variable, so that if a cohort of consumers reflects a consistent pattern in their perceptions that differ from those of another cohort, it is likely that culture plays a role in determining such differences. If these characteristics align with the general set of traits posited in the cultural literature, it is highly plausible that culture plays a significant role in behavioral differences between consumers: in the same way, where such differences in perceptions are not apparent between cohorts, it is likely that general symbolic meanings are salient.

This study builds on Srite and Karahanna’s (2006) cultural investigation method by conducting focus group discussions—a technique that has long been exploited to study ideas, feelings, and thoughts about specific topics of interest (Morgan 1988)—with randomly selected consumers in the environments of different context-cultural dimensions. Conducting focus group discussions with consumers can reveal finegrained insights into how group members perceive PCTs (Tremblay et al. 2010). Although it has often been overlooked in prior studies, this step is particularly important in terms of relating differences in consumers actual behavioral responses (as observed in the field experiments) to the socially constructed meanings held by the two sampled populations. Specifically, we examine how consumers in general describe a PCT “when they attribute explicitly a meaning to that artifact” (Bijker 2010, p. 68) and their perceptions as to its appropriateness for commercial purposes in their social settings. Understanding the similarities and differences between social groups’ perceptions about a particular PCT can help explain similar or dissimilar behavioral responses to product messages sent via that route.

Two focus group discussions were conducted, both by a moderator who was unaware of the whole scope of the study and had been trained to communicate clearly but not to express any personal views (Tremblay et al. 2010): their neutrality was important to ensure participants were not misled or guided toward offering “expected answers.” Eight participants (four female) from China and seven (three female) from Switzerland shared their thoughts and experiences in separate focus group discussions, which were videotaped and voice recorded (with their permission): all were recruited based on their participation in a popular social website in each country so as to ensure that they were active in their local online communities (and are thus more willing to share their ideas and thoughts about technologies). Each focus group discussion lasted 1.5 hours, and participants received monetary compensation based on a half-day’s salary at a national pay-scale (Table A3 in the online appendix summarizes their inputs). We recruited two coders in each country to code the focus group discussion data. Inter-coder reliability assessment indicated satisfactory consistencies between them, i.e., 96% and 90% in Switzerland and China, respectively, both higher than the 70% threshold recommended by James et al. (1984).

General symbolic meanings of PCTs. In terms of their general use of email and SMS, participants from both cohorts expressed generally consistent views about the meanings and purposes of the two PCTs, specifically noting that the design of SMS (with its limited message length) made it suitable for delivering short instantaneous messages, and that of email favored more comprehensive and more deliberate messages. A Chinese participant commented, “SMS is more instant than email. SMS is usually short whereas email is lengthy,” and a Swiss participant agreed: “0 0 0 information 6written in an SMS message7 is so short and sparse.” These responses showed the two PCTs’ characteristics have produced symbolic meanings that are common across different context cultures, essentially associated with the degree to which each fosters a particular style of social communication—consistent with the literature’s suggestions (Heinonen and Strandvik 2007, Kim et al. 2007, Miyata 2006) that SMS is suited to intimate, personal communication and email to more distant, less intrusive communications. Seven of the eight Chinese participants indicated they used SMS to connect with friends and people to whom they felt close, but email for nonintimate communication: two commented, “I don’t use my email to contact my girlfriend and my parents. I’ll call them or send an SMS”; “I think it is formal to send messages by email. SMS is a personal way of communication.” Swiss participants generally echoed the views that SMS is more intrusive than email—as one said, “I prefer email because it is less intrusive than SMS.”

Perceptions of the Appropriateness of Different PCTs for Commercial Message Communication. Despite the consistent views about the general symbolic meanings of the two PCTs, participants in the two environments of different context-cultural dimension demonstrated a definite divergence in terms of their preferences for receiving product discount coupons via SMS or via email. Simply put, the Chinese participants preferred the former and the Swiss the latter. Six of the eight Chinese participants confirmed that it was quite common to receive such coupons via SMS in China, and when asked about their coupon forwarding preferences, all eight Chinese participants preferred their friends to forward coupons to them by SMS—one remarked that passing on coupons to friends via SMS was “practical.” In contrast, four of the seven Swiss participants indicated that they preferred receiving coupons through email, with comments such as “I prefer email 6for receiving coupons7 because I consider SMS more private.” However, the other three Swiss participants preferred SMS, because of the mobile phone’s portability, even though one noted that “if they 6retailers7 start to send me too many messages 6SMSs7 with marketing offers, then that would be a problem for me.” When asked about which technology they prefer to use to forward coupons, five of the seven Swiss participants preferred email—one commented (and the others agreed) that “There are privacy issues. Forwarding an email coupon is easier 6than sending an SMS7. Another thing is, if I forward an SMS coupon, I have to inform my friends 6before sending it7.”

These differences provided an initial indication that the two countries’ different context-cultural environments played a role in influencing consumer responses to the dissemination of coupons via a PCT, and were generally in line with our arguments and hypotheses. We then proceeded to test these hypotheses by conducting matching field experiments in China and Switzerland that have distinct context-cultural dimensions.

## 6. Study 2—Field Experiment

In study 2, we conducted two matching field experiments in two different context-cultural countries—low and high—and sent commercial messages via two different PCTs—email and SMS. We chose to conduct field experiments, i.e., those conducted outside a laboratory in natural settings, to gain a better understanding of the use and propagation of the product offer, neither of which could be properly observed via a survey or a laboratory experiment. We could have used a secondary data approach, but this would have suffered from the lack of the experimental rigor needed to isolate causes.

Field experiments afford other important advantages. Two key benefits are that they achieve higher ecological validity, and that demand characteristics are reduced, as participants are less aware of the research and its focus: an enduring concern with conducting traditional laboratory experiments is that participants can guess the experiment administrators’ expectations and respond accordingly. In other words, the field experiment methodology, which involves a minimum degree of interference and experimental manipulation, affords the ideal methodology for our investigation of consumer responses to product messages disseminated via different PCTs in different countries. Notwithstanding these advantages, conducting field experiments in two environments posed three significant challenges.

First, the products offered had to be relevant to the target consumer populations in each environment— we addressed this issue by collaborating with a food retailer in each country: a Swiss sandwich retailer, and a Chinese retailer that offered biscuits. In each case, these products represented typical snack foods in their respective countries, and were chosen after we had conducted two unstructured surveys (of 30 people in each country), which asked them to indicate the types of products for which they preferred to receive discount coupons. The majority chose food-related products, so we asked them to list three most commonly consumed snack foods, using questions framed to avoid any specific overwhelming likes or dislikes. We then chose the most-cited products—sandwiches in Switzerland, biscuits in China—and arranged for our retailing collaborator to offer coupons for them. By keeping to a constant product type in each field experiment we were better able to compare the relative impact of the use of different PCTs for coupon dissemination.

The second problem was that conducting a full factorial designed experiment (i.e., two context-cultural dimensions <sub>×</sub> 2 PCTs) in “real-life” settings is often impossible because of varied environmental constraints (e.g., availability of cooperating retailers, identification and availability of suitable environments, differences in consumers’ demographic characteristics and willingness to participate in field experiments, etc.). We therefore conducted the experiments in two countries with different context-cultural dimensions and sent the product coupons out via two different focal PCTs in two periods, to which participants were randomly assigned (Figure A1 in the online appendix). In other words, each participant could only receive either an SMS message or an email message but not both. We had to address the possibility that the order of administering different PCTs to disseminate the product discount coupons might affect the results. For instance, subjects who received a coupon via email in the second period might have inadvertently learned about the coupon sent via SMS in the first period from their friends, and this could influence their responses to the coupon. To negate this potential confounding factor, we performed the experiment in two steps.

We recruited participants for the field experiments from two separate locations in each country. We chose two top-ranked universities in each city (denoted as university 1 and university 2), both of which functioned as complete learning, living, and commercial environments. Despite being located in the same city, people at one university seldom communicate with those at the other. In post-experiment analyses, we confirmed that no participants from either university received, used, or forwarded coupons originally sent to participants in the other university.

Within each country, we first administered the coupons via SMS (in period 1) followed by email (in period 2) to participants in university 1. Each period lasted two weeks and the coupons were only valid for 14 days (expiration dates were stated in the coupon message). To rule out the potential confounding order effects as explained, we administered the coupons via email to participants in university 2 in each country. However, resource constraints precluded us from administering the coupons via SMS in period 2 to participants at university 2 in each country. We were therefore unable to conduct a fully counterbalanced design. Consequently, we used the data obtained from the second university in each country (university 2) solely to rule out any order effects and excluded it from subsequent analyses.

To monitor and track the use and forwarding of coupons, we developed a system to handle the two communication technologies, equipped with twodimensional bar code generation and authentication features, as well as coupon tracking features (Figure A2 in the online appendix gives an architectural overview of this system and illustrates the user-centric nature of the process). Participants were offered a Web interface that enabled them to sign up for a “mailing list.” We also provided our collaborating food retailers with a Web-based interface that they could input data about the discounted products, which—given that we focused on a single product in each country—they only needed to do once. Our tracking system was also multilingual. To facilitate tracking of coupon dissemination, the system generated digital coupons with unique identification numbers using a two-dimensional bar code system, which was not visually interpretable. Coupons could only be redeemed once, by presenting them to the retailer, where they were scanned and authenticated instantly. To forward an SMS coupon, consumers simply added a friend’s mobile number to a special short number. Through a special arrangement with the local telecommunication service companies, when a consumer forwarded a coupon via SMS, our system acted as a gateway that received the forward request and rerouted the coupon to the intended recipient, generating a new unique identifier. Email coupons were forwarded (provided they had not expired) via a link embedded in each coupon—participants simply clicked on the link and inserted the email address of the intended recipient on the system’s webpage: the system acted as a gateway in the same way— generating a new unique identifier and rerouting the coupon to the intended recipient. If participants wanted to forward coupons to more than one recipient, our system generated unique coupon identifiers for each of them. Receiving and forwarding both email and SMS coupons<sup>7</sup> were free of charge. Snapshots of product coupon messages sent via SMS and email are available in Figures A3 and A4 in the online appendix.

## 6.1. Participants

There were 1,298 consumers (746 in China and 552 in Switzerland) who signed up for the two field experiments (refer to supplementary explanation box A2 in the online appendix for the recruitment details). In the Chinese sample, 243 participants received SMS messages in round 1 and 263 participants received email messages in round 2 at one university (university 1); 240 participants at the second university (university 2) received only email messages in round 1. In the Swiss sample, 184 participants received SMS messages in round 1 and another 184 participants received email messages in round 2 at university 1 in Switzerland; 184 participants at university 2 in the country received only email messages in round 1. Participants were told the study was to gain a greater understanding of consumer responses to product discount coupons, but not when the experiment would be conducted, how the coupons would be disseminated (e.g., through email or SMS), and what product would be offered, enabling us to understand consumer behavior without participants being self-conscious about being involved in an experiment.

Consumers signing up on our system website were asked for demographic information and to indicate their willingness to participate and have their behavior in the experiment tracked (a mandatory procedure to protect their information disclosure rights). Chinese participants were aged 23.34 years on average, and 32.82% were female, and Swiss participants were 23.38 years old on average, and 32.20% were female; all indicated having experience with both Internet and mobile phone use. Our examination of their demographic information suggested they were good representatives of their target populations. For instance, a 2011 report by iResearch (a leading Chinese online marketing consultancy firm) suggests online shoppers are mainly college students and young white-collar workers aged between 19 and 30. Young people are also reported as constituting a key cohort of the Swiss online shopper population (Längenbold 2011). We also tested for nonresponse bias by comparing the chi squares of the responses to demographic questions of the first and last 25% of those who signed up for the experiment (Armstrong and Overton 1977)—as there was no significant difference, we judged nonresponse bias was not a pertinent concern. After the study, participants were asked to complete a survey about their perceptions as to the value of the coupon, and were encouraged to do so by being entered into a draw for 20 cash prices (ranging from US\$10 to US\$200).

## 6.2. Variables and Measurements

This study focuses on three dependent variables: perceived coupon value (measured subjectively) and redemption and forwarding of coupons (both measured objectively). Perceived coupon value was measured using three items adapted from Urbany et al. (1988). Coupon redemption by consumers actually using coupons to buy products, and coupon forwarding by them passing coupons on to others were both tracked by our system. To account for differences between individual consumers, we also included control variables related to individual characteristics and the accessibility of the stores selling the products. These constructs included price consciousness, desire for enjoyment from shopping, and convenience of transportation to the store—all except the last were referenced from the prior literature (Table A4 in the online appendix depicts the constructs, their corresponding items, and the convergent and discriminant validity tests).

## 6.3. Tests of Order Effects of Administering PCTs in University 1

Recall that in university 1, each participant could only receive either an SMS message (which was disseminated in period 1) or an email message (which was disseminated in period 2), but not both; whereas in university 2, each participant only received email messages in period 1 (there was no period 2). When comparing the reactions of SMS recipients and email recipients in university 1, it could be that the order of administering the different PCTs to disseminate the product discount coupons affects the results. For instance, subjects who received a coupon via email in the second period might have inadvertently learned about coupons sent via SMS in the first period from their friends, and this could influence their responses to the coupon. To rule out this potential order effect, we compared the responses to the coupons sent via email to participants at both sets of universities (i.e., email recipients in university 1 versus university 2). If there were no significant differences in their responses to the coupons sent via email, we can rule out the potential confounding effects of the treatment order in university 1. Statistical tests revealed no significant differences in perceived coupon value (Chinese: mean difference 00165, p > 0010; Swiss: mean difference 00035, p > 0010), redemption of coupons (Chinese:  00309, z 1039, p > 0010; Swiss:  00688, z 1062, p > 0010), and forwarding of coupon (Chinese:  00505, z 0085, p > 0010; Swiss:  00448, z 1022, p > 0010). Based on these results, we concluded that the possibility for confounding order effects was minimal.

## 7. Study 3—Follow-Up Survey

We conducted a follow-up survey with the field experiment participants in both China and Switzerland to augment our understanding of the relationship between context-cultural environments and individuals’ preferential responses to coupons disseminated via different

PCTs. We had three specific objectives in developing this follow-up survey: (1) to further validate the perceptions and symbolic interpretations related to the focal PCTs (SMS and email) and the context-cultural dimensions; (2) to gain a deeper understanding of the theoretical mechanisms that explain the consumer responses observed in the field experiments; and (3) to rule out potential confounding factors that may have affected our results. Specifically, there could be differences in consumer access to related technological resources (for using SMS and email) between the contexts of China and Switzerland; also policy conditions associated with sending unsolicited commercial messages could impact the responses to the use of the PCTs for such message dissemination by consumers in different countries.<sup>10</sup> Thus, it is important to ensure that our findings were not confounded by these factors.

Earlier, we argued that different context-cultural environments would induce differences in receptiveness to commercial messages received via two PCTs (SMS, email). Specifically, the Chinese consumers in an environment of high context-cultural dimension, who are used to and comfortable with being intimately engaged, would demonstrate higher receptiveness to a coupon received via SMS than one received via email because of a fit of the former’s intimate and personal characteristics with their communication preferences. In contrast, Swiss consumers would be more responsive to a coupon received via the less intrusive email than one received via SMS, because of a fit between the symbolic meanings of email with their communication preference that emphasizes personal distance and space.

Our follow-up survey assessed the constructs of context culture,<sup>11</sup> perceived intrusiveness of SMS/email, comfort with intimacy, intention to redeem an SMS/email coupon and intention to forward an SMS/email coupon, to capture consumer responses to the coupon disseminated via the two PCTs. These assessments permitted us to ascertain the mechanisms through which culture influences individuals’ preferential responsiveness to different PCTs more directly. We also included accessibility to technological resources and policy conditions (regarding the dissemination of unsolicited commercial messages) to account for their potential confounding influences on our findings. At last, we also controlled for the following: individuals’ need for shopping enjoyment (as consumers who enjoy shopping may respond better to the shopping discount); price consciousness (as consumers who are more price conscious may be more likely to react favorably to the coupon); propensity to share (as consumers who tend to share with others may be more likely to forward the coupon received); experience with SMS/email (as consumers who have greater experience in using a PCT may be more receptive with receiving the coupon via the PCT); and basic demographics (age, gender, income, and education). Figure 2 depicts the conceptual model of the paths our surveys tested. The measurements of the constructs were adapted from prior literature where appropriate (see Table A5 in the online appendix)— some were self-developed for the study’s purpose, so a thorough instrument validation was conducted using the procedures given in DeVellis (2003).

In the Chinese sample, results show that the contextcultural environment of consumers in this sample is associated with them being more comfortable with being intimately engaged. Culture-induced comfort with intimacy is, in turn, negatively associated with their perceived intrusiveness of SMS coupons. This also supports the social construction perspective that social setting shapes perceptions of a PCT. Lower perceived intrusiveness of SMS coupons is associated with higher intentions to redeem and forward SMS coupons (i.e., a negative relationship). Intention to redeem SMS coupons is also associated with price consciousness (positive relationship), age (negative relationship), and income (positive relationship). With respect to the intention to redeem email coupons, the dependent variable is associated with price consciousness (positive relationship), age (negative relationship), and email experience (positive relationship). For intention to forward email coupons, it is associated with sharing propensity and email experience (both positive relationships). Perceived email intrusiveness does not influence both intentions associated with email coupons.

In the Swiss sample, perceived intrusiveness of both SMS and email is negatively associated with their intentions to redeem the coupon received via either PCT, whereas (the relatively low level of) comfort with intimacy does not act to reduce such intrusiveness perceptions. Intention to redeem email coupons is also positively associated with price consciousness and need for shopping enjoyment. With respect to the forwarding intentions, the results show that although perceived intrusiveness of SMS is negatively associated with the intention to forward SMS coupons, it does not influence the intention to forward email coupons. Additionally, both intentions are positively associated with Swiss consumers’ need for shopping enjoyment and their level of income.

Overall, the results from the follow-up survey (descriptive statistics and results of the structural model analyses) are generally in line with our field experiment observations, while affording several additional insights. Specifically, the follow-up survey allows us to demonstrate the theoretical chain of how context culture may contribute to the difference in responses to SMS versus email coupons between the Chinese and Swiss consumers. The insight is that in contrast to the Swiss consumers, a high context culture among the Chinese consumers promotes a high comfort with intimacy in them, which reduces their perceived intrusiveness of the SMS coupon, and is in turn associated with their high intention to redeem and forward the SMS coupon. This does not only support the social construction perspective that social setting may shape perceptions of PCT, but also the mechanism through which it operates. The results also demonstrate the favorable outcomes (enhanced consumer receptiveness toward the use of a PCT for disseminating commercial messages) resulted from a fit between PCT spirit and communication culture, both within a social setting (i.e., higher intentions to redeem and forward SMS coupons compared to email coupons among the Chinese consumers, and vice versa for the Swiss consumers); and across social settings (i.e., comparatively lower perceived intrusiveness of SMS coupons in the Chinese sample than the Swiss sample, which can be attributed to differences in their context cultural dimension). Nevertheless, it should be noted that the follow-up survey mainly serves the purpose of providing further support and insights to the field experiments’ results, and does not aim at offering a complete framework for understanding consumer responses to coupon redemption and forwarding.

## 8. Discussion

Taken as a whole, using data obtained from two field experiments and preexperiment validations, our research suggests that there are two important elements in understanding consumer responses to the use of PCTs for commercial message communication—the general symbolic meanings users attach to those PCTs and the context-cultural dimension associated with consumers’ specific social settings. We conducted two preexperiment validations to substantiate our considerations of different context-cultural dimensions and alternative PCTs. Through a survey, we validate the context-cultural dimension differences between Chinese and Swiss consumers. Building on this discrimination, we conducted focus group interviews to validate their perceptions of the PCTs selected for this research, observing that, although consumers across the two environments of different context-cultural dimensions generally expressed consistent views about the meanings and purposes of different PCTs, they also expressed strikingly different preferences for their use in the dissemination of commercial messages: Chinese (high context-cultural dimension) consumers preferred SMS over email for receiving commercial messages, whereas Swiss (low context-cultural dimension) consumers preferences were the reverse.

The results were substantiated with the two field experiments, which provided richer and more specific findings. Consumers in the environment of high context-cultural dimension (China) perceived digital product discount coupons as having a greater value, and exhibited a higher propensity to redeem and forward coupons sent to them via SMS than those sent by email. Holding all other variables constant, they were 14308% and 18505% more likely to redeem and forward a coupon when it was disseminated via SMS than via email. The environment of low context-cultural dimension (Switzerland) yielded contrasting findings: although Swiss consumers were indifferent to whether their discount coupon was received via SMS or email in terms of redeeming them, they saw the coupon that was received via email as more valuable than that received via SMS, and were more inclined to forward them. Holding all other variables constant, they were 8804% more likely to forward a coupon when it was disseminated via email than via SMS. Our follow-up surveys provided further support for our field observations, while also affording deeper insights into the theoretical chain of how the pertinent context-cultural dimension operated in leading to the different consumer responses toward coupons disseminated via email and SMS in each social setting.

## 8.1. Limitations

Before we discuss our study’s implications, readers should be aware of some caveats to our research findings. First, compared to laboratory experiments, the field experiment methodology adopted in this research gave us less control over the environment— but we judged the greater control typically offered by laboratory experiments worth trading off against the greater realism afforded by our real-life investigation setting.

Second, we focused on food coupons—for sandwiches in Switzerland and biscuits in China—which are typically smaller-ticket snack items. Consumer responses to other products beyond snacks (e.g., those of hedonic nature such as toys and games, and those related to work such as office equipment and productivity tools), or higher-end-ticket items (such as electronic appliances and jewelry), might differ. Furthermore, albeit belonging to the same category of snack products and assessed for their similarity in preferences of consumers for such products in their respective setting, different snack items were chosen for the field experiments across the two countries. Future investigations could extend this study to different types of products to see if product type also influences consumer responses to coupons disseminated via PCTs, and, as much as possible, choose identical products in their studies.

Third, the majority of the consumers in our field experiments were university students—although this representation is certainly limited, in fact, this population represents a major force in today’s markets,<sup>12</sup> and retailers see understanding their consumption behaviors as important. They are also today’s major technology users (Barboza 2010), which makes marketing communications initiatives aimed at them that leverage on PCTs particularly promising. Furthermore, we emphasize that their universities themselves were complete, functional commercial ecosystems.

Fourth, our field experiments involved only two countries with polarized context-cultural dimensions— future studies could make similar investigations in countries with communication cultures at intervening points along the high/low continuum of the context dimensions.

Fifth, this research examines only one dimension of culture at the societal/national level, i.e., the contextcultural dimension—there are several other dimensions that are not examined because of their relatively lesser relevance to this study’s objectives. Future studies could further enrich the exploration of consumer uses of PCTs in scenarios that make the consideration of other cultural dimensions relevant (Leidner and Kayworth 2006). For example, future research may investigate consumer responses to PCT-disseminated messages that promote sophisticated financial products and manipulate the clarity of the messages to examine how the uncertainty avoidance dimension influences consumer responses. Future research may also incorporate individual- or collective-based awards in the PCT-disseminated commercial messages to investigate how the individualism/collectivism dimension influences the tendency of consumers in different social settings to share the messages.

Finally, our empirical experiments involved comparisons of people in two countries and because of the natural setting of their context-cultural dimension, we were unable to achieve full randomization, i.e., having people randomly located in each country. Nonetheless, we maintain that our experiment design was reasonable as the people were exogenously located.

## 8.2. Theoretical and Practical Implications

Notwithstanding these caveats, we believe this study offers several important, and related, contributions in both theory and practice areas (see Table A11 in the online appendix). In terms of research, this study builds on and extends both the apparatgeist and the social construction theories, and demonstrates that these two apparently contradictory perspectives can be employed in combination to gain a richer understanding about user responses to the use of PCTs for communicating commercial messages. We conducted preexperiment validations and subsequent field experiments (including both subjective and objective dependent variables), which were further supported with follow-up surveys, to gain empirical findings that contribute toward the current dialogue about how PCTs should be understood, which has attracted the attention of academics and practitioners alike.

By triangulating consumers’ actual behaviors with their qualitative perceptual inputs (i.e., their perceptual and behavioral responses to messages delivered via different PCTs) through multiple methods, we were able to gain a comprehensive understanding of why consumers in environments of different context-cultural dimensions respond more/less favorably to product messages disseminated via different PCTs. For example, our focus group and survey results added depth to our statistical findings from the objective and perceptual data gathered in our field experiments: taken together, they explain not only which type of PCTs 4SMS or email5 should be used to deliver product offers to consumers in environments of high or low context-cultural dimension 4here, China and Switzerland, respectively5 to yield favorable responses, but also the underlying reasons for the different responses in these two environments of polarized contextcultural dimensions.

Our essential finding is that, regardless of their cultural environments, consumers unanimously perceived SMS as more intimate and also more intrusive than email—due to it being designed for short, instantaneous and personal communication. They also clearly expressed different preferences about the use of the two PCTs for communicating commercial messages, which depended on the prevailing interaction and communication values and practices in their respective cultural environments. These considerations called for the concurrent investigations of both a PCT’s general symbolic meaning (its spirit) and the social setting of its application, which was afforded by our research focus and design (two PCTs and two context-cultural dimensions), in contrast to the existing literature that seldom considers variations in technologies and social settings simultaneously. With respect to technology, most prior studies have focused on a particular technology in a single setting (e.g., Lee 1994, Sahay et al. 1994) or in multiple settings (e.g., Sahay and Robey 1996), or compared technologies, but in a single setting (e.g., Lei 2010, Miranda and Saunders 2003). Our findings warrant an integrated examination of the interplay between the PCTs deployed and the targeted social settings in future research. With respect to culture, our operationalization of culture adds to previous literature that conceives it at the country level without specifying a particular cultural dimension (e.g., Tan et al. 1998, Mejias et al. 1997). Such operationalizations might be distant from the fundamental locale in which a communication technology is used. By considering the context-cultural dimension of an environment, a dimension that is closely related to information processing and communication (Leidner and Kayworth 2006) but that has rarely been considered in relevant research, this study calls for reconsideration of how we should investigate the interplay between technology and culture in communication settings.

Although not the main focus of this study, our field experiment results also indicate source of information to be another important predictor of consumer responses to the commercial message in China and Switzerland. Specifically, source of information was a significant predictor of perceived coupon value and redemption in both China and Switzerland. It also influences coupon forwarding in China, but not in Switzerland. It could be that in an environment of low context-cultural dimension such as Switzerland, where consumers are keen to retain their personal space, the seemingly invasive action of forwarding a commercial message may either be downplayed by using less intimate and less intrusive PCTs such as email, or avoided altogether even if it was originally shared by their peers. In view of the more positive responses in terms of value perception and redemption rate of the commercial message when it was sent by peers (e.g., merchants), an interesting future research agenda is to explore in greater detail how the source of messages, especially commercial messages, varies across different communication cultures.

In terms of practice, this study provides retailers and service providers with important insights about using PCTs for effective cross-border commercial message communications. Given retailers’ increasing initiatives in using PCTs to further their foreign market expansion ambitions (Ferris 2007), our findings caution that it is vital they do not just adopt the most convenient approach, employing a single PCT for global commercial message dissemination in a “one-size-fits-all” manner, as has been typical in their use of such traditional mass media as television. Rather, they must dedicate deliberate attention to deploying the PCT most appropriate to a target consumer market, and this study offers specific guidelines to retailers contemplating the use of SMS and email to disseminate product offers. To obtain better responses—in terms of more favorable value perception, higher redemption rates, and more extensive forwarding—SMS (instead of email) should be the PCT of choice in environments of high context-cultural dimension (such as China)—but in environments of low context-cultural dimension (such as Switzerland), email (rather than SMS) is likely to induce more favorable value perception and more extensive forwarding.

## 9. Conclusion

Retailers’ increasing efforts to enter foreign markets have resulted in a growing need to understand how communication technologies can best be harnessed to achieve favorable outcomes. It stands to reason that, in the age of globalization, not understanding local consumers’ perceptions and preferences of PCTs can severely undermine the viability of cross-border product promotion. We built on both apparatgeist and social construction theories to propose a valid proposition that not only yielded valuable insights for research into cross-cultural comparisons, but also provided a theoretically driven principle to guide the selection of appropriate PCTs to support cross-border marketing initiatives.
