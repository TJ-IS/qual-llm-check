# Quality of health-related online search results

Brent Kitchens <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Christopher A. Harle <sup>a</sup>, Shengli Li <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Florida, United States

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 6 November 2012

Keywords: Health search Online information quality Online medical information Health website quality Search quality

## a b s t r a c t

Consumers are increasingly searching for health information online and using that information to inform their decisions and behavior. Because the negative consequences of basing decisions on inaccurate or untrustworthy health information may be particularly serious, it is important to understand the quality of online health information. This study empirically investigates the quality of health information that is returned by popular search engines when queried using a large, comprehensive set of health-related search terms. Findings indicate that a majority of such information returned by popular search engines is of a high quality but quality levels vary across different health topic areas. In particular, searches for terms related to preventive health and social health issues tend to produce lower quality results than terms related to diagnosis and treatment of physical disease or injury. While the overall prevalence of high quality information is greater than that of low quality, the observed variance across health-related terms has important implications for consumers, policy makers, health information providers, and search engines

## 1. Introduction

Online search for health information has become nearly ubiquitous as people increasingly use the Internet as a substitute or supplement to traditional sources of health care guidance, creating a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant need to understand the quality of online health information. Health consumers' search tends to focus on information about diseases, medical problems, treatments, and procedures [12], and “on any given day, more people are posing health questions to Google than posing health questions to their doctors” [6]. If this behavior often leads consumers to high quality information, we can expect that people will be more informed and make better decisions about their health. On the other hand, if these searches commonly lead to inaccurate or unsubstantiated health information, there is cause for concern about the effect on people's health beliefs and decisions. Therefore, it is important to know if typical information search behavior leads consumers to accurate and credible health information.

Sixty-one percent of U.S. adults seek health information online, and six out of ten members of this group report that their most recent search in<sup>fl</sup>uenced their health-related decisions [13]. Moreover, 65% of people who obtain health information online begin their inquiry through popular search engines, in contrast to 27% who begin at a health-related website [11], suggesting that search engine results may play an important role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing people's health-related decisions. Inaccurate or misleading results could lead people to ignore important symptoms and delay or even refuse recommended health care. Low quality results could also lead people to seek unnecessary health care or implement unproven or potentially harmful at-home treatments. While online health information seeking has the potential to create informed consumers who engage more actively with their health care providers, 46% of people who have retrieved health information online report that they never discuss this information with their physician [21]. This exacerbates the potential for a negative impact from poor quality online search results.

First-page results returned in a search engine query for the word “health” include well-known health care providers such as the Mayo Clinic (www.mayoclinic.com), a federal government health portal (www. health.gov), commercial health information providers such as WebMD (www.webmd.com), and the health news section of the New York Times website (www.nytimes.com/pages/health).<sup>1</sup> However, the same search also returns the Wikipedia entry for the word “health” and the Yahoo! Directory for health, each of which may be more likely to contain or link directly to less accurate or less trustworthy health information. Similarly, a query on the term “newborn vaccines” returned six (out of ten) <sup>fi</sup>rst-page results that linked to blog postings or community forum discussions about delaying or refusing medically recommended pediatric vaccinations. This search returned only one result from a well-known scienti<sup>fi</sup>c, healthcare, or news organization — a Nature news article (www. nature.com/news/2006/060425/full/news060424-3.html).

These examples highlight the importance of systematically characterizing the quality of results when typical health-related queries are performed using popular search engines. To our knowledge, the information systems literature has not comprehensively studied the relationships between search engine results rankings and health information quality.

This study contributes to the literature by assessing this relationship generally for a large corpus of health-related query terms and also across speci<sup>fi</sup>c health term categories.

## 1.1. Assessing online information quality

Much of the information systems research on website quality has focused on user-perceived quality attributes and their relationship to behavioral use intentions rather than more objective measures of information accuracy [22,25–27,42]. For example, perceived usefulness, ease of use, hedonic quality, and visual attractiveness have been shown to be independently perceived by users and contribute to their intentions to use a website [29]. These behavioral attributes may be related to objective measures of information accuracy. For instance, insofar as information accuracy can be perceived by lay users, people may rate websites with more accurate information higher on attributes such as usefulness and ease of use. However, for policy purposes, it is valuable to study website information accuracy directly. This is especially important in technical and popular search domains such as health care in which laypeople may not be able to reliably assess information accuracy and may base important decisions on the in formation they <sup>fi</sup>nd [13].

In 2004, Fricke and Fallis [14] raised concern about the accuracy of information found on the Internet due to the lack of “editorial controls of more traditional sources of information.” In terms of health information quality, Eysenbach and colleagues' review of 79 studies shows that the methods and rigor which have been used to study the quality of online information vary [8]. The studies they review have widely differing conclusions, although almost 70% conclude that there is a problem with the quality of health information on the Internet. Subsequent studies on speci<sup>fi</sup>c health topics also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the reliability and accuracy of online heath information vary widely [1,2,20,34,36].

Many instruments have been proposed for rating the quality of health-related websites [15], and <sup>fi</sup>ve general approaches to assessing their quality have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned: codes of conduct, quality labels, user guides, <sup>fi</sup>lters, and third party certi<sup>fi</sup>cation [41]. Codes of conduct are criteria applied during the development of websites to help ensure quality. Quality labels are logos or symbols displayed by websites to communicate their commitment to abide by a code of conduct. These labels may or may not be assigned by a third party, which in effect certi<sup>fi</sup>es websites against codes of conduct. Filters are gateways which may be used to present only websites that meet some set of quality criteria. Finally, user guides are tools that may be published by third parties to assist users themselves in judging websites' quality. Among these approaches, there is no consensus on a single best method for ensuring that consumers consistently obtain high quality information from health-related websites. However, common among all approaches is an emphasis on the accuracy and trustworthiness of a website's health information. For the remainder of this paper, we conceptualize quality as a website's information accuracy and trustworthiness.

The <sup>fi</sup>ve general approaches described above have been combined by well-known organizations as tools for evaluating or certifying health-related websites. The not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t Health On the Net Foundation (HON) established the Health On the Net Code of Conduct “for the provision of authoritative, trustworthy Web-based medical information.” HON certi<sup>fi</sup>es websites against this code upon request, which allows certi<sup>fi</sup>ed websites to display a quality label [18]. Over 7200 websites were certi<sup>fi</sup>ed by HON as of June 2010. Certi<sup>fi</sup>cation criteria include the authority of included information, attribution to sources of information, justi<sup>fi</sup>ability of claims, a clear distinction of advertisements and website content, and transparency. HON certi<sup>fi</sup>cation is free of charge, and certi<sup>fi</sup>ed websites are reviewed on an annual basis. HON was founded by representatives from the World Health Organization, the European Commission, and the US National Library of Medicine.

MedlinePlus is “the National Institutes of Health's Web site for patients and their families and friends” [30]. Among other resources, MedlinePlus hosts a health information search page that <sup>fi</sup>lters results to present only those from trusted sources. Instead of certifying websites, MedlinePlus chooses and links to a limited number of websites that are judged to provide authoritative but not redundant health information. MedlinePlus' inclusion criteria include the quality, authority, and accuracy of the health content; differentiation between content and advertising; and reliability of the other websites to which the evaluated website has links.

## 1.2. Search engine ranking and information quality

Despite the existence of HON, MedlinePlus, and other websites that consumers can use as direct portals to high quality health information, the vast majority of online health information is gathered through general search engines, such as Google [7]. One risk of these general search engines is that they may return and order web page results in a way that is unrelated to the accuracy or trustworthiness of information they contain. Furthermore, the ranking of general search engine results is important because people tend to limit their attention to the <sup>fi</sup>rst page (or even the <sup>fi</sup>rst few) results [7], and traf<sup>fi</sup>c to ranked results diminishes rapidly with decreasing rank [10]. Therefore, if search engines generally, or for speci<sup>fi</sup>c categories of searches (such as “newborn vaccines”), rank low quality results highly, there is an increased likelihood that consumers will receive inaccurate information, which may negatively affect their health knowledge, behavior, and decisions.

In the context of health information, it is unclear whether general search engines return and rank results in a way that correlates with information accuracy and trustworthiness. While the ranking mechanisms of general search engines are proprietary, it is known that they are based on measures of relevance, comprehensiveness, freshness, and speed [39], which do not necessarily align with quality in terms of information accuracy and trustworthiness. Google's PageRank algorithm [5], which formed the basis of its original search engine, ranks individual websites within search results by estimating their importance as determined by the number of links to the website and the importance of those websites that link to it. The ranking algorithm has evolved over time, but PageRank is still an important component [39]. Bing and Yahoo!, two other popular search engines, share a search algorithm [35], and its results are thought to be similar to Google's [32]. Thus, the importance of a website as determined by the number and strengths of referring links is key to its search ranking across most popular search engines.

## 1.3. Research objective and conceptual model

The objective of this study is to assess whether search engines, when queried using common health-related terms, tend to rank websites with high quality information higher in their results than websites with low quality information.

Fig. 1 provides a conceptual overview of the relationships being studied. As described previously, it is known that the number and strength of referring links are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors in a website's search engine results rank or importance. In this study, we have conceptualized website quality as a measure of information accuracy and trustworthiness, which, as often assessed by third-party certi<sup>fi</sup>ers, are a product of websites' authoritativeness, use of source attribution, justi<sup>fi</sup>ability of claims, distinction between advertising and actual health content, and transparency. The primary relationship of interest in this study is that between website quality and website importance. If quality is positively related to website importance, a majority of <sup>fi</sup>rst-page search results will contain high quality information.

One way for a website to achieve high ranking results is by providing accurate and trustworthy information that is deemed useful by consumers and other online information providers. This should lead to an increased number of referring links that are created by or for the use of these interested parties. Assuming that high quality information can be judged and is valued by consumers, the importance of high quality websites will increase as these links are created. Consider well known websites that were returned in our sample query above for the term “health:” www.mayoclinic.com, www.health.gov, and www.webmd. com. The stated intention of these websites is to provide accurate and trustworthy health information to the general public, and these websites have been evaluated as high quality by third parties, including HON and MedlinePlus. Almost certainly, their strategy of providing high quality information is related to their high rank in search results. These websites are more likely to link and lend importance to other high quality websites, and this should lead to a higher proportion and rank of high quality search results generally.

Just as high quality websites may achieve high search engine rankings, the search engine rank of some low quality websites may be also be high. For example, if lay health consumers incorrectly judge low quality websites to be high quality or otherwise judge such websites to be useful, there is an incentive for consumers and other online information providers to propagate and link to low quality websites. The earlier example of a search query for “newborn vaccines” showed that message board forums and blogs tended to be ranked higher than objectively higher quality websites, such as WebMD. This is one case where objectively lesser quality information has attained high search ranking, presumably by consumers and other information providers creating links to these social media websites.

Last, developers of either high or low quality websites may engage in various techniques, such as paying for links to their websites, in an attempt to increase their search results rankings. Many such practices are explicitly discouraged by search engines, but they still may have some effect on search results rankings outside the more organic process by which consumers and other providers of online information link websites that they judge to be high quality or important in other ways.

In summary, health information search rankings are a product of consumers and other online health information providers naturally linking to one another's content as well as more explicit attempts to optimize search results rankings. Under the assumptions that (1) consumers are, on average, more interested in high quality information than low quality information; (2) consumers are able to distinguish high quality information from low quality information in a majority of their searches; and (3) search engine optimization efforts tend to minimally in<sup>fl</sup>uence search results rankings, we propose that general search engines such as Google will tend to return high quality results in response to queries for health-related information. We also expect that the proportion of low versus high quality search results will vary across different health topics. This may occur for many reasons, such as consumers' decreased ability to judge information quality in a particularly technical area (e.g., medication selection and dosing), consumers' interest in low quality information (e.g., for entertainment purposes rather than health education), or marketers' interest in propagating low quality information (e.g., in order to sell a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product or service).

In this study, we use Health on the Net Certi<sup>fi</sup>cation status and inclusion in MedlinePlus to estimate the quality of websites returned by Google's general search engine when queried separately using over 2000 different health-related terms. Investigating the relationship between online health information quality and search engine results is relevant to information systems and health care research and may provide important insights for search engines, policy makers, health care professionals, and health consumers.

## 2. Methods

To create our analysis data set, we performed queries on Google's general search engine for each term in the National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus index of 2069 health terms as of March 9, 2011. MedlinePlus purports to be a resource for “patients and their families and friends,” to provide health-related information “in language you can understand,” which suggests that their terms match closely with actual terms that lay consumers use when creating health-related search queries. The index was used in a related study on health-related search queries [24] and includes terms related to body location/systems, disorders and conditions, diagnosis and therapy, demographic groups, and health and wellness. We included all terms, including acronyms, abbreviations, and occurrences of similar terms, such as “abdominal pain” and “pain, abdominal.” Preliminary searches showed that such permutations often provide different results or different rankings.

We used each term to perform a Google search on March 9, 2011. Searches were performed using a new Google pro<sup>fi</sup>le on a browser with no history or cookies stored, so that prior search history would not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the search results. The search location preference was set to “USA” so as to not include local search results, such as nearby physician of<sup>fi</sup>ces. For each search, we obtained the URLs for only the primary organic results returned in the <sup>fi</sup>rst page (the <sup>fi</sup>rst 10 results by default). Advertisements and vignettes, which are specialized results such as books, videos, and images, were excluded. Since vignettes occasionally appeared in place of primary organic results, the data contained between 8 and 10 (an average of 9.9) URLs per term.

Website quality was assessed at the domain rather than the individual page level because assessment criteria used by HON and MedlinePlus predominantly relate to domain-level characteristics. After obtaining the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of URLs returned for each search term (20,487 total), we manually extracted only the domain from each result. When present, the scheme, path, query string, and any other components were removed. For example, www.mayoclinic.com/health/brain-avm/DS01126 was truncated to www.mayoclinic.com. In all, 5249 unique domains were obtained from the 2069 pages of search results.

In the absence of a widely-accepted standard for evaluating website quality, we determined the quality of search results based on certi<sup>fi</sup>cation and evaluation by the HON Foundation and MedlinePlus, two independent and well-established organizations. This provided a transparent and objective quality measurement approach that is supported by prior literature [41]. Combining these two sources provided a more comprehensive quality assessment because HON's request-based certi<sup>fi</sup>cations capture relatively more commercial websites, while MedlinePlus evaluations capture relatively more government and non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t websites.

The quality of each of the 5249 unique domains was assessed on March 16, 2011 based separately on HON certi<sup>fi</sup>cation and MedlinePlus evaluation. For HON, a domain was considered high quality if a corresponding domain certi<sup>fi</sup>cation page was found on www.hon.ch. For MedlinePlus, we used its search tool at www.nlm.nih.gov/medlineplus to search for each domain. These searches produced three cases. For some domains, no results were returned. These were not labeled as high quality. Other searches produced one or more results which pointed directly to pages within the queried domain. For example, a MedlinePlus search for www.americanheart.org returned a result pointing to the page www.americanheart.org/presenter.jhtml?identi<sup>fi</sup>er=4439. These domains were labeled as high quality. Finally, some searches returned new domains that contained embedded links to the queried domain. While not directly evaluated by MedlinePlus, we judged these links as an indicator of quality for this third group because the queried domains were referenced by another website that had been evaluated as high quality. Based on this procedure, all domains were labeled as either certified (MedlinePlus), certified (HON), referenced, or not certified/referenced (Table 1). Our analysis below is performed separately considering only certi<sup>fi</sup>ed results as high quality, and considering both certi<sup>fi</sup>ed and referenced results as high quality.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Overall quality of search results

The primary unit of analysis was a search result set, and the proportion of quality results returned for each of the 2069 searches was the primary variable analyzed. For each term, we calculated the proportion of quality results returned on the entire <sup>fi</sup>rst page, and also the proportion of quality results at rank cutoffs ranging from 1 through 9 (Fig. 2). For example, at cutoff 3, this was calculated as the proportion of quality results ranked 3rd, 2nd, or 1st for each term.

Absent a more objective benchmark for judging the overall quality of a search result set, for each set we tested the mean proportion at each cutoff rank to determine if more than 50% of results were high quality. The mean proportion of quality websites returned in the full <sup>fi</sup>rst page of results was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than 0.50 for certi<sup>fi</sup>ed only and for certi<sup>fi</sup>ed plus referenced websites. The mean proportions of top 2 through top 9 results were also greater than 0.50, suggesting that high quality results are not heavily clustered around speci<sup>fi</sup>c positions on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of search results. Only the mean proportion of quality pages returned in the <sup>fi</sup>rst ranked position when measured using strictly certi<sup>fi</sup>ed websites was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than 0.50. This result is largely due to the fact that 36% of <sup>fi</sup>rst ranked results were from the domain Wikipedia.org, which is neither certi<sup>fi</sup>ed nor referenced by HON or MedlinePlus.

## 3.2. Differences in search results by health topic

Each MedlinePlus term is grouped into one or more of 44 categories by the National Library of Medicine (see appendix for a list). For example, the term “amnesia” belongs to three categories: “seniors,” “mental health and behavior,” and “brain and nerves.” To compare across categories, we used two measures for each term: average precision and quality proportion over the <sup>fi</sup>rst page. Average precision was used to determine the relative ranking, within the <sup>fi</sup>rst page, of high quality results across categories. Average precision is a summary measure of the relevance of a set of search result documents. In this study, relevance refers to health information quality. ## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Summary of results

This study has shown that the mean proportion of high quality results obtained when searching for health-related terms is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than 0.50, with high quality results approximately evenly distributed throughout the result set. Therefore consumers who exhibit common behavior by examining only the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of search results, or possibly only the <sup>fi</sup>rst few results returned, will be more likely than not to view results which lead them to high quality health information. Quality does, however, vary by category, with certain groups of health-related terms returning a higher proportion of quality results than others. In particular, search terms in categories such as “social and family issues,” “wellness and lifestyle,” and “food and nutrition,” which can more broadly be grouped as “health and wellness,” tend to return fewer quality results than terms in categories that relate more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to body systems, disorders and conditions, and diagnosis and therapy.

While this slight majority still allows for a reasonable probability that consumers obtain non-certi<sup>fi</sup>ed or non-referenced information, these percentages are likely a lower bound on the likelihood of consumers actually acquiring accurate or credible health information when their searches use MedlinePlus terms.

The search terms from MedlinePlus included several that are not exclusively health-related, such as “glasses” and “phosphorous.” The term “glasses” returned numerous results related to the purchase of designer eyeglass frames or sunglasses, while “phosphorus” returned a mix of health-relevant results and websites related to general chemistry. Similarly, the MedlinePlus terms included some ambiguous acronyms, such as “AVM” which medically refers to “arteriovenous malformation” but also refers to businesses in the automotive and technology industries. These terms and others represent under-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed queries which may return fewer health-relevant results [23]. During an actual health-related search, consumers are likely able to easily ignore such obviously non-relevant websites and may even create queries that automatically exclude these websites. For example, a search for the phrase “prescription glasses” or some other modi<sup>fi</sup>cation may return an increased proportion of high quality results.

This study used only HON and MedlinePlus as third-party indicators of the quality of health information contained in websites. While HON and MedlinePlus are unlikely to certify many low quality websites, they are also unlikely to cover all high quality websites. HON certi<sup>fi</sup>cation is granted by request only, and MedlinePlus explicitly states that it does not refer to multiple websites with redundant information. By nature of these methods, there will necessarily be high quality websites which are not certi<sup>fi</sup>ed or evaluated by either HON or MedlinePlus. For example, our data contained results that were likely high quality but were categorized as not certi<sup>fi</sup>ed or referenced, including an Oklahoma State Department of Health fact sheet on rabies and a description of rotavirus from a children's hospital af<sup>fi</sup>liated with Stanford University.

Additional evidence for this lower bound supposition can be found by looking at the top level domain name (e.g., .edu, .gov, .com, .org) for the search results in our data set. Top level domain is often indicated as an easy rule of thumb for judging health website quality — with a .edu website, for example, being likely to contain relatively accurate and trustworthy information. Only 18% of the 837 .edu websites in our data are certi<sup>fi</sup>ed and 51% are certi<sup>fi</sup>ed or referenced, which is likely an underestimate of the proportion of .edu websites that contain high quality information (Table 2).

## 4.3. Consumer behavior in evaluating online search results

There is also reason to believe that actual search behavior will lead consumers to acquire high quality health information more frequently than indicated by this study's result. While it is known that consumers tend to focus on a small set of highly ranked search results [7,10], it is naive to suggest that they indiscriminately click on links within the top few or <sup>fi</sup>rst page of results. It is more likely that health consumers use their experience and other signals when choosing which results to follow. For example, users can quickly judge the origin of a linked search result based on its top level domain, a preview of the website provided by the search engine, or the reputation of or prior experience with the company behind the website. Users may also attempt to discriminate between low and high quality after clicking through a search result, allowing them to return to the results as needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd high quality information. Assuming that consumers prefer high quality information and these processes, on average, yield more high quality than low quality, overall exposure to high quality information should be increased further.

Additionally, our study data indicated that the average certi<sup>fi</sup>ed domain was returned 11.15 times over all search results. This is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than the rate for the average referenced-only domain (3.61, pb0.001) and the rate for the average non-certi<sup>fi</sup>ed and non-referenced domain (2.08, pb0.001). As consumers repeatedly search for various health information over time, these differences will likely increase familiarity with individual high quality websites, which are returned over many search results. As familiarity and experience have been shown to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer intention to use websites [16,38], this may cause consumers to select high quality websites more frequently than their less familiar low quality counterparts.

## 4.4. Differences in search result quality between health topics

Several interesting results were observed when comparing search result quality between health topics. First, children's health and pharmaceuticals are two subject areas that have been scrutinized regarding the quality of information which can be found online [31,36]. However, the MedlinePlus category “genetic/birth defects,” actually had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher proportion of high quality results compared to other search terms, while categories “children and teenagers” and “pregnancy and reproduction” did not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower proportion of high quality results. They only indicate that searches on topics such as children's health or pharmaceuticals are no more likely to be lower quality than other health-related searches. Insofar as the absolute proportion of health-related search results is deemed inappropriately low (e.g., based on the potentially serious health risks related to obtaining low quality drug information), concern may still be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. There also appear to be general quality differences between categories related to health and wellness versus categories related to body systems, disorders and conditions, and diagnosis and therapy. Categories such as “wellness and lifestyle,” “safety issues,” and “food and nutrition” which are related to general health, wellness, and prevention have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower proportions of high quality results, while disorder or condition-speci<sup>fi</sup>c categories such as “diabetes,” “cancers,” and “infections” were all determined to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher quality proportions. The observed differences between categories may be caused by a number of factors, including actual disparities in information accuracy and trustworthiness or an imbalance in the topical focuses of online health information providers or third party certi<sup>fi</sup>ers. Another explanation is that the categories judged to be lower quality tend to return more clearly non-health-relevant results, such as discussed in the “glasses” query example above. The latter problem may be mitigated by health consumers' general ability to qualify their queries to ensure relevance or to simply ignore non-relevant results, but the former two have important policy implications. Given the importance of appropriate preventive health behaviors and service delivery [28,33] as well as social effects on health [4,19,37], there may be a need for increasing the availability, quality, and search engine importance of online information in these areas. If this information is equally available online but simply receives less attention from health information authorities, such as MedlinePlus, there may be a need for a more balanced focus by evaluators and disseminators of online health information. These points are underscored by the argument that health education pertaining to “health and wellness” is more useful to laypeople than are technical treatments of body systems or diseases. Information related to personal lifestyle, safety, and nutrition may provide more personally actionable advice to the average person than details about the endocrine or immune systems, which may be educational but less actionable without intervention from a health professional.

## 4.5. Limitations and future research

This study has additional limitations which have not been previously discussed. First, we only gathered results from a single search engine, as Google was the only popular search engine for which we were able to disable local results. Including local results might have more realistically imitated results obtained by an actual online searcher who obtains local results by default, but we believe doing so would have also increased the ambiguity and harmed the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Further, as discussed, Google is by far the most popular search engine in the U.S., and its closest two competitors, Bing and Yahoo, share an algorithm that is thought to be similar to Google's [32,35]. However, future work that includes local search results and results from additional search engines may be warranted to increase understanding of the relationship between online health information quality and search results. Additionally, this study only gathered results at one point in time. A longitudinal study of changes to search results and quality proportions could provide additional insights into how website quality and search ranking interact. For instance, such a study might <sup>fi</sup>nd that the event of certi<sup>fi</sup>cation by HON or MedlinePlus drives an increase in the relative search ranking for a website over time.

Second, this study did not measure actual consumer search behavior. Our corpus of search terms was chosen from a large, comprehensive repository that is focused on informing consumers about important health topics, but these may differ from actual search terms that consumers use in searching for health information. It has been shown that health consumers' online search tends to focus on information about diseases, medical problems, treatments, and procedures [12] — topics which are well represented in our corpus and roughly correspond to categories judged by our analysis to return average or above average proportions of quality results. However, we implicitly weighted each term in our corpus evenly, whereas in actual consumer search, some terms are certainly searched more frequently than others. Also, while experiments have shown that the majority of health-related searches are largely one word terms [7], consumers may use more complex phrases or combinations of terms in their searches, which also may impact the quality of results returned. To the extent that actual search behavior varies from a random draw on the corpus of terms used in this study, the quality of websites returned may also vary. In addition, as mentioned above, consumers are unlikely to indiscriminately click on links within the top few or <sup>fi</sup>rst page of results once they have performed their search. Future studies might bene<sup>fi</sup>t from analyzing both search engine query composition and website traf<sup>fi</sup>c statistics in order to investigate the relationship between consumer behavior such as term choice, search result characteristics including rank, and consumer information seeking behavior once search results are obtained.

Last, this study is limited to the health information domain. Obtaining quality information is a pervasive concern of consumers searching online. Health is arguably one of the most important domains given its ubiquitous personal relevance, but other domains may also warrant study, such as personal <sup>fi</sup>nance. In these other domains, a relevant challenge may be the ability to obtain a comprehensive, objective, and appropriate means of judging quality.

## 4.6. Implications

In this study, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the majority of information returned by popular search engines when queried for health information is of a high quality. This may help decrease concerns that health consumers are routinely obtaining and being in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by low quality health information on the Internet. However, because low quality information will never be completely removed from search engine results, there is opportunity for improvement. Terms related to social and preventive health were found to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower proportions of quality results, in contrast to terms related to diagnosis and treatment of physical injury and disease which were found to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher proportions. This may suggest that health information providers and interested policy makers should place an increased emphasis on disseminating additional high quality information in these lower quality areas and ensuring that existing information is properly assessed for quality.

Google or other search engines may also be interested in increasing the quality of information returned when consumers search for health information. While websites such as HON and MedlinePlus provide search engines which return only vetted websites, these tools are neither as well-known nor as widely used as Google or other popular search engines. Popular search engines are competing <sup>fi</sup>ercely in many areas (e.g., mapping services, payment services, academic search) to capture consumers. Inclusion of a health or medical search feature or display of certi<sup>fi</sup>cation status of results by popular search engines might bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers and increase demand for search engines which implement these measures. Alternatively, because channeling online consumers to health-speci<sup>fi</sup>c search engines may be challenging, policy-makers may be interested in supporting more visible approaches to certifying and marketing high quality online health information, so that ultimately consumers become more aware of and can more reliably distinguish between low and high quality websites.

## 5. Conclusion

This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that a majority of <sup>fi</sup>rst-page search results provide accurate and credible information when consumers search for a comprehensive set of health-related terms. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the quality of search results vary across different topic areas, which has important implications for providers of online health information and policy makers. Overall, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings combined with an assumption of a reasonable level of consumer savvy in searching for and processing health information suggest that the Internet and general search engines provide a valid resource for health information that can be used to augment more traditional health information delivery mechanisms.
