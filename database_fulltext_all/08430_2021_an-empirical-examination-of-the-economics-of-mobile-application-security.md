# AN EMPIRICAL EXAMINATION OF THE ECONOMICS OF MOBILE APPLICATION SECURITY <sup>1</sup>

Pallab Sanyal and Nirup Menon School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA U.S.A. {psanyal@gmu.edu} {nmenon@gmu.edu}

Mikko Siponen Faculty of Information Technology, University of Jyväskylä, FINLAND, {mikko.t.siponen@jyu.fi}

The growth of mobile devices coupled with advances in mobile technologies has resulted in the development and widespread use of a variety of mobile applications (apps). Mobile apps have been developed for social networking, banking, receiving daily news, maintaining fitness, and job-related tasks. The security of apps is an important concern. However, in some cases, app developers may be less interested in investing in the security of apps, if users are unwilling to pay for the added security. In this paper, we empirically examine whether consumers are less willing to pay for security features than for usability features. In addition, we examine whether a third-party certification of security features makes customers more willing to pay for security. Furthermore, we investigate the impact of risk perceptions on the willingness to pay for security. To explore these issues, we conducted a scenario-based experiment on mobile app users. Results from our analyses show that consumers are indeed less likely to pay for security features than usability features. However, the likelihood of paying for security features can be significantly increased by third-party certification of the features. Based on our analysis, we offer insights to producers of mobile apps to monetize the enhanced security features of their apps.

Keywords: Application security, mobile apps, willingness to pay, information asymmetry, risk perceptions

## Introduction

As mobile device ownership continues to grow, the global mobile applications (apps) market is expected to be worth over 400 billion by 2026 (Borasi and Baul 2019). With the explosive popularity and ubiquity of mobile apps, it has become paramount that apps are adequately secured. Vulnerabilities in an app not only exposes its users to security threats but also helps offenders use infected or hijacked devices (referred to as “bots”) to launch attacks against other devices. Despite its significance, however, app security has fallen short. A recent study found high-risk vulnerabilities in 38% of iOS apps and 43% of Android apps.<sup>2</sup>

The practitioner literature on mobile app security claims that firms producing apps have largely neglected the security of apps. For example, the Ponemon Institute (2015) reveals that nearly 40% of large companies, including many in the Fortune 500, are not taking proper precautions to secure the mobile apps they build for customers. Collet (2017) asserts that “security is still not a top priority in app design, with some apps allowing users to store or pass credentials in the clear or by using weak encryption.” In the broader context of software security, academics (e.g., Anderson and Moore 2006), and practitioners (e.g., Schneier 2004) alike have suggested that one of the reasons that application producers are reluctant to adequately invest in securing their products is that users are unwilling to pay for security features. However, there is some evidence that users may value security. Studies have shown that information technology (IT) security investments have a positive impact on stock prices, while information security breaches have a negative impact on the stock prices of firms (Chai et al. 2011; Spanos and Angelis 2016). Since the financial market’s valuation is a result of the potential for increased demand for a firm’s products and services from its consumers, it follows that consumers may indeed value security. Is it possible then that consumers value security but are unwilling to pay for it? In this study, we empirically examine both parts of this central question, i.e., (1) whether users are unwilling to pay for security features, and (2) whether they value security.

Researchers such as Anderson and Moore (2006), who claim that users are unwilling to pay for security, point to the difficulty of verifying security features as a probable contributing factor. For instance, two-factor authentications provided by apps vary in how well they protect accounts, but even security experts find it hard to evaluate different twofactor services (Brandom 2017). Similarly, most users are unable to differentiate the value provided by the competing security properties (e.g., encryption) of apps (Zhou et al. 2018). Therefore, even if vendors do secure their products, it is difficult for consumers to ascertain the quality of the security mechanisms in an app. Consequently, consumers may be unwilling to pay for something that is not easily verifiable.

Our research design relies on the premise that, although the security features of mobile apps (e.g., SSL encryption support) are difficult to assess, usability features (e.g., multitasking support) are not. Therefore, consumers could be expected to value usability features, which they can more easily verify. Based on this intuition, we argue that if verification difficulty is indeed responsible for consumers reluctance to pay for security, then we should observe a higher likelihood of paying for usability features compared to security features. To test our hypotheses, we use willingness to pay (WTP) as a proxy to compare consumers’ relative preference of security and usability features for a variety of mobile apps.

The potential difficulty of trusting claims about security features by a vendor is an adverse selection problem, which occurs when there is a lack of symmetric information prior to a deal between a buyer and a seller. A possible solution for mitigating this problem is an external certification attesting to the quality of goods or services, based on the understanding that buyers are likely to trust a third party in resolving their uncertainty about the quality of a product. In the context of mobile apps, ordinary users may not trust producers’ claims about security features in apps; however, they may trust the certification of a third party, analogous to web assurance seals (Kim et al. 2016) and privacy seals (Mai et al. 2010). To test the role of third-party certifications in the mobile app security context, we compare consumers’ WTP for security features with and without a third-party certification of the security features. We argue that if information asymmetry stemming from the difficulty of verification is indeed responsible for unwillingness to pay for security features, then we expect a higher WTP when security features are verified by a third party. A higher WTP for certified security features would also indicate that users do value security in mobile apps but may be unwilling to pay for security features without third-party verification.

## Research Questions and Contributions

In this study, we primarily examine two research questions: (1) Are mobile app users more willing to pay for usability features than security features? (2) Can the willingness to pay for security features be increased by third-party certification of the features? To answer these questions, we conducted a scenario-based experiment with mobile app users where we used WTP as a proxy to compare consumers’ relative worth of security versus usability features in a variety of mobile apps. In our experiment, we presented the respondents with scenarios in which they decide whether to purchase an app based on its features. As perceptions of information security are affected by an individual’s risk perception and propensity to engage in risky activities (Chen and Zahedi 2016), the WTP for security may be influenced by the individual’s attitudes towards risk. Thus, our third research question is: To what extent is the willingness to pay for security features influenced by individuals’ risk perceptions regarding security?

Various aspects of information security have been of interest to IS researchers in recent years, including secure software (e.g., August et al. 2019), adoption and continuance of secure behaviors (e.g., Boss et al. 2015), security training and compliance (e.g., Ormond et al. 2019), security performance (e.g., Kwon and Johnson 2014), and security investments (e.g., Angst et al. 2017). Our study contributes to this growing body of literature by conceptually differentiating usability and security features using the literature on product characteristics, and by empirically examining consumers’ preferences for the two types of features of mobile apps. Although some studies have posited that users may be unwilling to pay for applications’ security features, to the best of our knowledge, no one has empirically tested such claims. We not only test this assertion through comparison with the WTP for usability features, but also propose and test a theorybased solution to the problem. Our results indicate that consumers are less willing to pay for security than for usability; however, they do appear to value security, and their likelihood of paying for security can thus be generally increased by third-party certification of security features. Furthermore, the willingness to pay for security is significantly influenced by individuals’ risk perceptions.

## Theoretical Background and Research Hypotheses

Software security has been studied extensively from a technological perspective (e.g., Arp et al. 2014; Kosba et al. 2016; Tounsi and Rais 2018), but a growing body of literature has also examined the problems through an economic lens (e.g., August et al. 2019; Dey et al. 2015; Guo et al. 2016). Among these, several articles (e.g., Arora et al. 2008; August and Tunca 2006) address consumer valuation of security in an indirect or implicit manner, while others such as Telang and Wattal (2007), address it more directly. Telang and Wattal have shown, for example, that vulnerability announcements lead to a negative and significant change in a software vendor’s market value and conclude that “a more secure product can generate positive value for a firm” (pp. 554).

However, as indicated above, companies generally allocate a minuscule proportion of their budget to securing their mobile apps. Anderson and Moore (2006) have argued that vendors are capable of creating more secure software but have very little economic incentive to do so, even though security compromises can be costly for companies—both in terms of the cost to address the situation and in terms of lost customers. Producing secure software requires time-consuming testing and a focus on simplicity but consumers do not reward such effort. Rather, according to Anderson and Moore, buyers reward vendors for usability features and for being first to market. Our study tests the empirical validity of this claim— that consumers are more willing to pay for usability features than for security features.

A key focus of this article is on comparing consumers’ WTP for mobile apps based on their features—usability as well as security. WTP refers to the maximum price at or below which a consumer will definitely buy one unit of the product (Varian 1992). We use WTP as a proxy for comparing users’ preferences for paying for mobile apps based on different features. In particular, we compare consumers’ likelihood of paying for security features versus usability features. In the following subsections, we develop hypotheses regarding how features of mobile apps and the nature of verification of the features can be expected to influence the WTP for apps.

## Product Attributes

Our present study involves comparison of the WTP for a product, i.e., mobile apps, as a function of the features of the product. In information economics, the features of a product have been classified as search, experience, and credence attributes (Nelson 1970). Search attributes (e.g., color) can be verified prior to the purchase, whereas experience attributes can only be verified after the purchase or consumption of the product (e.g., taste). In contrast, credence attributes are difficult to verify even after use. A product may possess one or more of these three types of attributes. For instance, regarding mouthwash, a search attribute would be color, an experience attribute would be taste, and a credence attribute would be plaque reduction.

Similarly, mobile apps possess a number of attributes—some are concerned with the usability of an app while others relate to the security of the information transmitted or stored by the app. Usability features of an app are related to the functionalities of the app and refer to the ease of use of a given function. For example, presenting common amounts of tips (e.g., 10%, 15%, 20%) on the screen of an app used to pay a taxi fare is a convenient usability feature that enhances farepaying functionality. On the other hand, two-factor authentication of an email app could be considered a security feature that enhances the security at the expense of convenience (Tam et al. 2010).

The usability features of an app, such as whether it starts up quickly could be considered experience attributes because the features can be easily verified by users after purchase. Some security features, such as two-factor authentication, can also be verified by users, and could therefore be considered experience attributes. However, the vast majority of security features, such as whether the app securely transfers all the information, or whether passwords are encrypted using the 256-bit advanced encryption standard (AES) are difficult to evaluate, and therefore could be considered credence attributes. These attributes are not easy to verify even after purchase and immediate consumption, and therefore must be simply believed.

Generally, the more unambiguous the evidence of a product feature, the more likely consumers are to accept a claim (Hoch and Ha 1986). Using a product provides reasonably unambiguous evidence about experience claims but not about credence claims, which are difficult to adequately evaluate even after trial use (Wright and Lynch 1995). In other words, the level of uncertainty increases from search to experience to credence attributes (Murray and Schlacter 1990). It is more difficult for consumers to obtain prepurchase information to evaluate credence attributes than it is for consumers to evaluate experience and search attributes. Dulleck and Kerschbamer (2006) assert that concerns about being defrauded by expert sellers with credence claims may have consequences in the marketplace—consumers may postpone purchases or avoid such purchases altogether. Thus, the literature on goods and services with credence attributes suggests that consumers are less willing to pay for credence attributes than they are for search or experience attributes because they may be skeptical of credence claims (Mitra et al.

1999; Verbeke 2005). Hence, given the relative difficulty of evaluating the security features of an app, we believe that consumers will be less willing to pay for security features than for usability features. Based on this, we propose:

H1: Users of mobile applications are more willing to pay for usability features than for security features.

## Information Asymmetry

When buyers can observe product quality prior to purchase, it exerts pressure on sellers to provide high quality at reasonable prices. In contrast, when information asymmetry exists between transacting parties, a seller may be tempted to cut quality when it is not observable by buyers in advance (Akerlof 1970). One of the problems resulting from information asymmetry is adverse selection, which occurs when there is a lack of symmetric information prior to a deal between a buyer and a seller. Since the party with less information is at a disadvantage vis-à-vis the party with more information, the asymmetry causes a lack of efficiency in the price and quantity of goods and services (Varian 1992). Adverse selection situations may be resolved using third-party verification, signaling, and screening (Eisenhardt 1989). Because security features of mobile apps are credence attributes, buyers face an adverse selection problem arising from the difficulty of verifying the seller’s claims about a security feature. For instance, it is challenging for users to verify whether a home expenses management app indeed encrypts all data using authenticated AES 256-bit encryption. Thus, buyers may not be willing to pay for such features.

Trust in an online vendor is crucial to reducing friction in online purchases (Kim et al. 2009; Luo et al. 2010; Pavlou et al. 2007). Trust plays a role not only in reducing consumers’ perceived risk but also in increasing their perceived benefit, which together can lead to an increase in the willingness to purchase. A useful instrument for improving consumer trust in credence goods is third-party certification on the supply side (Roe and Sheldon 2007). The underlying assumption of third-party certification is that consumers have greater trust in independent certifiers than in private producers. For instance, third-party assurance seals enhance consumers’ trust and confidence in online retailers (Park et al. 2010). Similarly, privacy seals prominently displayed on websites enhance trust in the websites (Rifon et al. 2005). Most commercial websites enhance buyers trust by receiving accreditation from reputable institutions (Pavlou et al. 2007). We propose that if a third-party verifies security features and assured users that they exist, then users will be more confident about the quality of the product in terms of security.

H2: Users of mobile applications are more willing to pay for security features when the features are verified by a third party than when the features are not verified by a third party.

The hypothesis (H1) that consumers are less willing to pay for security features than usability features stems from the intuition that usability features are easy to verify, whereas advanced security features are not. However, we expect that the presence of third-party verification mitigates the nonverifiability issue (H2) and sufficiently increases the trust consumers have in security features, thus closing the gap between consumers WTP for usability features and their WTP for security features.

H3: Users of mobile applications are less or similarly willing to pay for usability features than for security features when the security features are verified by a third party.

## Risk Perceptions

Using software without adequate security is risky. However, there are no clear guidelines on what constitutes adequate security for apps. Consequently, consumers are likely uncertain about the level of security they should demand. Existing research suggests that individuals differ in the way they resolve decisions involving risk and uncertainty, and those differences are often described as differences in risk attitude (e.g., Pratt 1964). According to Slovic (2016), risk source characteristics and consumer characteristics combine to form a consumer’s beliefs about risk. The security behavior literature has focused on individual threat appraisals, and coping appraisals, which are two primary cognitive processes described in both protection motivation theory (PMT) (Rogers 1975, 1983), and technology threat avoidance theory (TTAT) (Liang and Xue 2009). Threat appraisal consists of the assessment of a threat— both in terms of the individual susceptibility to and the severity of the threat (Chen and Zahedi 2016). Perceived susceptibility refers to the likelihood of becoming exposed to a threat, whereas perceived severity is the impact of potential consequences posed by the threat. Susceptibility has been shown to influence risk perceptions. at least among people in Western cultures (see, for example, Weber and Hsee 1998).

Risk perceptions and attitudes are particularly salient in information security. Not surprisingly, many IS studies have explored risk perceptions in the domains of information security and privacy (e.g., Boss et al. 2015; Johnston and Warkentin 2010). In particular, researchers have used PMT to explain the adoption of anti-spyware software (Johnston and Warkentin 2010), secure behaviors of employees (Workman et al. 2008) and home users (Anderson and Agarwal 2010; Claar and Johnson 2012), and information security policy compliance (Herath and Rao 2009; Vance et al. 2012). In addition, Liang and Xue (2009) used TTAT to explain how threat severity and susceptibility contribute to the avoidance of spyware threats.

Similarly, privacy researchers have also used perceived risk to explain people’s willingness to share information about themselves online (Hong and Thong 2013; Xu et al. 2011).

To understand the effects of risk perception on making purchase decisions in cases of information asymmetry, researchers have used perceived risk theory in a variety of contexts, including electronic commerce, and information security (Featherman and Pavlou 2003; Jarvenpaa et al. 2000; Luo et al. 2010; Pavlou and Gefen 2004; Yang et al. 2016). For instance, Luo et al. (2010) show how risk perceptions influence the adoption of mobile banking services. Using a quasi-experiment, Yang et al. (2016) demonstrate the effect of risk perceptions on online shoppers’ attitudes toward purchasing, which in turn affects purchase intentions. Using an experimental survey, Jarvenpaa et al. (2000), show that reduced perceived risks increase consumers’ willingness to purchase from an internet store. These studies suggest that, when consumers’ perceived risk exceeds a certain threshold or tolerance level, then one or more risk-reduction strategies (risk relievers) will be employed to reduce the amount of perceived risk to a tolerable level.

Following this theory, consumers of mobile apps can be expected to value application security as a risk reduction strategy in ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the information they store or exchange. Furthermore, more risk-averse mobile app users can be expected to attach a higher value to mobile app security. Therefore, risk-averse individuals can be expected to be more willing to pay for security than risk-taking or risk-neutral individuals. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4: The willingness to pay for security features by users of mobile applications negatively correlates with individuals’ risk-taking attitudes.

## Research Design

We used a scenario-based experiment of mobile app users to compare their WTP for a variety of apps. In our scenarios, we referred to a hypothetical product because if we had used a product currently sold in the market, then respondents’ prior experience with that product could have confounded results. Multiple app categories (e.g., passwords manager, personal finance) were chosen to add robustness to our results. To ensure a reasonable completion rate and validity of responses, we chose categories that are relatively wellknown among app users and not generally available for free. To determine popular mobile app categories, we surveyed the trade press (e.g., pcmag.com, techradar.com) along with iOS and Android app markets. We considered only those apps for which usability and security were both important.

For instance, although sports scores or weather reports apps are popular, the significance of security in these apps may not be considered critical by users. If users are less willing to pay for security (than usability) in apps where security is critically important, then it is not likely that they would be willing to pay for security in apps where security is deemed unimportant.

After considerable exploration, we selected three categories: (1) passwords manager, (2) home expenses and income manager, and (3) medical records manager. We obtained a set of usability and security features for each category by studying the advertised features of several popular apps in each category currently available in the iOS and Android app markets. We considered these feature sets because users make decisions to purchase apps based on these features. Prior to using the features in the experiment, we conducted a pilot test with student subjects to confirm the classification of these features into usability and security. Table 1 lists the categories, the two features eventually chosen for each, and the pilot test results. Three quarters of the features were classified in the desired category by at least 85% of the subjects. One feature was classified correctly by 75% of subjects; although two features were classified correctly by only around 60% of subjects, this is not concerning because the two features were in opposite treatments (i.e., usability and security), and would therefore have a minimal impact on the results.

In the experiment, the participants responded to different options to purchase the app at a certain price based on the offered features. Each subject was presented with twelve offers, with each offer containing two versions of the product. Within an offer, the two versions were presented at the same price but they differed in terms of the two treatment dimensions, i.e., usability/security feature, and seller/thirdparty verification. The order of the treatment variables was randomized. As stated earlier, instead of asking the subjects to directly enter their WTP, we gauged their WTP indirectly through a series of offers at different price levels.

Figure 1 shows a sample survey question for the passwords manager app. In this example, Version A has an additional usability feature while Version B has an additional security feature. And both versions are seller verified. Note that choosing Version A indicates a clear preference for Version A over Version B at the offered price; similarly, choosing Version B suggests a clear preference for Version B over Version A at the offered price. Selection of the third (Either) or fourth (Neither) choice does not indicate a clear preference; however, these two options were necessary to cover all the possibilities (Dhar and Simonson 2003; Janssen and Hamm 2012). Each question comparing two versions of an app contained price offers chosen randomly in \$0.25 increments, with a range of \$0.24 to \$2.99.

## Measuring Risk Perception and Other Secondary Attributes

In the expected utility framework, differences in risk attitude are modeled using differing degrees of concavity (risk aversion) or convexity (risk seeking) of utility functions. However, the consideration of risk attitude as a stable personality trait has not received support when an individual’s risk attitude is tested in different contexts. For instance, MacCrimmon and Wehrung (1990) showed that managers have different risk attitudes when evaluating financial risks as opposed to recreational risks. Decision domains where respondents have been shown to similarly exhibit different perceptions of risks and benefits include gambling, financial investing, business decisions, and personal decisions (MacCrimmon and Wehrung 1986, 1990).

Based on these ideas about the significance of decision contexts in making decisions under risk, Weber et al. (2002) developed the domain-specific risk-taking (DOSPERT) scale that allows us to assess both conventional risk attitudes and perceived risk attitudes in a variety of domains such as financial, social, and health and safety. In surveys based on this scale, respondents rate their likelihood of engaging in domain-specific risky activities as well as their perceptions of the magnitude of the risks of the activities. The questions on our survey sought to measure respondents’ perceptions of riskiness in engaging in activities related to information security.

Several other pieces of information were collected in the survey to serve as control variables for ensuring the robustness of the results. First, we included questions to record respondents’ past experiences with security incidents and the timing of such incidents if they did occur because past experiences have been shown to influence risk attitudes. Individuals may be less willing to pay for security if they have not experienced a security breach or have not even heard about the security breach experiences of others. Second, in security situations, individuals often overestimate their ability to secure themselves (Rhee et al. 2012). The behavior stems from a psychological disposition called optimism bias, wherein individuals routinely overestimate their abilities and underestimate the risk they face, when compared to others (Weinstein 1980). Thus, individuals believe that breach incidents happen to others who are not competent enough to protect themselves. To control for this belief, we included four questions on the survey covering the relative likelihood of a breach perceived by the respondent compared to others. Third, the level of usage of mobile apps can also affect individuals’ WTP for security and other features. We included six questions on how often the respondent used various apps for sports, news, banking, etc. Finally, respondents’ demographic information was collected. The survey is provided in Appendix 1.

## Experiment Procedure

We conducted pilot tests with business undergraduate students to pretest the research instrument. The final experiment was run in Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), which has become a popular platform for conducting academic research, including IS (e.g., Lowry et al. 2016; O’Leary et al. 2014). Steelman et al. (2014) demonstrated that, with adequate controls, AMT samples are comparable in quality to those obtained with traditional methods. To control for method bias, we followed the before, during, and after data-collection procedures recommended by Jia et al. (2017): First, we used U.S.-based participants only to allow easier generalizability of our results to the U.S. population. Second, we chose high-quality subjects by using the filters available in AMT, to only select workers with a record of working on at least 5000 tasks with a task completion rate of at least 98%. Third, the survey included attention check questions to make sure that the subjects paid sufficient attention to the survey. If respondents failed any of the attention check questions, their responses were discarded.

Fourth, we explained the importance of the study to the subjects, and only permitted regular users of mobile apps to participate. Fifth, we gathered a large sample—200 for each of the three app categories (i.e., 600 responses in total). Further, the sets of respondents for the three categories were disjointed; in each survey—about 50% of the respondents were in the age range of 26-35 years; around 40% were female in the passwords manager and home expenses manager categories, and around 48% were female in the medical records manager category. Sixth, we removed responses that failed the quality control, e.g., we dropped the responses of twenty subjects who selected Either or Neither for all the questions. We believe that such respondents were most likely performing rote completion of the survey without paying attention to the price or features in each question. Thus, our final sample included 580 respondents across the three categories of apps. The average response time was 18 minutes, and each respondent was paid \$1.50.

In the experiment, the subjects responded to the WTP questions first, then the questions related to their past experience with security incidents, followed by the questions related to their self-confidence and mobile behavior, and then the questions on risk attitudes and risk perceptions. Finally, their demographic information was collected.

## Data Analyses and Results

In this section, first, we conducted a manipulation check of our premise that security features are more difficult to verify than usability features. Then we presented our results for the hypothesis concerning the comparison of WTP for security features and usability features (i.e., H1). Following that, we compared the WTP for security features based on the independent variables described earlier. We separated the analyses because variables such as third-party certification, risk perceptions and attitudes, self-confidence, etc., are expected to influence an individual’s WTP for security features only.

## Comparison of WTP for Security and Usability Features

The dependent variable is the customer’s likelihood of paying the offered price, a binary variable, coded as 0 if the offer is not accepted, and 1 if it is accepted. Thus, our analysis shows that third-party verification of a security feature increases the willingness of an individual to pay for it, to the extent that the willingness is no less than that for the usability features of the app. In other words, we demonstrate that third-party certification of security features can bridge the gap in WTP between usability and security— which is an interesting finding of this study.<sup>5</sup>

Third, the results in Table 5 (security features) show that RISKBEH is significantly $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ associated with the likelihood of paying for security. The negative coefficient of RISKBEH indicates that individuals who engage in risky behavior related to computer security have less willingness to pay for security features. The positive coefficient of RISKPERC $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ suggests that increasing (decreasing) risk perception leads to a higher (lower) likelihood of paying for security. Thus, H4 is supported.

Overall, our results support the central hypothesis that users are less willing to pay for security features than for usability features. We also demonstrate that consumer trust in security features can be increased by providing third-party verification of security features. In other words, the gap in willingness to pay for usability vis-à-vis security features can be reduced when security features are certified by independent third parties. Our results also suggest that consumers’ risk attitudes play a substantial role in their desire to pay for security—riskaverse individuals have a higher likelihood of paying for security than their risk-seeking counterparts.

Among the user level control variables included in Table 5 (Security Features), LSLFCONF is not significant, implying that individual self-confidence may not influence one’s WTP for security. MOBBEH is positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ which implies that heavy users of mobile apps are more willing to pay for security. The result suggests that users who use mobile apps frequently value security more than light users. Among the three variables related to experiences with security breach incidents, one’s own experience with a breach (OWNEXP) or that of an acquaintance (OTHEXP) does not appear to affect the WTP for security. However, hearing about a security incident (HEAREXP) appears to make users less willing to pay for security (HEAREXP is negative and significant at $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ . Although this result may seem counterintuitive, the behavior could be an illustration of a common psychological disposition referred to as optimism bias, wherein individuals routinely overestimate their abilities and underestimate the risk they face in comparison to others (Weinstein 1980). It appears that individuals may believe that breach incidents happen to others who are not competent enough to protect themselves. These findings punctuate the need for increased security training of mobile users in organizations. Finally, age, income level, and the gender of individuals appear to all significantly affect the WTP for security. Note that the estimates of these individual control variables in Table 5 are largely similar both in the all features context and the security features only context.

## Conclusion

Mobile apps are being used not just to check the news and weather but also to conduct online banking, store medical records, and manage passwords. Although it is essential that these apps have adequate security, even paid apps on the two major platforms—Android and iOS—have been shown to lack effective levels of security. Even though securing apps is considered critically important, firms have not invested sufficiently in doing so (Ponemon Institute, 2015).

Motivated by the need to better understand app producers apparent disregard for security in their products, we designed a study to investigate the economics of mobile app security from the point of view of consumers, especially the potential antecedents of individuals’ WTP for mobile app security. In this context, the contributions of this study are fourfold: (1) we theoretically and empirically demonstrate that users of mobile apps are more likely to pay for usability than security, (2) we offer a theory-based way to increase the likelihood of paying for security features, (3) we analyze the role of risk perceptions of individuals in their likelihood of paying for better security of mobile apps, and (4) we explore the role of past experiences with security breaches on the likelihood of paying for security features. Our primary contribution is empirical: we show the existence of a gap between the willingness to pay for security and usability features, and demonstrate that the gap can be shrunk by third-party certification of security features. Some security researchers (e.g., Anderson and Moore 2006) have conjectured that the reason producers are more inclined to invest in usability features than in security features is that this strategy is more profitable, i.e., users are more willing to pay for superior usability features. The findings of our study support such arguments. However, we believe that securing mobile apps adequately is nevertheless critical and thus demonstrated that independent third-party verification of security features can be a way to increase the likelihood of consumers paying for them.

The theoretical contributions of this study include mapping mobile application features to properties of goods. We used the literature on product attributes (Nelson 1970) to theorize that software security is a credence attribute, which is therefore difficult for consumers to verify. We validated this proposition using surveys of mobile app users.

Our study has broad implications for IS researchers as well as security practitioners. IS research on user security behaviors (Boss et al. 2015; Menard et al. 2017) suggests that although individuals profess to care about information security, their behavior, for example in the context of information security policy compliance, indicates otherwise (see Cram et al. 2019 for a recent review). IS researchers have attributed the lack of policy compliance to the cognitive biases of individuals (Anderson and Agarwal 2010). Furthermore, research suggests that employees attach a considerably higher cost to adopting burdensome security than the perceived benefit from following security policies (Yao et al. 2018). In the same vein, we show that consumers attach greater value to usability features of applications than to security features. As third-party certification of security features raises the WTP for consumers, we recommend that producers of mobile apps seek third-party certifications for the security features of their apps.<sup>6</sup>

Our study has some limitations. First, the empirical study was conducted with three app categories—passwords manager, home expense manager, and medical records manager. Although the three categories as well as the set of features for each were selected after due diligence, the app market has millions of apps and many features other than the ones we considered. Second, as explained earlier, we conducted pilot tests to confirm the classification of the features as usability and security, but we do recognize that the line between the two can sometimes be blurry. Notwithstanding these limitations, our experiment demonstrates that users do value security and are more willing to pay for them when features are verified by an independent third party.

## Feature Classification Pilot Survey

Mobile apps possess a number of features—some of which are concerned with the usability of an app while others with the security of the information transmitted or stored by the app.

Usability features of an app are focused on increasing the ease of use of the functionality expected from the app. For example, an app to pay taxi fare may offer the common amounts of tips (e.g., 10%, 15%, 20%) on the screen. This usability feature enhances the convenience of the fare-paying functionality.

Security features of an app are focused on enhancing the security of information transmitted, stored, or modified by the app. For example, the taxi fare app mentioned above may secure all credit card transactions. This feature does not contribute towards the convenience of using the app, rather it minimizes the risks of credit card numbers being stolen during transmission. Therefore, it is a security feature and not a usability feature.

## Feature Verifiability Survey

Products advertise many features, some of which may be easily verified by the consumers while other features may be difficult to verify by consumers.

For example, a mouthwash may advertise that it has a minty taste, and kills plaque bacteria. The minty taste is easy to verify by simply tasting the mouthwash. But whether the mouthwash really kills plaque bacteria is difficult to verify by the consumers themselves.

Similarly, mobile app sellers typically advertise many features of their apps. The presence of some of those features can be easily verified by the users of the apps, while the presence of others is difficult to verify by the users.

Log Odds Explanation

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\ln \frac {\operatorname * {P r} (W T P = 1)}{1 - \operatorname * {P r} (W T P = 1) _ {\{i j \} k l}}} \\ & {\qquad = a _ {0} + a _ {1} F E A T U R E _ {i k l} + a _ {2} T H P A R T Y _ {j k l} + a _ {3} P R O F F E R _ {k l} + a _ {4} O R D P R E F _ {k l}} \\ & {\qquad + a _ {5} R I S K B E H _ {l} + a _ {6} R I S K P E R C _ {l} + a _ {7} H o m e E x p D u m \quad + a _ {8} M e d R e c D u m \quad + C o n t r o l s _ {l}} \end{array}\tag{A2-1}
$$

In a logistic regression, the coefficient of a binary independent variable is the log of the likelihood of the dependent variable taking the value 0 or 1. Further, the log of the likelihoods of the dependent variable taking the value 0 or 1, given either of the two values (0 or 1) for the binary independent variable, is the negative of the other value of this binary variable. That is, from Equation (A2-1), and using the exponential expression of probability, it is possible to arrive at:

$$
\ln \frac {\operatorname * {P r} (W T P = 1 | F E A T U R E = 1)}{\operatorname * {P r} (W T P = 1 | F E A T U R E = 0)} _ {\{i j \} k l} = - a _ {1}
$$

In our analysis described in the Comparison of WTP for Security and Usability Features section in the main paper, we coded the usability feature as the reference level for the variable FEATURE, so that the log of likelihood of a usability feature is $a _ { 1 }$ and that of a security feature $\mathrm { i s } - a _ { 1 }$ . Similarly, because the seller is coded as the reference level, the log odds of seller-verification is $a _ { 2 }$ and of third-party verification $\mathrm { i s } - a _ { 2 }$

The log odds of each of seller-verified usability feature or a third-party verified usability feature is the sum of the log odds of each binary variable, meaning that the sum of log odds of a seller-verified usability feature and a third-party verified usability feature is:

$$
a _ {1} - a _ {2} + a _ {1} + a _ {2} = 2 a _ {1}
$$

Then, the difference (D) between the log odds of a third-party verified security feature $( - a _ { 1 } - a _ { 2 } )$ versus a usability feature (computed above) is:

$$
D = - a _ {1} - a _ {2} - 2 a _ {1} = - 3 a _ {1} - a _ {2}.
$$

The standard error of ?? is then based on the standard errors of $a _ { 1 }$ and $a _ { 2 } ^ { \phantom { + } }$ If $D$ is positive and significant, we have evidence that the likelihood of a third-party security feature is greater than the likelihood of seller-verified usability feature or a third-party verified usability feature.
