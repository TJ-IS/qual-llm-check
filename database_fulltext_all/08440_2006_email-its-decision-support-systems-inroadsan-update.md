# Email: Its decision support systems inroads—An update

Edward J. Lusk <sup>T</sup>

Faculty of Management and Economics, The Otto-von-Guericke University, Magdeburg, Germany

Available online 3 March 2005

## Abstract

Email is compared to four communication alternatives: telephone, face-to-face, facsimile, and letter. The survey and analysis are follow-ups to the work of Plaisent and Bernard (1993) [6] [M. Plaisent, P.M. Bernard, Electronic mail: Efficient and effective or a fad?, European Business Review 93 (1993) 27-30]. The results are that email is ranked higher on all five of the attributes analyzed: efficiency, convenience, preference, most-used, and importance. In addition, it appears that email is increasingly taking on the role of DSS, being used in problem-solving, and continues to constitute about half of the activit regarding the exchange of information. <sup>D</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Email; Decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

The challenges of decision-making in complex situations have given rise to two important developments: Media Richness Theory (MRT), introduced and popularized by Daft and Lengel [2,3]; and Decision Support Systems (DSS), comprehensively expressed in Turban and Aronson [7]. Their symbiosis is evident. DSS are ways to organize the necessary information richness required to enhance decision effectiveness and quality when faced with the myriad decision challenges now an ordinary part of the world-wide communication grid. In 1999, Carlson and Zmud [1] presented the results of a

MRT experiment, following on their ideas of Channel Capacity, which essentially demonstrated that experience in a communication channel is one of the principal determinants of channel effectiveness. The basic implication of the above is that MRT requires a careful organization of the various communication modalities, email now included; in the collection, archiving, and retrieval of data; as well as in-channel experience, to realize the benefits of MRT in DSS.

One of the first, really baseline, studies of email as an essentiality of computer-mediated communication systems with DSS implications was conducted by Plaisent and Bernard in 1993 [6]. Their study was based upon a sample of 95 utility executives. They report, in Table 1, percentage relationships relative to email utilization, categorized by its use in achieving the following three fundamental organizational functions: Control, Planning and Coordination; by the activity of Problem Solving; and finally by means of execution—i.e., Sending and Receiving Information.

This is a seminal study because it collected information just at the advent of the wide distribution of email as a possibility in the Intra-, Extra-, and Internet worlds. Recall that only in 1985 did email have its global coming-out, from its simple beginnings, in 1971, as part of ARPANET.

## 2. The study design

To investigate the way that email has progressed as a DSS, now more than 10 years after the Plaisent and Bernard study, a questionnaire was designed to: (1) collect information on the evaluation of the following modalities: email, telephone, face-to-face (F2F), facsimile (fax) and letters on the following five criteria: efficiency, convenience, preference, mostused and importance; and (2) address the temporal comparisons of email utilization for the following two variables reported by Plaisent and Bernard: Problem Solving and Sending and Receiving Information. The Problem Solving information was collected directly by including a survey question on email use in solving problems. Sending and Receiving Information was addressed by collecting information on eight component activities, which, taken together, are assumed to constitute most of the activity pertaining to sending and receiving information. These activities are: (1) solving Problems, (2) working on Projects, (3) interacting with Colleagues, (4) negotiating Contracts, (5) Informing Others of new products or services, (6) in job Interviews, (7) in scheduling Meetings, and (8) in place of Memos. Finally, one may observe from Table 1 that the three functions of Control, Planning and Coordination taken together account for about 60% of the email activity.

To provide a context for the functional and temporal email comparisons, the email systems in use at the time of the sampling are assumed to have had the following standard features: attachment possibilities for documents, pictures, and PowerPoint presentations; various font selections, color marking, inclusion of emoticons; highlighting through Bolding, Italics, or Underlining; forwarding and copying (route-listing). These attachment and styling possibilities are important enhancements relative to the email systems circa the Plaisent and Bernard study.

## 2.1. The sampled groups

The first group sampled was the membership of the Library Administration and Management Association (LAMA), a division of the American Library Association. For this group, n=296, of essentially senior managers in their respective library systems, more than 90% held master’s or PhD degrees and their range and median utilization of email was [3 to 20 years, median 10 years]. See Pagell et al. [5] for more demographic information of the LAMA sample. The second sample, n=36, was of individuals in the MBA program of the Goizueta Business School at Emory University, Atlanta, Georgia, USA. This group was on average younger than the LAMA respondents and in contrast came from rather diverse professional backgrounds. As expected, most held a bachelor’s degree (97%) and had been using email as follows: [5 to 21 years, median 8 years]. The third sample group, n=28, was of students in the International Management (IM) program of the Otto-von-Guericke (OVG) University, Magdeburg, Germany. Three-quarters of the students were from Europe, the rest being from China. They also predominantly held undergraduate degrees (89%) and had been using email as follows: (1 to 15 years, median 5 years). The final sample (Thai), n=32, was taken from individuals who attended a conference dealing, in part, with email and entitled: Knowledge Management for Library and Information Professionals, held in Chiang Mai, Thailand, March 2003. Here, most of the respondents held a professional post in their respective library systems; e.g., Librarian, Director or Information Specialist. Most held advanced degrees (70%) and had been using email as follows: [1 to 18 years, median 7 years].

## 2.2. The hypotheses

The survey investigates two aspects of the way that email’s evolution has impacted the organizational DSS milieu. One is a functional utilization comparison, examining how the sample respondents rate their email systems relative to the other four modalities, considering the five evaluation criteria noted above. The respondents were asked to score their communication preferences using a five-point scale, where 5 was <sup>b</sup>Most Preferred<sup>Q</sup> and 1 was <sup>b</sup>Least Preferred<sup>Q</sup>. For example, for efficiency, if the phone were the most preferred modality relative to efficiency, then the respondent would score 5 for phone. The second aspect is a comparative temporal evaluation using as the baseline the Plaisent and Bernard study. Consider the following four factors: (1) the vastly enriched email systems that existed at the time of the sampling compared to the relatively lean email systems in use in the early 1990s, (2) the increased decision complexity in the dynamic world of the Internet, (3) the increased familiarity and experience that most individuals probably have in the email channel compared to the individuals surveyed by Plaisent and Bernard, and (4) email’s relative simplicity in execution compared to the other modalities. Based upon these four factors, the following hypotheses are proffered:

## Modality Utilization Hypothesis

H1. Email will be more valued than the other four communication modalities over the five evaluation criteria.

Comparative Utilization Hypotheses: Email will be more utilized currently for:

## H2. Problem Solving

and

## H3. Send and Receive Information

than for the levels of utilization reported by Plaisent and Bernard.

The statistical analysis of the information in Table 3 was conducted using a two-way ANOVA, so that interaction could be examined. In this case, as is evident from the orderings of the four sampled groups, there was no statistically significant interaction at p<sup>b</sup>0.2. This suggests that the four sample groups rated the relationships among the five modalities, relative to the five criteria, in basically the same way. Also, using the Tukey–Kramer HSD multiple comparison test for the modality main effect at a=0.05, email was rated highest over all the criteria compared to the other four modalities; and, at the other end of the scale, fax was rated fourth, followed by the letter. Telephone and F2F clustered together and were rated between email and fax. Email’s place in this ordering is consistent with H1. Perhaps this result for email relative to the other four modalities, in particular the (telephone, F2F) grouping, may be explained by its relative immediacy in that the telephone or F2F modalities are, in many situations, difficult to organize compared to email. However, once connected in the telephone and F2F channels, these modalities usually offer superior spontaneity. Also, the above ordering may relate to an amelioration of the semantic context for emails produced by the enhancements noted above (see [4], p. 267).

To test the Problem Solving and the Send and Receive Information components of the temporal comparative hypotheses, the respondents were asked to report the frequency of their email use in executing the above-mentioned eight activities. For completeness, the following category was added: <sup>b</sup>To send notes with Non-work content<sup>Q</sup>. For each question there were the following frequency choices: <sup>b</sup>Always<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>Often<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>Sometimes<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>Seldom<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>Never<sup>Q</sup>. Because the pattern of responses by group was not statistically different, the results are aggregated over the four respondent groups and presented in Table 4 by the five frequency choices noted above for the nine activities.

Considering Problem Solving, 27.8% of the LAMA, MBA and Thai respondents, the comparison group for the Plaisent and Bernard study, scored Always and Often for Problem Solving uses of email. This tests statistically significantly different, at p<sup>b</sup>0.001, from the 9.5% reported for the utility executives surveyed by Plaisent and Bernard in Table 1 and thus supports H2.

Assuming that the Send and Receive Information category may be characterized by the first eight activities in Table 4, one finds that 50.5% of the LAMA, MBA and Thai respondents scored Always and Often for their use of email over these eight activities. This percentage does not test statistically different, at p<sup>b</sup>0.25, from the 50% reported by Plaisent and Bernard for Send and Receive Information in Table 1 and so does not support H3.

Finally, Table 4 offers the following two interesting results. One observes that about 75% of the time email is the modality of choice for non-work communication. This rationalizes the wisdom of adopting or perhaps enforcing an Internet utilization policy for employees. Also, email is rarely used for conducting interviews. Perhaps here, firms prefer the traditional F2F channel. However, there are simple ways that email could be used to collect pre-F2F interview information.

## 4. Summary

For the modality utilization hypothesis, H1, the results are clear. Email outperforms telephone, faceto-face, facsimile, and letter considering: efficiency, convenience, preference, most-used and importance.

This suggests that email is a valued facilitating aspect of the DSS milieu.

However for the comparative utilization hypotheses, H2 and H3, the results are mixed. Assuming that the LAMA, MBA and Thai respondents are a reasonable comparison group for the utility executives surveyed by Plaisent and Bernard, email has increased in importance as a facilitating feature as it relates to problem-solving thus supporting H2. However for H3, over the range of activities tested, the use of email seems to have remained constant and so fails to support the expectation that email would have increased relative to the 1993 study of Plaisent and Bernard. One may thus assume that email continues to account for around 60% of the Control, Planning and Coordination activity as it did in the 1993 study.

Finally, the results suggest some remaining possibilities regarding enhancement of email’s impact on the firm’s DSS in particular for conducting job interviews. Thus, re-considering the question posed by the Plaisent and Bernard study, Electronic mail: Efficient and effective or a fad?, it certainly seems that the former remain the case, in that unlike a fad which quickly fades into obscurity, email seems an integral, valued and evolving part of DSS.
