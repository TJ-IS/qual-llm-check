# Enabling collaboration and innovation in Denver’s smart city through a living lab: a social capital perspective

Valerie L. Bartelt <sup>a</sup>, Andrew Urbaczewski <sup>b</sup>, Andrew G. Mueller <sup>a</sup> and Suprateek Sarker <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business Information and Analytics, University of Denver, Denver, CO, USA; <sup>b</sup>Real Estate and Construction Management, University of Denver, Denver, CO, USA; <sup>c</sup>McIntire School of Commerce, Information Technology, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Recent information technology (IT) developments have enabled cities to collect and disseminate large amounts of data to improve their public services delivery. “Smart cities” allow the public sector to leverage IT infrastructure and smart devices to increase the value of services delivered to the community. Our exploratory qualitative study involved the examination of secondary data and interviews with public administration personnel at the City and County of Denver. We used the social capital perspective and literature on knowledge transfer to interpret the empirical material and derive abstractions. We found that the Living Lab methodology adopted within the Smart City initiative facilitated a number of outcomes, including improved collaboration and innovation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the “fail-fast” mentality was a characteristic of the Denver Living Lab that improved innovation.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 21 March 2018 Accepted 24 April 2020

KEYWORDS Smart cities; living lab; public sector; social capital; knowledge transfer; innovation

## 1. Introduction

This research explores how the living lab methodology used in the public sector within the smart cities context leads to better collaboration and innovation. While a bulk of past research on the value proposition of information systems (IS) has focused on the private sector, more recently, the public sector has garnered increased attention in this area of research (Anthopoulos & Reddick, 2016; Azad & Faraj, 2008; Bannister & Connolly, 2012; Chun et al., 2010; Cordella & Iannacci, 2010; Gil-Garcia et al., 2014; Hackney et al., 2008; Kankanhalli et al., 2017; Mergel et al., 2018; Soe & Drechsler, 2018; Wirtz et al., 2018; Yeh, 2017). Interestingly, IS frameworks from the private sector have often been applied to the public sector (Cordella & Iannacci, 2010; Layne & Lee, 2001), despite acknowledged di<sup>f</sup>erences in motivations and values between the two sectors (King et al., 1994; Liou & Nyhan, 1994; Raus et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2018). For example, while the private sector typically takes a pro<sup>fi</sup>t-driven approach, the public sector often considers multiple stakeholders with con<sup>fl</sup>icting agendas and policies which create challenges to collaborative decision-making and implementation (Campbell et al., 2009; Peizer, 2003). IS design and implementation in the public sector often involve di<sup>f</sup>erent environments, incentives, and time horizons (Bozeman & Bretschneider, 1986). The degree to which entities are public (government, public-good oriented, publicgoods producer, etc.) shapes their interaction with IS through varying di<sup>f</sup>erences in bureaucracy, mission, relations to end-users, and political cycles, among other factors (Bozeman & Bretschneider, 1994).

Smart cities have been a popular topic for research over the past couple of decades (e.g., Chourabi et al., 2012; Inkinen et al., 2019; Zanella et al., 2014), though a recent bibliometric analysis indicates that “smart-city research is fragmented and lacks cohesion” (Mora et al., 2017, p. 3). Smart-city projects involve technologyenabled initiatives that are often shaped by citizenusers (Voss & Carolan, 2012) to improve municipal operations, policies, and services, thereby increasing public value (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009; McGann et al., 2018). Examples include online sales of masstransit tickets, real-time transit departure and arrival times, estimated highway travel times, and variable noti<sup>fi</sup>cation signs on highways. What transforms a technology-using city into a smart city is turning individual systems into a coherent system of systems. Smart cities use existing data from current systems and then integrate them with new technologies, thereby creating a value-delivery system for improved safety, health, e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and natural environment. Additionally, prior research based on an Emergency Response System Smart City in Rio de Janeiro found both adaptive and collaborative approaches to be helpful when combining systems (Dodgson & Gann, 2011).

Similar to prior living labs research that was conducted in Botnia and Halmstad (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009), most are environments where ideas are germinated in a collaborative setting (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009; Følstad, 2008; Scha<sup>f</sup>ers et al., 2007). Living labs are often initiated by the government (McGann et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018), as seen in many locations, including North America (Tõnurist et al., 2017), Latin America (Acevedo & Dassen, 2016), and Europe (McGann et al., 2018). They serve as a testing ground, enabling teams to adjust during the design and development process before deployment. Our research explores why and how the living lab can be a transformative methodology within the smart city context. Existing research on living labs centres mostly on aspects of initial design and innovation (Bergvall-Kåreborn & Ståhlbröst, 2009; Tõnurist et al., 2017). A variety of innovations taking place within living labs have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed around the world (Puttick et al., 2014; Tõnurist et al., 2017). The theoretical basis leading to the success of living lab methodology within smart cities has not been adequately addressed in prior research. We seek to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap through qualitative inquiry into living labs. Our working research question asks whether the use of living-lab methodology improves outcomes, such as collaboration and innovation capabilities in a smart city context. We explore a multi-layer smart city context, with an embedded living lab.

## 1.1. Characteristics and challenges of IT innovation in the public sector

Public sector values are often based on virtuous characteristics, such as honesty and equity, in contrast to the goals of the economically driven private sector (Andrews, 2012; Bannister, 2001; Grimsley & Meehan, 2007; Klievink et al., 2016; Larson, 1997). Additionally, public sector organisations often rely on bureaucratic governance and are more process-driven (Harmon & Mayer, 1986; Hood, 1991; Willem & Buelens, 2007), which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence organisational design (Posner & Schmidt, 1996) and the nature of collaboration across stakeholder groups when compared to the private sector (Ansell & Gash, 2008; Hennala et al., 2011).

Research involving the value of technology in the private sector is generally less applicable to the public sector (Banker et al., 1993; Pang et al., 2014), due to di<sup>f</sup>ering motivations in the sectors (Borins, 2001; Hood, 1991; Osterloh & Frey, 2000; Vidal, 2006). Given that the public sector comprises a large portion of the overall economy (Moore, 1995), greater attention to this sector is needed.

Innovation that improves service quality and problem solving in the public sector has generated recent interest (Damanpour & Schneider, 2009; King et al., 1994; De Vries et al., 2016; Walker, 2014), with public innovations in technology, concepts, and products (Bekkers et al., 2011; Edquist et al., 2001; Moore & Hartley, 2010). Broad public innovation goals include bene<sup>fi</sup>tting citizens, increasing e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness/e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, solving society’s problems and increasing customer satisfaction and involvement (De Vries et al., 2016). Positive outcomes of public-sector innovation include improved practices based on diverse citizen input and increased trust (Mergel & Desouza, 2013; Reddel & Woolcock, 2004).

Investment in smart-city technologies enables the public sector to deliver value to the community (Grimsley & Meehan, 2007). Technology innovations can support public service delivery, public engagement, co-production, resource-building, and publicsector innovation (Pang et al., 2014). Managing issues resulting from a rapidly increasing population across a landscape of diverse stakeholders, high interdependence, and competing values can be challenging (Dawes et al., 2009; Dirks et al., 2010; Marceau, 2008; Rittel & Webber, 1973; Toppeta, 2010; Weber & Khademian, 2008). Thus, the living lab approach may be a useful way to roll out IT and related innovations in smart city contexts.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Motivation for studying Denver smart cities

While other examples of U.S. smart cities have been discussed in the literature – e.g., Austin, Texas, and Columbus, Ohio (Vock, 2017) – we strategically chose Denver, Colorado. Denver is the largest city in the Rocky Mountain region of the U.S. and one of the fastestgrowing areas in America. Rapid growth is straining its current systems, especially transportation networks (Vock, 2016). Cities facing similar challenges as Denver are also seeking smarter solutions to increase sustainability.

Denver Smart Cities (DSC) is representative of other smart cities, as it attempts to maximise $2 1 ^ { \mathsf { s t } } -$ century technologies while dealing with limited resource and space challenges within a crowded city setting. Denver hoped to leverage DSC to resolve multiple technological issues (Silverman, 2017a). Their collaborative initiative involved a “multidepartment e<sup>f</sup>ort with Transportation & Mobility, the Mayor’s O<sup>fi</sup>ce, Tech Services, and Environmental Health” (Silverman, 2017a, p. 1). DSC funding initiatives involved collaboration with community partners and government agencies, receiving over 20 USD million to date, with future plans for projects on safety, congestion, health, equity, and the environment. Moreover, the authors had unique access to DSC personnel and projects due to a longstanding relationship with the university that allowed them insights not possible in other cities.

## 2.2. Living lab background and Denver living labs

Living lab concepts gained popularity in the $2 1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ century as a methodology to guide the testing of emerging technologies in real environments (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009; Niitamo et al., 2006; Scha<sup>f</sup>ers et al., 2007). Prior research has explored living lab designs in different contexts (Cosgrave et al., 2013), including in university settings – such as at the University of Manchester in the United Kingdom (Evans et al., 2015), and in cities – such as in the European Union, where living-lab activities are “in the core of city policies . . . ” (Santonen et al., 2017, p. 32). Følstad (2008) de<sup>fi</sup>nes living labs as “environments for innovation and development where users are exposed to new information and communication technology (ICT) solutions in (semi-) realistic contexts, as part of medium- or long-term studies targeting evaluation of new ICT solutions and discovery of innovation opportunities” (p. 116).

The living lab is considered a human-centric approach to experimentation and learning, as “ICT innovations are co-created, tested, and evaluated in open, collaborative, multi-contextual real-world settings” (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009, p. 2). Pallot et al. (2010) highlight the user-group experience as the foundational link between the individual user experience and social elements for developing an understanding of living lab projects. Users and stakeholders from academic, corporation, and public sectors are often involved, as modelled in the Botnia Living Labs (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2009). Gascó (2017) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some insights into living labs in relation to innovation, including:

1) living labs provide opportunity for public agencies to meet with private sector organizations and thus function as innovation intermediaries, 2) implementing an open innovation perspective is considered more important than obtaining speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation results, and 3) scalability and sustainability are the main problems living labs encounter as open innovation intermediaries. (p. 90)

U.S. examples of living-labs adaptations include the Changing Place Lab/House at MIT, where an environment was created to study patterns of how people use new technologies (Niitamo et al., 2006). Cities outside of the U.S. have used the living-lab concept to explore the integration of newer technologies into city living. For instance, a suburb in Helsinki created a futuristic city environment called the Helsinki Virtual Village by synthesising city planning with new technologies (Cosgrave et al., 2013). And Bremen, Germany, worked with a market research company to test new mobile application products and advertising under real conditions (Niitamo et al., 2006).

Similarly, the Denver Living Lab (DLL) functions as a live testing ground for smart city-speci<sup>fi</sup>c projects with teams comprised of members from multiple city departments and external vendors. Each DLL project is centred around implementation and testing of a new innovation designed to improve city operation or service delivery. From the outset, DLL projects involve collaboration across departments and with external vendors, focusing on problem solving with a “failfast” and iterative work<sup>fl</sup>ow. With a “failing fast” and “failing often” mentality, innovative processes are encouraged with an understanding that many will not succeed. Not a lot of time is spent once teams realise the innovation will not work. This iterative work<sup>fl</sup>ow, often used to improve quality, is also a successful component of knowledge transfer theory (Osterloh & Frey, 2000).

## 3. Theoretical foundations

## 3.1. Social capital theory

To interpret our data, we use social capital theory as our primary lens (Walsham, 1995) to observe and interpret the living-labs methodology in action, speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally DLL in the context of the smart-city initiative. Our theoretical lens highlights the role of social capital in obtaining resources (Lin et al., 2017; Wasko & Faraj, 2005) to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the public good, (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Resources from social capital include “network ties of goodwill, mutual support, shared language, shared norms, social trust, and a sense of mutual obligation that people can derive value from” (Ellison et al., 2006; Huysman & Wulf, 2004, p. 1). Researchers have used social capital theory to study human capital, geographic locations, and assets obtained due to relationships and community connections (Coleman, 1988; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

Social capital is a central theme in much of public administration research (e.g., Kellog & Mathur, 2003) and its role in improving public services to citizens has been acknowledged (Andrews, 2012). In addition, social capital has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the democratic government setting for improving innovation (Ma, 2017) and has been linked to more e<sup>f</sup>ective knowledge sharing and teambuilding (Cabrera & Cabrera, 2005), especially within the IT area among U.S. federal agencies (Choi, 2016). Furthermore, social capital has been attributed to community goods, such as improving public health, reducing crime (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Ellison et al., 2006; Leana & Van Buren, 1999), increasing cooperation through collective action (Requena, 2003; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), and improving access to resources, including “important information, employment opportunities, personal relationships, or the capacity to organize groups” (Ellison et al., 2006, p. 7). Since social capital naturally aligns with community wellbeing, it suits public sector situations well.

We note that by selecting the social capital lens, we seek to highlight how social capital elements might contribute to desirable outcomes for the organisation. To this end, the paper speci<sup>fi</sup>cally explores how social capital dimensions (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1997; Wasko & Faraj, 2005) and related knowledge transfer (Osterloh & Frey, 2000) contribute to e<sup>f</sup>ective collaboration and innovation within the DLL and in the broader Smart City context, where employees are able to draw on knowledge and social relationships cultivated in the living labs.

## 3.2. Knowledge transfer theory

We also utilise knowledge transfer theory to determine the types of motivation and knowledge generation taking place at DLL. Osterloh and Frey's (2000) 2 × 2 design explores the synergy between motivation and knowledge generation and transfer types. Knowledge transfer is considered tacit if it is learned unconsciously, typically through intuition, experiences, and in other unarticulated ways that cannot be shared with others (Osterloh & Frey, 2000). By contrast, knowledge transfer is explicit if it is based on spoken language or illustrations, and can generally be translated into routines shared across di<sup>f</sup>erent teams and contexts (Osterloh & Frey, 2000). This paper focuses on the explicit, replicable knowledge transfer that takes place in DLL. Within Osterloh and Frey's (2000) knowledge-transfer model, we believe DLL functions as a “knowledge producing team,” where iterations are used to improve quality (p. 546).

Furthermore, intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation yield di<sup>f</sup>erent outcomes in knowledge generation (Osterloh & Frey, 2000). Extrinsic motivations are based on whether projects indirectly satisfy one’s needs, such as through increased income (Calder & Staw, 1975; Osterloh & Frey, 2000). Projects are intrinsically motivated as a result of one’s immediate satisfaction of a personal goal, such as successfully completing a living lab project that improves the inner city’s air quality (Calder & Staw, 1975; Loewenstein, 1999; Osterloh & Frey, 2000). Intrinsic motivation involves the satisfaction of achieving personal goals, as opposed to a pro<sup>fi</sup>t-driven extrinsic motivation (Osterloh & Frey, 2000). We believe DLL is intrinsically motivated due to it being a part of the public sector that is focused on the satisfaction of bene<sup>fi</sup>tting the community.

## 4. Research methodology

This study employs a qualitative approach to investigate the natural setting of DSC and DLL. Data collection involved multiple sources that were triangulated (Walsham, 1995) with no manipulation by researchers within the setting (Benbasat et al., 1987).

There are currently four DLL locations serving as functional testing grounds for new smart city technology. They all fall under the auspices of the living labs project coordinators, but the di<sup>f</sup>erent locations allow technology to be tested in multiple environments before implementation. The test sites are located in complex urban environments near DSC o<sup>fi</sup>ces downtown and east of the city, each encompassing several city blocks. Technology use cases are live tested in these small areas before large-scale implementation. This smaller, self-contained testing ground helps manage public safety associated with the pilot projects, such as new tra<sup>fi</sup>c controllers, and ensure satisfactory interoperability with legacy systems.

Since this research is in the exploratory stage, our results are based on the integration of secondary data and DLL stakeholder interviews using a theoretical lens. Additionally, we follow the Principle of Abstraction and Generalisation (Klein & Myers, 1999), wherein we interpret the data through the theoretical lens and o<sup>f</sup>er abstractions. Unlike survey research, this single-case study seeks to o<sup>f</sup>er a more in-depth and revelatory account of DSC and DLL. The approach allows us to answer our research question as to why the living lab methodology improves outcomes, such as collaboration and innovation.

## 4.1. Data sources

We conducted interviews with the DSC Program Manager and team leaders over the course of four months, beginning in Fall of 2017. Data collection involved triangulating sources from 1) articles and reports, 2) archival records, and 3) the aforementioned interviews. Our planning stage involved collecting speci<sup>fi</sup>c data based on our research question and developing a succession of questions that drilled down on speci<sup>fi</sup>c DLL collaboration and innovation topics of interest.

Initial Program Manager Interview. We <sup>fi</sup>rst interviewed the Program Manager by conference call for 60 minutes. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the successes of DSC, especially due to the DLL initiative.

Exploratory Team Leader Interviews. Our process involved a 90-minute exploratory interview with DSC team leaders, including DSC Program Manager, the Internet of Things (IoT) Lead Architect, the External Vendor for Systems Integration and Management Services, the Public Works Transportability and Mobility Manager On-Street Operations Manager, and the Advanced Transportation and Congestion Management Technologies Deployment Project Manager. The interview format was comprised of open-ended questions about DSC success areas. DLL was discussed in detail, including innovation projects as part of the public service initiatives, security issues, and funding sources. The interview session elicited information about DLL’s process, including the existence of several internal and external teams working together on projects, as well as the fail-fast mentality. Researchers took turns asking questions and were also responsible for notetaking during the meeting.

Secondary Data. Based on the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed living-lab themes, we collected secondary data comprised of articles, grant proposals, and detailed internal documents. The researchers also attended DSC-related community events, where additional secondary data were obtained on the inner workings of DLL.

Semi-Structured Team Leader Questions Delivered Asynchronously. We then re-contacted the Program Manager and various team leaders to clarify organisation, technology, and project and innovation themes based on the DLL initiative and its new “fail-fast” culture.

Theme-Based Team Leader Interviews. Another two-hour structured interview then took place with leaders of DSC teams. Attendees included the Program Manager of DSC, the IoT Lead Architect, two co-sponsors of DLL, and a vendor for DLL. The interview comprised of more detailed questions about DLL process as well as questions on the theoretical themes that emerged through the analysis of preliminary data collection. We inquired about the di<sup>f</sup>erences between public and private sector innovations; internal relationships and DLL knowledge-sharing; DLL successes and failures; and replication of the DLL model in other cities. Two researchers served as notetakers and one researcher asked most of the questions.

Following Yin’s (1984) within-case analysis process, detailed notes and thoughts about the interview were completed and discussed among the three of the researchers within 24 hours. The research team met and shared their impressions with one another.

## 4.2. Data analysis

We note that qualitative research can be of many genres, with drastically di<sup>f</sup>erent assumptions and methodological practices (Sarker et al., 2018). Our work may be broadly characterised as exploratory and interpretive, and thus, many of our methodological practices were consistent with the nature of the qualitative research we were pursuing.

For our case research analysis approach, we completed many iterations to ensure that the patterns emergent from the interviews were valid with respect to the entire corpus of data (Walsham, 2006). The process included searching for patterns in the data sources by identifying similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences among team leaders’ perspectives. If similar themes emerged, secondary data was used to derive greater insights from the context.

After one researcher began compiling interview responses into themes based on consensual responses, a second researcher con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the interview responses from the transcriptions. A third researcher then sought con<sup>fi</sup>rmation on these themes based on a consensus of multiple secondary data sources. The second author then con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the secondary data themes. The <sup>fi</sup>rst three researchers collaboratively developed the preliminary theoretically informed interpretation of the data. The fourth researcher critically examined the interpretations and iteratively enhanced the coherence of the understanding through discussions with the <sup>fi</sup>rst three authors. As mentioned earlier, the analysis was strengthened by using a broad theoretical perspective as our interpretive lens and for the subsequent formulation of <sup>fi</sup>ndings. ## 5. Results and interpretation

The below <sup>fi</sup>ndings follow a tri-dimensional organisation, including: 1) social capital – structural, relational, and cognitive; 2) themes – organisation, technology, and innovation; and 3) source of data – primary and secondary.

## 5.1. Context before the Denver living lab

Historically, DSC initiatives experienced collaboration challenges similar to those in other large cities. Communication between di<sup>f</sup>erent departments was often di<sup>fi</sup>cult, and departments did not typically go out of their way to help one another. The City Director relayed to the researchers that in-house expertise was often underutilised, as a consequence of either duplication of e<sup>f</sup>orts or purchasing systems that were incompatible across departments. An important goal of DLL was to increase unit cohesion within DSC.

## 5.2. Social capital theory and knowledge transfer within living labs

This paper employs a social capital lens to explore how the collaborative attitude fostered within the living lab contributes to knowledge transfer and innovation in this environment as well as in larger smart city initiatives. The literature indicates that interconnected community spirit within organisations, especially in the public sector, is a prime setting for enhanced social capital (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Prior research connects both competition (Mergel & Desouza, 2013) and cooperation (Ma, 2017) to government innovations.

## 5.3. Structural capital in Denver living labs

The structural capital dimension of social capital theory explores the network of the social system, typically examining how people or teams are linked. The strength of a network’s structural connection facilitates the exchange of knowledge as disparate teams join together (Granovetter, 1992; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). This applies to the DLL culture that brings internal and external entities together to share ideas.

## 5.4. Breaking down silos

Organisations often form various silos for technological, temporal, and political purposes (Bannister, 2001).

We found that DLL helped break down silos, functioning as a live testing ground across teams within, and external to, the city government. Outside vendors were considered strategic partners (rather than just external entities) due to the close working relationships that developed in DLL during the project pilot. This shift from siloed, transactional relationships to a collaborative, strategic partnership enabled outside vendors and city administrators to jointly focus on problem-solving to produce better solutions. This shift resulted in a joint sense of ownership and a partnered commitment to improving the project’s outcome.

Organisation Themes from Interviews: Our interviews revealed that DLL enabled a top-down restructuring that began with support and <sup>fi</sup>nancial provisions from the Mayor, who declared, “Do smart cities, so do it right.” Top-level support led to a reallocation of government resources that enabled strategic hiring for the DLL initiative, including the creation of a program manager position. Barriers across silos were broken as teams came together under DLL.

At the time of our research, over <sup>fi</sup>fteen agencies work together at DLL. Concerning the strong linkages between disparate stakeholders, the Program Manager stated that the DLL “paradigm shift was new and di<sup>f</sup>erent for the partner vendors,” and dynamics had changed “from a transactional relationship to a partnership.” According to the Program Manager, DLL was in the process of “co-designing the solution for two-way communications” and “using each other’s partnerships to share risk, reward, improve adoption, and create a win-win situation.

Organisation Themes from Secondary Data: Figure 1 displays the organisational structure of DSC program. The DSC organisational approach shows their commitment to breaking down silos while having accountability, in addition to supporting a multi-stakeholder approach to addressing problems in the city.

Project and Innovation Themes from Interviews: DLL enables a variety of expertise to come together. This process supports procedures involving coproduction and resource building (Pang et al., 2014). Due to the centrality facilitated by the initiative, barriers among projects were broken down as teams collaborated. For instance, as analog systems were updated, network security was addressed collectively, contributing to the project’s overall e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Also, public vehicles used data to make citizens aware of emergency situations in real-time to improve public safety.

The DSC initiative’s interconnectedness made a noticeable impact on city services. According to the Program Manager, “Instead of taking old stu<sup>f</sup> and applying it to the public sector, [DSC] now bene<sup>fi</sup>ts life and resident safety and it happens at a pace that brings bene<sup>fi</sup>ts ASAP.”

Project and Innovation Themes from Secondary Data: DLL has developed a collaborative identity. Project names are based on this blended DLL identity, as evidenced by the joint presentation by DSC, Denver Clean and Healthy City, and Denver Mobility teams (City and County of Denver [CCD], 2017a, 2017b). Each project is associated with DLL rather than separate departments. One example of combined e<sup>f</sup>orts across silos is Vision Zero – a project with the goal of eliminating all transportation fatalities by leveraging data collected from pedestrian, road, emergency vehicle, and public transportation data systems. This project has enabled the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of <sup>fi</sup>ve test sites where fatalities are the greatest (which is approximately 4% of the total high-injury network) (Silverman, 2017b). DLL addressed this problem by implementing a multi-modal approach that included connected signals at intersections for prioritising buses through routes, pedestrian and bicycle signal activation, emergency vehicle pre-emption, and accessible multi-modal transportation options to lowincome residents.

We infer that structural capital will increase the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of networks among various stakeholders that interact within DLL environment, resulting in reduced silos in DSC. The switch from viewing external partnerships as transactional to a more collaborative and integral part of DSC team is also attributed to breaking down silos and strengthening networks. Therefore, collaborative relationships within the neutral living lab setting will lead to decreased siloed units and strengthened structural connections in DSC.

## 5.5. Relational capital in Denver living labs

The relational component of social capital theory highlights the importance of strong interpersonal interactions, which result in relationships and consequently behaviours characterised by respect, friendship, and trust (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Putnam, 1993; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). These relationships were fostered within the neutral DLL environment.

Organisation Themes from Interviews: Our interviews suggest that DLL has strived to develop a culture of reciprocity and commitment across teams. Many successes are due to the resolution of unproductive con<sup>fl</sup>icts in inter-team projects. This top-down DSC initiative was not only re<sup>fl</sup>ected in structural reorganisation but also in interpersonal interactions with citizens at public meetings (see Abstraction 1a). According to a co-sponsor of DLL, support from the mayor empowered “agencies to want to work together,” further improving relationships, reciprocity, and commitment within the DLL environment. As the IoT Lead Architect explained, DLL creates “candid, open conversations, scheduled meetings with things done together involving mature adult conversations.”

Organisation Secondary Data Themes: We noted several changes to the collaborative nature of city projects after the DLL was created, including the following instruction: “When pursuing ideas, always be sure to have a diverse team and embrace a di<sup>f</sup>erence of opinion – develop a ‘yes and’ culture, embracing additive solutions to complex problems.” (Silverman, 2017a, p. 3)

Figure 2 demonstrates an overview of city challenges that DSC is addressing. The DLL approach assesses available existing technologies <sup>fi</sup>rst and then incorporates vendors who can provide enhanced technologies and services within the city. This process takes place iteratively, based on the priorities of various city initiatives.

Technology Themes from Interviews: DSC found many ways to overcome challenges in implementation – including collaboration on new projects, collective funding applications, and de<sup>fi</sup>ning clear roles and responsibilities.

## Project and Innovation Themes from Interviews:

Using the Living Labs approach gives vendors a di<sup>f</sup>erent language and timeframe to <sup>fi</sup>nd a new way to work together – such as a three-month live test. Due to increased relational capital, we infer that social interactions among those in living labs will increase and the type of social relationships fostered will generally be positive and supportive. Such relational capital carries over to the smart city initiatives as the projects move to DSC from the pilot project stage at DLL.

## 5.6. Cognitive capital in Denver living labs

The cognitive dimension of social capital is related to creating shared meaning and interpretation among teams. Cognitive capital can improve resources such as frameworks for sensemaking as well as knowledge for problem-solving in teams (Arrow, 1974; Cicourel, 1973; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). A major goal of the DLL was to provide a space to cultivate ideas.

Organisation Themes from Interviews: Due to the living-lab approach, communication barriers collapse across teams, including among <sup>fi</sup>eld personnel and executive decision-makers. One example of collaborative communication is PhotoVoices, which are projects that allow citizens to share photos that demonstrate a theme or answer a question from their community. PhotoVoices projects enable executives to talk directly with citizens and more naturally start a conversation about a particular community topic or policy. Referring to a student PhotoVoices project, the Director of the Denver Department of Public Health and Environment reported:

It was a pleasure interacting with students and talking to them. Those interactions always remind me of the importance of engaging some of our youngest citizens in the work that we do and the decisions that we make to further improve our great city, and it reinforces with them the importance of their involvement in driving outcomes within their communities.

Communications that engage the community can be reinvigorating and allow for a deeper understanding of the issues that concern citizens, highlighting the importance of a shared cognitive perspective.

Organisation Themes from Secondary Data: A knowledge-sharing mentality of DLL emerged through the process: “Working on multi-department projects has allowed us to use the best minds of our departments, together we have been able to solve problems from more angles than if just one department took over the project.” (Silverman, 2017a, p. 3)

Figure 3 provides one example of the detailed process that was jointly developed at DLL (Connected Mobility), with each project incorporated into larger DSC objectives. Pilot projects were initiated within a year. Mid-term output processes include testing the technology, data infrastructure, and analysis, which was completed within the <sup>fi</sup>rst two years. Long-term outcomes were assessed in two to <sup>fi</sup>ve years, with the possibility of increased funding and deployment.

We infer that cognitive capital was gained through the development of shared meaning among team members within DLL. DLL was able to connect experts from a variety of departments, using their combined knowledge to solve problems and strategize optimal solutions. Increased interaction among these experts fostered by the living lab led to increased cognitive capital collectively, and thus knowledge that was once contained within a particular department is now available across disparate teams. Consequently, as the cognitive capital increases in DLL, it carries over to smart-city initiatives as the projects move to DSC from the pilot project stage at DLL.

## 5.7. Cognitive capital and the fail-fast mentality

With an increase in shared resources, strategies, and intellectual capital through the cognitive dimension of social capital, the amount of knowledge obtained from teams will also increase (Arrow, 1974; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

Organisation Themes from Interviews: The DSC Program Manager stated the following about being able to quickly change structural ine<sup>fi</sup>ciencies:

From a governance perspective, DLL noted early on there were too many committees – including a technical working group, an implementation team, a leadership team and an executive steering committee. Within two months, DLL realized the implementation team had highly functioning, articulate project managers who could directly communicate with the executive steering committee, so they combined the implementation team with the leadership team.

Project/Innovation Themes from Interviews: Since projects involve shared knowledge within DLL, more risk and responsibility were taken on by vendors. As the IoT Lead Architect summarised, this fail-fast mentality involves “a mindset upfront of failure versus waiting until someone does the <sup>fi</sup>rst iteration [within the agile methodology].” The DSC Program Manager expressed one example:

DLL were working on use cases with a vendor for a few months. Then they came to DLL to test what they had developed. When they put the devices on the street, they didn’t work because it was too sunny. Denver is a very sunny city and we need devices that can work in all seasons. From that experience, DLL made a rapid reduction in the time from product ideation to product <sup>fi</sup>eld testing to not repeat these losses of implementation time.

Project/Innovation Themes from Secondary Data: The philosophy of DLL is to fail quickly, adjust their approach and investments, and then repeat. The general process includes breaking down small interventions that are linked to mid-term outcomes, which eventually will lead to long-term objectives of mobility, safety, health and wellness, climate, and quality of life.

The cognitive dimension of social capital that enables collaborations from disparate teams leads to more in-depth knowledge of team projects. This results in more e<sup>fi</sup>cient project development strategies, such as the concept of failing fast – where a project and/or methodology is aborted as soon as it appears to not be succeeding. The project can be abandoned more quickly in the living lab environment because teams are composed of experts who are able to jointly and rapidly agree on whether the outcome will be a success or failure. Thus, due to the increased knowledge gained within teams in the living lab, and shared understanding through the cognitive dimension of social capital, the “fail-fast” approach is leveraged during the development process.

Abstraction 1d: Cognitive capital developed in a living lab environment tends to result in a “failfast” mentality.

## 5.8. Motivations for knowledge exchange in the Denver living lab

Social exchange theory suggests the structural capital dimension facilitates the exchange of knowledge through the structural capital dimension (Blau, 1964; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Knowledge exchange typically begins with peoples’ intrinsic motivation to contribute to each other (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). People need to feel their contributions are valuable and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to both themselves and their organisation (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). The public sector may lend itself to people who are intrinsically motivated to help others. Thus, knowledge exchanged within DLL is based on the internal satisfaction of helping others in the community (Bozeman & Bretschneider, 1986). This synergy of exchange generated in DLL also bene<sup>fi</sup>ts DSC.

## 5.9. Motivations within the public sector

Osterloh and Frey (2000) explore tacit and explicit motivations that lead to knowledge generation and sharing. The public sector DLL within the smart city context <sup>fi</sup>ts neatly into their “Knowledge Producing Teams” model – consisting of explicit, or reproducible, knowledge with intrinsic, or satisfaction-oriented, motivations; for instance, having the goal of continually improving the quality of one’s organisation through imparting knowledge onto others through task forces (Osterloh & Frey, 2000).

Organisation Themes from Interviews: There was a consensus from all the team leaders that they enjoyed being a part of the public sector. This was articulated by a co-sponsor of DLL who noted that the public sector was more civic services-oriented, “which made most of them feel like they were being good stewards with taxpayers’ money.” According to another DLL co-sponsor, the experience with DLL has been “personally satisfying.

Moreover, the knowledge exchange allowed the city and its departments to think about projects more holistically. For example, the project manager stated:

Now we can look at some of the unintended consequences of pedestrian safety. These are things we hadn’t even thought about previously, but we have the ability and desire to integrate these data sources and answer these questions, perhaps before the public even asks them.

We need to incorporate our electric charging station infrastructure. DLL shows the value for bringing data into the EDM [Electronic Data Management System], layered for use for on-street charging. ## 5.10. Knowledge transfer and innovation

Innovation is an important initiative within the public sector (De Vries et al., 2016), which can be e<sup>f</sup>ectively cultivated within living labs. High social capital logically leads to the more e<sup>f</sup>ective transfer of knowledge (Osterloh & Frey, 2000), especially when there is high intrinsic motivation. In “knowledge producing teams,” the focus is on using an iterative process to improve quality (Osterloh & Frey, 2000, p. 546) of the innovation being rolled out to the smart city.

Project/Innovation Themes from Interviews: According to the IoT Lead Architect:

We have the mindset upfront that we are going to fail – we expect it. We understand it is an iterative process. We are willing to take on some of the risks with the vendors, and we are going to select a product that has been vetted . . . [Therefore,] the Living Lab eases the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between private industry and the public sector.

The knowledge transfer is applicable to other cities in the region, as they do not want to see the innovations and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to stop at the city limits. The DLL Project Manager noted:

There has been a groundswell of support in the region. DRCOG [Denver Regional Council of Governments] has been amazed by what we are learning and installing and wants to become a part of our enterprise data management sharing. It forces all the cities and vendors to think more regionally. For example, air quality is not magically bounded by a line on a map.

Because more knowledge is gained through disparate DLL teams, problems can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed sooner and more iterations result. We infer that increased iterations of the “fail-fast” mentality lead to increased comfort with risk, thereby improving the quality of innovations.

## 6. Discussion

This qualitative study o<sup>f</sup>ers a theoretical understanding of the success of smart cities by exploring one example, Denver, and the role of its living lab in contributing to greater collaboration and innovation. We found that public sector intrinsic motivations facilitated social capital, which led to outcomes such as improved collaboration, knowledge sharing, and innovation among disparate teams that had struggled historically to work together. This contributed to characteristics like the fail-fast mentality, which ultimately improved innovation.

In line with Walsham (1997, p. 478), who states that “Theory is our chronically inadequate attempt to come to terms with the in<sup>fi</sup>nite complexity of the real world. Our quest should be . . . for theory that is relevant to the issues of our . . . [focal phenomenon],” we draw upon the social capital theory (supported by the knowledge transfer theory) to make sense of the City and County of Denver’s experience with Living Labs. Through our early interactions with the organisational actors, and our engagement with the public management literature, we came to realise that: a) the phenomenon of interest was complex and multifaceted, and we needed to focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c facets that were potentially most relevant; and b) in public organisation contexts, it was not necessarily the logic of economic e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts that drive innovation and collaboration but relationships (i.e., social capital) that help actors navigate bureaucratic and territorial barriers. This led to our adoption of the social capital theory. Adopting the theory, automatically gave us the sensitivity to the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of social capital and how they might contribute to some of the outcomes of interest, and this allowed us to consciously and critically look for patterns in the data. Additionally, we found that little social-capital theory-informed research has been conducted within loose structures such as in the living lab environment, with most studies being conducted at the organisational level (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Villena et al., 2011). Thus, the living lab o<sup>f</sup>ers insights into the contribution of social capital within organisations that are not <sup>fi</sup>xed entities.

We found that all three dimensions of social capital serve as a basis for successful knowledge transfer within DLL and beyond. The structural capital dimension was found to break down the traditional siloed structure of public service delivery. This was achieved by allowing cross-collaboration among departments. DSC experi enced top-down support, as the Mayor was supportive of the initiative. The relational capital dimension encouraged disparate teams to work together. This dimension may have been successful due to DLL enabling the formation of trust relationships that helped deal with logistic tensions among teams – such as determining how government funding would be allocated within collaborative initiatives. Cognitive capital was bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in enabling siloed knowledge to <sup>fl</sup>ow freely within DLL. Due to the sandbox nature of DLL, departments were encouraged to try new improvements in public service delivery without the stigma associated with failure.

![](/api/attachments/2M7TJDCH/fulltext/images/a362288a9c9d48f683e3172f2539272f76f6377159efa0c8c31cfae565c3fd13.jpg)  
Entrepreneurial city process. (Silverman, 2017b, p. 5).

In the living lab, projects can be iteratively tested in a safe environment. The innovations are only introduced to the public after they have been fully vetted, thereby increasing trust in public services. This leads to good stewardship of funds, as projects are paid in full once they have been shown to deliver new services to the public. Such arrangements can only work smoothly with social capital at work. It goes without saying that alternate perspectives such as institutional theory and resource dependence theory could have been used to interpret the case data, which would lead to our ability to focus on routines and power respectively, leading to a di<sup>f</sup>erent set of abstractions and implications. However, as mentioned before, we felt that the key lesson we wanted to highlight in this work was the importance of social capital in engendering more e<sup>f</sup>ective innovations and collaboration, which our chosen perspectives allowed us to do.

## 6.1. Managerial implications

Our research suggests that smart-city managers should proactively cultivate social capital. This study demonstrates the ability of living labs to break down silos, improving collaboration and innovation, while also creating new challenges such as potential reallocation of funding across departments. In this particular case, the construction of a cross-departmental team demonstrates the usefulness of the living-lab approach as a tool to increase collaboration and experimentation in a relatively safe environment, with successes spilling over to the culture of the larger organisation. Less formal living-lab constructs may also provide similar bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that lead to increased innovation through interorganisational collaboration, learning, and experimentation on IT related projects in historically bureaucratic, public organisational structures.

Since full-time employees were allocated to DLL, they were able to jointly apply for external grants to fund new projects. As active participants in national conferences, the DLL managers can give back to the public sector by o<sup>f</sup>ering lessons learned and best practices, further increasing the public value of grants awarded to DLL projects.

Each smart city has its own unique approach for running e<sup>fi</sup>ciently, and innovations in smart cities will vary locally based on needs within each municipality. Therefore, information and communication technologies should be thoughtfully considered during the collective urban-planning process (Goodspeed, 2015). DLL o<sup>f</sup>ers one model that can be strategically adapted to other areas. Feedback during front-end development (Hennala et al., 2011) could o<sup>f</sup>er insights for adapting smart-city models to di<sup>f</sup>erent locations.

Denver has used DLL as a way to leverage social capital within its own workforce, and managers consider it a strategic and competitive advantage. In a follow-up conversation, the project manager noted:

We now are managing towards eighteen tenets of a successful city [as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by a DSC consultant]. One of those is to Grow Your Own In-House Talent, as it helps us to think of Denver as an integrated system rather than isolated departments. [Note: The tenets mentioned are from six best practices of a successful city, as DSC stated were from their federal government partner 18F – see 18F.gsa.gov].

The living-lab approach to experimentation, innovation, and collaboration is a highly adaptable tool that public organisations can shape towards their speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals. Insights garnered from experimentation may lead not only to new and useful methods for enhanced public service delivery, but may also identify avenues for cultivating and growing existing organisational assets.

## 6.2. Future research

More research is needed to determine the reasons behind smart city successes. As our research found, studies grounded in theory help clarify best practices that may lead to more e<sup>f</sup>ective living-lab approaches. One particularly interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding was that the shift in culture at the living-lab level revealed opportunities for a cultural change across the organisation. Further study of the practices that enable shifts in culture across di<sup>f</sup>erent types of organisations with varying degrees of public missions and operating environments is warranted. As well, improved understanding of how varying management structures of living labs bring about di<sup>f</sup>ering external results beyond technological innovation, such as cultural shifts and shifts in organisation’s missions, will also be important to future development of the living-lab methodology and best practices.

Future research could explore other factors, such as the community’s trust of public stewardship within smart cities. Also, since this research focused on the public sector with intrinsic motivations, further research is needed to determine the di<sup>f</sup>erences of social capital in the private sector with extrinsic motivations. Prior research has found that both public and private collaborations increase innovations (Bakici et al., 2013), but determining the di<sup>f</sup>ering degrees of the social capital dimensions within each context still needs further exploration.

## 6.3. Limitations

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on one living-lab location; thus, more research is needed to re<sup>fi</sup>ne, challenge, or further contextualise <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Social capital and knowledge transfer theories, like all theories, magnify certain factors and processes while rendering others invisible. Other theoretical perspectives could highlight di<sup>f</sup>erent factors and mechanisms of valuecreation within smart cities. We are also aware that social capital works di<sup>f</sup>erently across cultures, and thus our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may have limited transferability to other nations.

Moreover, this study observed one mediumsized city in the western United States. While we believe Denver is representative of many in the U.S., we do note that the abstractions o<sup>f</sup>ered based on observed are tendencies (Walsham, 1995), not deterministic relationships. While the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which social capital is shown to work may be di<sup>f</sup>erent in other contexts, the analytical generalisation is that development of social capital is aided by DLL, which then helps enhance collaboration and innovation in the broader DSC. While the theoretical sca<sup>f</sup>olding contributes to some level of analytical generalisability, we urge readers to be cautious in assuming wider transferability and recommend a) additional research and b) also paying heed to research conducted on living labs closest to their own contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

This study o<sup>f</sup>ers a theory-based understanding of the success of living labs in engendering collaboration and innovation within a smart city context. We provide insights regarding outcomes of the living labs using social capital and knowledge transfer theories that would not be otherwise obvious. This includes, but is not limited to, the observation that the fail-fast mentality, which was cultivated through increased cognitive capital within the Denver Living Lab and facilitated innovation through knowledge transfer. The Denver Living Lab also proved a useful tool for initiating a long-term shift towards a collaborative culture and for identifying synergistic opportunities within the smart city program and across departments within the city.
