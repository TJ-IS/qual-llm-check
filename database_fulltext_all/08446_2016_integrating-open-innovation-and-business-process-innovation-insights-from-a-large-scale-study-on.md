# Integrating Open Innovation and Business Process Innovation – Insights from a Large-scale Study from a Transition Economy

## Abstract:

Open innovation and Business Process Innovation (BPI) have been investigated by their respective research communities for decades. However, the important relationship between externally focused open innovation and internally implemented BPI is currently unexplored, especially in transition economies. The main purpose of our research is to contribute towards closing this important research gap. This paper describes the main findings of a research study of innovation practices of 224 companies operating in a transition economy.

We propose and validate a comprehensive model of integrative-innovation and offers some important insights into the relationship between externally-focused R&D collaboration and a firm’s internal process innovation.

Key words: Open innovation, Business Process Innovation, Survey, Transition Economy

## 1. Introduction

Today’s companies are faced with unprecedented environmental complexity created by rapidly changing technologies, dynamic information environments and new types of business models. To remain competitive, many companies are working on two fronts - within and outside of their organizational boundaries. Externally, companies actively pursue opportunities to collaborate with customers, suppliers, universities, research centers, consultants, other firms, and sometimes even with their direct competitors. With their closed (internal) innovation strategy reaching its limits (Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011), they are finding new ways to engage in open innovation (Chesbrough, 2003). Thus, by using external resources and capabilities, these companies significantly enhance their innovation capacity (Chesbrough, 2003). Competitive advantage is gained by accessing both new and complementary knowledge as well as unique resources that are not available internally. Those that are successful in open innovation tend to view partnerships as opportunities to acquire new technologies but also to absorb partners' skills and capabilities in order to use these relationships to build skills and diffuse new knowledge (Hamel et al., 1989).

In addition to external efforts, companies are also working internally, aiming to reinvent and transform their business processes (BPs) as a primary means of business value creation and delivery (Harmon, 2007). In many cases, these BP transformation projects are triggered by globalization and regulatory pressures (Mykityshyn and Rouse, 2007). Business process innovation (BPI) is often based on a strategy of active price competitiveness typical for mature markets with more intense competition (Antonucci and Pianta, 2002). Consequently, BPI has evolved into a core focus area for all successful organizations (Kirchmer, 2008). Kirchmer (2008) further suggests that any type of innovation involves BPI, resulting in processes with new structures, more accurate data, new organizational responsibilities, new activities or better process deliverables. It is important to note that BPI research tends to focus on process innovation within organizational boundaries as confirmed by a comprehensive review of BP research by Sidrova and Isik (2010).

The industry trends towards externally-focused open innovation and internally-focused BPI, as described above, have been investigated by their respective academic communities for many years, however important research has often been conducted within disciplinary silos. Focusing on R&D collaboration, we argue that in order to actively and effectively engage in open innovation, companies also need to re-consider their internal business processes as the main means of business value creation

#

and delivery to customers and other external stakeholders. At the same time, BPI is also required to enable companies to effectively absorb new knowledge and skills acquired through open innovation, leading to production and delivery of new/improved products and services. However, this important relationship between externally-focused open innovation and internally implemented BPI is currently unexplored by both open-innovation and BPI research communities. For example, research on managerial implications (i.e. internal consequences) of open/collaborative (external) innovations is still lacking (Giannopolou et al., 2010). Furthermore, Teirlinic and Spithoven (2013) argue that companies need absorptive capacity and the managerial skills of the internal R&D personnel to engage in external research collaborations and R&D outsourcing. We posit that both open innovation and BPI should be integrated into an «innovation loop» that spans the firm's internal/external boundary, thereby calling for multi-disciplinary research.

The main objective of our research is to contribute towards a better understanding of the relationship between a firm's open-innovation and BPI efforts, using a multi-disciplinary approach that spans the observed research silos of open-innovation and BPI research. Therefore, we focus on the following broad research question: What are the predictors of the relationship between R&D collaboration with external partners engaged in open innovation and the level of a firm’s (internal) BP innovations?

In practical terms we aim to contribute towards removing the boundary between external and internal aspects of firm innovation, thus enabling a more effective «innovation loop» to be established between external collaboration and internal means of value creation through business processes.

Using insights from this multidisciplinary literature review we propose a comprehensive conceptual model (framework) designed to combine the elements of a firm's open-innovation with BPI (i.e. a firm's «external/internal innovation loop»). The proposed model is then verified through a large-scale survey of 240 large and small companies from Bosnia and Herzegovina (B&H). Our choice of business context and our focus on a small country operating in a very challenging (post-war) transition economy makes our study even more significant for several important reasons. The current research efforts in both BPI and open-innovation (let alone their combination) have been predominantly focused on highly developed companies in Western economies, rather than small transition economies. Yet, „emerging markets and their unique conditions offer interesting opportunities for researchers and companies alike in the area of service innovation and digital technologies“ (Barrett et al., 2015; p.150). This is because these economies are fundamentally different from their Western counterparts in their institutional and economic structure (Barrett et al, 2015). National context is also important because open innovation is still „deeply rooted with the culture of a particular region“ (Hossain, 2015). Furthermore, while Western companies are driven by the need to sustain competitive advantage under familiar market conditions, companies in former socialist countries are forced to enter free market conditions, unfamiliar to their management and employees (Stata, 1989). Therefore, for these companies, the ability to innovate (both internally and externally), with very limited resources and in very different market conditions (Stata, 1989) becomes a matter of survival.

Our research confirms the importance of an integrative approach to combining a firm’s external (open) innovation with its internal business process innovation efforts. Apart from proposing and validating a model of integrative innovation our research also offers some important and interesting insights into the relationship between external R&D collaboration and a firms’ internal process innovation. For example, while the existing research confirms that companies operating in certain industries (e.g. hightech or technology-intensive) are more focused on business process innovation (Cabagnols and Le Bas, 2002; Dittrich and Duysters, 2007, Buganza and Verganti, 2009; Miotti and Sachwald, 2003; West and Gallagher , 2006; Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011), we found that in our sample of companies, industry type was not significant for a company’s ability to “absorb” the effects of open R&D innovation into their internal BPs. Also, the number and the variety of a company’s external partners were not recognized as significant determinants of the company’s ability to innovate its internal business processes. In other words, whether a company searches for new knowledge/technology widely and deeply or not was not found to be important for its capacity to use externally-acquired knowledge/technology within their internal BPI. While the relevant literature confirms the influence of Search Strategy (Exploit v. Explore) on innovation performance (Laursen

#

and Salter, 2003; Katila and Ahuja, 2002), we found that the nature of a firm's search processes in open-innovation does not impact on its BPI. Finally, our research identifies customers and suppliers as the prime sources of knowledge and technology for a firm's BP innovation efforts, prompting companies to focus on building valuable relationships.

## 2. Open innovation and BP Innovation: Background

Since proposed by Chesbrough (2003), the concept of open innovation has been extensively discussed by academic and managerial literature in different disciplines such as economics, management, information technology, psychology and sociology. „The basic premise of open innovation is opening up the innovation process“ (Huizingh, 2011; p.2). Inbound innovation refers to internal exploitation of external knowledge while outbound innovation describes external exploitation of internal knowledge (Huizingh, 2011; Schroll and Mild, 2011; Bogers and West, 2010; Lichtenthaler, 2009; Chiaroni et al., 2009).

In the open innovation literature, inbound and outbound innovation processes are also known as: outside-in and inside-out processes (Gassmann and Enkel, 2004, 2006; Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011; Enkel et al., 2009; Van de Vrande et al., 2010), technology exploitation and exploration (Huizingh, 2010; Giannopoulou et al., 2010; Van de Vrande et al., 2009; Herzog, 2008), or incoming and outgoing spillovers (Belderbos et al., 2004). Furthermore, open innovation is considered complementary to collaboration approaches (DeBresson and Amesse 1991; Faems et al. 2005; Hagedoorn and Duysters 2002; Küppers and Pyka 2002). Consequently, researchers tend to use both terms - open innovation and collaborative innovation - interchangeably.

So far, collaboration efforts to improve the ability to innovate tend to focus on firm-firm partnerships and associated mutual benefits such as: creating new products/services, new processes, etc. When considered in combination, the existing literature on inter-organizational collaboration and open innovation focuses on innovation performance in general, often without distinguishing between product and process innovations, see for example (Antikainen et al., 2010; Buganza and Verganti, 2009; Bughin et al., 2008; Chiaroni et al., 2009; DeWit et al., 2007; Dittrich and Duysters, 2007; Duarte and Sarkar, 2011; Ebersberger et al., 2012; Elmquist et al., 2009; Enkel et al., 2005; Gassmann and Enkel, 2004; Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011; Kutvonen, 2011; Laursen and Salter, 2006; Lazzarotti et al., 2011; Lichtenthaler, 2009; Van De Vrande et al., 2009; West and Gallagher, 2006). Compared to product innovation, which tends to be more prominent, there is a lack of literature related to the possible effects of open innovation on business process innovations, particularly in the collaborative context.

The second related area relevant to our research is Business Process Innovation (BPI). In general, BPI projects range from very radical process innovations disrupting the existing business models to incremental process innovations that are more common and include minor changes mainly based on existing technology, procedures and processes (Lazzarotti et al., 2011; Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011; Lichtenthaler, 2008; Laursen and Salter, 2006; Marsili and Salter, 2005). Incremental process innovations are often seen as process improvements. In addition, BP improvements are often perceived as innovations that are new to a firm, but not to the industry, while radical BP innovations are new to the particular industry (Reichstein and Salter, 2006).

#

While the existing literature focuses on BPI within organizational boundaries - see for example Tang et al. (2013) - our study aims to establish a link between externally-focused open innovation and a firm’s internal BPI practices. According to Chesbrough (2003) open innovation assumes that firms use not only external and internal ideas, but also internal and external paths to market in order to boost their innovation performance and value creation. At the same time BPs are considered as internal means of value creation (Harmon, 2007). Consequently, we posit that a firm's external open innovation initiatives need to be supported by its internal business processes. The same processes also need to be improved or innovated in order to absorb new knowledge and skills acquired through openinnovation efforts. While previous studies suggest that organizational design, practices and capabilities need to be aligned with open innovation strategies (Foss, Laursen and Pedersen, 2011; Salge et al., 2012; Keinz et al., 2012, Jansen, van den Bosch & Volberda, 2005), there is little research that explicitly links open innovation with innovation of corresponding internal business processes.

Focusing on R&D open innovation, our research aims to contribute to the closing of this important research gap - in particular the managerial implications for BPI caused by open innovation. The next section describes our research model derived from an in-depth literature review, as follows.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

This research aims to study determinants of the relationship between R&D collaboration with external partners and a level of firm business process innovations. An extensive literature review confirmed that the main determinants of this relationship include sourcing knowledge/technology from the external environment (outside-in innovation processes) and organizational absorptive capacity reflective of a firm's effective external knowledge/technology exploration and exploitation. Also, taking into account previous research that suggests that large firms tend to innovate more than smaller companies, it is necessary to consider a company's size. The resulting research model and key variables are explained as follows.

## 3.1. Outside-in innovation processes

The company’s openness to its environment can be beneficial for its business performance including its ability to innovate. External acquisition and exploitation are often designated as crucial for a company’s innovativeness and in keeping up with competitors (Giannopoulou et al., 2010; Lichtenthaler and Ernst, 2008). Knowledge transactions represent substantial learning opportunities for a firm (Kutvonen, 2011). They enrich a company’s knowledge through integration with stakeholders, mainly customers and suppliers, in order to increase the company’s innovativeness (Gassmann and Enkel, 2004). Particularly, customers and suppliers are identified as the prime knowledge sources (Laursen and Salter, 2003), the reason for which can be found in the company's needs and motivation for this kind of collaboration. For example, interaction with customers enables companies to gain better insights into the market needs and possibilities for opening up new markets. Also, by involving customers in the early phases of innovation process, companies are learning about their latent requirements and hidden application knowledge, all in order to minimize market uncertainty when introducing innovative products (Gassmann et al., 2010; Ebner et al., 2009; Von Hippel, 1976).

In contrast to collaboration with customers, which is generally associated with product innovation and market expansion, collaboration with suppliers is often related to cost reduction and process innovation. By collaborating with their suppliers, firms may achieve a higher level of input quality thus improving their processes and reducing costs whilst also enhancing innovation potential for completely new processes.

Process innovation and cost reduction are also specific for competitive and firm-firm R&D collaboration that is designated as horizontal collaboration (Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011; Belderbos et al., 2006, 2004; Miotti and Sachwald, 2003; Hamel et al., 1989). This type of

#

collaboration is mainly realized in the form of strategic alliances, joint ventures, and outsourcing agreements (Hamel et al., 1989; Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011) and it is particularly important in relation to firm size. For example, previous research confirms that smaller companies engage in open innovation quite extensively (Huizingh, 2011; Van der Vrande et al., 2009). In these partnerships small firms with limited resources can achieve advantages related to economies of scale and scope, but also synergetic effects by pooling their joint resources. On the other hand, such partnerships are also beneficial for large firms, considering the greater flexibility of small organizations and their ability to adapt rapidly to changes in market demands.

Furthermore, research agencies/centers, universities and public institutions/companies are becoming increasingly popular collaboration partners given that they are recognized as inexpensive sources of new specialist knowledge (Belderbos et al., 2004). In this context academic/professional consulting is the most common form of linkage that is mainly related to the introduction of completely new products and processes (Perkmann, 2006). It is interesting to note however that this type of R&D collaboration is mainly less structured and depends on personal contacts with academics, researchers and/or consultants (De Wit et al., 2007).

In summary, we assume that sourcing knowledge/technology from the different type of external partners such as customers, suppliers, competitors, consultants, universities, research agencies/centers and public institutions/companies contributes to a company’s process innovation level. Therefore, we focus on innovation through outside-in collaboration processes and hypothesize that:

H1. Level of Outside-in innovation processes significantly contributes to variance of Business Process Innovations level.

## 3.2. Absorptive capacity

The existence of affiliate networks and a large number of partners and partner variety are not a guarantee that a company will take advantage of this cooperation in the context of the new knowledge acquisition, especially when it comes to the firm’s ability to leverage and benefit from transferred knowledge (Tsai, 2001). Therefore, sourcing external knowledge combined with the ability to adopt and apply that knowledge to a significant extent determines innovation performance.

Organizations without absorptive capacity are restricted in new knowledge assimilation and exploitation, which are both important for innovation activities. Specifically, externally sourced knowledge is often crucial for the innovation process (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990) but R&D collaboration „requires a firm’s absorptive capacity to recognize the value of external knowledge and make use of it“ (Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011, pp. 503). In this way, a company would be able to identify and exploit new knowledge.

According to Cohen and Levinthal (1990) absorptive capacity refers to the „ability of a firm to recognize the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends“ (pp. 128). The importance of an organization’s ability to acquire and replicate externally sourced knowledge is also emphasized by Zahra and George (2002). They define absorptive capacity as a firm’s ability to generate, assimilate, transform and exploit acquired knowledge.

Prior research indicates that intensive and significant levels of internal R&D and investment in internal R&D, prior knowledge, and the existence of expertise as well as skilled researchers all contribute to a company’s increased ability to understand and apply new knowledge (Muscio, 2007; Mowery and Rosenberg, 1989; Arora and Gambardella, 1990).

In sum, we propose that:

H2. Level of Absorptive Capacities/Capacity within a company significantly contribute(s) to variance of Business Process Innovations level.

## 3.3. Firm size

Regarding the type of innovation performance, Reichstein and Salter (2006) found that large firms with significant R&D investments that cooperate with suppliers are more likely to be process innovators. Process innovations imply significant improvements of processes, in terms of their effectiveness but also automation which includes external technology exploitation and absorptive capacity (Mairesse and Mohnen, 2004; Baldwin et al, 2002; Laursen and Salter, 2006). Consequently, it is expected that internal R&D intensity contributes to a company's process innovation efforts.

Large firms are therefore considered to have all the necessary prerequisites to achieve more successful partnerships and collaboration with external entities than smaller ones. They also have more competences, internal resources, and most importantly, sufficient knowledge and expertise to gain benefits from R&D alliances. These companies are capable of managing complex interactions with external partners and they can afford greater resources, more time and highly educated and trained employees such as professionals and specialists that significantly facilitate external knowledge exploitation (Laursen and Salter, 2003).

The critical size and absorptive capacity of large companies gives them an additional advantage when it comes to their participation in collaborative innovation (Belderbos et al., 2004). Their critical size is often accompanied by structured innovation processes, formalized procedures and innovation practices. In line with this, large companies have clearly defined formal structures for licensing IP, venturing activities, and external participations, but also have the stable financial background required to manage large and diversified innovation portfolios in order to spread potential risks (Van de Vrande et al., 2009).

Large companies are also strong enough to protect their innovations from possible imitation by competitors and know how to commercialize their ideas. Larger firms have larger technology portfolios and wider technological knowledge suitable for commercialization (Lazzarotti et al., 2011; Lichtenthaler and Ernst, 2009). Therefore, they are not just capable of acquiring new knowledge but they also have a solid existing knowledge base for outbound innovation activities. They have the capabilities necessary to gather and distribute a large volume of information and can use competitive intelligence systems to interpret gathered information (Ketchen et al., 2008).

Hence, we suggest that:

H3. Firm size significantly contributes to variance of Business Process Innovations level.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection

## 4.1.1. Sample

Our total sample consists of 240 companies currently operating in Bosnia and Herzegovina (B&H). All of them meet the research requirements such as size, maturity, industry, etc. In the sample defining process, participation of both manufacturing and service sectors but also firm size (large and SMEs) were included in order to make an adequate comparison and to examine the research object in the context of these companies (see Appendix 1). When defining size classes we followed the Recommendation 2003/361/EC. Primary points of contact at these companies were heads of research and development departments if such existed, otherwise CEOs. Out of our sample, 224 companies returned completed questionnaires.

#

## 4.1.2. Survey instrument

The data for this study were collected using a questionnaire specifically designed for this research. The questionnaire comprised two main sections: the first section - demographics and general questions mainly associated with firm’s size, headquarter, industry, existence of R&D department, etc., and the second section - questions related to business process innovations and collaboration with different externals such as customers, suppliers, competitors, universities, etc.

Prior to final data collection a pilot study (interviews with CEOs) was conducted with 10 subjects in order to test clarity of items and the expected variances and co-variances as well as to confirm contextual sensitivity of the questions. After the pilot, research questionnaire was slightly revised. At the end of the data collection phase, data were imported into the SPSS 21 software. 20% of imported data were rechecked to assess consistency and accuracy. If a single questionnaire was found to have a mistake, an additional 20% were checked. This process was repeated until no further mistakes were found. Research database is kept in safe location locked with password, as required by the ethics procedure.

## 4.2. Operationalization of variables

## 4.2.1. Dependent variable

Level of Business Process Innovations [BPI] is a dependent variable and it is linear combination of measured BPI (production, logistics and supporting processes) as an average process innovation level.

Measured process innovations were derived from three questions in the survey related to the type of process innovations (1. process innovations within production methods and/or service providing; 2. innovations in methods of logistics, delivery and distribution of products/services/raw materials, etc.; and 3. Innovations in supporting processes such as: market research, accounting, education and training of employees, etc.). All definitions and questions formulation are generally based on Community Innovation Surveys (CIS) administered by EUROSTAT and used in EU countries.

## 4.2.2. Independent variables

Outside-in innovation processes (Outside-in collaboration) [OI.] include sourcing of knowledge/technologies from externals including Customers [OI.Cus], Suppliers [OI.Sup], Consultants [OI.Con], Competitors [OI.Com], Universities [OI.Uni], Research Agencies [OI.Res] and Public/Government Companies/Institutions [OI.Pub] in order to improve existing and/or innovate new own processes. These variables indicate how often companies collaborate with the external sources of innovations. The definition and measurement are based on three core process archetypes identified by Gassmann and Enkel (2006), but also supported by other studies (e.g. Inauen and Schenker-Wicki, 2011; Lichtenthaler, 2009,). It is measured on the basis of a five-point Likert scale (never, rarely, sometimes, often, very often).

Absorptive Capacity [Abs.Cap], as firms’ abilities to generate as well as assimilate, transform and exploit the acquired knowledge (Zahra and George, 2002), is measured through following independent variables: existence of Research and Development department [eR&D.d], relative number of employees with MSc or MA degree [p.Msc], relative number of employees with Phd degree [p.Phd], investment in Innovation processes as whole [Inv.ip], percentage of investment in Innovations through collaboration [p.Inv.ic], percentage of investment in Innovations through new technologies [p.New.t] and percentage of investments in Innovations through new information technologies [p.Inf.t]. In the existing literature absorptive capacity dimensions have been measured by simple R&D proxies based on potential and realized absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal, 1989; Zahra and George, 2002). In our research, company's absorptive capacity is determined by the existence of company's R&D department, educated and trained employees as well as R&D investments. More precisely, in order to identify the reliable and valid absorptive capacity measures appropriate for the context of the transition economy, the most relevant literature was reviewed and then a pilot study (interviews with CEOs) was conducted with 10 subjects. Pilot testing confirmed that some measurement concepts, proposed by literature (such as Flatten et al., 2011), were found to be too ambitious and not applicable to our research context. For example, the new technology adoption and the development of prototypes were out of reach for the surveyed companies.

Our survey also included “open” questions about absorptive capacity measures of R&D features and activities within company. These questions as well as measurement items were derived from the related research studies (e.g. Schroll and Mild, 2011; Jørgensen and Ulhøi, 2010; Lokshin, et al., 2009; West and Gallagher, 2006; Zahra and George, 2002; Cohen and Levinthal, 1990;)

In the absence of continuous variables, we used a categorical variable for firm size. More precisely, Firm size [F.size] was introduced as independent variable measured through the number of full-time employees (the „staff headcount criterion“) as the main criterion. To define size classes we followed Recommendation 2003/361/EC. We adopted this classification to allow international comparison of the research findings, in particular with other EU candidate countries also coming from the same economic region. It is important to point out that our choice of Firm size as categorical variable, rather than more common Firm revenue, was informed by the economic reality of our research context. The insights obtained during our pilot project confirm the bleak economic reality of this small post-war economy, with a large number of companies operating with minimal or almost non-existent revenue. Especially, if considered from Western economy’s perspective, the reported Firm revenue would be considered as negligible, and therefore would not be an appropriate proxy for the firm size.

Hence, our research model proposes that absorptive capacity, outside-in collaboration processes and firm size explain BPI variance as per equation:

## 5. Results and Analysis

## 5.1. Sample size and power analysis

The recommended sample size for linear regression modelling was calculated using the G Power application with following parameters: 15 independent variables, high effect size (0,35), error type I of 5% and degrees of freedom of 14/76. Recommended sample for achieving actual power of .95 was 91. Actual sample size was 224 producing statistical power of .9999989.

## 5.2. Descriptive statistics, outliers and data transformation

Data screening was initiated by looking at potential outliers that are caused by wrong coding and/or entering data in the system. This was conducted by screening frequencies of data for each variable and looking at out of known range values. Each variable distribution was tested against normal distribution using the Normal Q-Q plot, Kolmogorov Smirnov and Shapirov Wilk indices, skewness and kurtosis. Each variable with skewness and kurtosis falling beyond -1 and +1 was transformed. Firm size,

#

Outside in collaboration with customers, outside in collaboration with suppliers, and outside in collaboration with public institutions were transformed using logarithm while all other variables had to be dichotomized as logarithmic transformation did not yield normal distribution.

For all variables analysis of outliers was conducted at a single variable level using the Boxplot and Normal Q-Q plot and at multivariate level using the Mahalanobis distance for each case. Mahalanobis distance was calculated for all cases using the 15 independent variables. At 15 degrees of freedom critical value at 0,001 is 37,7. 16 cases have Mahalonobis distance value larger from the critical value, thus are multivariate outliers. All 16 cases (6,67% of cases) were deleted and further calculations are conducted on 224 cases. These cases were deleted in order to prevent outliers from affecting the relationships between variables in a manner not being generalizable for the rest of data. After deletion of multivariate outliers, there were no more univariate and multivariate outliers in data set.

## 5.3. Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Linear regression calculation was conducted 15 times with 15 independent variables rotating each as dependent. All VIF indices were below 2 indicating non-existence of multicollinearity issues.

## 5.4. Data Analysis

In order to assess predictive validity of absorptive capacity and outside-in processes proxies to explanation of Business Process Innovation (BPI) level, bivariate correlations between individual proxies and BPI were calculated to identify proxies with significant correlations. After calculating bivariate correlations, two step linear regression was employed. At the first, all predictors were entered into the regression model. Those independent variables without significant predictive ability were again entered into the model as a second block of independent variables in order to see if any of them become significant predictor after removing non-significant predictors from the first block of independent variables. Considering low predictive ability of some predictors having significant and medium in size bivariate correlation with BPI, we conducted principal component analysis with all predictors extracting latent factors influencing BPI. Factor analysis was conducted in order to avoid concluding that certain predictor was not having predictive validity for BPI if it was a part of a latent factor.

## 5.4.1. Regression model

## 6. Discussion

This study confirmed that firms that intensively source knowledge and/or technology from customers, suppliers and public institutions, are more likely to be a process innovator. Therefore, outside-in innovation processes suggest enriching a company’s knowledge through integration with external stakeholders, mainly customers, suppliers and public institutions in order to increase the company’s innovativeness (Gassmann and Enkel, 2004). Focusing mainly on product innovation with customers as external sources of innovation, numerous previous studies (e.g. Avermaete et al., 2004; Baldwin and Von Hippel, 2010; Belderbos et al., 2004, 2006; De Jong and Von Hippel, 2009; Ebner et al., 2009; Elmquist et al., 2009; Gassmann et al., 2010) confirm that R&D collaboration with customers is

#

motivated by market expansion and the intention to minimize the risk of new product introduction. Our study indicates that customers can be a significant external partner, not only in product innovation, but also in process innovation.

Moreover, Reichstein and Salter (2006) found that using customers as a source of knowledge decreases the probability of being a process innovator, while firms collaborating with other firms increase their potential of becoming process innovators. Our research showed that knowledge/technology sourcing from both types of externals (customers and suppliers) contributes to process innovation quite significantly. Results indicate that R&D collaboration with public institutions - at least those which are recognized as „research-driven“ - also contributes to process innovations quite significantly. Unlike the other forms of collaboration, this particular type of collaboration „provides access to a broader pool of open science knowledge that a company is unable to draw upon internally“ (Perkmann, 2006; p.36). These collaboration activities are mostly funded by government and realized through technology transfer centers, spin-offs and joint research projects. While this type of collaboration is mainly investigated in the context of high-tech and large companies, our study showed that public institutions can be significant external partners when it comes to process innovations in low-tech sectors (traditional and mature industries) which is still unexplored fertile ground for firms’ R&D collaborations.

Our choice of independent variables and their definitions point to potential interaction being present. Indeed, our results indicate the interaction effect created by combination of different variables. For example, companies which use at least one external source of knowledge also tend to draw knowledge from many other external entities, and therefore to be more „open“ in innovation activities. The degree of a company’s innovation process’ openness is often illustrated through partner variety with which the company collaborates in R&D activities, but also the intensity of R&D collaboration. Namely, companies that source knowledge from particular external entities show greater preferences to collaborate with other types of externals. These companies have more abilities to manage external relationships as well as learning capabilities to exploit accessed knowledge. This, in turn implies that higher numbers of external knowledge sources that a company uses has a positive impact on open strategy and innovativeness (Lazzarotti et al., 2011; Laursen and Salter, 2003). Thus, Ebersberger, et al., (2012) found that collective open innovation in terms of a broad-based approach to R&D collaboration has the strongest influence on innovative performances.

Also Laursen and Salter (2006) found that companies which search widely (i.e. engage in R&D collaboration with a variety of externals) and deeply (i.e. focus more on intensive R&D collaboration) tend to be more innovative. This is because they are better positioned to recognize and deploy innovative opportunities.

Although externally sourced knowledge is considered valuable for innovation performance, this is not sufficient. Indeed, the firm should be able to recognize that value first in order to incorporate it effectively into its innovation activities. Accordingly, it was found that Absorptive Capacity proxies significantly contribute to a firm’s process innovations. This is also supported by other authors including Reichstein and Salter (2006), Mairesse and Mohnen (2004), Baldwin et al., (2002) who indicate that a high level of absorptive capacity may help companies to exploit new technologies from external sources and to perform process innovations. We found that sampled firms with more employees with Masters degrees were more often process innovators than others. This finding is in line with the notion that the most common indicators and measures of a firm’s absorptive capacity are internal R&D activities, which often include learning capabilities and the skills of human resources (Muscio, 2007).

The unsupported hypothesis testing also resulted in some interesting findings. For example, the unsupported relationship between the existence of an R&D department, the number of employees with a PhD, investment in innovation processes as whole, and outside-in collaboration with competition,

#

consultants, universities and research agencies/centres as independent variables, and BPI as dependent variable indicated that independent variables covariate significantly and/or are being loaded by several latent factors. In order to investigate these possibilities, employed exploratory factor analysis showed that we could expect that three distinct latent factors significantly contribute to explanation of BPI variance. These constructs could be related to Absorptive Capacity, Outside-in collaboration/innovation processes with “commercialization-driven” externals (customers, suppliers, competitors and consultants-mostly as freelancers) and Outside-in collaboration with Public Institutions (including research agencies and universities) as “research-driven” externals. Such a BPI model would have three measurement models (confirmatory factor analysis for above conducted analysis) and a single structural path (from latent factor to BPI). However this model should be confirmed and validated by further research.

We also found that the firm size did not significantly contribute to the level of Business Process Innovations. Although large firms are widely recognized as both early and most common adopters of open innovation, small and medium-sized firms (SMEs) can achieve significant advantages by opening their innovation processes. The resulting impact could be greater than for large firms, due to SMEs’ „dependence on external knowledge and collaboration“ (Jørgensen and Ulhøi, 2010; pp. 397). Embracing collaborative innovation practices, SMEs can overcome size and lack of resources as liabilities (Gassmann et al., 2010). However, firm size is often related to organizational absorptive capacities as large firms have more competences, internal resources but also sufficient knowledge and expertise to gain benefits from R&D alliances. This is included in the proposed BPI model as a measured variable within the Absorptive Capacity construct based on exploratory factor analysis results.

Our results also indicate that investments such as p.Inv.ic - Percentage of investment in innovations through collaboration, p.New.t.-Percentage of investment in innovations through new technologies, p.Inf.t -Percentage of investments through new information technologies do not contribute to the level of Business Process Innovations. In fact, most companies are struggling to survive and they do not have sufficient internal resources to invest in (i.e. fund) any new technology. Consequently, they rely exclusively on “free” external sources of knowledge and technology through R&D collaboration with external partners. Therefore, instead of investing mostly non-existing funds in new technology and other resources, these companies were found to focus on building valuable relationships to ensure transfer of know-how necessary for innovation.

Finally, it is also important to discuss our research findings and research method against the backdrop of struggling transition economies. Our research findings confirm Hossain’s (2015) argument about open innovation being “deeply rooted with the culture of a particular region“ (Hossain, 2015). Being mindful of the local context and culture was found to be very important not only when interpreting collected data, but also for context- and culturally-sensitive research design. For example, the World Bank Technical Assistance Project for Bosnia and Herzegovina (2013) offers the following insight into the local context in relation to R&D and innovation. “The war in the 1990s had a detrimental effect on the R&D infrastructure in the country. Many institutions that were at the forefront of technological development were demolished during this period, and severe budgetary constraints have made it impossible to invest in new equipment.” (WBRIS-TA, 2013, p.6). Yet, despite a very difficult economic situation, companies are found to be pro-active and entrepreneurial. “In 2009, 30 percent of BiH firms had internationally recognized quality certifications, compared to 19 percent for the whole region of the Western Balkans.” (WBRIS-TA, 2013, p.6).

Our in-depth understanding of the local context, in particular the very difficult financial situation, also influenced our design of the theoretical model and selection of contextually appropriate variables. For example, rather than firm revenue as a proxy for firm size, we decided to select a more suitable categorical variable of number of employees. Even more, we found that survey questions focusing on firm revenue (as initially considered for the pilot survey based on prior literature) could be considered

#

as culturally insensitive and as such could antagonise participating companies, thus reducing the response rate. Based on these observations we offer some important guidelines to other researchers interested in conducting future studies in transition economies. Thus, in addition to creating research instruments that are contextually and culturally sensitive (and confirmed as such through a pilot project), it is also important to have a research team member working in the local context. This will ensure that a research project is perceived by the participants as “locally-situated”, rather than conducted “on local companies” by external researchers, regardless of their understanding of the local context.

## 7. Research contributions, limitations and recommendations

## 7.1. Contributions to theory and practice

This research study defined and empirically tested a theoretical model that combines a firm’s externally focused open innovation with its internally focused business process innovation (BPI). As such, this model makes a significant contribution to the existing literature, both in the BPI and openinnovation fields. Also, to the best of the researchers' knowledge this is the first research study focusing on the relationship between open-innovation and BPI in a small transition post-war economy, thus offering very important insights into innovative practices and challenges beyond well-researched Western economies.

Our research also makes some important practical contributions, giving managers a «road-map» of important factors that need to be considered when planning and implementing their innovative strategies as well as when improving their existing ones, especially for those focusing on increased cooperation in various types of R&D joint ventures. More specifically, managers can improve process innovations but also business performances by investing in employees’ learning capabilities (education, training, skills, etc.) and by developing skills in building valuable relationships with collaborative partners of different kinds.

In addition, considering that the most common indicator for innovation activity is the share of innovative enterprises in the economy, this study provides a real picture of a large sample of companies’ innovativeness in Bosnia & Herzegovina. The use of a CIS based questionnaire as well as firm size classes definition (following Recommendation 2003/361/EC) would allow future international comparisons, in particular with other EU Member/Candidate states in the region. This is especially the case with those countries where firm revenue cannot be taken as an appropriate proxy for firm size, due to its often negligible value (when considered from the Western economy’s perspective). Therefore, the results of this study could be very useful, particularly in the context of B&H’s EU perspective. This is also important for policy-makers in determining B&H's current standing in terms of the so-called „Lisbon strategy” and its promotion of science, technology and innovation as the key indicators of sustainable economic growth.

## 7.2. Limitations and Recommendations

The scope of this study included small, medium and large companies in B&H with different ownership structures including public companies, semi-public companies and private companies. However, the results should not be generalized to non-profit and government institutions. Also, our research mode does not consider innovation funding, because in the economic context of our research study (a small transition economy) an adequate support by government and innovation intermediaries does not exist (at present time). These limitations offer new opportunities and recommendations for future research.

Our results and the proposed BPI model offer solid foundations for a deeper-level analysis of cause and effects related to business process innovations and IT-supported collaboration. For example, Web 2.0 tools, including various portal technologies and their role in enabling collaborative innovation relationships, could be an interesting research area. Also, various low-cost enterprise social networking platforms such as wiki tools, free web conferencing tools, and also different cloud-based innovation management and collaboration tools should be addressed in further research providing

#

potentially useful practical implications for companies in transition economies that are struggling with very limited resources. Based on our research findings, an important recommendation for public innovation policy would be to promote R&D collaboration between market participants as well as educational and training institutions as a means for much faster innovation, necessary for all companies. This would also impact relevant university courses in BPI and open innovation, both in terms of their content and delivery methods. For example it is important to equip students with the necessary skills and knowledge to act as boundary spanners across internally and externally focused models of innovation as well as grow valuable relationships with the current and potential innovation partners.

## 8. Conclusions

The main objective of this study was to contribute to a better understanding of the relationship between external open innovation and internal business process innovation. We found that firms which intensively source knowledge and/or technology from customers, suppliers and public institutions, and at the same time have a higher level of absorptive capabilities will more often introduce radical process innovations. We also found a positive association between intensiveness of R&D collaboration with external partners and internal R&D activities, especially within companies with a more educated workforce (more employees with Masters degrees).

The existing literature shows that large firms, in being able to afford more resources and highly educated and trained employees, are better placed to significantly enhance their internal learning capabilities while facilitating external knowledge exploitation. However, these findings were made in the context of Western economies. In contrast, our study did not find firm size to be important. With all surveyed companies operating in a challenging post-war transition economy with very limited resources, their competitive advantage becomes closely linked to their ability to integrate their innovation efforts across the firm's internal/external boundary i.e. to integrate external open innovation efforts with their internal business process innovation. Our future work will include qualitative case studies of a group of leading companies from our sample of 224 companies, focusing on human capital and human-centered knowledge management as well as the role of technology in this context. We argue that this particular focus is important not only for companies finding the way to survive through “frugal innovation” (Zeschky et al., 2011), but also for those from Western economies aiming to compete through investment in relationships as practiced by the companies in our research study.
