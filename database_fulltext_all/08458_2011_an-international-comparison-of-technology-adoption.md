## A B S T R A C T

We examined the relationships of the constructs in the UTAUT model to determine how they are affected by culture. In our study, we used data from Korea and the U.S. to examine two technologies: the MP3 player and Internet banking. Results showed that the UTAUT model fits our data well. The comparison of Korea and the U.S. revealed that the effects of effort expectancy on behavioral intention and the effects of behavioral intention on use behavior were greater in the U.S. sample. The implications of this are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Venkatesh et al. [27] compared and tested the variables in eight different models of users’ technology acceptance and subsequently, proposed a Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). The UTAUT model consists of four core variables – performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions – and four moderating variables – gender, age, experience, and voluntariness of use. Despite its limitations, the UTAUT model is an important concept because it integrated eight major theories and was tested on a large real world data set.

We have observed a difference in the speed that various countries have adopted IT. For example, the penetration rates of broadband in 2006 greatly differed across countries – 29.1% in Korea, 19.6% in the U.S., and 17.1% in Germany [18]. These differences in the pattern of IT adoption are attributed to many factors such as government policy, industry lead, and market environment. Technology adoption is as much a cultural issue as a rational decision-making process. Because technology is used in many contexts, culture can be expected to play a role in technology adoption and use. For example, Chae [5] showed that the type of information provided by accounting systems varied across countries, which suggested that the way people and organizations use information systems is affected by culture. Another study [20] showed that the contents of websites significantly differ across countries.

Some see culture as separating people from non-humans while some define it as communicable knowledge. Past studies showed that national culture has a significant influence on the diffusion of consumer products. The role of culture in technology adoption and use has been examined in many studies. However, a closer investigation is desirable in the context of rapidly changing ITs, because the impact of culture may be greater within fastadvancing technologies.

The UTAUT model does not include cultural factors. ITs are being utilized in many countries and it is crucial to obtain a better understanding of the impact of culture on technology adoption.

In our study the role of culture in technology acceptance was investigated. More specifically, the main research questions were

1. How does acceptance of a technology vary across cultures?

2. What factors in the UTAUT model most significantly differ across cultures and what are the implications of these differences?

To partially answer these questions, the UTAUT model was examined for two countries with different cultures: Korea and the U.S. We tested how the coefficients of the constructs of the UTAUT model varied between these two countries.

## 2. Prior research and theoretical background

Technology adoption has been studied in many ways. Some studies took the ‘‘process’’ approach and examined its in-depth process [4,16]; others focused on relationships between technology adoption and the variables influencing it: UTAUT and TAM (technology acceptance model) were examples of this. Lai and Li’s [12] test of invariance of the constructs of TAM, Im et al. [11] and Wu and Wang [29] inclusion of perceived risk in the model, Yi et al. [30] integration of TAM with the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and innovation diffusion theory (IDT), Saade´ and Bahli [21] application of TAM in the context of online learning, etc. were efforts to extend the TAM model.

Fang et al. [8] added perceived playfulness and perceived security to the original independent variables in TAM. They investigated the effects of task type (general task, games, and transactions) on the model and found that users were influenced more by perceived playfulness when gaming and more by perceived usefulness and perceived security when conducting transactions.

After its introduction, the UTAUT model was tested and applied to several technologies, such as online bulletin boards [17] and instant messengers [15]. However, most of these studies simply applied the UTAUT model to different technologies in a single country.

Moderating variables examined for the UTAUT model, have found that experience, gender, and age as most significant.

## 2.1. International comparisons of innovation diffusion

With the development of fast communication technologies, the diffusion rate of products and services has increased, especially for digitized products and related services, such as MP3 players or Internet banking. The findings of prior studies on the relationship between culture and product diffusion have, however, been inconsistent [7].

The difference in the adoption of products across countries results from diverse macro-level economic and socio-economic factors. Collective national characteristics may explain the heterogeneity in the diffusion process of newly introduced goods or services [10]. In fact Hofstede’s work on national cultures has been widely used in past studies; his major dimensions of national culture are: individualism/collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity/femininity. Later studies added a fifth dimension: long-term orientation [9]. Individualism/collectivism is the preference to act either as individuals or as members of a group. Koreans tend toward collectivism whereas Americans and the British tend toward individualism. Communication contextuality involves the amount of information an individual is willing to offer. In low-context cultures, individuals provide little information, while in high-context cultures, individuals volunteer background details and other related information. High uncertainty avoidance describes individuals that demand detail and precise information in order to lower uncertainty. Such individuals have little tolerance for uncertainty. Low uncertainty avoidance cultures, such as the U.S. and U.K., need fewer rules, less structure, and so are more comfortable with ambiguity.

## 2.2. Country effects in technology adoption

After reviewing past studies on technology acceptance, Sun and Zhang [25] identified possible moderating variables and propositions that predict their impact. Although not empirically tested, a set of possible moderating variables was identified: voluntariness, the nature of tasks and professions, technology complexity, the purpose of using technology (work- vs. entertainment-oriented), individual vs. group technologies, gender, individual intellectual capabilities, experience, age, and cultural background. Employing Hofstede’s dimensions, they argued that users’ expectations for performance (or perceived usefulness) would have a greater impact on behavioral intention in cultures with lower power distance, more individualism, more masculinity, and lower uncertainty avoidance. On the other hand, they argued that social norm would have a greater impact on user behavioral intention in cultures with higher power distance, less individualism, less masculinity, and higher uncertainty avoidance.

Lee et al. [13] compared the acceptance of mobile technologies across Korea, Hong Kong, and Taiwan. They argued that the variances of users in various cultural groups in each country were often larger than the variances across countries; they measured four major cultural dimensions (uncertainty avoidance, individualism and collectivism, context, and time perception) at the individual level, instead of at the country level; their study showed that the cultural dimensions had significant effects on individual users’ adoption and use of technologies. One limitation of their study, however, was that they compared countries with similar cultures in East Asia.

Al-Gahtani et al. [1] investigated the applicability of UTAUT to Saudi Arabia. They tested the moderating effects of several variables, such as gender, age, and experience. Instead of comparing the results from their Saudi Arabian sample with those from other countries, they indirectly addressed the effects of country or culture. Although their study did not directly compare samples from different countries, they found that culture is a significant moderator of technology acceptance.

These studies are categorized in Table 1 according to the two dimensions of their research contexts – single country vs. multiple countries and organizational use vs. individual use. ‘‘Multiple countries’’ indicates that the study compared technology adoption across countries, and ‘‘in-between’’ means that the study did not. ‘‘Organizational use’’ indicated that the study was about a technology used in an organizational context, and ‘‘individual use’’ indicated that the study was about a technology used for individual purposes. The ‘‘organizational vs. individual use’’ dimension was selected because prior studies had shown that there are significant differences between these two types of technologies on users’ adoption behavior [11,26].

Our study directly compared the UTAUT model between two countries to examine how technology adoption varied across different cultures.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Past studies on the variation of technology adoption in different countries showed inconsistent or even conflicting results. For example, the moderating effect of culture on the relationship between perceived ease of use and behavioral intention was significant in one study but insignificant in another; possibly because of the differences in the type of technologies examined. The main goal of our research was to conduct an empirical study to provide insights on the diffusion of two different technologies in two countries that had significantly different cultural characteristics.

The most important part of the UTAUT model is the relationships between use intention and two independent constructs – performance expectancy and effort expectancy. The UTAUT model has two more constructs than the traditional TAM: facilitating conditions (environmental factors that make an action easy) and social influence (the degree to which a person perceives that important others believe that he or she must adopt a method or system).

Our first set of hypotheses is the base hypotheses of the UTAUT model:

H1a. The performance expectancy about a technology has a positive impact on users’ intention to adopt it.

H1b. The effort expectancy about a technology has a positive impact on users’ intention to adopt it.

H1c. The social influence surrounding a technology has a positive impact on users’ intention to adopt it.

H1d. The facilitating conditions of a technology have a significant impact on users’ actual use of it.

H1e. The users’ intention to adopt a technology has a significant impact on users’ actual use of it.

It seems obvious that the magnitudes of the coefficients in the model are influenced by culture. The impact of ‘‘performance expectancy’’ would be greater in countries that have a lower power distance and a more individualistic culture, as Sun and Zhang proposed. Users in a lower power distance and more individualistic culture are expected to make more independent decisions about adopting a technology. In Hofstede’s study, Korea has higher power distance and less individualism than the U.S. Therefore, the impact of performance expectancy on behavioral intention should be stronger in the U.S. than in Korea. Thus:

H2a. The impact of performance expectancy on behavioral intention is stronger in the U.S. than in Korea.

With the exception of Straub et al., [24] there have been no prior studies that provide a reason for making a hypothesis on the effect of effort expectancy on behavioral intention; Straub et al. found that the impact of ease of use on adoption of technology was stronger in the U.S. than in Japan.

Some predictions, however, can be inferred from past studies. In an individualistic and low power distance culture, users tend to make more independent decisions. This was the basis for H2a, where a stronger impact of performance expectancy was hypothesized. In the same vein, it can be inferred that the impact of effort expectancy would be stronger in a more individualistic and low power distance culture because users would make more independent decisions. In a more collectivistic and high power distance culture, users’ decisions on technology adoption will be affected more by other factors, such as social norms. This leads to:

H2b. The impact of effort expectancy on behavioral intention is stronger in the U.S. than in Korea.

The impact of social influence should be greater in countries that have a higher power distance and a less individualistic culture.

Users in a more collectivistic and higher power distance culture will be affected by others when making decisions on technology adoption; as hypothesized in Sun and Zhang’s study:

H2c. The impact of social norms on behavioral intention is stronger in Korea than the U.S.

In the UTAUT model, the facilitating conditions for a technology are positively correlated with its use. If there are more conditions that support the use of a technology, then people would be more likely to adopt the technology. Therefore,

H2d. The impact of facilitating conditions on use behavior is not different between Korea and the U.S.

A review on the role of culture in technology adoption by Leidner and Kayworth [14] showed that people from a high uncertainty avoidance culture were less likely to experiment with or adopt new technologies. This implied that the intention to use a new technology would be less likely to result in its actual use in countries with high uncertainty avoidance. Korea has higher uncertainty avoidance than the U.S., implying that with the same level of intention to adopt a technology, people in Korea would be less likely to use the technology than people in the U.S.

An individual’s behavioral intention in Korea is influenced more by others’ opinions and social norms than in the U.S. This implies that the adoption intention of people in Korea is less likely to result in actual use than in the U.S. leading to:

H2e. The impact of behavioral intention on use behavior is stronger in the U.S. than in Korea.

## 4. Empirical study

The hypotheses were tested using data collected from students and full-time employees in Korea and the U.S. A survey was developed using primarily instruments from past studies.

## 4.1. Measurement instruments

It has been shown that the measurement instruments of most UTAUT constructs are valid across multiple cultures [19]. Based on this, most measurement instruments employed were, with slight modifications, taken from the original UTAUT study. Behavioral intention (BI) was measured using a 7-point Likert scale, asking respondents to detail their intentions and plans to use the technology within the next year. Those who had already used the technology were asked to answer as if they had to make a decision about first adopting the technology. Use behavior (UB) was measured by asking about users’ actual frequencies of technology use, indicated on a 9-point interval scale (have not used, once a year, once in six months, once in three months, once a month, once a week, once in 4–5 days, once in 2–3 days, almost every day). All exogenous constructs in the UTAUT model were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions were adopted from Venkatesh et al.’s study.

## 4.2. Data collection

Our survey was administered in Korea and in the U.S. in the contexts of adopting MP3 players and using Internet banking. These contexts were selected because they had significantly different characteristics. An MP3 player is hedonic, mainly for entertainment, while Internet banking is utilitarian, for carrying out a given task. These contrasting characteristics made the data set for this study more general because technology selection biases are mitigated.

After the first draft questionnaire was completed, the survey items were refined through two rounds of pilot tests. In the first round, the validity of the questionnaire was tested. It was reviewed by two experts in IS and modified based on their feedback. In the second round, the questionnaire was administered to participants to test the measurement reliability and validity.

The questionnaire was initially developed in English and the final version was translated into Korean. In order to ensure the equivalence of measurements in the two languages, the translation was reviewed by a third party. The questionnaire was finalized after correcting a few minor differences in wording in the two languages.

Although we expected some differences in culture between samples from Korea and from the U.S., it was important for precise comparison to have samples compatible in other aspects such as background and job experience. In Korea, randomly selected college students and office workers responded to the questionnaire. In the U.S., undergraduate and part-time MBA students in a university on the East Coast of the U.S. answered the questionnaire. The part-time MBA students in the U.S. were also generally fulltime workers. Therefore, it was reasonable to assume that the samples in Korea and in the U.S. were compatible in terms of background and job experience. We decided to include office workers and part-time MBA students in the sample because we learned that not many undergraduate students use Internet banking. We distributed questionnaires to 660 people (480 in Korea and 180 in the U.S.) and a total of 550 responses were received (407 from Korea and 143 from the U.S.). Thus the initial response rates were 84.7% in Korea and 79.4% in the U.S. After discarding invalid responses, we had 501 responses left (363 from Korea and 138 from the U.S.).

## 4.4. Test of the UTAUT model

Before the data from Korea and the U.S. were compared, the UTAUT model was tested using the entire data set. A covariance SEM analysis was conducted using AMOS 4.0. In such analyses, there are several commonly used goodness-of-fit indices: GFI, AGFI, RMR, RMSEA, and CFI. A model is considered appropriate when its GFI, AGFI, and CFI are greater than 0.9 and its RMR and RMSEA are between 0.05 and 0.08 [2]. All GFIs of the model were satisfactory: x<sup>2</sup> = 168 (df = 80), GFI = 0.96, AGFI = 0.93, RMR = 0.04, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.05.

The various statistics in Fig. 1 confirmed that the UTAUT model was supported. Performance expectancy, effort expectancy, and social influence significantly affected behavioral intention. Behavioral intention and facilitating conditions had significant effects on use behavior.

## 4.5. Testing country effects

In the first step to compare Korea and the U.S., the means and standard errors of the measurement items were compared, see

Table 4. Except for the items of social influence, all responses were higher in the U.S. than in the Korean sample.

The coefficients of the UTAUT model for the two countries were analyzed separately, as shown in Table 5. All paths were significant in the Korean sample, while one path (social influence ! use behavior) was insignificant in the U.S.

The moderating effect of nationality (U.S. or Korea) was statistically tested. A common simple method to check the moderating effect is enforcing a constraint for the path coefficient to the analysis model. The steps to conduct this test are

Step 1: Run the models for two groups (U.S. and Korea) without any constraints (free model)

Step 2: Set the ‘‘equality constraint’’ for the two groups; i.e., run the models for two groups, constraining one group by the results from the other (constraint model).

Step 3: Compare the free model and the constraint model by testing the $\scriptstyle { \dot { \chi } } ^ { 2 }$ differences.

Step 4: If the test in Step 3 is significant, test the difference of each path coefficient.

Thus, it is concluded that a path coefficient is significantly different across the two samples if the difference between the $\chi ^ { \dot { 2 } }$ with constraints and without constraints is significantly large.

For example, the third row, which is labeled as (b) is the test result of the coefficient of effort expectancy ! BI. The chi-square difference $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } )$ between the default model (without constraints) and the constrained model (the model whose coefficient b for the U.S. is fixed the same as the coefficient b for Korea) is 5.3 (258.7–253.4). Since the difference in degree of freedom (Ddf) between these two models is 1, Dx<sup>2</sup>/Ddf is 5.3/1 = 5.3. The probability for 5.3 is 0.02, which is smaller than the a value (0.05). Therefore, it can be concluded that there is a significant difference between the default model and the constrained model for the coefficient b.

The magnitudes of the two path coefficients (effort expectancy ! behavioral intention and behavioral intention ! use behavior) are significantly different in the two models, with and without constraints. This implies that these two coefficients are moderated by country.

For a more rigorous comparison, the two models with the U.S. and Korean sets of data were analyzed using a recent analysis method. Dabholkar and Bagozzi [6] argued that simple moderating effect analysis cannot distinguish the effects by coefficients (true moderating effects) from the effects by differences in the error variances (variance effects). They suggest conducting tests using four different models to test the moderating effect of each variable.

\- Model A has all factor loadings constrained across groups and error variances of the items for endogenous variables are also constrained.

\- Model B has the factor loadings free, but error variances constrained.

\- Model C has both factor loadings and error variances free.

\- Model D has factor loadings constrained, but error variances free.

The differences between Models A and B (or Models C and D) are the factor loadings. The differences between Models A and D (or Models B and C) are from error variances. Therefore, if the variable of interest is a true moderator, Models A and B (or Models C and D) should be significantly different. Dabholkar and Bagozzi recommended a two-step analysis. First, compare Models A and D. If there is a significant difference in the chi-square value, it indicates that the difference between the two groups being compared comes from the differences in error variances. Second, if Models A and D are not significantly different, compare Models A and B. If there is a significant difference in the chi-square value, it means that the difference comes from the factor loadings, which implies that a moderating effect exists. Since the effects of variance differences are separated out, this method is particularly effective when samples have different variances. From Table 4, it is clear that the Korean and U.S. samples have different variances, and therefore, this method is appropriate.

This method is similar to the ‘‘invariance analysis’’ employed in past studies (for example, [23]). Both methods have the same fundamental idea – separating the effects of variance from the effects of coefficients. The biggest difference between invariance analysis and Dabholkar and Bagozzi’s method is that the invariance analysis compares one coefficient at a time. Following the logic of Dabholkar and Bagozzi, another set of tests was conducted to check the moderating effect of nationality. The results are summarized in Table 7.

## 5. Discussion

The hypothesis test results are summarized in Table 8. Not surprisingly, all H1 hypotheses (original UTAUT model) were supported. Among the H2 hypotheses, H2b, H2d, and H2e were supported.

The impact of performance expectancy on behavioral intention was not significantly different between the two countries and this may indicate that performance is an important factor affecting technology adoption equally across countries.

It is interesting that effort expectancy has a greater impact on behavioral intention in the U.S. than in Korea. This implies that the U.S. users’ decision-making on technology adoption is affected more than Korean users by how easy the technology is to use.

One surprising result is that there is no significant difference in the impact of social influence between Korea and the U.S. It is generally accepted that social influence is larger in Asian cultures. Although statistically insignificant, the coefficient of social influence was a lot larger in the Korean sample. On the other hand, the standard error of the coefficient in the U.S. sample was much larger than that of the Korean sample. The impact of facilitating conditions was not different between the two countries, either. The magnitudes of the impact were not significantly different across Korea and the U.S., as hypothesized.

The impact of behavioral intention on use behavior was greater in the U.S. sample than in the Korean sample. This indicated that U.S. users were more likely to use the system if they had the intention to use it, in contrast to Korean users.

## 5.1. Implications of the results

The results of our study carry several implications for practitioners and researchers. First, the magnitudes of the impacts of the variables in the well-accepted UTAUT model vary across countries. This implies that marketers should consider different marketing strategies when planning to market a technology in different countries. When deciding to adopt a technology, U.S.

users seem to give more weight to its fundamental characteristics and ease of use than do Korean users.

Second, it seems that users in the U.S. make more rational decisions, while users in Korea are more influenced by factors other than their own use intention. This helps explain the recent quick adoption of ITs such as broadband Internet and social networking systems (SNS) in Korea. Once a technology gains in popularity, people in Korea tend to adopt it more quickly because they are more susceptible to the influence of external factors such as trends and social groups.

## 5.2. Limitations

Our study had several limitations, mainly in sampling and the technologies surveyed. The respondents were mainly young people whose behavior might differ somewhat from the population average. They are generally more innovative and faster to accept new technologies, and this may have biased to the results.

Another limitation of our study was that the Korean sample contained more full-time workers than the U.S. sample. However, many of the part-time MBA students in the U.S. sample work fulltime, which makes the two samples compatible.

While this study had some limitations, it nevertheless compared the UTAUT model across different countries. The technologies we investigated were MP3 players and Internet banking, which are fast-changing digital products and services. Our study thus provided an understanding of how culture influences the adoption of technologies. From the ‘‘behavioral science vs. design science’’ perspective, UTAUT is a behavioral model that aims to explain the behavior of people or organizations in their use of IS. This study, however, can be a stepping stone to a complementary research cycle between behavioral science and design science in that it provides effective methods for marketing information technologies across different cultures.
