# Click trading: A case study of Moneynet

Des Laffey

Kent Business School, University of Kent, Canterbury CT2 7PE, United Kingdom

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 5 September 2008 Accepted 13 January 2009 Available online 25 February 2009

Keywords: Comparison websites Aggregation e-Commerce Online value chains

## a b s t r a c t

Moneynet, launched in 1997, was the United Kingdom’s first comparison website in financial services. It enables its users to specify their needs and then select from suitable product providers, who in turn benefit from potential customers with refined needs.

This article makes both a theoretical and applied contribution to the Information Systems literature. It develops an amended version of the Porter value chain, which it terms the click chain, as a theoretical framework which is then used to analyse Moneynet. This framework analyses how Moneynet creates value and in doing so identifies key implications for comparison websites and product providers.

## 1. Introduction

Comparison websites, also known as aggregators, bots or agents, have become a major part of e-commerce. They lower search costs for buyers by allowing them to compare products from a range of providers, in terms of price and key features. Comparison websites also appeal to product providers as they generate a stream of customers whose needs have been refined. Revenue is generated through advertising and/or commission payments when users click through and request information or purchase a product.

The first comparison websites emerged in the mid 1990s and they have consequently developed across most consumer sectors in nations with significant online markets. Although most comparison sites cover a range of markets, examples of coverage include:

\- Financial services, for example Bankrate in the US and Moneysupermarket in the UK.

\- Consumer goods, for example the Yahoo owned Kelkoo in Europe, Bondfaro in Brazil, Price Express in Russia, Kakaku in Japan, and Ding9 in China.

\- Travel, for example Sidestep in the US.

\- Car rental, for example Oodles in Australia and New Zealand.

\- Home energy, for example uSwitch in the UK.

Comparison websites are particularly important for products where there is an ongoing financial commitment, for example personal finance, communications and energy services, as the savings here are often potentially far greater than is the case with a one-off purchase. However, this is only the case if consumers are able to easily switch providers. As an example in the UK consumers have been able to choose their electricity and gas supplier since 1998 whilst some European markets only deregulated in 2007, as is the case with France (European Commission, 2008). These regulatory factors and high levels of broadband adoption help to explain why the UK has seen a significant emergence of comparison websites in utilities (for example uSwitch) and elsewhere. However, even where consumers are able to choose suppliers levels of switching vary widely. Waterson (2003) writing on competition policy offers the extreme cases in the UK of motor insurance where changing can be done with ‘‘relative ease” and current account banking which is ‘‘a cumbersome procedure”. The former has a standard contract length of one year and clear pricing structures whilst banking in contrast has ongoing contracts, hidden charges, the problems of moving automated payments and the potential loss of goodwill when becoming a new customer.

## 1.1. Objectives

This article models comparison websites using an amended version of Porter’s value chain (1995) termed the click chain. The article applies this approach through use of an in-depth case study with the comparison website Moneynet, used because of the exploratory nature of this research. The case study offers evidence to support the click chain model as an effective representation of the manner in which users and product providers interact with comparison websites and other online resources, in particular search engines. The relevance of related frameworks (Rayport and Sviokla, 1995; Stabell and Fjeldstad, 1998) to Moneynet and the comparison sector is also noted.

## 1.2. Structure

The structure of the article is as follows. Section 2 covers the existing literature on comparison websites and the essential background to comparison websites from a technology and business perspective. Section 3 then briefly reviews the value chain framework, its key criticisms and later theoretical developments. Section 4 outlines the methodology for the case study and Section 5 analyses the case findings within the click chain framework. Section 6 covers the implications for practitioners and academics before the article concludes.

## 2. Background to comparison websites

## 2.1. Existing literature

Whilst there are a large number of papers in the computing field which focus upon the technical considerations which face comparison websites (for example, Doorenbos et al., 1997; Embley et al., 1999; McGurrin et al., 2001; Myllymaki 2002; Chang et al., 2006), there is relatively little research on this subject from a business and management perspective.

Maes et al., (1999) analyse software agents – the dominant term at that time – from the perspective of consumer buying behaviour research and identify the industries most affected by the phenomenon as those dealing with perishable products, surplus inventory and products which are of standard quality. Madnick and Siegel (2002) argue that organisations should look upon aggregation as both a threat and an opportunity and offer guidance to all participants. Kocas (2003, 2005)) stresses the importance of loyalty in countering the threat of comparison websites. Su (2007) identifies three consumer choice strategies used under conditions of incomplete information: these are expected value, which looks to maximise utility in terms of price, brand and expected credibility; brand seeking which looks for the most well known organisation in the comparison, and price aversion, which chooses lowest cost. His study, which used a sample of graduate students and two products, digital cameras and books, found that these strategies explained about 70% of total choices

Wan et al., (2007) analyse what they term Product Comparison Agents (PCAs) using the analogy of the food chain. They categorise sites according to the nature of the assistance and information they provide to users. This includes:

\- Objective information from online vendors, for example prices and terms and conditions, which they term product differentiation.

\- Subjective numeric information from online users, for example customer ratings, which they term product evaluation.

\- Subjective text information from online users, for example customer reviews of service which they term consumer prefer ence identification.

However, most comparison websites also offer other information they have written, or commissioned, about the industry, such as product guides and news. This information does not fit easily into Wan’s framework as it comes from the comparison website itself, rather than vendors or users.

## 2.2. Revenue models

## 2.2.1. Fees

Some product providers pay comparison websites for either click throughs from the comparison website, known as costper-click (CPC), and/or (more commonly) on the basis of actions from such click throughs, known as cost-per-action (CPA). The most common example for CPA is a sale although this may also include other things, for example customers who complete an enquiry form.

The charging of fees raises a dilemma for comparison sites; whether to include firms who do not agree to pay. Comparison site promote themselves on their breadth of coverage, but as commercial operations they clearly need to generate revenues

The standard approach to this is to cover the whole market in comparisons, but to only provide the opportunity to click through (by providing an Apply button) to those that do pay. This is an example of versioning as identified by Shapiro and Varian (1998) as the product providers have been separated into two groups, those who are willing to pay and those who are not. The basic version of the product is available to all (here, the display of products in a comparison table) whilst the full version of the product (the ability for potential users to click through) has to be paid for.

## 2.2.2. Advertising

Most comparison sites offer some forms of advertising, and Google’s Product Search (formerly known as Froogle) relie entirely upon it and has no fees.

## 2.3. Technology

## 2.3.1. Screen scraping

Screen scraping involves using automated software that copies the information from a product provider’s web pages for use on the comparison site. However, there are major problems with screen scraping as HTML documents are ‘‘designed for presentation purposes, not automated extraction” (Myllymaki, 2002, p. 635). This means that the structure of each page has to be analysed and if the content changes this process has to be repeated, making it a cumbersome process (McGurrin et al., 2001).

## 2.3.2. XML

An efficient technical alternative to screen scraping is the use of XML to integrate data from back-end systems with the comparison website. Moneysupermarket states, in describing the use of XML for insurance comparisons, that this is ‘‘is faster and more effective than screen-scraping” (Moneysupermarket, 2008). However, this does rely upon organisations cooperating with comparison sites.

## 2.3.3. In-house collection or via industry standard data feeds

Some organisations collect their own product information by employing specialist staff, with Moneynet, the subject of this in-depth case study, adopting this approach. Some organisations go a step further and collect their own data which they also supply to other comparison websites. A variety of methods are used including direct liaison with product providers, provider documentation/updates and website information.

## 2.4. Ethics and disclosure

Comparison websites, in financial services and utilities in particular, gain constant media coverage from their focus on high profile markets where it is commonly believed that organisations do not offer value to consumers. Inevitably this means that comparison websites will be subject to scrutiny themselves. They face three main criticisms:

Commercial relationships – some websites clearly state that they are paid commissions by product providers, whilst others do not. Criticisms have also been made on the lack of clarity between impartial results and advertising tables.

Inappropriate matching – a problem which emerged in financial services was that comparison websites assumed that users had good credit records. This led to those with a bad credit record being matched with products requiring a good credit record, and so being rejected which in turn led to a worsening of their credit profile.

Privacy – some comparison websites require personal details to be entered before even simple comparisons can be done. This is done so these details can be used for marketing purposes, which is usually explained via a link in small print.

## 3. Value creation

To provide a sense of focus the case study undertaken for this article considered how comparison websites create value. Before this case could be considered a review of the relevant literature was therefore necessary.

## 3.1. The value chain

Porter states that ‘‘the value chain disaggregates a firm into its strategically relevant activities in order to understand the behaviour of costs and the existing and potential sources of differentiation” (1985, p. 33). Value is defined as being what buyers are prepared to pay for a product, coming either through being a cost leader or through obtaining premium prices through differentiation.

An individual firm’s value chain is connected to the value chains of both its suppliers and buyers, in what are termed ‘‘value systems”; its suppliers provide inputs to the firm’s value chain whilst the output of the firm’s own value chain creates inputs for its buyer’s value chains

In related work Porter and Millar (1985) suggest that IT was transforming the value chain, in terms of the individual activities and also the linkages between activities. Their discussion of IT permeating the value chain noted the impact of remote terminals on Sales and Marketing, with the impact of the Blackberry a more recent example. In the same way linkages in the wider value systems which were achieved through EDI are now being achieved through Extranets.

Rayport and Sviokla (1995) argue that alongside the value chain (the marketplace) there is a virtual value chain (the marketspace) which can create new value. A classic example of this is enabling users to gain access to newspaper archives online, something that is time consuming and expensive when done manually. Rayport and Sviokla further state that some activities can be moved from the physical value chain to the virtual value chain, giving the example of product design.

Whilst Porter’s value chain has been seen as a useful tool in analysing traditional industries, particularly manufacturing, its relevance to the service sector has been questioned (Peppard and Rylander, 2006).

Other criticisms have been made of Porter’s model, namely its sequential nature and its failure to capture interdependencies. In response to these limitations Stabell and Fjeldstad (1998) present two additional value configurations, the value shop and the value network. The value shop focuses upon knowledge intensive environments which solve unique problems, such as medical professionals and lawyers, whilst value networks look at how value is created through intermediaries who enable connections to be made, such as financial institutions and telecommunications companies.

## 4. Primary methodology – case study

It was decided to adopt a case study approach for this paper as there is little existing research on comparison websites. This fits with the advice of Yin who writes ‘‘In general, case studies are the preferred strategy when ‘‘how” or ‘‘why” questions are being posed.” (1994, p. 1). The author had already undertaken research in this field which had stimulated the theoretical framework used to analyse this case study, based on Porter’s value chain and covered in Section 5.

In November 2007 a request for an interview was sent to a number of the leading comparison websites in the UK, judged to be significant through media and financial coverage by the author. Richard Brown Chief Executive of Moneynet, responded to the request, and after a telephone conversation agreed to an in-depth interview on the company’s premises.

Prior to the interview the author explained the purpose of the research and made it clear that this information would be in the public domain, so any confidentiality concerns could be noted. The interview, in December 2007, lasted one hour and fifteen minutes. The interview was semi-structured in nature, starting off with general questions on the company background to put the respondent at ease. Detailed questions based on the value chain and related frameworks were then used to gather information, with other questions included so as not to limit the information collected. Care was taken not to produce expected answers and flexibility was allowed in the process which enabled an effective two-way dialogue to emerge.

To ensure internal validity the interview was transcribed and sent to Richard Brown for confirmation of accuracy and to check that no commercially sensitive information had been included. Analysis of the case was then undertaken based around the key issues which emerged, whilst also making use of information on the company’s website and other information in the public arena.

As this research focuses on one in-depth case study with one individual obtained through convenience sampling it does not claim to be generalisable to all comparison websites. Furthermore, the research does not include the views of product providers or users and so there are issues of bias. However, given the seniority of the interviewee and his experience as the founder of the UK’s first financial services comparison website, the data was rich and provided much scope for analysis.

## 4.1. Background to Moneynet

Moneynet is a trading name of Sterling Business Consultants which employs 17 people and is based in Bromley, near London. Moneynet, whose founders came from a background of financial services, was launched in 1997 as the first UK comparison website for financial services, initially only offering mortgage comparisons. Moneynet soon expanded into a broad range of financial services products and also offered gas and electricity price comparisons

Richard Brown contrasts the operations of Moneynet with his experience as a financial adviser, when he arranged mortgages and other financial products. As a financial advisor it was not in his interest to provide full information to clients.

‘‘I spent 10 years as a broker; one of the things that was obvious was that all people wanted was the best deal; so you tried not to divulge the details of the provider until someone was ready to sign up. Otherwise they would go direct to the lender.”

Offering financial comparisons over the web aimed to change this.

‘‘We felt that if we could get all this information in one place and make it user friendly that there was a potential market here.”

‘‘There was a great hunger for information. People cannot find it easily and could only then get it through reading tables in magazines, which were often out of date and hard to understand, or trawling the high street or making various phone calls.”

Moneynet does not charge for inclusion in its results and generates revenue from the typical dual model in the industry: commissions for sales and advertising. It uses the versioning method outlined in Section 2.2.1, meaning that users can click through to the websites of the product providers who pay fees. It does not disclose how it is funded on its website.

## 5. Analysis using the click chain framework

An amended version of the Value Chain framework, termed the click chain, will be used to analyse the case (see Fig. 1). In this model, users are the final product, and they are intimately involved in the process, being refined into a higher quality click (user) who is more likely to buy a product. Value is measured by the willingness of the product provider to pay for such clicks. This is not inconsistent with placing users’ interests first, as ultimately without users comparison sites have little to offer product providers. To quote Richard Brown:

‘‘We would always like to think that we are the consumer champion, that we are on the consumer’s side.”

## 5.1. Primary activities

Inbound clicks – Moneynet attracts visitors, termed inbound clicks, who are looking for financial comparisons. The sources of these clicks need to be identified for performance monitoring and also because some of them incur payment. Richard Brown broke down the inbound traffic as follows:

40% direct to Moneynet;

30% through search (mostly organic, with a small amount of paid search);

5% affiliates;

25% through advertising.

Thus nearly 70% of Moneynet’s traffic is non-paid (the first two categories above).

Operations – users are asked to enter their requirements and circumstances, including any credit problems, to enable effective matching (Moneynet does not attempt to collect personal data for routine comparisons).

Richard Brown describes the design of this service as follows:

‘‘What we had was a blank sheet of paper on which we had to develop everything. What we’ve found – maybe it was so obvious – is that all comparison sites have developed down the same route we developed in 1997. Compare a product, click to apply – we had to think it all through.”

Moneynet does not rely on a technological solution for the challenge of collecting data from the product providers. From its inception it has used an in-house team who collect data from the product provider ‘‘to make sure data is shown in a common format and that it all works for the users”. The data is checked on a regular cycle and Moneynet have been applauded for their high level of accuracy in the sector (Foundation Resolution, 2007).

Outbound clicks – when a user clicks through an Apply button from Moneynet they are taken to a deep link in the provider’s site. This process involves the user being transferred directly to an application form which is populated with their data. If an outbound click then purchases a product from the provider Moneynet are paid a fee, for example £25 for a credit card.

Marketing and Sales – all comparison sites, have to appeal both to their users and the product providers who ultimately fund them. Initially some of the larger product providers attempted to obstruct what Moneynet were doing but the smaller players were very positive, as they felt that they could compete on a level playing field. The marketing and sales role combines business-to-business marketing, developing relationships with product providers, and consumer marketing, attracting visitors to the Moneynet website.

Service – an example of service given by Porter (1985) is product adjustment. Moneynet originally offered solely mortgage comparisons, but found that this was not something users wanted to transact online. In response to this other comparison tools were developed and The Lead Partnership was introduced, whereby users looking for mortgages online were referred to conventional financial advisors who would contact them for an appointment.

## 5.2. Support activities

Two of the secondary activities require some elaboration.

## 5.2.1. Procurement

As Moneynet are paid on the basis of outbound clicks, the purchase of inbound clicks is a fundamental aspect of their business. Two key areas paid inbound clicks come from are as follows:

\- Paid (sponsored) search – paying on a cost-per-click basis to appear alongside the organic search results on Google AdWords or through Yahoo and MSN’s paid search. There is a risk issue here as paid search is paid for irrespective of performance, whilst Moneynet’s commission revenue is based on performance (i.e. conversion).

\- Affiliates – affiliates are websites that are paid for generating relevant traffic. Moneynet generates about 5% of its traffic from such websites. Moneynet pays affiliates when an affiliate click through uses a Moneynet comparison and then clicks through to a product provider

## 5.2.2. Technology development

Technology development is also vital to comparison websites, as with all web based businesses. As noted Moneynet has focused on interpretation and understanding of data rather than technology solutions. However, effective monitoring of traffic (in particular paid traffic) – where it came from, activity on the site and where it goes to – is crucial for success. Improved functionality on the website, for example by offering web chats and forums, offer further examples of technology development.

## 5.3. External value systems

As noted above inbound clicks can come either directly to the site or by other means. It is possible to model these as a series of value chains, here termed click chains. This process fits the idea of the value chain well as the outputs from each stage, a more refined and higher value click, become the inputs for the next stage (see Fig. 2).

The following diagrams were presented to Richard Brown whose view was ‘‘Yes those are accurate ways of modelling it.”

## 5.3.1. Moneynet’s value systems

5.3.1.1. Direct traffic. 40% of Moneynet’s traffic comes direct to the website. A user coming direct is the ideal, for they incur no direct cost as is the case with paid traffic. To quote Richard Brown:

‘‘The problem is that financial services is such a competitive market that if you’ve got to pay for traffic it’s expensive. Competing against other comparison sites or the big product providers – for example credit card companies or loan companies.”

But, in addition to the cost issues, Richard Brown points out that ‘‘our best quality users are the ones that come direct”. To achieve such direct visitors the role of public relations has been crucial.

‘‘In those days it was really newsworthy – personal finance is always topical, and also the Internet was topical at the time.”

## 5.3.2. Search engines

30% of Moneynet’s traffic comes from search engines, which is mostly through organic (free) search as opposed to paid search. Organic search has been emphasised as the costs of paid search have increased.

Paid search has an appeal as it ‘‘is immediate, and you can control the volume. But it’s expensive to do”. Moneynet made extensive use of it when it first emerged in the UK, using it as a source of cheap traffic, which could then be sold on to the product provider after the user's needs had been refined

‘‘In those days you could make a lot of money using Google, because lots of people didn’t understand it, but we were one of the first to grasp it. Then, inevitably other people came in and bid the prices up.”

This increased demand included product providers bidding for terms using paid search, effectively disintermediating comparison websites.

![](/api/attachments/CEHKC7DZ/fulltext/images/a2008e204818af6a442d564218062469ce679bd62743ec856670ab142b9a4fc2.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Moneynet’s external value systems.

‘‘If you’re competing on Google for the term credit card, then you’re up against MBNA who if they get a customer will get several hundred pounds or more from the customer, whereas we’ll get 25 quid. So it’s hard to compete on the budgets they have.”

The resulting increased costs of inbound clicks via Google meant that such traffic has to be carefully tracked through the site to ensure money is not being wasted. Examples of costs per click can be viewed through Google’s estimator tool, with the term ‘‘credit card” having an average cost of £6.80 on September 4, 2008. It should be pointed out that whilst this seems a high figure, value will be added as the customer becomes more likely to convert and thus more valuable, with Moneynet paying £6.80 per click, charging £25 per conversion and, as noted in the quote above, the credit card provider making on average hundreds of pounds. These figures also show the importance of conversion for Moneynet as inbound clicks from Google AdWords with these costs and fees, requires a conversion rate of over 27% simply to break-even.

Moneynet can be seen here as transforming search queries which are generally broader in make-up. by users who are not sure of their requirements. Research by Ozmutlu et al., (2004) suggests a mean number of words per search of 2.5, which makes it hard to match segmented financial products with their target audience. In simple terms, a search for ‘‘credit card” or ‘‘low interest loan” may result in hundreds of potential products, but if the searcher is taken to the Moneynet website they can refine their requirements to find a more appropriate product.

## 5.3.3. Affiliates

Affiliates are third parties which refer users on to another website, in return for a fee either per click or per action. Affil iates operate in a number of ways, through a website, by sending emails or as search affiliates.

Affiliates have presented problems for many websites including Moneynet.

‘‘once you start using an affiliate site the quality of traffic is poor, this kills the conversion rates. You are paying for clicks and the conversion rates are really poor”

Affiliates are also notorious for what is known as click fraud, whereby click throughs are generated by the affiliate and their associates, for the sole reason of generating revenue, and not because they are interested in the website’s products.

## 5.4. The virtual value chain

In terms of the virtual value chain of Rayport and Sviokla these online value chains offer the ability to target customers more effectively and then offer them products which suit their needs. Possibly the most effective aspect for online marketing in general is the information which can be collected about visitors and used to make improvements. An interesting twist on the virtual value chain concept is that Moneynet moves activities back from the marketspace to the marketplace, that is, in the opposite direction to that observed by Rayport and Sviokla. Moneynet do this through their Lead Partnership initiative, explained earlier, as Richard Brown points out.

‘‘You could also go through Moneynet to a broker in the middle and then back to a provider (e.g. mortgages and life cover). Going online and offline.”

## 5.5. Alternative value configurations

This article will not consider the alternative value configurations of Stabell and Fjeldstad (1998) in any depth. However, the link with financial advisers (brokers) seen above can be seen as a link to a value shop style of operation. Indeed, comparison websites can be viewed as ‘‘self-service” financial advisers as they offer assistance in choosing products which are not viable, in terms of fees, for financial advisers to offer advice upon.

The value networks idea is also relevant in terms of connecting buyers and sellers of financial products, often through other networks such as search and affiliates. Affiliate networks exist to bring together those looking for traffic and the affiliate sites, whilst handling management issues and controlling the issue of click fraud.

## 6. Discussion

The amended version of the Porter model offers a useful method for analysing Moneynet, and in particular how it generates its margins. This process fits the idea of the value chain well as the outputs from each stage, a gradually more refined and higher value click, become the inputs for the next stage.

Some key lessons come from this article, both for Moneynet, and for the comparison sector at large.

## 6.1. Maximising non-paid traffic

The model shows the importance of maximising free traffic – direct traffic, in the form of new and repeat customers – through free links and search engines.

## 6.1.1. Brand awareness through public relations

The media’s constant coverage of financial services provides opportunities for coverage for any comparison website. They naturally complement features on personal finance and the regular features on ‘‘rip-offs” such as high charges. This coverage will lead some readers to visit the Moneynet website or enter it as a search term, a point developed below. Such online coverage has the added advantage of facilitating free click throughs and Moneynet has benefited from features in high traffic websites such as the BBC, The Times and The Guardian.

## 6.1.2. Organic search

An overlooked area with regard to search is public relations, for two key reasons. Firstly, raising of brand awareness makes it more likely a user will enter Moneynet’s name into a search engine rather than a product or industry term. This is crucial as Moneynet should appear at the top the results of a search for their name, whilst for product or industry related searches there is huge competition. Second, links achieved through public relations improves the Google PageRank<sup>TM</sup> of a page, with links from prestigious organisations such as the BBC being invaluable.

## 6.1.3. Loyalty

It is imperative that once a user visits the site they are retained. Whilst the ideal new user is through a free click, acquiring new users through paid means is acceptable, if these users can be retained over time. This has led Moneynet, and all comparison sites, to offer a wider service to its users.

‘‘People expect more than price comparisons, moving more towards education with people looking at our newsletters – these go out once a week on a topical issue offering warnings and tips on saving money.”

There is a certain irony in this focus on loyalty and retention given that the raison d’être of comparison websites is to encourage disloyalty by their users towards the product providers.

‘‘The danger for product providers is that people who use comparison sites are probably very price sensitive . . . . . . there is probably little loyalty, particularly for things like credit cards. Aggregators sites do encourage the mentality of switching, moving products around and just getting the best deal all the time.”

This is supported by the work of Kocas who states that ‘‘firms with a large percentage of loyal customers should never attempt to use price promotion to attract switchers” (2005, p. 129).

These key lessons are clearly relevant for the product providers, as they face the same challenges of attracting and retaining potential customers at low cost.

It is recognised that there are limitations to this model. For examples it assumes, implicitly, that users operate in a sequential method, following the value systems in Fig. 2. In reality of course, usage will vary with some users operating in this way and others in a different manner. For example, a user may come across Moneynet through a link, and then return directly at some later point or may view information on Moneynet identify a product, and visit a product provider independently at the same time or later, thus breaking the click chain as Moneynet lose their revenue stream

These limitations clearly identify the need for future research, analysing user behaviour and also looking at the views of product providers. However, as with all research this will depend on what levels of access are possible.

## 7. Conclusion

When Moneynet emerged in 1997 it was genuinely innovative and, in the words of Richard Brown, offered ‘‘a shining example of what the Web should be used for”. For users comparison sites provide the opportunity to scour the market and obtain value for money. Comparison sites are also clearly useful for product providers, but are inherently dangerous. Product providers fund comparison sites, but compete in the effort to control users and maximise non-paid traffic, precisely the aims of comparison sites.

This article has offered insights into Moneynet and the comparison sector by using the amended click chain model. The click chain model fits with the comments of Richard Brown and in particular on how users come to his company’s site and the costs and revenues incurred. The role of public relations in attracting users to websites and improving search engine rankings has been emphasised, an area which is worthy of further research.

As comparison websites become more high profile and possibly subject to regulation it is essential that the subject is researched in more detail so that all parties, consumers, comparison sites, product providers, regulators are fully aware of their operations and limitations.
