# Management of trust in the e-marketplace: the role of the buyer’s experience in building trust

## Abstract

The e-marketplace, or broker-managed online market, is one of today’s most profitable e-business models. In these marketplaces, buyers routinely engage with businesses and individual sellers with whom they have had little or no prior interaction, making trust one of the most important issues in the e-marketplaces. Therefore, a clear understanding of the trust building process in the e-marketplace is important for the success in the market. In this study, we analyze the process by which e-marketplace customers develop trust in both a market-maker and sellers. Additionally, this analysis provides a theoretical framework to identify the antecedents of trust. In the framework, we suggest that the impact of trust on transaction intention is moderated by the customer’s previous experiences in the e-marketplace. The theoretical model presented here was tested on survey and transaction data collected from 692 respondents. The results indicate that customers value the importance of trust in the market-maker and sellers differentially by the level of transaction experience. It is also shown that market-maker’s characteristics (reputation, website’s usability and security) and seller’s characteristics (expertise) play an important role in the formation and development of trust toward a market-maker and sellers, respectively.

## Introduction

he e-marketplace has emerged as an efficient and important vehicle for transactions in the e-commerce industry. An e-marketplace is a commercial site consisting of buyers and sellers who exchange product information and make transactions using the technology of the Internet (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). With the growth in popularity and the success of online auction marketplaces, the practical and academic interest in the e-marketplace is growing (Lee and Lee, 2000; Ba and Pavlou, 2002; Dellarocas, 2003). By bringing together numerous buyers and sellers and by managing transactions automatically, these online markets give buyers more options and allow sellers access to more potential customers (Kaplan and Sawhney, 2000). In this marketplace, a market-maker who is able to effectively manage participants, control the flow of information, and conduct business transactions on their own websites can earn vast revenues by extracting fees for the transactions occurring within their e-marketplaces. Typical examples of these marketplaces are online knowledge markets and online auction markets (Lee and Clark, 1997; Skyrme, 2001). In our study, we analyze the nature of trust in an e-marketplace, specifically, an online knowledge market.

The importance of trust as a key facilitator of electronic commerce is increasingly being recognized in academic and practitioner communities (Mishra, 1996; Davis et al., 1999; Hoffman et al., 1999; Bhattacherjee, 2002; Huang et al., 2003). Customer trust is essential to the success of transactions in the e-marketplace for two reasons. First, e-commerce often requires the sharing of sensitive personal, corporate, and financial information among transacting parties (Lee and Turban, 2001). Second, in the e-marketplace, buyers routinely transact with sellers with whom they have had little or no prior interaction (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). Unlike in online storefronts that are operated by one identifiable Internet merchant, in the e-marketplace buyers are perceived to be at greater risk of being exploited by opportunistic sellers (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). In fact, lack of trust is one of the most frequently cited reasons for customers not purchasing from the Internet (Lee and Turban, 2001; McKnight et al., 2002a). Therefore, fostering customer trust is an important research issue and a big challenge for online businesses (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; McKnight and Chervany, 2002; Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004; Kim and Ahn, 2006).

In the e-marketplace, a unique type of trust exists. Considering the structure of an e-marketplace, we can assume that a customer’s trust will be fostered by both the market-maker and the seller, unlike in online shopping malls or storefronts where trust is built solely by a seller who simultaneously acts as the market-maker. In e-marketplaces, market-makers are responsible for fostering institutional trust; they are expected to provide mechanisms to structure, organize, and legitimate the commercial activities that occur in their space (Bhargava et al., 2000). Marketmakers not only provide places for exchanges but also implement institutional rules to standardize and legitimate exchanges made within their facilities (Lee and Clark, 1997); sellers, on the other hand, are Internet vendors who are primarily responsible for the quality and timely delivery of the products or services they sell. While trust in the seller is still an important issue, trust in the e-marketplace is also built based on a third party, the market-maker. This type of trust is known as institution-based trust (Zucker, 1986; Shapiro, 1987). Institution-based trust is especially suited for e-marketplaces where buyers predominantly transact with new and unknown sellers under the aegis of third parties who provide an institutional facility for the transaction (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). Therefore, we can assume that buyers must trust both the market-maker and the seller before conducting a transaction in the e-marketplace as in Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) research.

In this study, our first objective is to empirically test whether the market-maker and the seller play equally significant roles in building buyer’s transaction intention in the e-marketplace or not. In our research model, we first assume that there are two targets of trust from the buyer’s perspective: the market-maker and the seller. We identify the factors that allow a customer to trust the market-maker and seller, and we assume that trust in these two targets affects transaction intention in the website. Second, we analyze the dynamics of these two targets of trust from the perspective of long-term relationships. Many researchers agree that trust is a dynamic concept that evolves over time (Ratnasingham, 1998). In the context of web-based commerce, gaining the initial trust is very important. Market-makers must rely on their websites’ features and business policies to prove to potential customers that they are trustworthy (Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004). Thus, market-makers play an important role in initiating trust, especially for customers new to e-marketplaces. On the other hand, more experienced customers may put more emphasis on the importance of trusting the seller than the market-maker, because their past experiences have made the purchasing process within the website familiar and comfortable to them. Therefore, in terms of the transaction intention, we expect that the dynamics of trust for both the market-maker and the seller will change over time as the customer becomes more experienced. Our study is, therefore, a further step in the research on trust following the direction of past research by Pavlou and Gefen (2004).

We offer several contributions to the emerging MIS and current marketing literature on online trust. First, we analyze trust in the context of Internet purchasing and focus specifically on the online knowledge market, which have yet to be well researched from this perspective. Second, we provide a richer understanding of trust by placing both targets of trust (the market-maker as well as the seller) in our research model.

The rest of this paper is as follows. First, we discuss the nature of trust. Subsequently, we explain a theoretical model that outlines the antecedents and consequences of trust in the e-marketplace. The next several sections explain the empirical study design to test hypothesized relationships, report analysis results, and discuss their implications for theory and practice. Finally, we conclude by outlining study limitations and present an agenda for future research.

## Literature review

The concept of trust has received a great deal of attention in various disciplines (Doney and Cannon, 1997; Bhattacherjee, 2002), including psychology (e.g. Lindskold, 1978), sociology (e.g. Strub and Priest, 1976; Lewis and Andrew, 1985), and marketing (e.g. Dwyer et al., 1987; Anderson and Barton, 1989; Ganesan, 1994). Each discipline has a unique perspective on the nature and definition of trust (Granovetter, 1985; Shapiro, 1987; Yamagishi and Yamagishi, 1994). Mayer et al. (1995: 712) proposed an integrative definition of trust as ‘the willingness of a party (trustor) to be vulnerable to the actions of another party (trustee) based on the expectation that the other (trustee) will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party (trustee).’

## Conceptualizing trust

There are at least two reasons for the confusion over the concept of trust. The first reason is that every discipline views trust from a unique perspective based on a specific research context, which often results in narrow conceptualizations that are tailored to specific research (McKnight and Chervany, 2002). The second reason is the multidimensional nature of trust (McKnight and Cummings, 1998; McKnight and Chervany, 2002; McKnight et al., 2002b). For example, past research on trust usually considers three dimensions, namely competence, benevolence, and integrity (Lee and Turban, 2001; McKnight and Chervany, 2002). However, in a transaction-related context, the most frequently cited trust dimensions are benevolence and integrity (e.g. Ganesan, 1994; Doney and Cannon, 1997; McKnight and Chervany, 2002; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004).

Considering the transaction-related e-marketplace context, the definition of trust proposed in our study reflects two dimensions, benevolence and integrity.

Benevolence is the extent to which a trustee is believed to intend doing good to the trustor, beyond its own profit motive (Mayer et al., 1995). Bhattacherjee (2002: 219) has argued that ‘In an e-commerce context, it may be difficult to anticipate consumer needs for designing benevolent services or it may be very expensive to deliver such services.’ Integrity refers to the trustor’s perception that the trustee will adhere to a set of principles or rules of exchange acceptable to the trustor during and after the exchange (Mayer et al., 1995). Bhattacherjee (2002: 219) summarizes this by saying, ‘In an e-commerce context, rules of integrity refer to: (1) completion of online transactions, (2) customer service policies following a transaction, and (3) firm’s use of customer’s private information.’ Online firms can foster integrity by stating their rules of transaction (such as, delivery policies, product return policies, and customer’s information privacy policies) on their websites, keeping customers informed of any change in rules, and abiding by and enforcing the rules they set (Bhattacherjee, 2002). In addition, objective integrity of a trading partner results from a partner’s competence and reliability (Lindskold, 1978; Ganesan, 1994; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). Therefore, this dimension encompasses the predictability, competence, and reliability over a partner’s expected behavior (Ganesan, 1994; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004).

From the multiplicity of trust conceptualizations in diverse disciplines, commonalities can be extracted. First, trust can be regarded as a general personality trait or as a domain-specific psychological state (Mayer et al., 1995; Bhattacherjee, 2002). Personality traits reflect stable intrinsic characteristics formed by developmental and social factors extraneous to a given context (Webster and Martocchio, 1992; Bhattacherjee, 2002). In contrast, psychological states are affective states that vary with situational conditions and can be affected by the person’s interaction with a situation (Bhattacherjee, 2002). Second, it is commonly agreed that a multidimensional view of trust can best capture the nature of the trust construct (Bhattacherjee, 2002). As noted previously, prior studies have revealed several related but distinct dimensions of trust, including benevolence, honesty, faith, dependability, predictability, fairness, confidence, security, hope, openness, integrity, and promise fulfillment (McKnight and Chervany, 2002).

In this paper, we regard trust as a multidimensional concept and define it as ‘the perceived benevolence and integrity of a participating party in the transaction.’ Perceived benevolence refers to the extent to which one partner is genuinely interested in the other partner’s welfare and motivated to seek joint gain (Doney and Cannon, 1997), while integrity focuses on the objective credibility of a trading partner based on product or service quality or other customers’ evaluations of the exchange partner.

## Online trust

Regarding research on online trust, the majority of studies focus on the short-term and transactional side of e-commerce, and fail to examine the formation of trust as a process from the perspective of long-term relationship building (Papadopoulou et al., 2001). To understand how trust is formulated and cultivated, our review of the literature on trust in online stores identified several studies that provide the necessary theoretical background to aid our endeavors towards filling this gap.

Mcknight and Chervany (2002) provided a conceptual typology on e-commerce distinguished from the traditional trust construct in physical markets through interdisciplinary analysis. In their research, they justified a parsimonious interdisciplinary typology and related trust constructs to e-commerce customer actions, defining both conceptuallevel and operational-level trust constructs. They argued that conceptual-level constructs consist of disposition to trust, institution-based trust, trusting beliefs, trusting intentions, and trusting behaviors. And the link among trusting beliefs, trusting intentions, and trusting behaviors is natural because their model is based on the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975).

According to Tan and Thoen (2001), model of trust for ecommerce consists of two basic components, party trust and control trust, based on the concept that trust in a transaction with another party combines trust in the party and trust in the control mechanisms that ensure the successful performance of the transaction. In addition, there are also some studies to identify key factors affecting trust in an online firm or online store. Jarvenpaa et al. (2000) found that customers recognized differences in size and reputation among Internet stores, and those differences influenced their assessments of store trustworthiness and their perception of risk, as well as willingness to patronize the store. Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa (2004) suggested that perceived company reputation, willingness to customize products and services, perceived web site usefulness, ease of use, and security control can significantly affect initial trust. They defined initial trust as a ‘willingness to rely on a third party after the first interaction with that party.’ Gefen (2000) found that familiarity is a precondition for online trust.

Regarding the research on TAM (Technology Acceptance Model) and trust, Gefen et al. (2003) suggested an integrated model using the widely accepted factors in studies on TAM, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, and trust. Pavlou (2003) developed a model with perceived risk as well as TAM and trust. He found a mediating role of perceived risk between trust and intention to transact.

## Developing trust

Trust is a complex, multifaceted, and dynamic concept because relationships are rarely completely static and the levels of trust may change and develop over time even within the same relationship (Lewicki and Bunker, 1996). Although most researchers agree that trust can be developed and changed over time through repeated experiences, only a few researchers have attempted to conduct a research on the trust development processes.

Shapiro et al. (1992) have suggested a three-staged model for trust development. According to their research, there are three forms of trust: deterrence/calculus-based trust, knowledge-based trust, and identification-based trust.

These forms are interdependent as one form of trust leads to the next.

Doney and Cannon (1997) maintained that the development of trust is based on the formation of a trustor’s expectations about the motives and behaviors of a trustee. Their work (1997), which integrated theories from several distinct disciplines, identified five trust-building processes through which industrial buyers can develop trust in a supplier firm and its salespeople: the calculative process, prediction process, capability process, intentionality process, and transference process.

## Research model and hypotheses

Trust development processes provide insight into how trust in a market-maker and seller are formed and affect the transaction intention in the e-marketplace. Market-makers provide a source of transaction intention, namely the institutional trust factor, through their transaction policies, payment infrastructure, and website content and layout (McKnight and Chervany, 2002). However, the marketmaker cannot fully guarantee the quality and delivery of the product or service, which is the seller’s responsibility, although some risk related to the seller is mitigated by institutional facilities such as seller’s credit system and escrow service. Therefore, besides the institutional factors provided by the market-maker, the seller provides another important source of trust for the transaction in the e-marketplace: the interpersonal trust factors (McKnight and Chervany, 2002). In this regard, we posit that, ideally, buyers must trust both the seller and market-maker to some extent before they purchase a product from an e-marketplace while in the Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) research, they hypothesized that buyer’s transaction intention is affected by trust in the community of sellers and risk from the community of sellers. In our study, we are focused on the effects of these two trust targets on transaction intention. Because these two targets of trust consist of both institutional and interpersonal trust factors, the impact on the transaction intention is likely to differ with varying degree of customer’s past transactional experience in the e-marketplace.

Figure 1 represents a conceptual model of this study. Hypothesized antecedents to trust represent characteristics of the market-maker and seller, which provide a clue for buyer to infer trust. Then, the trust in the market-maker and seller leads the buyer to form a transaction intention. Detailed descriptions of the constructs of this model are described and research hypotheses underlying our theoretical model are proposed in the following.

Antecedents of trust in a market-maker: characteristics of the market-maker

In our research, we considered company reputation, web usability, and web security as antecedents to the buyer’s trust in a market-maker. Reputation is an aspect of the actual market-maker, while web usability and security are related to characteristics of the market-maker’s website.

## Company reputation

Customer trust can increase significantly when the company is perceived to have a good reputation (Doney and Cannon, 1997).

## Web usability

Web usability is a set of website design principles, including consistency of the interface, response time, and interaction style (Nielsen, 1993). We regard ease of use, searching function, and site navigational structure as aspects of web usability in our study. A well-designed website can be one of the company’s competences (Roy et al., 2001), and this makes customers believe that a company has the capabilities to fulfill its promises (Chow and Holden, 1997). Therefore, a well-designed website that is useful and userfriendly requires certain resources and capabilities on the part of the company (Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004).

## Web security

A major concern of online customers is how secure their transactions are on the web. Customers expect that an online company’s website will provide methods for secure exchange of financial information (Ranganathan and

## Antecedents of trust in sellers: characteristics of the seller

In the traditional dyadic (one-to-one) sense, trust has been defined as a belief that the seller will behave according to the consumer’s expectations by showing integrity and benevolence (Luhmann, 1979; Doney and Cannon, 1997; Gefen, 2002; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). While there is a consensus that dyadic trust promotes online transactions, trust in an entire group of sellers (one-to-many) in an electronic marketplace has been studied only infrequently (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). In this paper, we focus on the concept of trusting a group of sellers in a specific e-marketplace, as opposed to a single seller as in Pavlou and Gefen (2004). Pavlou and Gefen theorized about ‘trust in generalized others,’ in which the target of trust could be either an individual or a group (Rotter, 1967) and considered a way such trust in a group forms people’s evaluation, beliefs, and behavior. They noted that ‘the nature of e-marketplaces makes one-to-many trust deserving of special attention’ (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004: 40). In contrast to Mayer et al.’s (1995) focus on a person’s disposition to trust others, they focused on trust in an identifiable population of a specific marketplace.

Pavlou and Gefen (2004) maintained in their research that the characteristics of sellers with whom a buyer has a history of transactions will affect the buyer’s trust in a community of sellers. In our study, we use the same logic. In other words, the buyer’s overall opinion on the expertise and reputation of sellers from prior interactions is related to his or her trust in a whole group of sellers in the e-marketplace.

## Expertise

Many researchers maintain that a salesperson’s expertise plays an important role in building consumer trust. Busch and Wilson (1976) found that customers view salespersons with higher levels of perceived expertise as more trustworthy. Crosby et al. (1990) showed that the perceived expertise of an insurance salesperson is a significant predictor of customer trust. In other words, the higher the level of expertise a seller has, the higher the level of trust a customer will have in that seller.

## Reputation

As noted in our discussion of the market-maker, reputation of the transaction counterpart is positively associated with trust in that partner. As evidenced by the successful reputation-feedback systems used by eBay, the positive impact of reputation on customer trust in online marketplaces has been reported (Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004).

Therefore, hypotheses for this group of factors are:

H<sub>4</sub>: Seller’s expertise is positively related to trust in sellers.

H<sub>5</sub>: Seller’s reputation is positively related to trust in sellers.

Propensity to trust and trust in a market-maker and sellers

## Propensity to trust

Another factor that will affect the trust a customer has involves the customer’s inherent personality trait. Some people are simply more likely to trust than others (Mayer et al., 1995), and the propensity to trust is a personal trait that influences trustworthiness of the trustor. In this regard, we can assume that people who have a higher propensity to trust tend to have more trust.

Therefore, hypotheses for this group of factors are:

H : Propensity to trust is positively related to trust in a market-maker.

H<sub>7</sub>: Propensity to trust is positively related to trust in sellers.

## Trust and transaction intention

Regarding the relationship between trust in a market-maker and trust in sellers, market-makers identify and remove problematic sellers or take legal steps to protect buyers from fraudulent sellers (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). In addition, market-makers provide information of sellers to help buyers in judging the trust in an individual seller. Hence, trust in sellers is partially attributable to the trust in a market-maker, because sellers should abide by the rules and policies of the market-maker in order to sell their products in their space. Therefore, when a buyer decides to purchase a product, trust in sellers can be inferred on the basis of perceptions of the market-maker’s trustworthiness (cf. Doney and Cannon, 1997; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004).

We define the transaction intention as the extent to which a buyer has a willingness to engage in online exchange relationships (cf. Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). According to the TRA (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975), attitude is determined by an antecedent belief. In our study, trust in both a market-maker and sellers can be viewed as a belief and transaction intention can be regarded as an attitude toward transaction behavior. McKnight and Chervany (2002) and Pavlou and Gefen (2004) showed that trusting belief in specific vendor affects transaction intention in the online context using the same logic. In the e-marketplaces, buyers are faced with uncertainty, not knowing what the trading partner will do (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). Pavlou and Gefen (2004) explained that ‘In this situation, trust can be one of the uncertainty reduction mechanisms.’ In this regard, we assume that trust in both a market-maker and sellers influence transaction intention positively.

Therefore, hypotheses for this group of factors are:

$\operatorname { H } _ { 8 } \colon$ Trust in a market-maker is positively related to trust in sellers.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 9 } \colon$ Trust in a market-maker is positively related to transaction intention.

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 0 } } \mathbf { : }$ Trust in sellers is positively related to transaction intention.

## The role of transactional experience

Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) and Tan and Thoen’s (2001) research have examined customer’s trust in the e-marketplace considering both the role of market-maker and seller. In their trust models for electronic commerce, they suggested that the trustworthiness of the other party in the transaction, the Internet merchant, and the reliability of the control mechanisms are main factors of transaction intention. The term ‘control mechanism’ designates procedures and protocols that monitor and control the successful performance of a transaction, activities that are the responsibility of the market-maker. In a similar research context, Lee and Turban (2001) investigated the main antecedents influencing consumer trust in Internet shopping, including the trustworthiness of the Internet merchant and trustworthiness of the Internet as a shopping medium, as well as other factors. Likewise, we hypothesize that the transaction intention from the buyer’s perspective is affected by both trust in the market-maker as well as trust in sellers and these are further moderated by the customer’s transactional experience.

Trust is a dynamic concept that evolves over time. Customers will form initial opinions of the transactions in the e-marketplace and may change their opinions with further information or experience. Because customers’ initial trust can be influenced by the characteristics of the website (Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004), trust in the initial stages of transactional experience in the e-marketplace will be influenced by such things as ease of use and web security.

However, as customers gain more experience in the e-marketplace, they will be less likely than before to base their opinions on factors involving institution-based trust. In other words, as the familiarity with the transaction process increases in a certain website, customers with a lot of transactional experience are apt to focus less on the trustworthiness of the market-maker and therefore will care less about factors like transaction policy, transaction process, and payment security. On the other hand, the impact of trust in sellers on transaction intention will be stronger. Whenever customers buy a product or service in the e-marketplace, they evaluate sellers in terms of credibility, reliability, and the quality of product/service. Therefore, for a customer who has already successfully completed a number of transactions, trust in sellers may have a stronger influence on the buyer’s transaction intention than trust in the market-maker.

Therefore, hypotheses for this group of factors are:

H<sub>11</sub>: As a buyer gains experience in the e-marketplace, the impact of trust in a market-maker on transaction intention will be less.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 1 2 } \colon$ As a buyer gains experience in the e-marketplace, the impact of trust in sellers on transaction intention will be greater.

## Method

## Sample and data collection

The instruments in this study were developed based on the existing literature and on the results of the prior interviews with Internet users who have had transactional experiences in the e-marketplace. To collect data, an online knowledge market was chosen as the subject of the study.

Two types of data were used in the analysis of our study: survey data and transactional experience data of survey participants. After conducting an online survey, we traced the participants of the survey using the buyer’s ID in the website to extract that buyer’s transactional data from the database of Company $\mathbf { A } ,$ a leading company of the online knowledge market in South Korea. The transactional experience data were measured by the accumulated number of transactions from customer database in the company. Incidentally, Swan and Nolan (1985) proposed that buyer’s experience can be measured by the number of orders placed and filled.

## Online knowledge market

A knowledge market is a place where knowledge is traded. In many fields, knowledge trading is already an established activity. There are vibrant content businesses, like publishing and broadcasting, as well as people-based businesses, like management consulting and recruitment agencies. For some of these businesses, there are ‘active markets’ where buyers and sellers can go to trade. There are also markets for intellectual property, such as copyrights and patents. According to Kafentzis et al. (2004), the growth of the knowledge market is in line with the previous prediction of analysts. Although online knowledge markets are not as developed as those for conventional products and services, many are emerging as important places to seek out and trade various kinds of knowledge (Skyrme, 2001).

Our survey was conducted with the customers of Company A whose annual sales grew \$1.2 million in 2002 to \$6 million in 2005. Currently, there are over 2 million users in this company’s website. Company A is a kind of online knowledge shop that sells all kinds of written materials, from papers for university students to professional reports for researchers. Buyers and sellers are mainly university students. The process of a transaction is as follows. Sellers upload their documents (mainly MS Wordt and PowerPointt files) with detailed information (title, abstract, contents, category of the subject, keywords, references, number of pages) and their prices. The price of a document is approximately 1–5 US dollars. Potential buyers search products based on their interests using the search engine in the website. Then, they evaluate the products and decide what to buy based on the product descriptions, prices, and other information including the evaluations of previous buyers and the seller’s level (seller’s level was graded by the market-maker based on the evaluations of previous buyers and the number of sold products). After the buyers decide to purchase and pay the price through the payment system using mobile phone or credit card, they download the file and the market-maker, or Company A in this case, transfers to the corresponding seller the sales revenue minus its transaction fee.

## Survey

For the survey, a questionnaire was developed in two phases using the seven-point Likert scale ranging from $\cdot _ { 1 } \cdot$ strongly disagree to $\mathfrak { e } _ { 7 } ,$ strongly agree. In the first phase, a set of items was created based on a review of previous literature and on interviews with buyers experienced in the online knowledge market. Then, a pretest survey was conducted with 100 of Company A’s customers through the online survey system in its website. In the second phase, survey items were refined based on comments from the pretest survey. Finally, an online survey was conducted on Company A’s website for its users.

Generally, online surveys have two major problems (Korack-Kakabadse and Korack-Kakabadse, 1999). The first problem is related to the representativeness of the sample; in this case, the survey is confined to Internet users, but this is not problematic because the research context here is an e-marketplace on the Internet. The second problem is the difficulty of applying a reward scheme of the offline survey to the online survey. To resolve this problem, Company A offered respondents a reward of 1000 points (cyber money), which can be used to purchase products on its website, for participating in the survey. Since other surveys on the same site offered only between 50 and 100 points, the 1000 points was considered a sufficient reward. Of the 1000 samples, 269 respondents who had no prior experience of making purchases from an online knowledge market were removed, after referring to Company A’s customer database. Thirty-nine samples with inconsistent and missing value were also removed. The remaining 692 samples were used in data analysis.

## Characteristics of the sample

Respondents were primarily in their 20s and 30s (90% and 7%) reflecting the fact that younger Internet users are the main customers of the online knowledge market. In terms of gender, 51% was male and 49% was female. In addition, respondents had joined Company A, on average, 1.5 years before participating in our survey. The mean and median of the number of customer transactions was 10.4 and 7.0, respectively.

## Results

## Measure development and validation

We developed multi-item scales based on a review of the relevant literature and on interviews with practitioners and buyers. Statistical procedures used to validate the measures included scale reliability, unidimensionality, and convergent validity. We analyzed them using LISREL 8.20 and SPSS 10.0. Before we performed confirmatory factor analysis, principal component factor analysis using the varimax rotation approach was conducted to verify the concept validity of the measurements. From the exploratory factor analysis, factor loadings of each factor were higher than 0.5 except one measurement item, market-maker’s reputation 3 which is shown in Table 1.

## Results of the confirmatory factor analysis

We evaluated the measures through confirmatory factor analysis procedures. Although the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistics was statistically significant $( \chi _ { ( 4 1 9 ) } ^ { 2 } = 1 1 0 \bar { 6 . } 6 1 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , this is not unusual in large sample sizes (Doney and Cannon, 1997; Jarvenpaa et $\bar { a l . } ,$ 2000). The goodness of fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and incremental fit index (IFI) were 0.91, 0.93 and 0.93, respectively. Root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was 0.05. Table 2 shows the results of confirmatory factor analysis and Cronbach’s a. Together, the results suggest that these data provide a good fit with the hypothesized measurement model.

Trust in a market-maker captures the faith in the marketmaker. Items were generated by literature review and interviews with experienced buyers. The measure of trust in a market-maker exhibited high reliability (Cronbach’s a ¼ 0.82) and factor loadings of observation variables all exceeded 0.78. We also modified scales to measure the market-maker’s reputation, web usability, and web security based on past research (e.g. Doney and Cannon, 1997; Jarvenpaa et $a l . ,$ 2000; Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004). These measures exhibited good reliability, with the lowest value of Cronbach’s a being 0.71, for the market-maker’s reputation. Transaction intention is composed of four items. These were adopted from Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) and Jarvenpaa et al.’s (2000) research. This four-item scale exhibited high reliability (Cronbach’s a ¼ 0.83). Factor loadings of observation variables were all over 0.85. Therefore, the measures are well explained by the construct, transaction intention.

From past research, the measures used for evaluating trust in a salesperson were adopted for the e-marketplace context of our study. The four-item scale of trust in sellers exhibited high reliability (Cronbach’s a ¼ 0.81). Two constructs, seller’s expertise and reputation, were hypothesized to influence the trust in sellers. Because little empirical research has been conducted to examine these factors in the e-marketplace, new scales for these antecedents were developed in this study. Measurement items were generated using interviews with experienced buyers and practitioners in Company A. The measures exhibited good reliability, with Cronbach’s a values being 0.66 for sellers’ reputation and 0.72 for sellers’ expertise. In addition, we also measured propensity to trust based on the literature (e.g. Lee and Turban, 2001). The values of Cronbach’s a were 0.81 and factor loadings exceeded 0.77.

## Antecedents of trust in a market-maker and sellers

Three of the four hypotheses tested regarding the antecedents to market-maker trust were supported. As expected, market-maker’s reputation (0.208; $\hbar < 0 . 0 5 )$ usability (0.229; $\hbar < . 0 5 )$ , and security (0.456; po0.05) of market-maker’s website had a positive impact on the customer’s trust in the market-maker. However, the customer’s propensity to trust (0.037) was found to be unrelated to trust in a market-maker. Therefore, $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { H } _ { 2 } ,$ and H were supported while $\mathrm { H } _ { 6 }$ was not. Regarding the characteristics of sellers, sellers’ expertise (0.258; $\hbar < 0 . 0 5 )$ was positively related to the trust in sellers while reputation (0.079) was not. In addition, propensity to trust (0.093; po0.05) and trust in a market-maker (0.620; po0.05) had a positive effect on trust in sellers. Thus, H , H , $\mathrm { H } _ { 8 }$ were supported while $\mathrm { H } _ { 5 }$ was not.

## Moderating effect of customer’s experience

Before analyzing the moderating effect of buyer’s experience on transaction intention, we examined the relationship between trust in both a market-maker and sellers and transaction intention. As we expected, trust in a marketmaker (0.445; $\hbar < 0 . 0 5 )$ and trust in sellers (0.217; po0.05) were positively related to transaction intention. Therefore, both ${ \bar { \mathrm { H } } } _ { 9 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 }$ were supported.

To test hypotheses 11 and 12, we conducted the moderated regression analysis (Atuahene-Gima and Li, 2000). The procedure is as follows: First, the overall significance of a model comprising all antecedents and moderators is evaluated. Then, we add the interaction terms to the regression model and examine the increase in $R ^ { 2 } .$ If the increase in $R ^ { 2 }$ is significant, it indicates the presence of the moderating effect. When the moderator is transactional experience, we found that addition of the interaction terms with the antecedents to the regression equation yielded a significant increase in $R ^ { 2 } ( \dot { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which supported hypotheses $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 1 }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 2 }$ (Table 4).

## Discussion

The results from this study provide strong support for the framework presented in Figure 1. Specifically, we confirm that online customers in the e-marketplace develop trust qin the market-maker and in sellers. Therefore, when they purchase a product or service, we can assume that they consider the trustworthiness of both the market-maker and the seller. Another interesting observation in this study is about the dynamics of trust for the market-maker and sellers. The impact of both trust in the market-maker and sellers on transaction intention are moderated by the customer’s experience, that is, by the number of completed transactions they have made in the past.

Although there have been a few studies on the trust-building process, they provided only a theoretical foundation and did not test the existence of these processes through empirical studies. In our study, we confirmed that transaction intention stems from the market-maker as well as from the seller. In addition, our analysis results showed that the impact of trust in a market-maker and sellers on the transaction intention is moderated by the customer’s past experience in the e-marketplace. Second, this research extends trust-related research to a new context, the online knowledge market. Some of our results confirm previous empirical research, but others do not correspond with prior findings. Previous empirical research primarily has been conducted for offline channels of distribution (e.g. Anderson and Barton, 1989; Ganesan, 1994; Kumar et al., 1995), online auction sites (e.g. Tan and Thoen, 2001; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004), and Internet stores (e.g. Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Lee and Turban, 2001; Koufaris and Hampton-Sosa, 2004). Contrary to prior findings, the buyer’s propensity to trust was found to be unrelated to trust in the market-maker. ## Managerial implications

We believe that this study has several practical implications regarding ways for online market-makers and Internet merchants to foster consumer trust. One of the key implications of our study is how to manage trust in the market-maker. Regarding this, our results show that buyers use the reputation of the company, web usability, and security as a basis for their trust in a market-maker. It means that buyers bestow trust by judging the reputation, usefulness, and security of websites upon market-makers. Therefore, it is important for the market-maker to manage customer perceptions of website reputation, usefulness, and security – perhaps through clever advertising and publicity. This suggests that the market-maker should develop, maintain, and update the website constantly to make it a safe and reliable place for transactions.

Second, the market-maker needs to understand how trust in sellers is developed. The impact of the trust in sellers on the transaction intention was moderated by the experience of the customer. The more experience the buyer has, the more the buyer focuses on the seller. Our study indicates that buyers judge sellers who have expertise in their field to be trustworthy. A seller’s expertise can bolster the buyer’s confidence in predicting the seller’s future behavior. In this regard, market-makers can encourage the development of trust in the e-marketplace by providing the customers with information on the sellers’ credibility as well as information on their product or service. Sufficient information will help a buyer to evaluate the feasibility of transacting with the sellers.

Third, trust in the market-maker is positively related to trust in sellers. This indicates that trust in sellers is partly based on encounters with a market-maker in the e-marketplace. Therefore, market-makers should develop trust in a website and enhance their images through promotions to increase trust in sellers of their own sites. Finally, sellers should be rewarded for their important roles of strengthening the link between the market-maker and the buyer. Granting a low transaction fee to sellers with high evaluations is an example.

## Limitations and further research

Early studies in trust in online environments suffer from many problems typical of empirical research: measures, sample, procedures, and so on (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000). This is not an exception in our study. First, our study focused only one website: an online knowledge market. Researching a single e-marketplace or online knowledge market raises questions of external validity and generalizability, and the extent to which the observations are applicable to a broader set of e-marketplaces is unclear. Additionally, we did not formulate a hypothesis in terms of the company size, because none of these prior studies examined this variable with one website. Regarding the size of a company, many researchers suggested that a perceived company size contributes to a consumer’s impression of the company’s trustworthiness (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000). Therefore, we regarded the company size as a control variable in our study. We wanted to analyze the impact of the perceived company size on the trust in a market-maker. The perceived size of a company was unrelated to the trust in a market-maker. This may result from the research method, research on one website. However, we expect that research on online knowledge market will provide a better understanding of issues in the e-marketplace, especially trust.

Further research is needed to consider additional important variables such as risk and satisfaction in this research framework. Additionally, the analysis of other emarketplaces including auction sites and B2B sites is also required in the future, especially where risk is interwoven with trust (McAllister, 1995). Therefore, by analyzing these, we can have a better understanding of how customer’s trust grows and declines in the e-marketplace.

Second, we measured trust in both a market-maker and sellers as just two dimensions, whereas many prior studies by other researchers have demonstrated three dimensions of trust. Even if our construct of integrity overlaps with competence in this study, it is needed to conceptualize trust as three correlated dimensions in the further study. Regarding the survey method, we used an online survey. Although the online survey is a major survey method in empirical research, it still has several problems, as noted previously: the representativeness of the sample, the reward schemes for participation, the absence of monitoring, and so on. Some researchers have argued that the online survey should be used with other survey methods, such as telephone interviews and focus group interview. However, because of the research context and cyber money reward system used in our study, the two major problems faced by online surveys are partly addressed. In addition, we designed our survey for experienced buyers in the online knowledge market. However, it is very important to understand the role of trust and its antecedents on the transaction intention of a buyer who had not previously made purchases in online marketplaces. Therefore, research needs to be conducted on the comparison of the role and sources of trust in the e-marketplace from the perspectives of experienced buyers and non-experienced buyers.

## Conclusion

Trust is an important issue in the e-marketplace. The successful trading relationship is characterized by the higher trust and cooperation (Nakayama, 2000). Buyers are often uneasy about conducting transactions with unknown sellers through the online functions provided by a market-maker. This can affect whether they make a purchase or not in the e-marketplace. However, the majority of past studies on trust, which have used Internet stores as their context, fail to examine the formation of trust as a process in the perspective of the long-term relationship by emphasizing the short-term and transactional side of e-commerce (Papadopoulou et al., 2001). The objective of this paper is to fill the gap by focusing on analyzing the buyer’s trust in the e-marketplace.

Our analysis resulted in two key findings. First, in the e-marketplace, buyers build trust in both the market-maker and the sellers, in contrast to an online shopping mall in which trust in a seller is paramount. In addition, antecedents of trust in the market-maker and sellers are identified separately. Second, it is noted that the impact of trust in the market-maker and sellers on the transaction intention changes with the duration of the relationship. For less experienced buyers in the e-marketplace, their transaction intention is heavily influenced by the degree of the trust in a market-maker. For more experienced buyers, their transaction intention is more affected by the level of trust in sellers than a market-maker.

In this regard, a market-maker has to make a commitment to continuously develop and maintain customer trust. In addition to guaranteeing the successful completion of the transaction process, the market-maker should also qualify the seller’s trustworthiness partly. Although the process of building customer trust is expensive and complex (Doney and Cannon, 1997), enhancing trust will be a critical factor for success in the e-marketplace.
