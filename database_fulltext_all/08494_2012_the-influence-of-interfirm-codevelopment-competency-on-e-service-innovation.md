# The influence of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation

Hung-Tai Tsou <sup>a,1</sup>, Ja-Shen Chen <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Global Marketing and Logistics, Ming Dao University 369, Wen-Hua Rd., Peetow, Chang-Hua 52345, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> College of Management, Yuan Ze University 135 Yuan-Tung Road, Chung-Li 32003, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 10 February 2011 Accepted 24 February 2012 Available online 13 April 2012

Keywords: e-Service innovation Interfirm codevelopment competency Partner match Knowledge integration mechanisms Technology integration mechanisms

## A B S T R A C T

Because of customer demand, rapid product life cycles, and advances in IT, an expanded view of e-service innovation is needed. Our study used concepts from resource dependence and contingency theories to examine the mediating effects of knowledge and technology integration mechanisms (KIMs and TIMs) on interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation, as well as investigating the moderating effect of them on partner match. A field survey was conducted in IT departments of financial and information service firms; we found that companies in these industries emphasized interfirm codevelopment competency in developing e-service innovation but used different types of mechanisms to enhance e-service innovation, which can be viewed as a practice of embodying partner match in interfirm codevelopment competency. We concluded that IT managers should mobilize interfirm codevelopment competency in conjunction with KIM and TIM to achieve e-service innovation. Furthermore, selecting compatibility of partners is crucial in enhancing e-service innovation. We discuss the theoretical and managerial implications of the findings and conclude with comments on limitations in our study.

## 1. Introduction

Today, many firms are concentrating their efforts on service issues in their industry, but are finding that when services become homogeneous, competitive advantage is almost impossible to sustain. Thus they must be sensitive to change and encourage innovation. However, new technology (such as e-commerce) has allowed the range of service options to expand considerably. More demanding and innovative customers demand high quality and efficient operations, which must be delivered more cost effectively. Thus it is no longer sufficient to rebuild organizational structures and reallocate service resources; firms must be open to codeveloping new services with external customers/partners.

A number of studies have attempted to identify the factors crucial to service innovation [1,7,8,14,23,33]. However, we focused on e-service innovation in terms of interfirm codevelopment, which introduces new products and services or improves the quality of existing products and services [4,15]. Firms achieve collaborative behavior by selecting partners, codifying workflow, and providing IT tools that facilitate collaboration [13]. However, relatively little research has focused on identifying the collaboration competency that results from innovative practices.

Swan et al. [32] suggested that there are specific mechanisms through which an organization is likely toadopt innovative practices. However, we argue that the literature is lacking in two ways. First, ‘‘integration’’ is conducive to innovation but existing literature has mainly considered knowledge integration as a mediator of innovation [11,12,25,30]. Yet research on the role and consequences of KIMs in a service innovation context is limited. Second, studies have empirically identified the enabler role of technology integration in achieving successful innovation practices [2,31]. We identify these as technology integration mechanisms (TIMs) and focused on their mediator role in a service innovation context.

KIMs have been regarded as a moderator between internal and external capabilities and innovation outcomes. Similarly, e-service innovation is the product of the fusion of electronic technologies and novel service methods. However, to be combined effectively, each of these resources and capabilities must be integrated to match the other firms codevelopment technical platform. This is the focus of the TIM moderator.

KIMs and TIMs act as both mediators and moderators of eservice innovation but their mediating role in interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation remains a mystery. In our model, based on the work of Deck and Strom, ‘‘partner selection’’ was modified to partner match and ‘‘IT tools’’ was changed to TIM, while ‘‘processes’’ became e-service innovation. Fig. 1 shows our theoretical model. The main purpose of our study was to examine the mediating and moderating effects of KIMs and TIMs between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

## 2.1. e-Service innovation

Service innovation can be regarded as the set of improvements in service processes or service-logic innovation. It may be regarded as the development of new processes for the delivery of core products and services [26]. This requires partnering and cannot be realized by an individual organization. Hinnant and O’Looney [20] used institutional and environmental factors to develop a model of e-innovation to characterize it in terms of perceived need, technical capacity, and risk mitigation. We therefore borrowed from Eisingerich et al. and Agarwal and Selen to define e-service innovation as a service process innovation that responds to the needs of customers and is created by a service provider using technical capabilities involving interaction with partners through electronic technologies to reduce service production risk.

## 2.2. Interfirm codevelopment competency

Deck and Strom’s codevelopment model identified a set of integrated practices on three levels: a strategy for developing chain design, process, and governance structures that define how the partners work together, and IT that supports collaborative development. We define codevelopment as cooperation between two or more firms to either introduce new products or improve the quality of existing product lines. Codevelopment includes knowledge accumulation, product specifications, strategic decisions, and supplier involvement. We define interfirm codevelopment competency as the ability to perform cooperative activities between firms concerned with exchange, sharing, developing, or improving products, services, or technologies for mutual benefit. In our research, based on [16], we use the term interfirm codevelopment competency to cover three areas: absorptive capacity (the capability of a firm to assimilate, identify, transform, and make use of new information from the environment, especially its partners [19]), coordination capability (ability to build a knowledge-intensive interface with other firms and organizations), and relational capability (enhancing knowledge exchange across boundaries [24], bringing together various sources of expertise, and increasing lateral interaction [22]). Thus we believe that interfirm absorptive capacity requires collaborative partners that have sufficient knowledge to facilitate sharing and learning and thus provide value. Coordination capabilities are mainly conceived as internal: relating to tasks and activities within the firm. The essence of relational capability is the integration of domainspecific and tacit knowledge with that of external partners. Firms with high quality relational capability are likely to have strong relationships with their partners.

## 2.3. KIMs

These are the formal processes and structures that ensure the capture, analysis, interpretation, and integration of knowledge among various functional units in a firm. KIMs operate according to four mechanisms (i.e., exchange, direction, socialization, and internalization) and focus on the integration of knowledge rather than the conversion of tacit to explicit knowledge or vice versa [29].

## 2.4. TIMs

Collaboration between two firms is required to achieve integration of IT platforms within or across organizational boundaries. Technology integration is required to establish data consistency and high-speed transfer of information within and across the organizations’ boundaries. Integration among backoffice IS and internal databases and those in the partners’ domain are essential [35]. When firms wish to make a connection between employees, suppliers, customers, and partners, they must integrate and translate operational data into a consistent form. An effective means of achieving technology integration is to coordinate the applications of interdependent IT and EDI processes.

## 2.5. Interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation

Resource dependence theory (RDT) explains how an organization’s strategy, structure, and survival depend on its organizational behavior and interorganizational dependency. In a business-tobusiness context, firms own resources that affect their partners survival. Because organizations are rarely self-sufficient, they enter into collaborative relationships with others to obtain critical resources. Companies struggle to find a balance between what they must own and what they must achieve through collaboration, partnering, alliances, joint ventures, etc. [34]. According to RDT, interfirm collaborations allow a firm to secure access to new resources which positively affect innovation [21]. Such relationships are generally designed to produce some form of innovation. We propose that new process design, new process technologies, and new process rules often arise in interfirm collaboration and thus are related to service innovation. However, although collaboration plays an important role in improving organizational processes, its role in achieving service innovation with nonelectronic goods is central. Accordingly, we hypothesized:

H1. Interfirm codevelopment competency has a positive association with e-service innovation.

## 2.6. Interfirm codevelopment competency and KIM/TIM

Interfirm collaboration can increase the exchange of knowledge and allow a firm to acquire knowledge outside its boundaries [9] and through it firms create common knowledge, shared routines, similar ways of thinking, and tacit knowledge [18]. Firms improve their integrated knowledge base by collaborating with partners, and interfirm codevelopment leads to KIM. Hence, we hypothesized:

H2. Interfirm codevelopment competency has a positive association with KIM.

Because most companies have operational data in many different places, IT must integrate the data. Interfirm collaboration at this level must reassess processes and structures, provide technical support, combine and/or share resources, or engage in joint problem solving. Cooperation places stress on existing data and systems [28]. Therefore, an integrated IT mechanism could enhance consistent, real-time transfer of information across partners. Thus, we hypothesized:

H3. Interfirm codevelopment competency has a positive effect on TIM.

## 2.7. Mediating effects of KIMs and TIMs on e-service innovation

Contingency theory examines the effects of related variables on firm performance. We used it in a fit-as-mediation view, which posits that managers choose organizational structures, processes, and strategies that reflect the circumstances of their organizations because the salient aspects of a firm’s integration mechanisms, KIMs, account for the effect of interfirm collaboration behaviors on innovation practices. Managing knowledge is critical for increasing the development of new products and services. Innovation often results from the combination of existing pieces of knowledge [17]. KIMs, therefore, provide formal patterns of interactions for the transformation, recombination, and distribution of knowledge from different places. Thus, we propose that well-developed KIMs enhance innovation practices and act as a mediator between interfirm collaboration activities and service innovation practices. We therefore hypothesized:

H4. KIMs mediate the influence of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation.

Technology integration leads to successful innovation practice. Firms use this approach to choose and refine the technologies used in a new product, process, or service. Collaborating firms work to integrate technology into their innovation practices. This suggests that TIMs are appropriate for standardizing internal data and integrating interorganizational applications between partners’ IT practices, which ultimately affects innovation practices. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H5. TIMs mediate the influence of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation.

## 2.8. The moderating roles of KIMs and TIMs

Structural contingency theory emphasizes both external and internal fit [27]. Thus, we next consider the fit-as-moderation view. This proposes that a firm’s performance is attributable to a match between its strategic behaviors and the internal and external environment conditions [5]. In this view, firm performance is the dependent variable, interorganizational collaboration behavior is the predictor variable, and integration mechanisms (internal environment conditions) are the contextual variables. We propose that eservice innovation is attributable to a match between interfirm codevelopment competency, KIMs, and TIMs. The ability to be competitive in e-service innovation due to interfirm codevelopment competency is contingent upon the level of KIMs and TIMs. We hypothesized that when a firm participates in collaborative projects and activities meet customers’ changing needs, KIMs and TIMs have moderating effects on the ability of that firm to achieve the e-service innovation. If these moderating effects are not taken into consideration, the impact of interfirm codevelopment competency on eservice innovation may be masked. When firms use collaborative development relationships to support e-service innovation, KIMs and TIMs can strengthen e-service innovation. Thus, KIMs and TIMs moderate the effect of interfirm codevelopment competency on eservice innovation. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H6. KIMs, in conjunction with interfirm codevelopment competency, positively affect e-service innovation.

H7. TIMs, in conjunction with interfirm codevelopment competency, positively affect e-service innovation.

## 2.9. The moderating role of partner match

Selecting a suitable partner is critical [10]; the degree of effective past associations, the level of compatibility and trust between the partners, and organizational size or structure are important. Partners should have similar management styles and company cultures. Furthermore, through prior collaboration, firms develop ‘‘history relationships’’ with partners that enable them to judge their partners’ compatibilities, to feel comfortable with and trust their partner. Organizations can improve their innovation by developing collaborative relationships. According to contingency theory’s fit-as-moderation view, partner selection is the contextual variable. Hence, the degree of match between partners is expected to moderate the relationship between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation. The expectation is that a high level of partner match between firms will result in more effective innovation practices. Thus, we hypothesized:

H8. Partner match, in conjunction with interfirm codevelopment competency, is positively associated with e-service innovation.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Instrument development

To test our model and hypotheses, we designed and used a questionnaire. The 4-page self-administered questionnaire was part of a wider examination of interfirm codevelopment competency, e-service innovation, KIMs, TIMs, and partner match. We adapted, modified, and extended existing scales. Because the study was conducted in Taiwan, the survey instrument was in Chinese. Using a parallel-translation method, one person translated the survey items into Chinese, then another translated the Chinese version back into English. The two translators then jointly reconciled all differences in wording. The suitability of the Chinese version of the questionnaire was then reviewed by three professors and tested in interviews with seven managers from the financial and information service industries. To gain insights into their experiences, opinions, aspirations, and attitudes of interfirm codevelopment competency, e-service innovation, KIMs, TIMs, and partner match, interviews were scheduled for 90 min to 2 h. Afterwards, the interviewees were asked to review and complete the questionnaire to identify ambiguities and suggested improvements. An examination of the feedback led to further refinement and eventually a final version. All constructs were measured with scales of multiple closed-ended items; responses were based on 5- point Likert scales ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree (see Appendix A). In particular, we asked managers for their opinions on what e-service innovation practices their firm had implemented and how they had done so, regardless of their success or failure. We also measured the level of implementation of innovation practices.

## 3.2. Sample and data collection

A field survey of IT departments in financial and information service firms was conducted to test our model. The sample consisted of 393 financial and 280 information service firms selected from the ‘‘Top 5,000 (2008)—The Largest Corporations in Taiwan,’’ published by the Taiwan Credit Information Center. The same sampling and data collection process was used for both types of firms. IT managers were the key informants, because they were likely to have a comprehensive view of e-business projects, to have a broad view of the role of knowledge and technology integration in e-business, and expected to provide reliable and objective data. We assumed that these informants would have the knowledge necessary to respond to the e-service innovation issues.

We mailed a package containing a cover letter that explained the purpose of the research, the survey questionnaire, and a business reply envelope to each IT manager in the firm. We stated that we would provide the respondent with a summary of the survey results after receiving his or her response. Three weeks after the initial mailing we had received responses from 63 financial and 66 information service firms. Phone calls were subsequently made to IT managers of firms that had not replied. Of those firms contacted, 68 financial and 45 information service people could not participate because of their company’s no-survey policy; also, 8 financial and 10 information service IT departments were no longer operational and another 7 financial firms were excluded because of bankruptcy or takeover. Thus, the total number of responses was 118 from the financial and 109 from the information service firms. This represents response rates of 31% for the financial and 40% for the information service firms.

We tested for non-response bias by comparing the early and late responders on key demographics by industry. Responders to the first mailing were classified as early (n = 63 for financial firms, n = 66 for information service firms), whereas follow-up contacts were considered late (n = 55 for financial, n = 43 for information service). Independent-sample t tests revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of capital, years established, or number of employees. This indicated that the two groups were statistically similar to the rest of their industry on all demographics. Follow-up showed that non-response bias was not present.

Because the data were self-reported, we used Harmon’s onefactor test to examine the presence of common method bias. The items used to measure the independent, mediating, moderating, and dependent variables were all entered in an exploratory factor analysis. In our analysis of both firms, no single factor emerged, and the first factor accounted for 33% of the total variance; and the eight extracted factors accounted for 71% of the variance. This suggested that there was no common method bias. In addition, the empirical study validated the research model using a triangulation research methodology, initially with a convergent interviewing method; followed by Harmon’s one-factor test followed by SEM model building and model validation.

## 4. Measures

## 4.1. Dependent variable

Consistent with our earlier conceptualization, we modeled eservice innovation as a second-order construct formed by three firstorder factors: process innovation (measured by nine items that dealt with improved organizational processes and service delivery), technical capability (by two items that measured the knowledge base that the organization could bring to bear on e-service innovation), and risk reduction (from two items that measured the reduced possibility of e-service innovation failing or producing undesired effects or not functioning as originally conceived).

## 4.2. Independent variable

Interfirm codevelopment competency was modeled as a secondorder construct with three formative first-order dimensions: absorptive capacity (by four items that measured the capability of firms to assimilate, identify, transform, and make use of new information or knowledge from the environment), coordination capability (by three items that measured the capacity of firms to build a knowledge-intensive interface with other firms and organizations, to bring together various sources of expertise, and to increase lateral interaction between areas of function and content), and relational capability (by three items that measured the ability of firms to forge, develop, and govern partnerships).

## 4.3. Mediator variables

KIMs were adapted from de Luca and Atuahene-Gima’s measure which included six items that reflected structures and processes that ensured the capture, analysis, interpretation, and combination of knowledge within the firm. TIMs were adapted mainly from Rai et al. and Ward and Zhou’s measures (modeled as a second-order construct from two first-order dimensions: data consistency and cross-functional application integration).

## 4.4. Moderator variables

Partner match was adapted from Bucklin and Sengupta’s [6] work and modeled as a second-order construct from two firstorder constructs: organizational compatibility and prior history of business relations. Prior history of business relations was measured by three items that depended on the extent of prior business relationships.

## 4.5. Control variables

We controlled for firm age, size, and capital in our model, as these variables reflected a firm’s resources and power to exploit existing competencies, build new ones, and develop innovations in the market. We included these variables in our model to assess the effects of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation.

According to the fit-as-moderation view, we operationalized eservice innovation practices as firm performance. Firm age has a significant influence on firm innovation and performance. Established firms may have a first-mover advantage that results in sustained superior performance, etc. Therefore, we included firm age as a control variable and measured it as the number of years the firm had been established. Firm size also influences firm performance. To account for this, we controlled for firm size as measured by the number of employees. Firm capital also has a positive effect on sales growth. We proposed that firms that are able to accumulate more funds and resources grow faster. Therefore, we included firm capital as a control variable.

## 4.6. Construct reliability and validity

The internal reliability of the measurement models was tested using Cronbach’s alpha (a) and Fornell’s measure of composite reliability $( \rho _ { c } ) .$ . Cronbach’s alphas for all constructs were above the threshold of 0.7 (range = 0.74–0.94), indicating a high degree of internal consistency. Likewise, all $\rho _ { c }$ values were above the suggested threshold of 0.7 (range = 0.86–0.97), indicating that the measures were reliable. Table A2 in appendix shows the properties of the measurement model. We assessed convergent validity using the average variance extracted (AVE), or the ratio of construct variance to total variance among indicators. The AVE values for the 11 constructs all exceeded 0.5, confirming that all measures demonstrated satisfactory convergent validity. We examined discriminant validity with a correlation matrix. The values of the square root of the AVE for the measures in the diagonal were all greater than the correlations among the measures off the diagonal. Hence, discriminant validity was satisfactory. The means, standard deviations, correlations, and AVE for the key constructs in the study are shown in Tables 2 and 3.

## 5. Results

We decided to use partial least squares (PLS) analysis because we had a relatively small sample size (n = 118 financial firms and n = 109 information service firms) and our model had formative constructs. Also PLS, which uses components-based algorithms, can estimate formative constructs. In the research model, interfirm codevelopment competency, TIMs, and e-service innovation were formative. We used PLS Graph 3.0 to perform SEM and to evaluate both the quality of the measurement model and the interrelationships of the constructs separately for the financial and information service firms. A bootstrap resampling procedure was conducted, and coefficients were estimated.

## 6.2. Results for the mediating effects

To assess the extent of mediation in the model, we followed Andrews et al. [3], who indicated that four specific criteria must be met: (1) The independent variable should significantly influence the mediator variable; (2) the mediator should significantly influence the dependent variable; (3) the independent variable should significantly influence the dependent variable; and (4) after the mediator variable is controlled, the impact of the independent variable on the dependent variable should no longer be significant (for full mediation) or should be reduced in strength (for partial mediation). In our study, the independent variable was interfirm codevelopment competency, and the proposed mediating variables were KIMs and TIMs. The dependent variable was e-service innovation.

We tested the four conditions using PLS analysis. As Table 5 shows, Model 1a did not meet the second condition. Furthermore, only KIMs affected e-service innovation. Model 2a met the third condition. Model 2a did not include the mediator of KIMs and appeared to fit the data reasonably well. The fourth condition held if the effects of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation became insignificant or less significant after the mediator of KIMs was included. Model 3a results showed that entering the mediator of KIMs indeed decreased the impact of interfirm codevelopment competency from Model 2a to Model 3a. In particular, the impact of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation was diminished (but still significant), indicating partial mediation. Correspondingly, KIMs partially mediated the relationship between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation; thus, Hypothesis 4 was supported for the financial firms. Moreover, TIMs had no mediating effects between interfirm codevelopment competency and eservice innovation; thus, Hypothesis 5 was not supported for the financial firms.

In contrast, as reported in Table 6, Model 1b met the first two conditions. That is, interfirm codevelopment competency affected both KIMs and TIMs. Furthermore, KIMs and TIMs affected eservice innovation. Also, Model 2b appeared to fit the data reasonably well. However, Model 3b results showed that only entering the mediator of TIMs indeed decreased the impact of interfirm codevelopment competency from Model 2b to Model 3b. Similarly, the impact of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation was diminished (but still significant), indicating partial mediation. Correspondingly, KIMs had no mediating effects between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation; thus, Hypothesis 4 was not supported for the information service firms. Moreover, TIMs partially mediated the relationship between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation; thus, Hypothesis 5 was supported for the information service firms.

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

Our primary objective was to highlight how a firm’s innovation activities are influenced by interorganizational behaviors. The results support the main premises of our research model. We believe that incorporating interfirm codevelopment competency into our analysis leads to a more comprehensive view of the strategic behavior of firms. Using data collected from financial and information service firms that have implemented e-service innovation, our study provides evidence to suggest that the mediating effects of KIMs and TIMs on e-service innovation may vary across industries. Furthermore, the opposite moderating effects of KIMs. TIMs, and partner match on e-service innovation between financial and information service firms potentially reinforce the contingency theory, which requires a more sophisticated managerial approach to e-service innovation across industries. The data suggest that the firms in both industries emphasize interfirm codevelopment competency in developing e-service innovation but use different sets of integration mechanisms to enhance e-service innovation.

7.1. Influence of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation

The path coefficient between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation for both the financial and information service industries was positive and statistically significant. Financial and information service firms were virtually identical in terms of their collaboration competency for developing e-service innovation. This indicated that interfirm codevelopment competency plays a positive role in e-service innovation practices. Our study has provided one of the first direct tests of the idea that interfirm codevelopment competency in e-service innovation practices is necessary for deriving benefits from collaboration partners. The results further demonstrate that absorptive capacity, coordination capability, and relational capability, which form and facilitate interfirm codevelopment competency across organizational boundaries, can play important roles in e-service innovation.

## 7.2. Effects of interfirm codevelopment competency on KIMs and TIMs

The effect of interfirm codevelopment competency on KIMs for both the financial and information service industries was positive and statistically significant. Absorptive capacity, coordination capability, and relational capability had significant loadings on the construct of interfirm codevelopment competency. This suggests that absorptive capacity, coordination capability, and relational capability as components of interfirm codevelopment competency enhance the KIMs in firms’ e-service innovation practices. Also, the effect of interfirm codevelopment competency on TIMs for both the financial and information service industries was positive and statistically significant. Collectively, this competency promotes KIMs and TIMs across organizational boundaries and is positively associated with the development of integration mechanisms for knowledge and technology. Comparison of the standardized path coefficients of KIMs and TIMs for both the financial and information service industries provides an empirical basis to suggest that interfirm codevelopment competency is relatively influential in facilitating the integration of KIMs and TIMs in e-service innovation practices.

## 7.3. Mediating role of KIMs and TIMs

The results support the idea that interfirm codevelopment competency influences e-service innovation in part because it facilitates KIMs and TIMs. For financial firms, the relationship between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation is partially mediated by KIMs (see Fig. 2). This suggests that interfirm codevelopment competency, in addition to being positively associated with KIMs, has a direct and positive association with e-service innovation. However, for information service firms, the relationship between interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation is partially mediated by TIMs (see Fig. 3). TIMs can include the application of technological resources and organizational knowledge that are codified in knowledge databases. Overall, mediation by KIMs and TIMs lends empirical support to the theoretical perspective that e-service innovation relies on the ability of the organization to apply KIMs and TIMs to e-service innovation.

## 7.4. Moderating role of KIMs and TIMs

KIMs and TIMs moderate the influence of interfirm codevelopment competency on e-service innovation in the financial industry only. There is an additive effect of the set of integration mechanisms on building e-service innovation such that the effect of each integration mechanism depends upon commingling with collaboration competency. We found that the association of building interfirm codevelopment competency with e-service innovation is contingent on the presence of both KIMs and TIMs. When KIMs and TIMs were included in the model, the explained variance in e-service innovation increased to 40% (KIMs) and to 39% (TIMs). As our results show, financial firms more so than information service firms ‘‘bundle’’ KIMs and TIMs to enhance eservice innovation. This indicates that financial firms need to know their customers and their interaction behaviors. Customer information that may be stored across organizational boundaries or in partners’ knowledge databases needs to be integrated, and when financial firms implement customized programs, they must use technology to integrate and translate these data into a consistent form to permit real-time exploitation. The moderating effects of KIMs and TIMs were not significant in the information service industry. These findings are contrary to our expectations.

## 7.5. Moderating role of partner match

As shown in Tables 7 and 8, partner match had a significant interaction effect for financial firms indicating that financial firms recognize the benefits of partner match and are willing to search for compatible partners for e-service innovation. However, our results for the interaction effects of interfirm codevelopment competency and partner match on e-service innovation in information service firms are inconsistent.

## 8. Research contributions

We have developed a theory around the idea of interfirm codevelopment competency in e-service innovation practices which demonstrates that KIMs and TIMs are associated with improved innovation. The perspective that the integration of knowledge and technology in e-service processes is a key mechanism through which new e-service development and improvements can be realized extends research on improving e-service innovation in organizations. The results suggest that organizations can exploit partners’ knowledge and technology by integrating them in codevelopment processes, and they highlight the centrality of the roles of KIMs and TIMs in e-service innovation. We also showed that interfirm codevelopment competency influences the extent to which firms integrate knowledge and technology across organizational boundaries for e-service innovation. Interfirm codevelopment competency has a stronger effect on KIMs and TIMs. Also absorptive capacity, coordination capability, and relational capability are all necessary for effective interfirm codevelopment competency, and overemphasis of one over another can lead to missed opportunities for improvements in KIMs and TIMs.

## 8.1. Implications for practice

Our study has significant implications for management. First, the results suggest that IT managers should consider interfirm codevelopment competency in new e-service development. They should identify areas in which their firms may be lacking and develop specific capabilities for improving interfirm development competency; such an assessment should precede the implementation of any large-scale e-service innovation project.

Second, IT managers can enhance interfirm codevelopment competency by establishing KIMs and TIMs. Firms considering large-scale implementation of e-service innovation should first develop strong relationship competencies. Then they should select well-matched partners and link them to core research and development teams to develop knowledge and technology integration platforms. We propose that IT managers should mobilize collaboration competency in conjunction with KIMs and TIMs to achieve e-service innovation.

Third, the selection of an appropriate partner is important. Managers must be aware that ‘‘match’’ has a major influence on the selection process. The firm’s goals and objectives need to be consistent with those of the partner firm and both sets of managers need to have similar management styles. Firms should work closely to maintain stable and enduring collaborative relationships; frequent interactions between managers and salespersons to explore the mutuality of their interests and needs should be encouraged.

Finally, e-service innovation project managers need to ensure that project objectives are clearly defined in terms of service process innovation from both the customers’ and the organizations’ perspectives, the involvement and support of top management is secured, standard project management processes are used to mitigate the risks of e-service development, and sufficient technological resources and capacity are dedicated to getting the eservice innovation project done in the time allotted. This sequence of resource picking and capability building may serve as an effective roadmap for firms contemplating e-service innovation implementation.

## 9. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the model is not robust enough to include all possible factors related to e-service innovation. Second, because we used the key respondent approach and directed our questionnaire to IT managers, the results may be subject to single-informant bias. A reasonable argument can be made that IT managers’ knowledge about e-service innovation practices may reflect a positive bias. Third, although we controlled for firms’ age, capital, and size, we did not include other potentially influential covariates such as industry maturity and research and development strength. This might limit the evaluation of the relative importance of the interfirm codevelopment competency and e-service innovation. Fourth, our research model suggests that achieving interfirm codevelopment competency requires paying attention to absorptive capacity, coordination capability, and relational capability.
