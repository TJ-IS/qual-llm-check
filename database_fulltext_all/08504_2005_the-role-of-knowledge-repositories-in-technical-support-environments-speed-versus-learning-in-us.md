# The Role of Knowledge Repositories in Technical Support Environments: Speed Versus Learning in User Performance

PETER H. GRAY AND ALEXANDRA DURCIKOVA

PETER H. GRAY is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh. He has a background in electronic commerce and online information services, and has worked in a variety of information technology and management consulting positions. His research interests include knowledge management and knowledge management systems, organizational learning, computer-mediated communication, and communities of practice. Dr. Gray has published in MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information Technology & People, Information and Management, Decision Support Systems, and Communications of the AIS, as well as various national and international conference proceedings. He is a member of the editorial review boards of Organization Science and Information Technology and People.

ALEXANDRA DURCIKOVA is an Assistant Professor at the Eller College of Business and Public Administration, University of Arizona. She has a Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Pittsburgh. She has worked as an experimental physics researcher in the area of superconductivity and as an instructor of MBA and executive MBA students prior to returning to academia to pursue her Ph.D. Dr. Durcikova’s research interests include knowledge management and knowledge management systems, the role of organizational climate in the use of knowledge management systems, and IS issues in developing countries. Her work appears in Communications of the ACM, Electronic Journal of Knowledge Management, as well as various national and international conference proceedings.

ABSTRACT: Knowledge repositories are commonly used by technical support analysts in call center environments as a way of capturing and reusing solutions to common problems, and are generally expected to improve service quality, reduce costs, and enhance analyst learning. This study investigates why technical support analysts seek out and access knowledge from these repositories, as opposed to more traditional sources of such knowledge—colleagues and manuals. Focusing on the demand for—rather than supply of—knowledge in organizations, our research elaborates the role played by analysts’ learning orientation, perceived work demands, and risk aversion in predicting their knowledge sourcing behavior. Our results include several counterintuitive findings that suggest there is not very much learning going on via technical support knowledge repositories. Analysts seem to be focused on finding recipes for solving customers’ problems rather than building a better understanding of the products they support. Implications for research and practice highlight the need for more effective technologies to speed searches, the utility of a formal and visible mechanism for validating knowledge, and the inherent tension between efficiency and learning in these environments.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: knowledge management systems, knowledge repositories, knowledge sourcing, learning orientation, risk aversion, technical support help desks.

IN THE ONGOING QUEST for sustainable competitive advantage, organizations are paying increasing attention to their roles as stewards of the resources over which they have influence [6]. One such resource that has received considerable attention in the past decade is knowledge (e.g., [3, 8, 45, 88]), and firms continue to pursue a plethora of efforts intended to improve returns to their knowledge under the banner of knowledge management (KM) (e.g., [1, 57, 63, 87]). Such efforts have become increasingly important to organizations [31, 48] and, as a result, KM is “becoming a critical business discipline as enterprises seek to enhance competitiveness in real time” [14, p. 1].

Within the multibillion-dollar (U.S.) global market for KM products and services [56] is a sector of software vendors offering information technology (IT)–based solutions for managing customer support knowledge.<sup>1</sup> Customer support knowledge includes expertise, insights, and opinions about the causes, symptoms, and nature of problems that customers may experience with a firm’s products or services, as well as prescriptive advice for solving those problems. Firms have been buying and implementing IT-based systems for capturing and reusing customer support knowledge since the mid-1980s [22, 31] as a way of improving the efficiency of knowledge transfer and learning among call center analysts (also variously termed help desk analysts, customer support representatives, customer support specialists, or technical support personnel). Analysts provide a form of postsales service by answering telephone calls (and e-mails) from customers who have questions about, or are experiencing problems with, a firm’s products or services. It has become well accepted that analysts can benefit from accessing each other’s knowledge through some form of electronic repository of customer support knowledge [33], which may take the form of a simple database, a purpose-built call center software package, or a case-based reasoning system [22]. These repositories of codified knowledge therefore function as an element of organizational memory [89] by providing a common resource to help analysts solve customers’ product- or service-related problems [24].

In order to maximize the economies of scale of knowledge reuse, organizations clearly would prefer that analysts first consult a knowledge repository when facing a customer problem for which they have no immediate answer and consult directly with colleagues only after no suitable solution can be found in the repository. Yet industry reports indicate that there remains considerable variation in analysts’ willingness to reuse knowledge held in these systems [19]. The problem of motivating employees to reuse the knowledge held in a repository has also been identified in other contexts outside customer support (e.g., [23, p. 102, 61]). Motivating analysts to first check the knowledge repository remains a challenge for customer support center managers [15, 36]. When analysts’ reluctance to draw on knowledge repositories causes them to spend extra time recreating solutions to problems that have already been codified and stored, organizations are not able to realize the full benefits of knowledge reuse.

You can have the best technology in the world but if you can’t get it implemented and have people trained and actually using it, you can’t make the benefits real.

—Call center manager, quoted in [76], emphasis added

Obviously, without acceptance by the agents, the value derived from implementing [customer support knowledge repositories] will be less than optimal. . . . [T]he lion’s share of the benefits are derived by companies whose people embrace knowledge sharing—not from doing it just because “my boss said so.” When they use a system by direction only, then they use it in perfunctory way, and do not understand how it helps them or the company. Companies deriving significant value from KM are likely to have employees and managers who understand that knowledge sharing raises the intellectual gene pool of the organization, and increases the competitive viability of the company in the long run.

—Manager, customer support knowledge repository vendor

Understanding the factors that shape and guide analysts’ choices about where they prefer to find knowledge, in general, and why they do—or do not—source knowledge from technical support knowledge repositories, in particular, is thus an important first step toward increasing the effectiveness of such repositories. In this paper, we investigate why technical support analysts prefer some sources of knowledge over others. We draw on a recent study of knowledge sourcing [47] that advances a generic set of factors to explain why individuals in organizations choose to seek out and access others’ knowledge. We expand on knowledge sourcing theory to explain technical support analysts’ choices from three competing sources of knowledge available to them—namely, their colleagues, official company documents such as product manuals, and the solutions available in technical support knowledge repositories. An improved understanding of why some analysts seem to avoid sourcing knowledge from repositories stands to help practitioners understand the issues that drive resistance to these technologies and may ultimately affect how these repositories are designed and managed to increase utilization by analysts. The results of this study, therefore, both inform the growing body of research that seeks to explore the demand side of KM and also provide useful guidance to managers trying to maximize the benefits of knowledge repositories in technical support environments.

## Technical Support Work

ORGANIZATIONS ARE INCREASINGLY BUNDLING services along with the physical products they sell. El Sawy and Bowles [33] described this evolution over the past 150 years in the kinds of support firms provide to their customers, including product training, parts and service, warranty claims, complaint handling, returns and refunds, and dispute resolutions. More recently, many firms have begun to offer technical support via call centers [39] staffed by analysts who provide a form of postsales assistance by answering customers’ questions, assisting them in integrating the product into their environment, and offering remote troubleshooting of problems (e.g., [54, 74]). Technical support analysts are particularly effective in situations where no product repair is necessary.<sup>2</sup> In the computer hardware and software industries, technical support is a “key business factor in retaining and expanding market share” [22, p. 195] as “an unsupported product is hardly considered a product at all” [82, p. 51].

Technical support call centers have become an important part of the value proposition that firms offer their customers. Technical support analysts in such centers assist customers in obtaining the maximum benefit possible from the product or service that they have purchased (e.g., [33]) by “reducing differences between the desired and realized performance of a technological artifact” [21, p. 418]. This, in turn, produces value for the firm in a variety of ways. In some cases, call centers charge customers on a per-incident basis, but, in many instances, this service is offered free of charge, presumably on the basis that the cost of maintaining a call center is less than the losses due to the dissatisfaction of customers who cannot correctly and effectively operate the company’s products or utilize its services to their fullest capacity. A separate value stream may result from cost savings generated because call centers are more economical than field service for solving problems that do not involve malfunctioning parts. When firms use call centers as a first line of troubleshooting, they can reduce their dependence on more expensive field repair personnel and thereby reduce overall support costs. In addition to their role in boosting customer satisfaction (and thus, perhaps, repeat purchases, goodwill, and positive word-of-mouth) and reducing service costs, technical support call centers may also help firms understand underlying problems with products’ design or manufacturing. These problems can be fed back to designers and engineers as a way of helping them learn about the shortcomings of present designs and opportunities for improvement of future versions [80].

Technical support analysts provide customers with assistance in solving problems with their firm’s goods or services, often in real time, based on a limited understanding of customers’ situations. They must grapple with a wide variety of problems— some routine, many not [34]—in an environment characterized by ongoing evolution in the products and services they support and the purposes for which they are used [21]. These problems are often complicated to both understand and diagnose (e.g., “I’ve got an o-c-4 at k-d-f-s plus 1110, followed by looping 1313 messages” [81, p. 533]). To succeed, analysts must apply a body of knowledge that is broader and deeper than the customer’s to the problem at hand. Yet the body of knowledge applied by most analysts is also larger than that which resides in their individual memories. The economic reality is that individuals who are true experts in all facets of a product are expensive assets and are thus better deployed in areas such as research and new product development. As a result, less-expensive (though still technically competent) individuals are generally used to provide technical support; their knowledge is often far less complete than that of the engineers or developers who created the product [22].

When facing a problem that is outside the scope of their personally held knowledge, analysts can choose from three high-level strategies. First, they can engage in a problem-solving dialogue with the customer in an attempt to discover the solution to an underlying problem via a series of experiments [82]. Second, analysts may reassign the problem to other analysts [81] who have greater expertise. Third, they may turn to external memory systems [77] and often do because “[m]uch of the knowledge used in technical support problem solving is stored outside the heads of analysts, in the physical and social environment of the work place” [21, p. 429]. These external memories are the focus of this research effort and exist in at least three principal forms: (1) analysts’ colleagues, (2) the literature produced by the product’s manufacturers, and (3) the successful solutions used to solve past problems that are captured and stored in a knowledge repository.

Clearly, these three forms of external memory systems are not identical and provide varied kinds of support for problem solving. For example, technical support analysts described by Pentland [81, pp. 536–538] obtained knowledge from other analysts by asking for help either in the form of a “quick question” or through more involved requests to “take a look.” Such kinds of direct interaction with colleagues allow for dialogue about the nature of the problem being faced and support the matching of knowledge to context, which creates the possibility of interactive sense making [20]. However, the process of searching for an individual who has knowledge of rare or unusual problems may well be long and tedious. This, in turn, may limit the effectiveness of asking colleagues in technical support environments, where quick answers are often needed to satisfy customers.

Asking colleagues differs considerably from consulting the kinds of official documents described by Das [21, p. 420] in a technical support environment, such as product manuals, documentation, technical specifications, and compatibility requirements. With broader range—but often less depth—these official documents represent a better-indexed, though perhaps less-current, form of technical support knowledge. Finally, both colleagues and official documents differ from an IT-enabled knowledge repository that captures successful solutions to past problems in a searchable format and enables analysts to quickly locate candidate solutions that may solve part or all of the customer’s problem [22]. It is this third and last source of knowledge that will be expanded on in the next section.

## Knowledge Repositories in Technical Support Environments

INFORMATION TECHNOLOGIES THAT PROVIDE A FORM OF SUPRA-INDIVIDUAL memory are a class of KM system [2] that serve to promote knowledge reuse [40, 68]. In technical support environments, these knowledge repositories index, store, and make available successful solutions to customer problems [68, p. 60], and thus serve to codify and share best practices [2] about solving customers’ technical problems. As noted by Grover and Davenport, “what companies actually manage under the banner of KM is a mix of knowledge, information, and unrefined data” [48, p. 7]. Clearly, customer support knowledge repositories will contain some simple facts and some processed information, but often synthesized in a way that transforms such content into knowledge through “the addition of insight, experience, context, interpretation” [48, p. 7]. There seems to be no hard distinction available yet to offer an unambiguous differentiation between knowledge repositories and information repositories. We therefore adopt the terms used by managers and users in our subject firms, who described them as “knowledge management” efforts and as “knowledge repositories,” respectively.

Access to others’ expertise and insight into particular problems may help analysts locate ready-made solutions that can be easily reused in part or in whole (e.g., [21]). Davenport and Klahr [22, p. 197] described how enhancing analysts’ access to customer support knowledge via a well-designed repository resulted in a variety of quantifiable benefits across a range of different firms, including improving the quality of solutions delivered to customers, establishing consistency, increasing first-call resolutions, reducing cost per call, reducing repeat calls, reducing field service costs, moving to a less technical frontline staff, accelerating training, increasing staff satisfaction, and increasing customer satisfaction. Such systems thus provide a capability for integrating knowledge across distributed organizational elements to ensure that what is known by customer support personnel is “made explicit and available for future use, complete with contexts, rationales, and outcomes” [89, p. 97].

Knowledge repositories have become commonplace in the technical support domain [39] and are probably more successful here than in other areas of firm operations for at least four reasons. First, the breadth of the knowledge domain in technical support is extremely narrow, dealing only with products, problems with those products, and solutions to those problems. This kind of highly focused knowledge domain limits the complexity of the knowledge that is stored and sets standard expectations for the context of its use (e.g., [7]). Second, the knowledge that is captured has a very specific and testable end-goal; that is, one can easily follow the advice recorded in a technical support knowledge repository and find out whether it actually solves the problem in question. Quality is thus easier to assess, and low-quality solutions are easier to identify and purge. Third, the individuals who are reusers of technical support knowledge are all performing essentially the same kind of job. Issues of translation across different occupational communities and misinterpretation by individuals from different functional areas [68] are thus minimized. Finally, the economic benefit of capturing knowledge is a function of the number of times a given piece of knowledge is reused (e.g., [59]). Given that many customers end up encountering what are often very similar problems, a single solution may be reused dozens or even hundreds of times, making the cost of the repository and the cost of capturing and validating the knowledge small in comparison to the benefits. Together, these four factors would predict that knowledge repositories should be effective KM tools for technical support analysts.

I do find it useful if the help desk is looking [in the knowledge repository] first, because if they’re looking there first, then my knowledge has been transferred down to them, and it might save me a phone call in the middle of the night if they look there first. I might have already documented the steps that I would normally—you know, if I’m on call, and they call me at 2 in the morning, that’s the kind of thing I want to put into [the repository] and know that they are using. Because if it’s something standard, that I would normally walk them through these same steps, you know, no matter what . . . then that is useful to put it in there.

—Product support specialist

A central theme in establishing a technical support knowledge repository is to improve employees’ efficiency by reducing their need for person-to-person contact with colleagues in situations where known solutions to problems exist. For example, analysts may learn from a repository about related problems, similar symptoms, or other topics that have the same context of occurrence [22]. By helping analysts quickly identify and reject known solutions to problems, such repositories may improve their ability to delineate a problem and focus the scope of a potential solution. However, these repositories are not intended to reduce the collective problem-solving behaviors that analysts engage in when faced with novel or unusual problems (e.g., [79]). To the contrary, by enhancing analysts’ efficiency in dealing with routine kinds of problems, such repositories may free up analysts’ time to better deal with these more demanding kinds of novel problems. By carefully capturing knowledge in a searchable IT-based repository, technical support departments thus stand to improve the efficiency of both routine and nonroutine analyst’s work in a variety of ways. The result is what El Sawy and Bowles term more learningful technical support staff, meaning that they “build on each other’s knowledge and on that of more experienced senior colleagues and experienced customers” [33, p. 474].

Given the apparent benefits, both individual and organizational, of accessing knowledge stored in a repository, it remains puzzling that practitioners report that motivating technical support analysts to draw on the knowledge held in these systems remains a considerable managerial challenge [15, 19, 36].

Many companies do experience cultural resistance from their help desk and call center analysts in utilizing [technical support knowledge repositories]. . . . I’d say that motivational problems are the number one barrier to KM success.

—Manager, customer support knowledge repository vendor

I only use it if I’m desperate. I use it more as a last resource.

—Technical support analyst

As discussed earlier, and for reasons that are not entirely clear, analysts sometimes do not turn first to a repository to identify existing solutions (or rule out known problems) when solving technical support problems. The following section draws on knowledge sourcing theory to propose a theoretical model that may help explain why this is so.

## Knowledge Sourcing Theory

TO UNDERSTAND WHAT CAUSES ANALYSTS to make use of various sources of knowledge, we turn to research that draws on the adult education [53, 58] and educational psychology [30, 35] literatures to model the naturally occurring individual-level demand for knowledge in firms [47]. Levitt and March [64] described two categories of learning behaviors: individuals can learn directly from their work environments (for instance, through experimentation, experiential learning, or learning by doing) and can also learn from the experiences of others. When analysts ask colleagues for solutions, check manuals, or use a knowledge repository to locate and obtain solutions recorded by other analysts, they are accessing the products of others’ learning activities and are thus engaged in Levitt and March’s [64] second kind of learning behavior, which has been termed knowledge sourcing, and defined as the “extent to which individuals intentionally access other’s expertise, experience, insights and opinions” [47, p. 821].

Seminal research in the adult education literature [53] identified two instrumental categories of factors that explain why individuals pursue adult education, and these categories help explain why individuals engage in knowledge sourcing behaviors [47]. In particular, individuals pursue adult education because of (1) a need to cope with real-life tasks or problems [58, p. 44] or (2) their basic disposition toward learning [12, p. 233]. A recent study of knowledge sourcing [47] has operationalized the first of these two classes of factors as the degree to which individuals believe their work environment to be intellectually demanding. Intellectually demanding work places a greater burden on individuals’ cognitive resources, leading them to outsource some portion of that cognitive load by drawing on others’ knowledge. The same recent study operationalized the second class of factors as the extent to which individuals hold a positive orientation toward learning. Those with a strong learning orientation are more likely to source knowledge as a mechanism for improving their skills and abilities. Research conducted with a sample of manufacturing engineering employees found support for both as predictors of knowledge sourcing behavior [47] and established the reliability and validity of these constructs.

While there are many motivational theories that could hold some explanatory power in this context, theory based on the adult education literature has several benefits in helping understand knowledge sourcing in particular. Because the factors are proximal to the learning behavior in question, they are more tightly connected—theoretically speaking—to the phenomena of interest than are more generic theories of human motivation. Further, because knowledge sourcing theory does not presuppose a particular channel by which knowledge is communicated, it is ideal for comparing across heterogeneous sources of knowledge (people, documents, and computers). However, it is clear that work context and individual disposition toward learning cannot be the only causal antecedents of knowledge sourcing behaviors; other theories of motivation may provide different and valuable perspectives on this topic.

Recent research into knowledge sourcing [47] has been conducted in the context of ad hoc, voluntary knowledge sourcing; that is, subjects sometimes sourced knowledge to help them deal with tasks, problems, or issues, but this was a small part of their daily activities. This stands in contrast to most technical support analysts, who must engage in high levels of deliberate knowledge sourcing to perform their jobs well. Analysts face a constant barrage of questions, many of which will require them to source knowledge from colleagues, documents, or knowledge repositories. Indeed, sourcing knowledge in a technical support context is neither ad hoc nor occasional; analysts are generally expected to source knowledge whenever they do not know the solution to a problem to avoid wasteful duplication of effort and ensure consistent responses to customers. Although the analysts in our study had the option of using or not using the system, there was a strong expectation among management that they would. Hence, the social norms of the organization were encouraging mandatory use.

Most companies use some variation of a “percentage of cases utilizing the knowledge base” as a metric to evaluate if people are using the system. So, for example, they might have a goal of using the KM system for 85 percent of all contacts. . . . Typically, they compare the number of contacts received (either through their CRM [customer relationship management system] or their ACD [automated call distribution] switch) with the number of times the knowledge base is accessed [and then] provide feedback to people who ignore or misuse the KM system.

## —Manager, customer support knowledge repository vendor

A primary goal of this research is to determine whether previously identified antecedents of knowledge sourcing behaviors will hold in technical support contexts, where knowledge sourcing is not optional but, rather, essential to good performance. By doing so, we provide not only a replication of past research into knowledge sourcing but also a detailed account of why technical support analysts chose to obtain knowledge from among the various sources available to them.

## Learning Orientation

Individuals vary in the extent to which they seek to acquire new knowledge or skills and, thus, to improve their abilities [29]. Those with a strong learning orientation believe that their own skills and abilities can be improved through effort and experience, and have a desire to do so. As such, they “persist, escalate effort, engage in solutionoriented self-instruction, and report enjoying the challenge” [10, p. 864]. Learning orientation has been used to predict real-world performance in both experimental and natural settings, including exam grades, course grades, and achievement test scores [43]. Individuals with a strong learning orientation demonstrate better performance because they direct increased attentional resources toward learning behaviors [10]. As such, they are more likely to source knowledge to improve their knowledge, skills, and abilities [47].

An individual’s disposition toward directing increased attentional resources toward learning behaviors is therefore an important predictor of his or her knowledge sourcing behaviors. In a technical support context, analysts regularly face problems for which they have no solution available in their own memories [21]. As noted earlier, in such situations, analysts may consult their coworkers, product manuals, or a knowledge repository to learn about potential solutions to customer problems. Consistent with knowledge sourcing theory [47], when analysts are not able to solve a problem using their own skills and expertise, those with a strong learning orientation are more likely than those with a weak learning orientation to source knowledge from colleagues, manuals, and a knowledge repository.

P1: Analysts with stronger learning orientations will engage in higher levels of knowledge sourcing behavior.

H1a: Analysts with stronger learning orientations will source more knowledge directly from their colleagues.

H1b: Analysts with stronger learning orientations will source more knowledge from official company product-related documents.

H1c: Analysts with stronger learning orientations will source more knowledge from a technical support knowledge repository.

## Intellectual Demands

A second antecedent of knowledge sourcing is the level of intellectual demands experienced by an individual in his or her job. Work that is highly demanding produces psychological strains that cause individuals to pursue coping strategies (e.g., [62]) designed to reduce strain (e.g., by changing work methods or upgrading skills). Intellectually demanding jobs consume a greater proportion of individuals’ cognitive capacity, which restricts their ability to cope with their tasks and responsibilities [91]. Knowledge sourcing frees up cognitive resources, as importing the required knowledge from other employees generally requires less cognitive effort than developing it by learning through direct interaction (e.g., though experimentation or learning by doing) with the environment [47]. When individuals believe their jobs are more intellectually demanding, they will decrease the level of strain placed on them by outsourcing some portion of their cognitive load—that is, they will engage in more knowledge sourcing behaviors.

P2: Analysts facing higher perceived intellectual demands will engage in higher levels of knowledge sourcing behavior.

H2a: Analysts facing higher perceived intellectual demands will source more knowledge directly from their colleagues.

H2b: Analysts facing higher perceived intellectual demands will source more knowledge from official company product-related documents.

H2c: Analysts facing higher perceived intellectual demands will source more knowledge from a technical support knowledge repository.

However, there may be two related, but distinct, reasons why work can be intellectually demanding. First, work can be intellectually demanding because it is cognitively complex, requiring considerable mental activity to accomplish a given task. Thus, one interpretation of intellectual demands is a set of tasks that are not especially numerous, but each requiring extensive cognitive activity to accomplish. This corresponds to the Gray and Meister [47] conceptualization of intellectual demands but differs from a second possible interpretation of the idea. In particular, work could also be intellectually demanding simply because there are a large number of tasks to be performed. The frequency and pace of the tasks thus contribute to this second conceptualization of how intellectually demanding work is, rather than the cognitive effort required per task. Each of these two characterizations of the work environment may be a source of intellectual demands, irrespective of the other. Thus, we hypothesize that analysts may also react simply to higher levels of time pressure in the same way as they would toward cognitively challenging tasks—that is, by sourcing more knowledge from colleagues, manuals, and the knowledge repository.

P3: Analysts facing higher levels of work-related time pressure will engage in higher levels of knowledge sourcing behavior.

H3a: Analysts facing higher levels of work-related time pressure will source more knowledge directly from their colleagues.

H3b: Analysts facing higher levels of work-related time pressure will source more knowledge from official company product-related documents.

H3c: Analysts facing higher levels of work-related time pressure will source more knowledge from a technical support knowledge repository.

## Risk Aversion

Individuals who are risk-averse prefer certain outcomes over uncertain ones [85]. Although there is presently no consensus about whether risk aversion constitutes a stable personality trait that persists across domains or is a domain-specific attitude [11], it seems likely that at least part of an individual’s risk aversion is relatively stable across situations [13, 65]. A recent study of knowledge sourcing [47] controlled for individuals’ levels of risk aversion on the assumption that risk-averse individuals would tend to source less knowledge because firsthand learning might be less risky than relying on others’ knowledge. While no systematic effect of risk aversion on individuals’ general tendencies to source knowledge was found, this may be the result of risk aversion being undertheorized in this past research. In particular, we believe that different sources of knowledge have different levels of perceived riskiness. If this is true, then risk-averse individuals may not simply source less knowledge; instead, they may obtain less knowledge from sources they see as risky (e.g., possibly inapplicable, unhelpful, erroneous) but may obtain more knowledge from those sources that they judge likely to contain appropriate, useful, and valid knowledge. We therefore theorize the relative riskiness of sourcing knowledge from colleagues, via company documents and through a knowledge repository, as a way of assessing the extent to which an individual’s level of risk aversion influences his or her knowledge sourcing behaviors.

In a knowledge sourcing context, the perceived riskiness of a knowledge source is determined in part by how likely an individual believes the knowledge obtained from that source will be erroneous, misleading, or in some way damaging if relied upon. A second related risk is the possibility that consulting a particular source will take a considerable period of time and not produce a result. Both of these risks are particularly acute in technical support environments, where analysts must often solve customers’ problems in real time; procedures that fail to solve a problem, take too long, or create new problems will harm analysts’ effectiveness and may generate negative feedback from customers. The burden of coming up with the right solution the first time is high in a technical support environment, where analysts are judged in part by such time-based metrics as average call completion time and percent of problems solved on the first call [22]. We therefore expect technical support analysts to be particularly sensitive to the relative risks (i.e., across different knowledge sources) of obtaining unhelpful solutions or of wasting time searching where no solution can be found.

Of the three methods for sourcing knowledge that are available in typical technical support environments, perhaps the most risk-free sources of knowledge available to analysts are their own colleagues. Over time, analysts learn who to go to on different topics and learn who is helpful and who is not [81]. They can quickly ascertain whether a colleague has any candidate solutions to offer. Then they can dialogue with him or her to clarify a point and to ensure that it applies to the problem at hand. Dialogue allows individuals to “explain their needs and collaboratively define their knowledge requirements” [77, p. 825]. They can challenge other analysts, and gauge the degree of confidence others have that their knowledge would apply in the given context. Together, the ability to select colleagues with topical expertise, quickly assess their ability to help, dialogue with them, and interactively gauge the applicability of the knowledge they hold to the problem at hand makes analysts’ local colleagues a lowrisk source of knowledge. Because the knowledge they obtain is more likely to be dependable and accurate, analysts who are risk-averse are more likely to source knowledge from their colleagues.

H4a: Analysts with higher levels of risk aversion will source more knowledge directly from their colleagues.

A second source of knowledge is the body of official documents produced by an organization about its products and services. These often include repair manuals, bulletins about known problems, and troubleshooting guides. To a technical support analyst, the riskiness of the knowledge held in such documents is generally high. On one hand, manuals are typically well organized and often written by highly skilled technical staff, who may even be the creators of the particular product that analysts must support. However, research has shown that the canonical practices described in such documents are often difficult for analysts to understand and apply, and frequently contain errors that render them useless in troubleshooting problems (e.g., [78]). Given the very real risk that the knowledge in a manual will be wrong, outdated, or insufficiently precise to solve a given problem, risk-averse individuals are likely to avoid relying on such documents.

H4b: Analysts with higher levels of risk aversion will source less knowledge from official company product-related documents.

A third and final source of knowledge for technical support analysts is an IT-based repository of technical support knowledge that captures the pairing of known problems and related solutions. In contrast to the knowledge that can be elicited through a conversation among colleagues, the knowledge captured in a repository may be difficult to trace to a specific author. Further, entries in a repository often describe solutions to context-specific problems, and these solutions may fail when certain unstated (and quite possibly unknown) aspects of the customer’s context change. Such entries thus cannot anticipate the enormous range of potential confounds that can occur in other specific customer situations and, as such, often do not provide guidance for adapting instructions to a particular setting nor recovering from situations where it becomes impossible to proceed. There is frequently no ability to dialogue with the entry’s authors [77] to ascertain the importance and applicability of each step to the particular situation facing the analyst. Because adopting knowledge that is transferred in such a context therefore entails greater risk [41], including the risks of inapplicability, delay, or creating new problems, analysts with higher levels of risk aversion will be predisposed against using the knowledge stored in a repository.

H4c: Analysts with higher levels of risk aversion will source less knowledge from a technical support knowledge repository.

## Controls

A key limitation on the potential effectiveness of any IT-based system is its ease of use—that is, the extent to which a system is difficult to use limits the extent to which users will actually incorporate it into their daily routines, irrespective of the power of the underlying functions provided [42]. It follows that one reason why analysts may not source knowledge from a repository is that the technology is not sufficiently easy to use—that is, it may be awkward, slow, or difficult enough to use that analysts may believe that the benefits do not outweigh the costs. The idea that technology varies in terms of its ease of use is well established in the information systems literature [25] and, in this context, would be a reflection of the quality of the software itself. To ensure that the hypothesized effects of the other antecedents of knowledge sourcing identified above are not confounded by software quality issues, we controlled for ease of use as an alternate predictor of knowledge sourcing from knowledge repositories.

A recent study [47] has found that individuals’ job tenure significantly—and negatively—predicted their level of overall knowledge sourcing. This was ascribed to the idea that experienced employees might source less knowledge because they already knew much of what they needed to know to perform well [92]. In technical support environments, there are two possible measures of experience. First, analysts are commonly separated into at least two levels, depending on their degree of experience. Level 1 analysts are the first point of contact with customers who call with problems; they often have less experience and skill at troubleshooting problems and less domain knowledge. If a call cannot be answered by a Level 1 analyst, it is often escalated to the next tier, where a more skilled and experienced Level 2 analyst will attempt to solve it. Of course, within each level, analysts may also vary in experience. We therefore controlled both for analyst level and for job tenure as alternate predictors of knowledge sourcing behaviors. Based on our interviews with analysts and customer support managers, we would expect Level 2 analysts to source significantly less knowledge from a knowledge repository.

## Research Method

THIS SECTION FIRST DESCRIBES THE RESEARCH instrument development and data collection processes. The measurement model is then assessed and research hypotheses are tested using partial least squares (PLS Graph, version 3.00) following the general procedures laid out by Chin [18] and Hulland [55]. Following hypothesis testing, implications for research and practice are discussed.

## Data Collection

To obtain a cross-sectional sample of technical support analysts who had access to knowledge repositories, we approached a leading developer of knowledge repository software. Senior managers at Alpha (a pseudonym) agreed to sponsor this study by providing us with a sampling of their customer list and endorsing our research to customer support managers on that list. Of the 26 managers contacted, a total of seven agreed to participate (27 percent). Through semistructured interviews, we gathered qualitative data about their call center operations, including (1) the nature of the products/services supported; (2) the processes by which calls were distributed, answered, and tracked; (3) the form and structure of knowledge repositories used by analysts; (4) whether other sources of knowledge were available to, and used by, analysts; (5) how new analysts were trained and how analysts were promoted; (6) analysts’ work environment and the nature of the work analysts performed; and (7) the number of analysts and their physical distribution at each site. Call centers ranged from eight to 40 people, for a total of 150 possible subjects for this research. With the endorsement of each manager, we contacted each of these analysts by e-mail and asked them to participate in our Web-based survey.

Managers of each call center confirmed that both Level 1 and Level 2 analysts helped customers by providing technical advice and engaging in advanced troubleshooting. New analysts in these subject companies were put through training regimens ranging from three to six months before being allowed to take supervised customer calls. After proving their abilities under the watchful eye of a supervisor, analysts were permitted to take unsupervised customer calls, and they were also asked to document and submit new entries to the knowledge repository. All of the companies we studied included analysts’ rate of submission of new entries as a criterion used in their annual performance review. These technical support analysts thus differed from other kinds of call center employees who answer relatively simple questions, have little or no training, and follow a standard script for each call.

A cross-sectional survey instrument was constructed following Dillman’s [28] methods. Items measuring learning orientation, intellectual demands, and risk aversion were taken from Gray [46], while items measuring knowledge sourcing from people, documents, and knowledge repositories were adapted from the same study. Items measuring ease of use were adapted from Davis [25], time pressure from Koys and Decotiis [60], and the remaining items were developed. All the data were self-reported by technical support analysts. Following past work in knowledge sourcing [47], we asked respondents to think of knowledge as “expertise, opinions, insights, and experience.” The instrument was first reviewed by ten fellow researchers, and the items were refined through a set of ten card sorts<sup>3</sup> following the general procedures laid out by Moore and Benbasat [70]. The final items used in this study are shown in Table 1.

A total of 110 responses (73 percent) were received within 28 days from respondents at the seven target companies. Respondents ranged from 26 to 64 years old (average 42), with mean job tenure of five years. Thirty-five percent were female. Sixty-eight percent worked as Level 1 analysts (first contact with customer) and 32 percent were Level 2 analysts. Following Podsakoff and Dalton [84], mono-method bias was considered by testing for a common method influence across all responses and none was found. A factor analysis of all items revealed nine factors explaining 71 percent of the variance, with no single factor featuring significant (p < 0.10) loadings for all items. The full set of responses was analyzed using PLS, a structural equation modeling technique that employs principal components analysis, path analysis, and regression to simultaneously evaluate data and theory [73].

## Measurement Model

Prior to entry into PLS, a set of confirmatory factor analyses was used to identify items that loaded poorly on their intended constructs; in total, five items were dropped from the remaining analyses on this basis (COL2, ID2, RA2, TP3, and DOC3). Three common tests of reliability [55] were then performed to assess the strength of the measurement model employed in our PLS analysis. First, the loadings of items on their intended constructs were examined and all were found to be greater than 0.7, indicating that there was more shared variance between a construct and measure than there was error variance [16]. Second, the internal consistency of scales was assessed using composite reliability [93], with all constructs exceeding Nunnally’s [75] 0.7 guideline. Third, average variance extracted (AVE) [37], which measures the average amount of variance that a construct captures from its indicators relative to the amount due to measurement error, was calculated for each scale; all scales exceeded Chin’s [18] guideline of 0.5, meaning that at least 50 percent of variance in indicators is been accounted for by its respective construct. Table 2 provides the results of these measurement model analyses.

To assess discriminant validity, we examined the correlations of items with their intended constructs and found that all items correlated most strongly with their intended construct (see Table 3). We also noted that the square root of AVE for each construct (see Table 2) exceeded all respective interconstruct correlations, providing further evidence of discriminant validity. This provides additional evidence that monomethod bias was not an issue in these data and that multicollinearity was not a potential confound.

In summary, it appears that technical support analysts behave much as knowledge sourcing theory would predict when sourcing knowledge from colleagues, but depart somewhat from expectations when sourcing knowledge from manuals or a repository. While our findings thus provide some support for the importance of understanding learning orientation and intellectual demands as predictors of knowledge sourcing, as advanced in an earlier study, there are some notable exceptions. Time pressure did not predict sourcing from colleagues, suggesting that intellectual demands was the primary work-related driver here. However, neither time pressure nor intellectual demands predicted sourcing from company manuals; it seems that analysts simply did not anticipate a conservation of cognitive effort effect when consulting manuals. Further, analysts apparently did not view these manuals as particularly risky, as risk aversion was not a significant predictor. Finally, the finding that both learning orientation and time pressure were significantly related to sourcing from repositories, but in the opposite direction to that theorized, suggests that these repositories were not useful in helping analysts improve their mental models of the products and services they support. These interesting departures from knowledge sourcing theory are discussed in detail in the following section.

## Discussion

P1 POSITED A POSITIVE EFFECT OF LEARNING ORIENTATION on knowledge sourcing and was supported in these data for knowledge sourcing from colleagues (H1a) and documents (H1b), but the results for knowledge repositories (H1c) were opposite to the direction hypothesized. Given past empirical support for this proposition, this contrasting finding is both surprising and interesting. Recall that theory had predicted that individuals with a higher learning orientation believe more strongly that their knowledge, skills, and abilities can be improved, and are thus more likely to direct attentional resources toward learning behaviors such as knowledge sourcing. The significant negative path coefficient indicates that analysts do not see knowledge repositories as a resource to help them learn and improve their knowledge, skills, or abilities. Indeed, the opposite is true; it is the analysts with the weakest learning orientation—who care least about learning new things—who make the most use of knowledge repositories.

If analysts do not utilize repositories to improve their knowledge, then why do they use these systems at all? One answer is that the knowledge sourced from repositories may not be useful for helping analysts learn more about the products that they must support. This interpretation makes sense in light of the solution-oriented nature of technical support knowledge, where the goal is to lead an analyst of unknown expertise through a series of steps that solve the customer’s problem, without necessarily teaching him or her about why the problem occurred. Repositories of technical support knowledge may therefore focus heavily on what Moorman and Miner [71] term procedural knowledge (how problems are solved) and not provide enough rich description of why the problem occurred or how the solution should be understood in light of the product’s overall functioning.

So, this is generally how a [typical] article would present itself. [Points to areas of screen.] It would give you the solution. If there was a [problem description], that would be listed above. . . . It lists out the details in terms of steps, how to go through each step and, you know, perform the actual task that you want.

—Technical support analyst

It may simply be that analysts are not recording detailed explanations when they input their knowledge about the solution to a problem into a repository. Given the considerable pressure analysts face to process as many incoming calls as possible, some expediency in their recording of solutions seems likely. This kind of expediency has been noted in the knowledge repository literature (e.g., [61, 68]), where extensive documentation of context, or the provision of detailed rationales, are perceived by users to be burdensome, and thus are avoided. As noted by Gruber and Russell, it is difficult to “anticipate the space of questions that might be asked by the reader, and formulate possible rationale explanations in advance” [49, p. 340]. Documenting, in advance, answers to all possible questions and providing all possible rationales may simply be too much to ask of technical support analysts in these conditions.

However, the lack of detailed descriptions may also be a function of demand characteristics. That is, because analysts know that others will need to utilize solutions quickly (as they are often on the telephone with a customer and do not have time to read all about why a problem happened), there may be a natural tendency to omit the “why” and focus instead on the “how-to” in such repositories. Adding extra explanatory detail in a repository entry may thus decrease its usefulness because it slows down the application of the solution by other analysts. Yet such an approach may also introduce new problems, as knowledge that lacks such contextual information may not be fully understood by the reuser [61], which can lead to both problems in application and decrease analysts’ confidence in the quality of the knowledge repository.

This presents a natural conundrum. Putting too much detail lessens the efficiency of the solution but increases both its learning value and the likelihood that it will be correctly understood, and vice versa. It may well be that we have tapped into research settings where norms of knowledge repository use value efficiency over learning. Whether this is optimal, or even desirable, remains an unanswered question for future research. However, Grant argues that “the key to efficiency is to achieve effective [knowledge] integration while minimizing knowledge transfer” [44, p. 114]. Following this logic, analysts are simply bending to the pressures for efficiency that cause them to obtain the products of each others’ knowledge (prescriptive solutions) while minimizing the time spent trying to learn everything known by each other.

I care why [customer problems] happen, but . . . we just need to fix it. It’s not our job to really care why it happens—I don’t care, as long as it’s working, that’s fine.

—Senior technical support analyst

It is useful to note that this effect seems to be at odds with the research that claims one benefit of knowledge repositories to be to “accelerate training” because “accessing knowledge becomes part of everyone’s job” [22, p. 197]. Here, the KM literature apparently endorses both efficiency-through-reuse and effectiveness-through-learning, contrary to Grant’s [44] arguments that knowledge integration and knowledge transfer are distinct processes that have entirely different effects on efficiency and learning. Managers certainly seem to believe that these repositories help analysts learn about the products they support:

[Using the knowledge repository] is a great way to train staff.

—Call center manager

We are trying to give some details about “why”—it’s not just a recipe book.

—Level 2 analyst

We expect that this tension between efficiency and learning will persist as a longterm issue in knowledge reuse—both via technical support repositories and other kinds of knowledge repositories. Here, it may be useful to consider the literature on “explanations” in the knowledge-based systems literature [66], which suggests that incorporating more explanations alongside prescriptive instructions would be of particular benefit to knowledge reusers who are novices. Thus, perhaps the answer to the speed versus learning question depends, in part, on the level of expertise of the reuser involved (e.g., [68]).

P2 and P3 examined alternate conceptualizations of individuals’ hypothesized tendency to reduce their cognitive loads by sourcing knowledge, positing a positive effect of intellectual demands (P2) and time pressure (P3) on knowledge sourcing. Support for P2 was mixed, with intellectual demands predicting sourcing from colleagues (H2a) and repositories (H2c), but not company documents (H2b). This may be a function of the purpose for which knowledge is made available in each instance [68]: both colleagues and repositories provide knowledge that is intended for technical support analysts to use, whereas manuals may be intended for a wide variety of different audiences [78]. As such, it may well be that the knowledge that analysts can find in manuals and other official documents requires added cognitive effort to process, whereas the knowledge provided by colleagues and through repositories does indeed reduce their cognitive loads.

P3 did not hold up to empirical testing. The measure of perceived time pressure offered no support for the idea that simple volume of work, rather than cognitive complexity of work, triggered knowledge sourcing behaviors. Interestingly, a separate and nonhypothesized effect did appear (H3c), where analysts’ perception of time pressure was negatively related to their use of knowledge repositories. The fact that analysts who felt they were under more time pressure sourced less knowledge via the repository (H3c)—but not from people (H3a) or documents (H3b)—suggests that the process of finding and accessing knowledge in the repositories we studied remains too time-consuming. This indicates a problem that may either be technical in nature, dealing with the kinds of search features provided [4], or related to content, where searches may be fast but yield too many candidate solutions of unknown relevance. This latter possibility is consistent with past research that has shown that the possibility of obtaining irrelevant documents is the most commonly named negative outcome associated with the use of knowledge repositories [69, p. 822].

In the demanding call center environment, sources of knowledge that take too long to yield results are thus sometimes avoided:

It is a double-edged sword because you want to take care of your customer first, and sometimes utilizing [the knowledge repository], you know, I may not look in there, and I may go right to a [colleague], and it may have been in [the repository], but I need to get this issue done now, and I don’t want to take the time to look in [the repository]. . . . What tends to happen, with any place that’s busy, it’s like, you just yell around to your coworkers, and say “Do you know?”

—Senior technical support analyst

Finally, our hypotheses regarding analyst risk aversion found reasonably good empirical support; risk aversion appears to influence knowledge sourcing behavior depending on the perceived riskiness of the source. Analysts who were risk-averse tended to source more knowledge from their peers (H4a) and tended to avoid knowledge repositories (H4c), but the effect on documents (H4b) was nonsignificant. It may well be that the validation processes used by many companies (e.g., [41]) to ensure the accuracy of content before it is added to a knowledge repository are insufficient to persuade risk-averse analysts to rely on them. In our research sites, senior analysts or product designers confirmed that newly submitted solutions to problems were likely to be true and accurate; yet, despite this validation process, analysts were still reluctant to rely upon them. It may well be that the perceived quality of the knowledge— analogous to the idea of information accuracy, timeliness, and recency in the information quality literature [5, 27]—is tied into analysts’ reticence. Alternately, it is possible that analysts were unwilling to risk putting considerable time and effort into a search of the repository that yielded no results. This corresponds closely to ideas of search effectiveness [4, 17] and system responsiveness [83], but may also relate to the completeness of the knowledge base [38, 50, 72]; that is, failure to find a result could stem from failures of the technology or could happen because the desired knowledge was simply not there in the first place. Users are likely to be discouraged from using a system that lacks in quantity of content [51] and quality of useful documents [68]. Whether the answer lies in even stronger validation processes (e.g., [52, 94]), superior search capabilities, or elsewhere remains a question for future research. However, future research that considers these aspects of repository design and content should take into account variation in users’ levels of risk aversion, as this may make users particularly sensitive to technical or content shortcomings.

Finally, it is also possible that the general tendency of firms in our sample to omit the names of the individuals who originally submitted new entries to the repository may have decreased analysts’ confidence in the content of these repositories. Knowledge repository research suggests that author names serve as an important signal of quality (e.g., [23, 68, 94]). Perceived riskiness of the repository could thus be reduced by adding author names or by making better efforts to inform analysts of the kind of screening and validation processes (e.g., [94]) that new entries go through prior to being posted for general consumption.

## Implications for Research

This research has applied a knowledge sourcing lens to better understand what causes analysts to source knowledge in technical support environments. It has extended the literature on knowledge sourcing beyond ad hoc, occasional behaviors and into a context where high levels of regular knowledge sourcing are necessary and unavoidable. In the process, our research has uncovered evidence that highlights the difference between transferring knowledge and transferring the products of knowledge. Whereas much of the KM literature treats these two ideas interchangeably ([44] is a notable exception), our findings suggest that repositories containing mainly prescriptive “howto” advice are fundamentally different from other sources that can also answer “why” questions. Organizations that focus on the former at the expense of the latter are clearly focusing on efficiency but possibly at the expense of longer-term learning benefits. Our findings thus bear more than a passing similarity to the old adage cited at the outset of this paper—“Give a man a fish and you have fed him for today. Teach him how to fish and you have fed him for a lifetime” (Laozi). While this aphorism clearly suggests that the latter is preferable to the former, our research has identified some conditions under which the transfer of the products of knowledge is apparently preferable—at least from an efficiency sense—to the transfer of knowledge itself. This distinction may provide a useful analytical device for developing theory to explain these two fundamentally different ways that an organization’s knowledge can be beneficially managed. Note that this is not identical to March’s [67] concepts of exploration and exploitation; rather, we seem to have found evidence for two different kinds of exploitation—shallow exploitation involving the transfer of products of knowledge, and deep exploitation, involving the transfer of the knowledge itself.

Our finding that risk-averse analysts tended to avoid repositories and prefer colleagues offers empirical evidence to support conventional wisdom that argues for the importance of trust in successful KM efforts (e.g., [23]). Given the importance of the risk versus trust trade-off, this may well be a key finding that helps explain the limited use of knowledge repositories in other contexts—although this remains to be demonstrated empirically in those contexts. Further, our finding that trustworthiness (degree of riskiness) can generalize to a repository itself—as opposed to individual contributors—provides empirical evidence for the importance of content management practices (e.g., [94]) for managing user perceptions. However, it also suggests the need for the development of a theory of source credibility (e.g., [90]) as applied to knowledge repositories, which would deal specifically with the technological features and content characteristics that inspire confidence in their use—or, alternately, cause users to distrust them.

More generally, this research demonstrates the value of operationalizing and testing knowledge sourcing theory in specific contexts. As a replication, many of the basic tenets of knowledge sourcing theory were supported—namely, that learning orientation, intellectual demands, and risk aversion affect knowledge sourcing. We have also shown that the general tendencies documented in a past study of knowledge sourcing [47] provide a solid starting point for understanding why technical support analysts choose to obtain knowledge from different sources. It may therefore be useful to continue research in this vein by examining how knowledge sourcing behaviors play out in a variety of specific work contexts to build a cumulative and more complex theory of knowledge sourcing. In a related vein, it may also be useful to understand the extent to which the present findings generalize beyond technical support analysts. Given that the sometimes routine, sometimes fluid, problem-solving environment inhabited by these analysts has strong parallels with other forms of knowledge work that involve both creativity and knowledge reuse, we would expect that the theoretical processes discussed here may also apply more broadly than just in technical support contexts.

## Implications for Practice

Several of our findings have implications for managers who are seeking to improve the acceptance of technical support knowledge repositories in their organizations. First, the trade-off between speed and learning discussed earlier has important implications for the purpose and functioning of knowledge repositories. Given the two apparently conflicting goals of such systems, managers should make an explicit choice of the primary goal of such a system as either efficiency or learning, and treat the other as purely secondary. It seems that companies are faced with the choice between automating and informating [95]: either provide prescriptive knowledge in the form of efficient answers to problems that analysts can quickly follow to solve a customer’s problem or provide more descriptive knowledge that may help the analyst better understand product functioning, but (initially) take longer to resolve the problem at hand. Managers choosing efficiency may focus on goals such as increasing the consistency of responses provided to customers and reducing the amount of time required by analysts to obtain such a response. However, they should do so knowing that an efficiency focus may hamper learning-related goals such as speeding up training and enhancing analysts’ knowledge of the company’s products. Thus, it seems unlikely that the learningful behavior described by El Sawy and Bowles [33] will occur when the focus is on rapid, efficient reuse of prescriptive knowledge.

It seems clear that the speed versus learning issue is not purely a function of repository design, but hinges more critically on organizational goals and the structuring of the work environment. On the surface, a technical solution to this problem is relatively easy—simply structure each entry in the knowledge repository into two sections, namely, a short procedurally oriented solution that links to an optional longer, more detailed description of the underlying causes. Although such a system would reduce the difficulties of wading through detailed descriptions when on the phone with a customer, it would not address the fundamental problems that (1) recording detailed rationales and explanations takes time and is thus often avoided [61], and (2) technical support analysts face a steady stream of calls that may not provide them with the time necessary to read and digest these longer descriptions, even if available. Thus, the issue of managerial choices about the intended purpose of such systems noted earlier—and the effects of those choices on the activities expected of analysts and on the pace of their work—should affect the design of the system.

Another lesson for practitioners that emerges from this research is the role of time pressure: we were surprised to find that a system designed to save time was used less frequently by analysts who felt that they were under significant time pressure than by analysts who did not. It appears that analysts believe that other sources of knowledge provide faster answers. Of course, from a company perspective, the issue is not purely analysts’ time to answer but also the total firm resources (both question asker and question answerer) dedicated to finding that answer. Sourcing knowledge from colleagues therefore may well remain a more expensive—to the organization—option, which places the emphasis back on the efficiency and effectiveness of knowledge repositories. Improvements in search and indexing technology and refinements in how analysts can navigate these systems to find their needed solution may be the answer. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that, under pressure, analysts revert to older—rather than better—patterns of knowledge sourcing. Managers may therefore need to make additional efforts to focus analysts on repositories as their primary source of knowledge under all circumstances, with colleagues as a backup in those situations where the primary source fails.

A related finding highlights the importance of implementing strong and visible validation mechanisms for knowledge contributions as a way of negating the tendency of risk-averse analysts to avoid knowledge repositories. If some analysts doubt the truth value or applicability of the solutions contained in knowledge repositories, then managers will need to put in place better practices to ensure that the content of repositories are both valid (that is, true, correct, and useful) and timely (that is, not outdated). Such efforts will surely increase the cost of each submission into a repository. However, they may also increase the perceived benefits to analysts and thus not substantially damage (or perhaps, even improve) the cost–benefit calculations that analysts contemplate when choosing from among competing sources of knowledge. As an added beneficial effect, this kind of content validation may also help address the negative effect of time pressure noted earlier. In particular, a repository that contains knowledge that is more likely to be both valid and current will take less time to use, and analysts may be more likely to use it when under time pressure. Thus, given that higher-quality content produces a virtuous spiral of increased use and decreased search time that magnifies the benefits of codification efforts, managers should allocate significant resources toward ensuring that repository content is of uniformly high quality.

## Limitations

From a methodological perspective, this research is subject to the anticipated limitations of cross-sectional survey-based research, including an inability to conclusively determine causality and the possibility of common method variance. However, the presence of strong positive, strong negative, and insignificant effects—all among the same small set of variables in these data—is inconsistent with a method effect. Another methodological issue is the use of respondents from across a small number of organizations, all of which used the same repository software, which limits the generalizability of these findings to different populations. Although the knowledge repository utilized by these organizations seems to incorporate many of the features offered by competitors and is not fundamentally unique in what it provides, it may be unique in some unidentified ways. Thus, only repeated replication in other samples can confirm whether these results are truly generalizable.

From a theoretical standpoint, this research is limited in that there are surely other sources of knowledge available to analysts, which we did not assess. And there are other motivational theories whose constructs we did not include in this research. However, although other theories may help explain more of the variance in sourcing behaviors, they are unlikely to invalidate these results.

## Conclusion

THIS RESEARCH HAS DEMONSTRATED THE VALUE of considering the specific knowledge sourcing behaviors undertaken by technical support analysts, and building an understanding of what causes them to choose from among different kinds of knowledge sources. By contrasting analysts’ knowledge sourcing from IT-based repositories against their sourcing from colleagues and other documents, we offer a new way of understanding the utility of these repositories to analysts’ jobs. In the process, we have uncovered a set of antecedents that have markedly different effects on different knowledge sourcing behaviors. The results paint a picture of technical support knowledge repositories that may not be able to enhance both analysts’ speed of response and their learning simultaneously. Regardless of managers’ choices from among these two goals for primary emphasis, our results also underscore the need for strong and public content validation mechanisms and faster and more accurate search technologies. A knowledge sourcing perspective on technical support work thus provides an interesting and useful way of understanding the role of knowledge repositories used by technical support analysts.
