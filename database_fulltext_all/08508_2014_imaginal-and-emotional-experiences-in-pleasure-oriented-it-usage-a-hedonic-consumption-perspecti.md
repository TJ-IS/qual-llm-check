# Imaginal and emotional experiences in pleasure-oriented IT usage: A hedonic consumption perspective

## A B S T R A C T

Based on the marketing theory of hedonic consumption, this study examines the effects of six types of imaginal and emotional experiences on using pleasure-oriented IT. Associated hypotheses are tested using questionnaire responses from 443 online game players. The results provide strong support for the major hypotheses and indicate that the determinants of behavior may differ in using pleasure-oriented versus productivity-oriented IT. This paper contributes not only to the application of a marketing theory to IS research, but also to improved understanding of using pleasure-oriented IT and of why people use technologies that are not particularly useful from a productivity standpoint.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) research traditionally investigates user behavior in the context of productivity-oriented (or utilitarian) systems, such as word processing [1], spreadsheets [3,63], operating systems [48], customer account management systems [85], groupware [77], and electronic tax-filing systems [86]. The identified salient determinants of system-use behavior are primarily perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU), both of which reflect the utilitarian nature of productivityoriented systems [90,82].

PEOU is defined as the degree to which a person believes that using an information system would be free of effort, whereas PU refers to the degree to which a person believes that using an information system would enhance his or her job or task performance [20]. These definitions suggest that PEOU and PU are of importance because the former reduces user efforts and the latter helps improve productivity or performance. Productivity, in general, refers to the effectiveness in bringing forth results or benefits [51]. Applying this definition in the context of information systems, productivity can be defined as the effective use of an information system in bringing forth job- and task-related results.

However, many of the information technologies (IT) integrated into our daily lives have substantial pleasure-oriented qualities and are mainly used for fun and relaxation [10]. Typical examples of such technologies are virtual worlds, online gaming websites, and social networking systems. These technologies aim to provide selffulfilling value to users and are employed in homes and leisure settings for pleasure and breaks from the daily grind [74]. Consequently, IS researchers have expressed concern that the usage of such pleasure-oriented (or hedonic) systems may not be fully explained by PU and PEOU, which are conceptualized as motivators for using utilitarian systems in workplace settings [82,45,52,89].

This concern calls for research to empirically investigate the motivators for using pleasure-oriented IT and the issue of the fit between system-use contexts and research models [74,7].

Such an investigation is of managerial and technical value in two ways. First, it helps researchers ascertain whether the determinants of system-use behavior differ in the context of pleasure-oriented versus productivity-oriented IT [89]. The results of such an investigation can shed light on the issue of whether special system-use models for pleasure-oriented IT are needed. Second, it offers an organization’s system developers valuable guidance about improving their designs of both pleasure-oriented and productivity-oriented systems. Therefore, such an empirical investigation is important not only for scholars eager to identify paths through which behavior manifests but also for practitioners striving to leverage IT to enhance their system results and their business strategies.

Not surprisingly, prior research shows an effort in this direction. In particular, researchers have focused on intrinsic motivators to capture the recreation facet of using pleasure-oriented IT [82,45,52,59]. Two primary examples of such motivators are flow and enjoyment. Nevertheless, IS researchers have paid little attention to theories that may shed more light on using pleasure-oriented IT and have placed even less emphasis on studying other intrinsic motivators, such as escapism and fantasy. Given the increasing importance and popularity of pleasureoriented IT, additional studies that have their bases in relevant and established theories are necessary [82].

The present work is one such study. Specifically, the purpose of this research is to (1) examine the effects of six types of imaginal and emotional experiences on system-use behavior and (2) provide a substantial foundation for follow-up studies on pleasure-oriented IT. This research draws on a key marketing theory—the hedonic consumption perspective—to capture the entertainment nature of the technology of interest. By integrating marketing theory with a traditional technology acceptance framework, our research model reflects that in the context of pleasure-oriented IT, an individual often has a double identity as a consumer and a technology user. We test the research model and hypotheses using data collected from users of online gaming technology, the pleasure-oriented IT examined in this study.

The research described here makes several primary contributions. First, this study extends and advances Van der Heijden’s [82] work by not only classifying systems as productivity- and pleasureoriented but also tracking back to the underlying marketing theory for the classification—the hedonic consumption perspective—and applying it to system-use behavior. Therefore, this paper contributes to the theoretical foundation of IT acceptance. Second, the study reported here uses the theory to focus on six types of imaginal and emotional experiences and to identify key determinants for using pleasure-oriented information systems. Thus, the study contributes to the IS literature by introducing some potentially important but under-studied intrinsic motivators, such as escapism and fantasy. Third, measures of these constructs are adapted from the marketing literature and are further refined during the research process. Thus, other scholars can conveniently employ these measures for future research. Finally, this study finds that in the context of online gaming, a type of pleasure-oriented IT, behavioral intention is predicted by enjoyment but not by perceived usefulness. This finding not only contributes to a further understanding of the fit between system-use contexts and research models but also conveys an important theoretical meaning to researchers and practitioners. That is, pleasure-oriented IT is useful not mainly because of its ability to improve job- and task-performance, as described by perceived usefulness, but rather largely because of its function of providing end users with pleasure, as manifested through enjoyment, escapism, and arousal.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Two types of information technology

In general, information technology can be categorized into two non-mutually exclusive types: productivity-oriented and pleasure-oriented [82,45]. Performing specific information processing jobs for end users, productivity-oriented IT aims to improve individual, group, and organizational productivity. Usually, the technology achieves such a goal by enhancing end users’ abilities to acquire, create, store, retrieve, distribute, and otherwise process knowledge needed for performing relevant tasks, such as decision making [41].

For the purpose of this paper, we define pleasure-oriented IT as information systems that aim to provide end users with pleasure. With such an aim, pleasure-oriented IT often incorporates hedonic elements, such as enjoyable content, animated images, appealing sounds, and aesthetically pleasing visual layouts [41]. Most of this type of IT has been developed recently and is based on the Internet. The current research focuses on online gaming, a technology situated far to the pleasure side. Other very important and popular pleasure-oriented IT includes virtual worlds (e.g., Second Life), video sharing (e.g., YouTube), social networking (e.g., MySpace), and Internet Protocol Television (IPTV).

However, productivity- and pleasure-oriented IT may not necessarily be at opposite ends of the spectrum [15]. Some computer technology may be used for both work and fun [73]. For example, as an enjoyable medium, instant messaging can also be used at work for communication. Therefore, it is reasonable to accept that some information systems can play dual roles in human life, improving productivity and providing pleasure— which, perhaps, can reinforce each other. Fig. 1, adapted from Chesney [15], illustrates the information technologies classified in a two-dimensional diagram of productivity versus pleasure.

## 2.2. The hedonic consumption perspective

During the 1950s, marketing researchers noted that ‘‘People buy products not only for what they can do, but also for what they mean’’ ([58], p. 118), and thus have become concerned with the symbolic aspects of products [29]. To help explain the phenomena observed, Hirschman and Holbrook ([38], p. 93; [39]) developed the hedonic consumption perspective, in which ‘‘the researcher is concerned not so much with what the product is as with what it represents. Product Image, not strict reality, is a central focus.’’ This perspective encourages marketing researchers to view products not as objective entities, but rather as ‘‘subjective’’ symbols. More importantly, it helps in understanding such consumer behaviors as appreciating the arts or watching sports games, which have often been overlooked [42].

According to the perspective, hedonic consumption can be defined as consumption that emphasizes the multi-sensory, fantasy, and emotive aspects of a consumer’s experience with products [38]. By multi-sensory, Hirschman and Holbrook mean ‘‘the receipt of experience in multiple sensory modalities including tastes, sounds, scents, tactile impressions and visual images’’ ([38], p. 92). With such a definition, the hedonic consumption perspective suggests that the main driver of hedonic consumption is the unique consumption experience [46]. This perspective is often used to investigate why an individual forms the intention to consume aesthetic products such as ballet, music, drama, and movies [39,54].

In subsequent work, researchers identified two main types of hedonic consumption experience, namely, imaginal and emotional experiences [55,87]. Imaginal experience refers to the mental activities of imagining things and events that are not perceived as real. Imaginal experience often occurs when sensory information is represented in working memory, and most of the time, it is unspoken, inner, and private [33,60]. Research in psychology indicates that being in an imaginal experience usually motivates people to maintain that experience [32]. Hedonic consumption is tied to imaginal experience because a product may help consumers imagine things and events that they desire [38]. Hedonic consumption research suggests that imaginal experience is comprised of three primary types: fantasy, escapism, and role projection [38,37].

Emotional experience refers to affective states that people experience in relation to specific activities [28]. Emotional experience is often classified as positive (e.g., pleasure) or negative (e.g., sadness) and is examined in terms of frequency, intensity, duration, and stability [13]. Positive emotional experience is often linked to activities that are entertaining and demand less regulation, while negative emotional experience may result from chores that demand more regulation and are less ‘‘fun’’ to do [81]. In general, people intend to regain and maintain positive emotional states and thus are more likely to attend the corresponding activities frequently [32]. Psychologists contend that emotions are central to human functioning, guiding thought, and action and that older people are better at regulating their emotional states [13]. Hedonic consumption involves emotional experience because a product may evoke consumers’ feelings [38,61]. Additionally, prior work on hedonic consumption indicates that emotional experience consists of three major types—emotional involvement, enjoyment, and arousal—and each corresponds to one type of emotional state [54,5,80].

Several studies have applied the perspective to consumption situations. For example, Holbrook et al. [40] investigate the effects of emotional experience on playful consumption, which usually includes leisure activities, hobbies, creativity, games, sports, and aesthetic appreciation. They argue that all such consumption behaviors are intrinsically motivated and involve the expenditure of time on activities that produce enjoyable experiences. The results of Holbrook’s study suggest that emotional experience is positively associated with performance of the activities.

Focusing on music consumption, Lacher and Mizerski [55] examine listeners’ experiences created by new rock music and the influence of their experiences on their intention to later buy the music. Lacher and Mizerski believe that the hedonic consumption perspective is appropriate for their research because music has important emotional and imaginal components and is usually purchased for the experience it creates. Based on data collected mostly from young people (aged 19–24), they find that both imaginal and emotional experiences have an impact on the intention to consume music.

The hedonic consumption perspective has also been employed to study a special hedonic consumption behavior—gambling. Titz et al. [80] build on the perspective to posit that there are relationships between casino play and imaginal and emotional experiences. They argue that the perspective is a good fit for studying gambling behavior because players are mostly motivated by special gambling experiences that they enjoy. Titz’s findings suggest that table game players are more aroused, emotionally involved, and pleased than slot game players.

## 2.3. The hedonic consumption perspective on using pleasure-oriented IT

We contend that the hedonic consumption perspective should be helpful for deepening our understanding of pleasure-oriented system usage for several reasons. First, pleasure-oriented IT is usually a web-based information system that delivers online interactive services (e.g., gaming service and communication service) and/or products (e.g., movies and music). Users of pleasure-oriented IT are thus not simply technology users but also consumers who consume the services and/or products for free or for a fee [52]. Therefore, it is important to study the consumption dimension of technology acceptance in the context of pleasure-oriented IT. Given that the hedonic consumption perspective was developed to study consumer activities, it should fit for this unique group of technology users and provide insight into their behaviors.

Second, the hedonic consumption perspective employs imaginal and emotional experiences to explain behavior. These experiences are likely to capture the pleasure nature of the technology of interest and thus shed light on the key determinants of IT acceptance. This can be buttressed by prior research findings that, in the context of pleasure-oriented IT, the emotional experience of enjoyment is a dominant predictor, whereas traditional determinants, such as ease of use and usefulness, tend to play a weak role [82,45,52,59].

Finally, the hedonic consumption perspective mainly focuses on explaining leisure and recreation activities, which are important because they are as motivating as any other gratifying experience in our lives [72]. Using pleasure-oriented IT can certainly be viewed as such an activity and thus fits this perspective. More to the point, the technology users considered here can be expected to have as much of, if not more than, the same imaginal and emotional experiences as aesthetic product consumers.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Drawing on the hedonic consumption perspective and prior empirical findings, we contend that fantasy, escapism, and role projection are three primary types of imaginal experiences that motivate individuals to use pleasure-oriented IT, and emotional involvement, enjoyment, and arousal are three major types of emotional experiences that also influence system-use behavior. The model (Fig. 2) examines the effects of these experiences on behavioral intention, with the presence of four controls: ease of use, usefulness, subjective norms, and playfulness. Below, we describe and discuss the variables and conceptual links in the research model within the context of pleasure-oriented IT examined in this study: MMORPGs.

## 3.1. Imaginal experience and system-use behavior

Fantasy is defined as the mental activities of building an illusory world or reality that meets an individual’s psychological needs and interests. Fantasy can range from unreal pure fantasy to ‘‘realistic’ fantasy, a carefully prepared plan developed through detailed imagined scenarios [94]. Regardless of the specific fantasy, it is always shaped by an individual’s experiences and stocks of knowledge. Therefore, it is believed that fantasy originates in everyday human activity [94]. Previous research suggests that fantasies are sometimes created by an individual as a response to external multi-sensory inputs [8]. For example, a gambler’s excitement and anticipation when rolling the dice on a craps table has to do with the internal fantasy of what could be done with the winnings [80].

Prior work indicates that fantasy impacts behavior. For instance, Bormann [9] argues that fantasy is important to some actions that help people imagine a desired world or reality. Similarly, Hudson and Murray [46] posit that the more the fantasy is created, the more likely it is that individuals will consume aesthetic products, such as movies and operas. Indeed, in an investigation of the determinants of hedonic consumption, Hirschman [37] provides strong evidence that fantasy is significantly related to the act of purchasing certain products. In addition, focusing on the relationship between experience and behavior in the context of family, Swanson [76] analyzes data from 250 college students and finds that fantasy experience is highly associated with decision making patterns.

Engaging in fantasy involves the development of a desired ‘‘unreality’’ where impossibilities often seem possible [62]. This remedy provides relief for people imagining a world in which they are better placed than they are in reality [36]. Online gaming technology delivers such ‘‘unreality’’ by leveraging key elements: virtual worlds, game story, graphics, sound, and music. These elements immerse players into the virtual reality and make them feel better by relieving them from stress and unhappy experiences. Thus, they are more likely to play continually. This view is in line with the argument that a successful game should present a ‘‘big and real’’ virtual world to which players can attach their fantasies [24]. This view is also supported by Douse and McManus’s [25] finding that fantasy elements are critical to the subsequent play of video games. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. Fantasy has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

Escapism refers to the mental activities that satisfy an individual’s desire to escape unpleasant realities or to distract their attention from problems and pressures. The definition indicates that the desire for escapism is mainly triggered by one’s social situation [35]. When an individual is facing a difficult reality, an unhappy event, or high pressure, that person may have the inclination to retreat from those negative situations through mental diversion. Therefore, escapism can be viewed as resulting from a person’s wish to enter a more desirable state than the one presently experienced [37]. It is believed that in today’s stressful society, escapism is increasingly necessary for many people.

Individuals engage in some behaviors to escape unpleasant realities or to distract themselves from problems and pressures [37]. Such behaviors are often pleasure-oriented, such as playing video games, listening to music, and watching movies or television. Previous research confirms that escapism motivates individuals to purchase music [36]. Previous research also finds that watching television is an activity that is likely to be chosen by individuals wishing to escape from worrisome stimuli or from the demands of reality [53].

Like movies, music, and television, online games are an important avenue of escapism [49]. By presenting an interesting story, impressive graphics, and amazing sound, a MMORPG creates a virtual world in which players solve puzzles, crack problems, and fight aliens to protect endangered human beings. Such mental activities help players escape from the stress of daily life and distract attention from unpleasant events. Thus, they are likely to perceive online gaming as a valuable avenue of escapism and to continually return to the virtual gaming world. This is in accord with Barton’s [6] observation that one important motive for online gaming is to avoid thinking about real-life problems. Consequently, we expect that, just as it impacts the behavior of watching television, escapism also impacts the intention to use MMORPGs.

Hypothesis 2. Escapism has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

Role projection refers to the mental activities that enable individuals to project themselves into particular roles or characters [37]. Role projection is usually related to story-based entertainment such as novels, movies, and operas because such entertainment assists individuals in imagining themselves as one of the characters in the story [38]. Role projection may also be associated with images projected via common products such as cigarettes [38]. For example, a consumer may smoke because he enjoys imagining himself as a ‘‘Marlboro Man.’’

Role projection can be linked to behaviors that provide a role into which an individual desires to self-project. In the hedonic consumption study noted earlier, Hirschman [37] suggests that people may attend movies or theaters because there is a particular role into which they want to project. Focusing on drama in education, Haine [33] also indicates that individuals may appreciate drama because they imagine themselves in the roles of their favorite characters: James Bond, Batman, Cinderella, and so forth. Similarly, Dormans [24] argues that one important reason that video games are so attractive is that they allow players to build an in-game character who determines his own fate by acting within the time and space of a fictional world.

Online games, especially MMORPGs, are likely to have the attribute of role projection because a MMORPG creates a fantasyor reality-based virtual world in which players choose a character and use it to interact with the game. Moreover, MMORPGs are based on a storyline describing the circumstances of events and experiences of in-game characters. These elements enable users to psychologically project themselves into particular roles with which they identify, care about, or admire [11]. As a result, users are likely to be motivated to play more because the mental activity of role projection satisfies their needs and enables them to get away from difficult realities. Following this line of thought, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. Role projection has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

## 3.2. Emotional experience and system-use behavior

Emotional involvement is defined as the emotional state in which an individual is psychologically engaged in a behavior. Emotional involvement can vary not only in intensity but also in duration. In other words, deeper emotional involvement is often indicated by more intense and longer lasting behavior. Moreover, emotional involvement indicates the level of interest evoked by external stimulation [64]. Previous research suggests that emotional involvement usually occurs in entertainment-related behavior [54]. It is also believed that when individuals are emotionally involved in a behavior, they may feel as if they have been ‘‘carried off’’ by it, or as if they have become part of it [55].

Previous research suggests that emotional involvement can be viewed as an important determinant of behavior. Examining the differences between slot machine gamblers and table game players, Titz et al. [80] find that emotional involvement influences gambling behavior in an operating casino environment. In an investigation of consumption emotion in services, Mudie et al. [64] indicate that emotional involvement has an impact on the extent and formality of a shopping decision making process. Lacher and Mizerski [55] look for reasons for music consumption and observe a significant effect of emotional involvement on purchase intention.

Playing MMORPGs is often a fun-related behavior [93]. Thus, it is no surprise that MMORPGs can provide stimulation that helps users to achieve emotional involvement. Such involvement is likely to have a relationship with the intention to use the technology because when users are emotionally involved in their interactions with a system, they are likely to perceive such interaction as appealing and be motivated to use the system more. This is in line with the argument that if computer game players are emotionally involved, they tend to play longer and more often [34]. Thus, we hypothesize that emotional involvement impacts system-use behavior. Formally,

Hypothesis 4. Emotional involvement has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

Enjoyment is defined as an emotional state in which an individual experiences pleasure and joy in performing an activity. Enjoyment can occur not only in the case of physical activities, such as dancing, but also in the pursuit of mental activities, such as playing chess. In addition, enjoyment can be general or situationspecific. An example of general enjoyment would be that a user perceives interacting with computers as fun, no matter what the user is doing on them, while an example of situation-specific enjoyment would be that a user enjoys working with a specific IT tool, such as a blog.

Prior research provides empirical support for a link between enjoyment and behavior. For example, in a study on online shopping, Koufaris [52] finds that enjoyment predicts consumer intention to return to a web-based store. Similarly, Li et al. [59] find that users who perceive the use of instant messaging as enjoyable are more likely to have an intention to continue using it. In their empirical investigation of Internet usage, Teo et al. [79] find that enjoyment has an effect on the frequency of using the World Wide Web. All of these findings are in keeping with the view advanced by Van der Heijden [82]: enjoyment, as perhaps the most important hedonic construct, is believed to play a crucial role in motivating individuals to use pleasure-oriented IT.

Previous research suggests that the most basic goal of online gaming is to derive enjoyment [22]. That is, online gaming is experience-oriented and players are mainly motivated by the positive emotional experience associated with playing. In a virtual game world, players have ‘‘fun’’ solving puzzles and fighting alien species, and experience the joy of figuring out the puzzle and winning the war. Thus, players consider the game as an attractive one and form the intention to re-visit the virtual world. Conversely, if players are bored with the game, they perceive it as having low play value and thus are less likely to continue. As noted by Igbaria et al. [47], individuals who experience immediate pleasure and joy from using an information system are likely to use it more extensively than others. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5. Enjoyment has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

Arousal refers to the state of emotional activation, excitement or alertness elicited by external sensory stimulation. Arousal is important in all emotional and mental functions. It contributes significantly to attentiveness, perception, memory, passion, feeling, and information processing [56]. Without arousal, an individual may fail to notice what is happening because he or she is not attending to the details. Arousal is also crucial for motivating certain behaviors, such as fighting and fleeing. The stimulationarousal relationship is generally presented as a U-shaped curve where the most activating state appears to be in a band between too little stimulation—leading to boredom—and too much stimulation—leading to stress or even panic [31]. Thus, to perform optimally, an individual must have the right level of stimulation.

Like other emotional variables, arousal has an impact on entertainment-related behaviors, such as listening to music, gambling, watching television, and adventure traveling. In their empirical investigation of the responses that new rock music creates in a listener, Lacher and Mizerski [55] suggest that arousal plays an important role in purchase intention. Titz et al. [80] find that arousal is a key determinant of gambling participation for both table game players and slot machine users. Focusing on TV commercials, Alwitt et al. [5] suggest that viewers are likely to watch a commercial through to the end if they maintain a high level of arousal during the course of the commercial.

We argue that the link between arousal and behavior carries over into the context of MMORPGs. That is, if players experience arousal, they are likely to enjoy playing the games and thus form the intention to keep playing. This argument is in accord with the assertion that, by integrating key elements of storylines, characters, graphics, sound, and music, computer games can directly elicit the emotional experience of arousal that prompts people to play [26]. Previous IS research supports this argument. For example, Kim et al. [50] find that in the context of mobile Internet services, arousal indirectly affects intention via attitude. Gill [31] provides empirical evidence that arousal is an important factor motivating the use of expert systems. Therefore, we are led to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6. Arousal has a positive effect on the intention to use MMORPGs.

## 3.3. Behavioral intention and usage

Drawing on the TRA, behavioral intention is the primary determinant of voluntary behavior [4]. TAM also posits that behavioral intention is the key predictor of system usage [21]. Subsequent work related to TAM confirms the significant impact of behavioral intention on usage [77,67]. Following this line of research, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7. User intention to use MMORPGs has a positive effect on the usage of the MMORPGs.

## 3.4. Controls

PU, PEOU, playfulness, and subjective norms are included in the research model as controls because past studies have recognized their potential importance to the use of pleasure-oriented IT. In IS research, playfulness has been studied as a motivational characteristic of individuals and defined as ‘‘the degree of cognitive spontaneity in microcomputer interactions’’ ([88], p. 204). More specifically, it is an individual difference variable and describes a person’s tendency to interact spontaneously, inventively, and imaginatively with an information system. Previous research suggests that behavioral intention may be predicted by playfulness [88,83]. Subjective norm is an individual’s judgment about whether most people who are important to the individual will approve of a particular behavior under consideration [27]. As the definition implies, when people decide whether to perform particular behaviors, they consider the normative expectations of others they view as important, such as friends, coworkers, and schoolmates. In IS literature, subjective norms are recognized as a type of social influence that has an effect on system-use behavior [45,23]. Therefore, playfulness and subjective norms may be critical to the current study and are thus examined as controls. Moreover, as noted earlier, PEOU and PU are two primary determinants of system-use behavior, and thus, they are also integrated into the model as controls.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Survey participants, pilot test, and data collection

To test the research model, data were collected via an online survey instrument that uses multi-item scales to measure each construct. As described in Appendix A, all of the scales were adapted from previous studies. The survey participants of this study were players of three MMORPGs: EverQuest, Star Wars Galaxies, and Matrix Online, all of which are operated by Station.Sony.com. EverQuest is about races in the fictional world of Norrath. Each of its players creates a character by selecting one of the races in the game. A player uses his character to explore the fantasy world and to fight monsters and enemies for treasure and experience points. Like Star Wars movies, Star Wars Galaxies is about galaxy-wide war following the destruction of the first Death Star. Its players choose to be one of the species and professions in the game and use their characters to fight foes from the Star Wars universe or to support those fighters by crafting necessary items or providing entertainment that inspires combatants to fight harder. Like the Matrix movies, Matrix Online is about a war among humans and intelligent machines that created the Matrix to control humans and use their bodies’ heat and electrical activity as energy sources. Its players choose to side with one of three factions (Zionist Humans, Exiles, or Machines) and join the ongoing war against the other two factions.

The sample was recruited from Station.Sony.com, and the email addresses of 6600 players were randomly collected from the website. The meta-analyses by Wu and Lederer [91] and Lee et al. [57] suggest that the lowest average correlation in a TAM model could be approximately .20. Thus, with a desired power value of .80 and a significance level at p < .05, the required sample size for this study is 153 [18]. Past similar online studies with random user populations suggest that the response rate for this study may be approximately 9% [65,77]. Given that our main study used a sample of 5600 players, we thus expected to receive more than 400 useful responses, which is much larger than the required sample size and thus is sufficient for testing the research model.

To pilot test the survey instrument, 1000 players of the three MMORPGs were contacted with an email message explaining the purpose of this study and inviting them to participate. One week later, these 1000 players received another email message thanking those who had participated and reiterating the importance of the study and the value of their contribution. The URL link to the website-based survey instrument was provided in both of the email messages. The invitees were assured that the results would be reported in aggregate and that their anonymity would be guaranteed.

Of the 1000 players, 135 emails were undeliverable. From the remaining 865 pilot test candidates, 83 responses were obtained. Of the 83 responses, three were unusable because their answers were incomplete. Following a factor and reliability analysis of the survey instrument, the items were modified to ensure that they were clear, precise, and easy to understand. Table 2 in Appendix A shows the final survey items.

The same website-based data collection methods and procedures were then applied for the main data collection, except that we sent one additional round of emails to remind players about the survey (three rounds of email messages in total). This covered a five-week period in which 5600 other players were contacted, with 1110 of the emails being undeliverable. There were 462 responses obtained, resulting in an effective response rate of approximately 10.3%. This response rate is comparable to those of similar online studies with random user populations [65,78]. Previous research suggests that the response rate is usual because in such online studies, email communication is the only method for contacting participants, and an online questionnaire is the only means for them to participate in the study [65]. Following Pavlou and Gefen [67], non-response bias is assessed by verifying that respondent demographics are not distinct from those of current MMORPG players.<sup>1</sup> Of the 462 responses, 19 were discarded because the answers were incomplete. Thus, a total of 443 valid responses were used for the data analysis.

Of the 443 usable respondents, 370 were male and 73 were female. Most respondents (87%) were under 40, with the average age being 28. The average years of online gaming experience was 5, with 72% of the respondents had more than 3 years of experience. The average weekly online gaming time of the respondents was approximately 20.6 h, with 47% spending 20 or more hours per week on gaming. Most respondents (75%) had an education level of some college or higher. Finally, home was the dominant location for the respondents (97%) to play online games.

## 4.2. Test of the model and hypotheses

We used Partial Least Squares (PLS) Graph Version 03.00 to test the research model and measure the reliability and validity of data. PLS is a structural equation modeling (SEM) tool widely used in information systems research [17,68].

Before testing the research model, we employed convergent and discriminant validity and internal consistency reliability (ICR) tests to evaluate the psychometric properties of the measures for the twelve latent constructs. The convergent and discriminant validity of latent constructs with reflective indicators can be assessed by using the following criteria: (1) the standardized item loadings (similar to loadings in principal components) should be no less than .707; (2) the items should load more strongly on their respective constructs than on other constructs; (3) the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) by a construct from its indicators should be no less than .707 (i.e., AVE should be no less than .50), and (4) the square root of AVE should be larger than the correlations between that construct and all other constructs [16,19]. Item loadings and cross-loadings can be obtained by correlating construct factor scores with all standardized item scores. AVE values are available in a PLS bootstrapping report.

ICR, also known as composite reliability, is similar to Cronbach’s alpha because both are used to measure reliability [92]. ICR is considered adequate if its value is no less than .70 [19]. ICR values are also available in the PLS bootstrapping report.

Table 1 shows the constructs’ means and standard deviations (SDs), ICRs, square roots of AVEs, and correlations between constructs. The three highest means are for constructs of enjoyment (5.75), behavioral intention (5.56), and ease of use (5.43). This indicates that most survey participants perceive online gaming systems as easy to use, consider their interaction with the systems as enjoyable, and have the intention of using them continually. Only one mean (3.32), which is for subjective norm, is below the middle value of 4. The lowest mean suggests that significant people in the survey participants’ lives tend to believe that the participants should not play online games. The SDs are between 1.21 (emotional involvement) and 1.69 (usage). While the lowest SD suggests a high level of agreement of the participants regarding emotional involvement, the highest SD indicates that they are comparatively inconsistent in their use of online gaming systems.

All ICRs are larger than the minimum reliability criterion (.70). In addition, all square roots of AVEs (on the diagonal in bold) are larger than .707 and in all cases larger than the correlations between that construct and all other constructs. Table 3 in Appendix A shows the loadings and cross-loadings. Except for the fifth item of emotional involvement, all items exhibit high loadings (>.707) on their respective constructs and load more strongly on their respective constructs than on other constructs.<sup>2</sup> Overall, the results provide strong evidence of convergent and discriminant validity, as well as reliability of the measurement instrument.

The hypotheses were tested by examining path coefficients (similar to standardized beta weights in a regression analysis) and their significance levels in the research model. As presented in Fig. 3, the results confirm that escapism, enjoyment, and arousal each has a significant positive effect on behavioral intention, thus supporting Hypotheses 2, 5 and 6. Consistent with Hypothesis 7, behavioral intention has a significant positive effect on usage. Finally, among the four controls, only ease of use has a significant positive effect on behavioral intention to play.

## 5. Discussion

As an important contribution to the literature, this study applies a marketing theory—hedonic consumption perspective—to the behavior of using pleasured-oriented IT. The results indicate that the theory fits well with this system-use context. Thus, the current study enriches the theoretical basis for IT adoption and usage.

## 5.1. Insights into the findings

This study finds that escapism, enjoyment, and arousal significantly influence behavioral intention, but perceived usefulness does not. This finding has two important theoretical implications. First, it suggests that intrinsic motivators, such as escapism and enjoyment, are the most salient drivers for using pleasure-oriented IT, whereas, as evidenced by extant TAM studies, extrinsic motivators manifested through perceived usefulness are the most important drivers for using productivity-oriented IT. In other words, this finding indicates that, when information systems vary from productivity-oriented toward pleasure-oriented, the most important determinants shift from extrinsic to intrinsic motivators. This implication supports the contention that the entertainment nature of an information system may be an important boundary condition for the validity of traditional technology acceptance models [82]. More specifically, these extrinsic-motivator-based models may not be able to tell the whole story, or even the most important part of it, in understanding antecedents of pleasure-oriented system-use behavior.

Second, this finding by no means suggests that pleasureoriented IT is not useful. In fact, it indicates that the mission of pleasure-oriented IT is to please users rather than to enhance their performance in jobs or tasks—the latter being captured by the TAM construct of perceived usefulness. This implication is in line with the notion that pleasure-oriented IT is usually used for the ‘‘fun’’ experiences it creates; people continually use it because they are keen to repeat such experiences. Perceived usefulness is less important because it primarily focuses on a system’s utilitarian nature of improving performance or productivity.

Imaginal experience has been studied in the consumer behavior literature, but has previously received little attention from researchers in information systems. This study finds that of the three types of imaginal experience, escapism predicts behavioral intention but fantasy and role projection do not. This finding could be explained in two ways. First, escapism is a more prominent type of imaginal experience than the other two; most players use MMORPGs primarily for escapism but not for fantasy or role projection. That is, they mainly play MMORPGs to escape from the stress of daily life and distract themselves from the pressures they feel. Second, the MMORPGs examined in this study may excel at providing players with a rich imaginal experience of escapism but not of fantasy and role projection. In other words, the players may be unable to easily attach their fantasies to the games and unable to psychologically project themselves into particular roles in the games.

This study also finds that of the three types of emotional experience, enjoyment and arousal both predict behavioral intention but emotional involvement does not. One possible explanation for this finding is that enjoyment and arousal are more prominent types of emotional experience than is emotional involvement, and thus, they are relatively more effective in predicting behavior. In other words, enjoyment and arousal are two more optimal emotional states for playing MMORPGs than is emotional involvement. This explanation is supported by the results reported by Sparks [70]. Sparks finds that enjoyment and arousal are both excitation states that are the key determinant of entertainment-related behaviors.

While Dickinger et al. [23] and Hsu and Lu [45] suggest that subjective norms have an effect on behavioral intention, we find no evidence that subjective norms significantly influence system-use behavior. Our finding is, however, in accord with the findings reported in other entertainment-oriented IT studies (e.g., [43,69]). One possible reason for this insignificant result is that subjective norms do not affect continued behavior by users who have already accumulated extensive experience in using the system [43]. Recall that our subjects are players with more than 3 years of gaming experience, on average. Pressure from others may be critical only in the initial usage of the system; the importance of the pressure subsequently decreases as regular system usage is established [69]. Another possible reason is that the effect of subjective norms on behavioral intention may be attenuated by the contradictory pressures received from peer players, who likely encourage gaming, versus from parents or spouses, who may view the activity as a waste of time.

This study does not find evidence that playfulness significantly influences behavioral intention. One possible explanation for this result is that playfulness may mainly indirectly influence behavioral intention through perceived ease of use. A brief review of past research provides consistent evidence for this explanation. For example, Venkatesh [83] and Venkatesh and Bala [84] conceptualize playfulness as a key determinant of PEOU, which, in turn, predicts behavioral intention. Their results provide strong empirical support for this conceptualization. Similarly, Sun and Zhang [75] empirically demonstrate that PEOU mediates an indirect effect of playfulness on behavioral intention to use the Internet. Notably, this explanation is also in accord with the current study’s results showing that playfulness is significantly correlated with PEOU (r = .32) and that PEOU has a significant effect on behavioral intention.

## 5.2. Implications for practitioners

The results of this study provide important implications for practitioners. Our finding that a rich user experience is a key driver of playing MMORPGs suggests that vendors of MMORPGs need to make such experiences possible for online game players from the outset. Specifically, when they are designing and developing MMORPGs, vendors should pay particular attention to the elements that will evoke the experience and incorporate as many of these elements into the games as possible. These elements often involve innovative features, animated images, and appealing sounds and music. For example, an online game avatar may be designed to have a photo-realistic face with slight dynamic changes in expressions. Those changes, such as subtle smiling and blinking of the eyes, are non-controllable by users but are employed to enhance the avatar’s liveliness and thus the players’ gaming experiences.

Additionally, these elements are also of great value to online vendors of other pleasure-oriented technologies, such as virtual worlds and instant messaging (IM). These systems usually offer the advanced feature of allowing a user to have a display image (either a picture or an avatar). This display image may not be the picture of the user but of someone that the user identifies with, cares about, or admires. Therefore, the feature provides the opportunity for imaginal experience and thus helps the user escape from problems and pressures. As a result, the user is likely to view systems with such a feature as attractive and to continue using them.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Although the data support the hypotheses that escapism, enjoyment, and arousal impact behavior, interpretation of our results may be subject to limitations. One is that self-selection bias may have affected the results [67]. Our sample includes active players but not former players who have quit playing the MMORPGs. This truncated population sample may have exaggerated the power of the paths in the model. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as explaining only the behavior of current players. Whether the results can be generalized to former players requires additional research.

A second limitation is also related to the generalizability of the results of this study. The target information system in this study is the pleasure-oriented IT of MMORPGs and, consequently, the research model and hypotheses are tested in this specific system-use setting. Therefore, the generalizability of the model and its findings to other types of pleasure-oriented IT may be limited.

A third limitation relates to the survey participants. Of the 443 useful surveys, approximately 83.5% came from male players. This indicates that males are much more likely to be fans of MMORPGs featuring fighting and shooting. The dominance of male respondents may have affected the results of the study. Thus, caution should also be taken here.

Consistent with the findings of Agarwal and Karahanna [2] and Van der Heijden [82], the results of this study show that ease of use remains a dominant predictor of intention. Gefen and Straub [30] argue that ease of use relates to assessments of the intrinsic characteristics of IT and typically affects system-use behavior when users are intrinsically motivated. Because playing MMORPGs is usually intrinsically motivated, the argument thus fits our observation and explains our finding regarding ease of use. However, Koufaris [52] finds no significant effect of ease of use on intention, and Li et al. [59] argue that ease of use is not a kev determinant and thus exclude it from their research on pleasure-oriented IT. Other prior studies include ease of use but do not test its impact on behavior (e.g., [45,23,44]). Given these inconsistent findings, future research should further examine the role of ease of use in pleasureoriented IT.

Although this study used important variables as controls, it is not feasible to use all of them without making the model cumbersome and blurring our primary research objective. One example of such an important, but excluded, variable is flow. As a composite construct, flow has four dimensions, and its most important dimension is enjoyment [52]. The current study does investigate enjoyment and thus likely captures the key aspect of flow. Nevertheless, to better understand flow, future studies should examine its role as a whole in the context of pleasureoriented IT. Moreover, it will be beneficial, both theoretically and practically, to conduct studies on the predictive power of each dimension of flow.

In line with previous studies on flow, the current study suggests that theories other than TRA, TPB, and TAM may be necessary and important to improve our understanding of pleasure-oriented system-use behavior. Additional work is thus encouraged to identify more and useful theories and to apply them to IT acceptance and usage. Such theories are usually developed in other disciplines, including psychology, sociology, marketing, and management. Further work in this direction may enhance not only the theoretical basis but also the research model for technology acceptance.

Given the global nature of the Internet, it is necessary to investigate whether cultural differences between countries impact the usage of pleasure-oriented IT. Srite and Karahanna [71] find that national culture moderates the relationship between perceived ease of use and behavioral intention. Chau et al. [14] find that people in different countries use the Internet for different purposes that may lead them to form different impressions of the same websites. In light of this finding, future research should test the effects of imaginal and emotional experiences in different cultures and compare the results.

## 6. Conclusion

This study applies a marketing theory—hedonic consumption perspective—to the context of pleasure-oriented IT and examines the effects of imaginal and emotional experiences on system-use behavior. The results suggest that the determinants of technology acceptance may differ in using pleasure-oriented versus productivity-oriented IT, yielding a key theoretical contribution by determining why people use technologies that, from a productivity perspective, are not useful. As such, this research constitutes an important step in investigating pleasureoriented IT and provides a substantial foundation for follow-up studies.
