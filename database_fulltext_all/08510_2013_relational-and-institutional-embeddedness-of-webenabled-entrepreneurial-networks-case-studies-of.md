# Relational and institutional embeddedness of Web-enabled entrepreneurial networks: case studies of netrepreneurs in China

Chrisanthi Avgerou\* & Boyi Li<sup>†</sup>

\*London School of Economics – Department of Management, London, UK, email: c.avgerou@lse.ac.uk, and <sup>†</sup>London School of Economics, London, UK, email: b.li2@lse.ac.uk

Abstract. Drawing from a multidisciplinary literature, we form a theoretical perspective of the social embeddedness of economic activity as interpersonal ties that are formed in relation to the institutions of an economy. From this perspective, we then seek to explain the emergence of networks of micro-entrepreneurs that conduct their business on the Taobao Web platform in China, and we derive theoretical propositions about the social embeddedness of Web platform-based economic activity. Two case studies of such networks show how Web platform tools and services create conditions of possibility for developing online business by micro-entrepreneurs and for overcoming cultural reluctance of economic exchange with strangers. Our research suggests that economic activity on Web platforms is embedded in a mix of virtual and community-based relations that are shaped by and alter the behavioural norms of a local culture. It also points to the government and the corporate Web platform service provider as important organisational actors enabling the creation of entrepreneurial networks.

Keywords: Web platform, social embeddedness, institutions, China, Taobao, netrepreneurs

## INTRODUCTION

There is currently a new wave of optimism among Information and Communication Technologyfor Development (ICT4D) activists and scholars about the potential of ICT to enable development. It stems mainly from advancements on two technology fronts: the wide diffusion, beyond expectations, of mobile phones even in the poorest regions of the world (ITU, 2011, p. 603) and emerging Web-based platforms that enable economic and social interaction beyond local communities (Heeks, 2008; Thompson, 2008; Heeks, 2010; Smith & Elder, 2010). The developmental significance of both these technologies is associated with their capacity to facilitate networking among producers, traders and remote customers. Such networking is widely recognised as a fundamental mechanism of the contemporary global economy (Castells, 1996).

However, extrapolation from the affordances of technologies is a poor method for predicting ICT-enabled socio-economic changes. Studies of the use of mobile phones and the internet in developing countries suggest nuanced processes of accommodating them in socio-economic activity according to local institutions and social norms (Overå, 2006; Chen et al., 2007; Donner, 2007; Molony 2007; Martinsons, 2008; Molony, 2009). Thus, the motivation for ou research is to understand whether and how new Web-based technologies enable the development of livelihood-making business in developing countries. Insofar as economic activity is socially embedded, we need to address the question of how Web-based economic relations acquire qualities of social ties that economic sociology and organisational theory suggest as being important for business and economic success. The concept of social embeddedness is subject to continuing research regarding internet-based business practice and exchanges, but findings in information systems research are contradictory and inconclusive. (Fowler et al., 2004; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Moreover, to address the conditions of economic activity in developing countries, we believe we need a broader concept of social embeddedness that associates interpersonal relations with underpinning institutions within which particular forms of social relations of economic activity become possible, meaningful and effective.

Empirically, we seek to explain the emergence of networks of micro-entrepreneurs who conduct their business on the Taobao Web platform in China. In a very broad sense, techno organisational formations of Web platform-based business networks are cases of e-commerce. Expectations in the 1990s that e-commerce would allow developing countries to participate in global business networks led to disappointment (Kenny, 2006). But Web platforms that appeared in the last 10 years possess distinctive features that create conditions of possibility for sustaining networks of economic activities in developing countries which were not commonly available in e-commerce before. Importantly for the purposes of our research, Web platforms, such as the Taobao platform that we study in this paper, provide tools for social interaction and involve enabling institutional actors that open new possibilities to foster micro-entrepreneurial activities. Particularly important for entrepreneurs is the availability of multiple services that support communication, collaboration and transactions with remote business partners and customers, as well as business software applications as a service. Moreover, Web platforms such as Taobao aggregate information content produced by exchanges between enterprises and their suppliers and customers, thus creating value by harnessing customer-generated data (O’Reilly, 2007; Han, 2011).

The entrepreneurs in our study draw eclectically from the Taobao platform tools, data and value-adding services to develop their business<sup>1</sup>. They also benefit from off-line support they get from the corporation of Taobao. We examine how this Web platform opens opportunities for building and enhancing a livelihood-making business within the institutional setting of the emerging economy of China and what particular services are selected and blended with traditional social relations and institutions to create locally sustainable economic activity. We study two networks of micro-entrepreneurs that have been created on the Taobao platform. We refer to these micro-entrepreneurs as ‘netrepreneurs’, a term that we found widely used in China and which already exists in the international literature, albeit without a clear definition (Lowery et al., 1998).<sup>2</sup>

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: In the next section, we derive theoretica propositions on the social embeddedness of economic activity drawing mainly from the fields of industrial networks and economic sociology. We then present Taobao as a corporation and Web platform and the two case studies of netrepreneur retailers and furniture makers. Our narrative traces the profile of the netrepreneurs and their interactions with business partners, service providers and supporting institutions. In the analysis section, we examine the socia embeddedness characteristics of the Web-based economic activities of the two cases. Finally, in the discussion and conclusions section, we draw attention to the complementarily of virtual and community relations and to the significance of the Web platform service provider in the formation of netrepreneurs’ networks. Also, we comment on the generalisability of our findings and limitations of this research.

## THE EMBEDDEDNESS OF ECONOMIC ACTIVITY NETWORKS

Entrepreneurship is defined as the creation of new enterprises. In developing countries, entrepreneurial ventures take the form of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), often micro-enterprises, which tend to specialise in labour intensive sectors.

## Entrepreneurial networks

Very small scale entrepreneurs are unable to achieve economies of scale in the purchase of equipment and raw material, face difficulties in acquiring finance and consulting services and have limited capacity to develop their business through technology innovation, market intelligence and professional management capabilities. These problems are overcome to some extent by forming networks of suppliers, competitors, customers and service providers. Although such networks tend to be concentrated geographically and have been studied extensively as regional clusters (Martin & Sunley, 2003; Rocha, 2004; Boschma, 2005), the significance of co-location has been recently contested, not least because firms increasingly use internet tools for communication and knowledge sharing<sup>3</sup> (Rocha, 2004; Boschma, 2005). What is uncontroversial and continues to be elaborated in the interdisciplinary literature on entrepreneurial networks is the significance of the dense relations of competition and collaboration among the multiple economic actors of industrial networks (van Dijk & Rabellotti, 1997; Cassiolato et al., 2003; van Dijk & Sverrisson, 2003; Ceglie & Stancher, 2008).

Networks are understood to result in ‘collective efficiency’ larger than the sum of the efficiency of individual firms and thus the creation of regional competitive advantage conducive to growth (Schmitz, 1995). When embedded in industrial networks, entrepreneurs in developing countries benefit from competition among suppliers and service providers, a pool of skilled labour, business associations and policy inducement such as tax incentives and favourable finance (Parrilli, 2009). Networks foster motivation for business success, reward reputation for fair practice and create social norms for reciprocity and cooperation (Stewart & Ghani, 1991; Rabellotti, 1997). They also play an important role in formal and tacit knowledge sharing and learning through the day-to-day interaction of multiple economic actors (Lundvall, 1992; Maskell & Malmberg, 1999). In short, entrepreneurs who are embedded in industrial networks gain capabilities to develop their business which they could not achieve in isolation.

## The meaning of social embeddedness

Theoretical views of the social embeddedness of economic activity in networks have been elaborated across disciplines (Polanyi et al., 1957; Granovetter, 1973; Granovetter 1985; Powell, 1990; Burt, 1992; Uzzi, 1997). The concept of social embeddedness suggests that economic activity needs to be understood in the context of the interpersonal relations and institutions that sustain it. Taking into account the way economic and non-economic activities are interrelated within their social context significantly alters the perspective of atomistic rationality of maximising individuals’ economic utility. Sociality explains why networks of smaller firms achieve competitive advantages and manage to survive in competition with large corporations (Young, 1928; Coase, 1937; Richardson, 1972; Teece et al., 1997; Penrose & Pitelis, 2009).

In contrast to the ‘under-socialised’ arm’s length economic relations studied in neoclassica economics, studies of industrial networks suggest that economic agents are profoundly socia in their economic thought and action (Granovetter, 1985; Granovetter 2005). Industrial networks thrive because they form complex socio-economic relations and are rich in ‘socia capital’, that is, exchanges are based on trust, reputation and community norms, which enable collective market advantage and innovation capabilities (Coleman, 1988).

In a study of arm’s-length ties and embedded ties in industrial networks, Uzzi (1997) showed that, by sustaining embedded relations, industrial networks regulate the expectations and behaviours of exchange partners via three mechanisms: trust, transfer of fine-grained information and tacit knowledge and joint problem-solving arrangements. These mechanisms address the problems of opportunism, uncertainty and complexity in arm’s length economic exchange. They are developed through face-to-face multiplexed interaction with both economic and social dimensions, require reciprocity, voluntary contributions and altruism and bear substantial costs to exit.

The interpersonal relational ties highlighted in Granovetter’s and Uzzi’s notion of social embeddedness are co-constituted with the institutions of their milieu (Polanyi, 1957; Krippner, 2001; Ghezzi & Mingione, 2007; Owen-Smith & Powell, 2008). They are shaped by and contribute to the constant changing of their historical, spatial and cultural setting of economic activity. In contemporary economies, the social context of economic activities comprises behavioural norms that stem from local culture and institutions that structure economic exchange in markets and hierarchies (Williamson, 1975; Thornton, 1999). Thus, observed over-reliance on interpersonal relations in developing countries has been explained by the existence of weak institutions for market exchange and business relations among strangers (Peng & Luo, 2000; Fafchamps, 2004; Martinsons, 2008).

The literature suggests a variety of organisational forms of industrial networks within which small enterprises can be developed and sustained. Influential examples include the cluster of small manufacturing firms in the region of Emilia Romagna in Italy, which has been formed in a socio-cultural environment of community trust and collectivist socialist ideology and is facilitated by the local government (Pyke et al., 1990); the cluster of SME suppliers to car manufacturing corporations in Germany (Cooke & Morgan, 1994); and the cluster of high-tech start ups in Silicon Valley, which is sustained by a culture of mobility and innovation and linked to local universities (Saxenian, 1994).

## The social embeddedness of internet-based networks

Foundational studies on social embeddedness assumes that social ties are formed through face-to-face communication networks bounded in place and through common history (Granovetter, 1985; Uzzi, 1997). Published research on internet-based networks provides mixed conclusions about the nature of their social embeddedness. Fowler et al. (2004) argued that the relations afforded by networks initiated and maintained through the internet, which they cal ‘virtually embedded ties’, are different from both arm’s length and socially embedded relations of economic activity. Such networks address the exchange problems of opportunism, uncertainty and complexity by providing digital means for transparency, widespread sharing of private and public information and community-based voluntary problem solving. In contrast, research on internet-based relations among professionals and economic actors by Wasko & Faraj (2005) and Schultze & Orlikowski (2004) found supporting evidence for the continuing significance of Uzzi’s mechanisms of interpersonal relations.

Less well understood is the institutional embeddedness of economic activity conducted via the internet. Martinsons’ (2008) study of online retailers in Beijing and Shanghai explained the slow take up of online commerce in comparison to the USA by highlighting the lack of rule-based institutions and organisations that are necessary for impersonal exchange transactions. Martinsons observed that China has not historically developed legal frameworks for commerce, and that economic activity came to rely heavily on interpersonal relational trust. Of interest to us is how, in such a context, Web-enabled entrepreneurial activity is formed by combining three organising elements: technology-mediated relations, traditional interpersonal relations and formal organisations.

In conclusion, from this review of social embeddedness of networked economic activity, we identify two lines of investigation for the case studies that examine:

1 the way Web platforms assist in forming and sustaining social relations of trust, information sharing and joint problem solving among economic actors, and

2 the way business development and market exchanges of Web platform-enabled networks are influenced by culture and are linked with the organisational structure of the economy

## RESEARCH METHOD

China’s economy has been growing fast in the last three decades, but there are still more than 150 million people living with under 1.25 US dollars a day. Economic inequality across regions, as well as within even the fastest growing regions, has been a persistent problem. Economic growth owes much to SMEs, which by 2005 accounted for 58.5% of the country’s GDP and 75% of urban employment.<sup>4</sup> Most SMEs in China are labour intensive and low tech, finding it difficult to raise finance and innovate. In this context, the two networks of netrepreneurs that we study in this paper are remarkable because they conduct their business by advanced digital applications and data services.

The research method is interpretive case study. We collected data on two networks of netrepreneurs through ethnographic interviews, which is a data collection technique that studies subjects in situ, accompanying interviews with ethnographic observation. (Denzin & Lincoln, 2000; Heyl, 2001). We video recorded most visits at the sites of the netrepreneurs. Appendix 1 shows the most important data sources for each case study. In addition, we interviewed 13 managers (32 hours in total) in the e-business and e-commerce companies Alibaba and Taobao to understand their role in the development of the netrepreneurs business.

We identified the Yiwu case during a study of the Taobao company. Interviewees there brought to our attention the concentration of individuals who successfully make a living from online commerce in this city. The Chinese author first met and interviewed netrepreneurs from Yiwu in 2009 in a Taobao conference in Hangzhou. That was followed by three field trips. The first trip involved one week ethnographic interviews by the Chinese author. In the second trip, both authors spent one day with selected netrepreneurs. Few interviewees spoke English, and the Chinese author also acted as translator. However, sharing observations was important for developing a common interpretation of the case study. In the third field trip, the Chinese author spent in Yiwu another week conducting additional ethnographic interviews.

The Dongfeng village case study was identified by the Chinese author from the local press. We started data collection by approaching the government of the town of Shaji, which supervises the industrial development of Dongfeng. The official we interviewed there put us in touch with the owners of furniture-making firms. The same three stages of field trips conducted for the Yiwu case were followed in the research of the Dongfeng village, with the second visit involving both authors.

Theory development, data collection and interpretive analysis of the two cases progressed incrementally. We started with an espoused general theoretical position that economic activity is socially embedded. An exploration of existing literature led to a view of embeddedness as comprising both social relations and underpinning institutions, as outlined in the previous section of this paper. Our empirical research was then oriented towards identifying the nature of these two components of embeddedness in cases of economic activity based on Web platforms.

The two case studies of entrepreneurial networks unfolded in parallel, and in the analysis of each case we reflected on the salient findings of the other. During the first two data collection trips of each case study, we collected stories and observations on the day-to-day business of typical entrepreneurs, including descriptions of their history, their day-to-day business activities, supporting technologies and services and relations with customers, service providers, suppliers, banks and government agencies. From these stories and observations, we developed overall narratives for the formation and growth of the two netrepreneurs’ networks. Then, through reflection and discussion, we identified in the narratives of the cases the ways that online tools and services complemented face-to-face social relationships and the maior institutional actors upon which the netrepreneurs' business development has relied. We thus formed tentative theoretical propositions about the combination of online and face-to-face communications in economic activity that is conducted through Web platforms and about the significance of the service provider corporation and the government for the netrepreneurs’ business development. In the third field trip, we revisited and extended our data sources, with the aim to clarify meanings in the original stories of our interviewees and fill gaps concerning the relational and institutional forms of each of the two networks’ embeddedness. We thus confirmed and refined our understanding of the embeddedness of Web platform-enabled entrepreneurial networks and shaped the theoretical arguments that we present in this paper.

## THE TAOBAO WEB PLATFORM

Over 90% of the micro-enterprises we studied are operating on the Web platform of Taobao, a company that is part of the Alibaba Group. Alibaba was established in 1999 as a B2B company, and in 2003 it launched Taobao as a C2C company. Since then Taobao has grown impressively, and by July 2011 it had 370 million registered users and accounted for three out of four of online sales in China (Economist, 2011). Although the two companies serve different segments of demand, they share a multiplex of platform technologies and services. Sellers and customers can access a host of Alibaba software on the Taobao platform, such as Alipay for online payments and Aliwang-wang for instant messaging.

In its basic function, the Taobao platform is a free of charge virtual market place. It also provides software and data services for sellers to run a business. These include software to setup online retail shops and to present merchandise with descriptions and pictures, instant communication tools to develop customer relations and marketing services on Taobao sites. Chinese online buyers like to communicate with sellers to query the qualities of their merchandise, negotiate prices before making a deal and chat. The inbuilt instant messaging and VoiP service, Aliwang-wang, was so much appreciated by online buyers that it quickly set Taobao ahead of its competitors (Chen et al., 2007; Ou & Davison, 2009). Similarly important for the netrepreneurs’ business is the online payment system, Alipay, with an escrow function that withholds payment until goods have been received. Customers are encouraged to rate the sellers’ services and products and to post reviews. Such ratings are aggregated and managed by Taobao to produce overall indicators of trustworthiness that enable Chinese consumers to overcome their reluctance to trust strangers and to buy from unknown sellers.

There is a host of other online services available on Taobao for online entrepreneurs, such as social networking tools, wikis and assessment of entrepreneurs’ credibility to allow them to get bank loans (see Appendix 2). One of the biggest assets of the company is the huge amount of data it collects from those using its platforms: ‘(h)alf a billion credit histories’ according to The Economist (2011). Taobao already provides analytics for identifying market trends and consumer preferences, and these data and processing capabilities are a major competitive strength of the company.

## RETAILERS’ NETWORK IN YIWU

Yiwu City has a population of about 1.2 million and is located in central Zhejiang Province, about 100 km south of the provincial capital, Hangzhou. It is nationally and internationally renowned for its wholesale market, a compound of more than 16 000 wholesale businesses selling all kinds of consumer goods. Since 2005, some of the neighbourhoods of the city have been attracting retailers who trade electronically. Proximity to the wholesale market is a deceptively obvious reason for such concentrations of netrepreneur retailers in Yiwu. Many netrepreneurs do not source their business from the city’s wholesale market. We studied the neighbourhood of QingYanLiu, which currently hosts more than 1000 online micro-enterprises.

At present, everything in this community of entrepreneurs is in small monetary numbers: the value of stock, the prices of products traded, the cost of services and their earnings. Yet, our interviewees were confident that they had prospects for growth.

## A typical retail netrepreneur in QingYanLiu

Lin, now in her early 30s, was born in Yiwu but left after secondary school to study in Shanghai. She developed an interest in health food, natural ingredients and substances used in tradi tional medicine and cosmetics and located a source of such products in Thailand. In 2007 she solicited the help of a professional graphic designer to set up her site, ‘You You Life Pavillion’, on Taobao and started online trading based in QingYanLiu.

She runs her shop from her one bedroom flat with six sales assistants and the help of her family. The bulk of her trade is currently cosmetics, but she is continuously exploring other product lines – in 2010 she was testing a brand of instant coffee blend from Sumatra which she believed had good prospects in the Chinese market.

Lin maintains contact with her customers through the instant messaging and chat services of Taobao and uses Alipay to handle credit card and bank payments. She uses a sophisticated array of online visibility and reputation tools to promote her online shop. She pays Taobao a fee to have advertisements displayed on Taobao sites related to products similar to the ones she is selling. Her reputation in Taobao is critical for attracting customers. She relies on Taobao’s customer rating service for online shops, which is based on a number of criteria, such as on whether the site occupies un-auctioned keywords (popular keywords are auctioned in Taobao, and the price paid determines the order of appearance in a customer search), on the quality of her Web pages and on the frequency with which they are updated. Services to maintain pages have become another area of entrepreneurial activity in Yiwu, and the streets of the city are littered with makeshift notice boards displaying handwritten notices from people who offer to help netrepreneurs improve their online reputation.

Lin uses a number of other local products and services to fulfil customer orders, such as packaging materials and logistics. These small businesses have appeared, multiplied and grown in tandem with the number of netrepreneurs active in the city, and many of them have only netrepreneurs as their customers.

## The local e-commerce college

Many of the QingYanLiu netrepreneurs were trained in the Yiwu Industrial and Commercia College (YICC) which specialises in business and commerce education, offering courses on al topics that would normally comprise a business administration curriculum. The college offers extensive training options on netrepreneurship, including hands on experience on the Taobao platform and services. A major part of the programme is an exercise to set up an online shop. Students are required to identify potential products to sell both on- and off-line and are assessed on the basis of their enterprise performance. Often, they source their products from wholesalers and manufacturers on the Alibaba platform who welcome the opportunity to have their products distributed by netrepreneurs such as Yiwu College students. In essence, students often graduate as netrepreneurs with their own start-up running.

## The Yiwu e-business association

In 2007, three local netrepreneurs founded the e-business association of Yiwu on the social network site of Taobao. Initially, virtual networking provided a forum for discussion and sharing of experience, also attracting members from outside Yiwu. With time, though, the association became more of a local off-line rather than a virtual community. With assistance from the local government it acquired office space in QingYanLiu and recruited YICC students for the administration of its online and off-line activities. Among the key off-line activities of the association are ceremonial social events and e-business training sessions in collaboration with the Taobao company and YICC. Social events are well attended and valued as opportunities for meeting friends face-to-face, for dating and for popular sports tournaments. Such socializing is particularly important for the ‘new Yiwuers’, that is, members of the association who moved to Yiwu from different provinces of the country as students of the college and then set up their online shops in the city, or came directly in search of business opportunities.

The association gives the netrepreneurs an official status that facilitates their contact with local government, such as urban planning and tax offices. The association enjoys the support of the Yiwu municipal government, which is keen to maintain and enhance the commercial hub status of the city. It is also recognised and assisted by Taobao, receiving attention when members have problems of service and being invited to trade fairs in various locations in China – a premium Taobao service which is not available to individual netrepreneurs.

## THE NETWORK OF FURNITURE MAKERS AT DONGFENG

The village of Dongfeng is located in the northern part of Jiangsu Province and has about 1200 households. Supervised by the township government of Shaji, Dongfeng is a typical Chinese peasant community, with the majority of the population traditionally engaging in agriculture. Jiangsu is among the most affluent provinces in China but has a regional economic divide. The southern part of the province has the country’s most dynamic industrial clusters bordering Shanghai and Zhejiang Province but the northern part, where Dongfeng is located, lacks economic connections with the south and is lagging behind in industrialisation.

In 2008, several farmers from the village started producing and selling over the internet ready-to-assemble wooden furniture. Since then, over 300 Taobao-registered online shops have been created in the village, and collectively achieved a sizable capability in this manufacturing sector. According to a local government official in Shaji, in 2010 the overall annua revenue of the town’s furniture online shops was over 50 million CNY (around 8 million US dollars).

## The producer netrepreneurs

A key person in the success story of the Dongfeng furniture makers’ network is Sun Han, a young man who started the first ready-to-assemble furniture business in the village in 2007. He had spent a couple of years at a mobile telephone company in a city near the village, during which he developed the idea for a business such as the Swedish IKEA, making and selling ready-to-assemble wooden furniture at a price and style suited for the Chinese market. Such furniture making could be sourced from local wood production and be sold over the internet through a Taobao shop. He needed a large enough range of products and a critical mass of production to maintain an online presence, and this could be achieved by the engagement of other families from the village in the making of furniture kits. Indeed, with the support from the local government many others followed Sun Han’s example and set up ready-to-assemble workshops and Taobao shops. Most of them are young men and women, between 20 and 30 years old, with a modest level of education. They started their business with minimal formal training and learned from each other how to run a business on Taobao. In 2009 Sun Han’s business had a turnover of three million CNY, making him the most successful Taobao netrepreneur in the region of the Shaji township.

## The local government

The creation of this network of netrepreneurs owes a lot to local government support. Since the introduction of the market economy in the 1980s and following central government policy, the Shaji township government has actively encouraged private entrepreneurship. Prior to specialising in furniture making, the Dongfeng village had been encouraged by government to try a number of other businesses, such as noodle making, pig and poultry farming and plastic material manufacturing. Therefore, there was already some experience of entrepreneurship in the area, and this, Sun Han told us, influenced his ambition to make his own enterprise. In recognition of his success, his company was appointed by the government of a neighbouring town as the ‘training centre for young entrepreneurs’. Such official government blessing is an important factor for success in the Chinese market.

The communist party secretary of the village was manager of the Shaji Town Industria Group before he got his Dongfeng government post. He considers himself a businessman, and told us proudly how he helped Sun Han to get a loan from the local bank to launch his business. Also, the communist party secretary of the Shaji town was instrumental in persuading Sun Han to share his knowledge and skills with others in the village in order to develop a range of products and critical mass of business and thus achieve collective competitive advantage in the ready-to-assemble furniture market. These government officials see their role as facilitators of mutual trust among the entrepreneurs and between entrepreneurs, banks and other service providers. They keep close contact with the enterprise owners. One interviewee told us that officials from the township government visit his office at least twice a week and are wel informed about his business performance, plans for development and problems.

The smaller netrepreneurs we spoke to were apprehensive about increasing competition and falling profit margins and uncertain about their future. They expected the government to oversee the village’s industrial venture and act to secure its success. At the time of our visit, government officials in the village and the township were preoccupied with this problem and were trying to find a way to reduce negative effects of internal competition. They had plans fo a ‘furniture e-business association’ to regulate quality standards and prices and to oversee brand management. They were also helping local entrepreneurs to secure investment for a large-scale manufacturing plant to produce good quality furniture material that would subsequently improve the quality of their products.

## Suppliers and service providers

The network of furniture makers in Dongfeng shows many of the features that explain clus tering of entrepreneurial activity in the industrial networks literature. It is located in a region that produces timber, the raw material for their manufacturing ventures. It benefits from sharing expensive specialist equipment and from making collective deals with suppliers. Furniture micro-manufacturers also benefit from the emergence of competition among local service providers. Indicatively, when the first workshops and online shops were established, there was only one branch of a logistics company in a nearby town. It charged high prices according to the weight of each product. In 2011 there were about 20 agents of various logistics companies in Dongfeng, managed as local family business firms, and delivery prices had dropped substantially.

Local networking contributed to mastering the Taobao tools and creating tacit knowledge on effective ways to manage relations with customers and suppliers. Some furniture makers started making more advanced use of Taobao services for their business development. Consider for example the case of Mr Liu, one of the most successful netrepreneurs of the village, whose business grew within 5 years to employ 15 carpenters, seven administrators and four salespersons. One of his major concerns is getting the pricing of his products right which, he told us, ‘is very much about guessing how much your customers are willing to pay; to do that you need to know your customers and your competitors’. For this guesswork, he was considering paying for access to Taobao customer tracking and user behaviour data and analytics. ‘It’s all on Taobao, you need to dig it out’, he said.

## ANALYSIS

In this section, we first consider how the Taobao platform supports interpersonal aspects of social embeddedness: trust, information and knowledge sharing and problem solving. We then examine the institutional underpinnings of the entrepreneurial networks.

## Interpersonal network ties

Trust

Three technologies on the Taobao platform are extensively mobilised in both cases to create trust between sellers and buyers as well as between netrepreneurs and their service providers.

First, the instant communication tools make online transactions possible in a culture of suspicion and avoidance of exchange with strangers (Xi & Pearce, 1996; Hwang, 2000; Chen et al., 2007; Martinsons, 2008; Ou & Davison, 2009). Second, the Alipay system with its escrow function enables the financial transactions. Third, customers’ evaluations and comments and Taobao ratings of business performance and customer satisfaction provide indicators of credibility. Reputation and credibility data are important for influencing not only buyer decisions but also lenders and business partners.

## Information and knowledge sharing

Several tools on the Taobao platform serve the information and knowledge requirements of netrepreneurs. Most of the netrepreneurs we studied made sense of the fundamental concepts of business and learned the techniques of managing an enterprise (such as stock control, product presentation and accounting) through their use of Taobao software. The Taobao platform offers online training on many of its services. It also provides various tools, such as wikis and blogs, to share knowledge and experiences. But for the netrepreneurs we studied, these are of secondary importance in comparison to sharing experience by socializing in the community. Our interviewees sought and received a great deal of help to master the tech niques of operating a business from neighbours and friends.

Similarly, netrepreneurs benefit from learning from each other how to be effective sellers on the Taobao platform. The netrepreneurs of Yiwu and most sellers of the Dongfeng village develop their skills for presenting themselves as trustworthy vendors and convincing their customers about the qualities of their merchandise mainly by socializing in their communities. These are crucially important skills for making effective use of the technology communication tools available on the Web platform. The e-commerce college also plays a primary role in the development of such skills, in the Yiwu case, but they mostly learn by sharing experience, as the development of the off-line activities of the Yiwu e-business association suggests.

## Problem solving and coping with complexity

The netrepreneurs rely on a mix of Taobao tools, interpersonal relationships and support from other organisations to sustain and develop their business. In their everyday conduct of business, improvisations of netrepreneurs in collaboration with their local suppliers are the most prevalent problem-solving mechanisms. Getting a local packaging company to devise an impromptu package for an odd-shaped merchandise item and teaming up for the purchase of expensive machinery for furniture manufacturing, are only two examples of problem-solving that mobilise social connections.

Some, like Mr Liu in Dongfeng have started considering analytics and data mining. For others, such possibilities emerge through the intermediation of Taobao, whose analysts monitor internet traffic to identify netrepreneurs with growth potential and proactively advise them to develop their business, suggesting for example different types of merchandise and suppliers and offering advertisement discounts.

## Institutional enablers

Two institutional aspects stand out as important enablers of the entrepreneurial networks: the culture of commerce and consumption in modern China and the existence of two powerful organisational actors in the Chinese economy, the corporation of Taobao and the government.

## Entrepreneurial and consumerist culture

The entrepreneurial networks are the continuation of a culture of commerce that has old historical roots, survived the Maoist regime and has revived in the era of the country’s economic liberalisation (Huang, 2008). These two cases are examples of an energetic capitalist development which celebrates private initiative and the creation of wealth. Importantly, their business is sustained by an attitude of consumerism which creates demand for all manner of products. Consumerism is highlighted in many analyses of the post-communist era, and some analysts consider it as a necessary cultural transformation from the identity of ‘we the people’ in the communist era to the identity of ‘citizen’ in a capitalist society (Davis, 2000; Cu & Liu, 2001; Keane, 2001; Li, 2010). In our cases, consumerism provides ample opportunity for the manufacturers of cheap fashionable furniture in Dongfeng and intermediary traders such as the retailers of Yiwu

## Powerful organisational actors

The importance of Taobao as a Chinese corporation at the frontiers of digital services cannot be overemphasised. Not only does it provide software technologies and content suitable for the Chinese entrepreneur and consumer, it also ‘educates’, shapes attitudes and nurtures entrepreneur winners according to market competition criteria (Chen et al., 2007; Ou & Davison, 2009). Its influence is both functional and symbolic and is exercised through its online platform as well as off-line activities, such as visits to the business associations and conferences.

Consider for example the influence of Taobao on the Yiwu e-commerce college and the netrepreneurs’ association. Taobao staff maintains regular contact with the college teachers. They gave it the status of ‘top college in terms of good value for their offering’ thus boosting demand for its programmes. For the college students and the netrepreneurs, Taobao is a convenient set of technology tools and useful data as well as a source of aspiration. Jack Ma, the founder of Alibaba and Taobao, is the entrepreneur par excellence of modern times, and his picture on the college walls is a constant reminder of the wealth-making opportunities afforded by the internet. Taobao staff regularly visit the Yiwu netrepreneurs’ association, monitor the business development of their members and offer advice for product lines, suppliers and business strategies.

The power of Taobao’s influence is partly derived from its vast databases. Decisions based on data are promoted as the way to avoid fraud and prove merit. In effect, Taobao data create legitimacy for non-face-to-face meritocratic relations, thus making it possible to conduct business online.

Our cases also show the influence of government on entrepreneurial activity. In these two cases, the role of the government too is both functional and symbolic. Functionally, loca governments are directly involved in supportive actions, such as constructing infrastructures and favourable tax regimes and facilitating loans. In the Dongfeng case, government officials are constantly engaged with the overall development of the industrial network, attracting new entrants by encouraging farmers to take entrepreneurial risk, facilitating investment and mediating the governance of the industrial network to minimise antagonistic behaviour. Symbolically, government support provides legitimacy to entrepreneurial risk taking and creates a public perception that shifting the local economy from agriculture to manufacturing and trade is social progress.

The state is also significant in its relation to corporate capitalism. Alibaba and Taobao are privately owned, and their success overwhelmingly depends on market competitive strategies. Nevertheless, we were given to understand that there was an agreement of corporate socia responsibility with the Chinese government which involves support for the development of poor regions of the country. We have limited data on the details of action through which such responsibility is realised, but its symbolic significance is clear: in a context where growth is pursued in a competitive market regime, support of large corporations for developmenta initiatives gives legitimacy to government policies and politics concerned with improving the living conditions of the poor.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Insofar as the creation and growth of enterprises is socially embedded, our case studies help us understand the way social relations between economic actors are achieved on Web platforms which are rich on facilities that support economic exchanges with strangers. They also help us form a clearer idea of the institutional mechanisms that foster such IT-enabled economic activity. The economic activity of the Chinese entrepreneurs is achieved with a mix of virtual and face-to-face social ties; it is made possible in a culture conducive to entrepreneurship and consumerism; and relies substantially on government and the corporate provider of the Web platform services.

The cases suggest a different mix of virtual and community dependent relations in exchanges with customers (B2C relations) and in the interactions of members of the entrepreneuria business networks (B2B relations). In buyer/seller exchanges, the Web platforms contribute to the formation of trust through reputation and institutional support as deemed necessary for exchange transactions. Unlike Fowler et al. ‘s (2004) analysis, in which market opportunism is addressed in online exchanges by impersonal techniques of transparency, in our cases, Web technologies do not substitute but modify the traditional way of forming trust through persona contact. Reliance on traditional face-to-face relations for economic exchange is moderated by virtual communication, reputation and escrow mechanisms. Thus more scope is created for impersonal exchange on a routine basis with strangers. B2B relations, however, continue to be largely community confined, with the exception of one major business partner, Taobao. The development of successful netrepreneurial ventures substantially benefits from co-locationa community ties and learning processes, as suggested by the literature on industrial clusters.

Culture is implicated in various ways in Web platform-based economic activities. It influ ences behavioural attitudes to strangers. Netrepreneurs need to take such attitudes into account in their combination of virtual and direct personal relations with customers and suppliers. It also shapes values regarding entrepreneurial success and community engage ment. Cultural traits such as these and the consumer culture we pointed out above are dynamically related with each other and with the possibilities for change provided by tools and services on Web platforms. Although we were not able in this research to study the way behavioural norms in local communities are affected by the emergence of the netrepreneurs, our empirical studies indicated change rather than cultural preservation.

The organisational structure of the economy which enables the netrepreneurial networks involves both big government and a big corporation. The entrepreneurial networks ride on the wave of socio-economic transformation of modern China in which competitive capitalism creates giant corporations capable of innovation comparable to that of the most advanced Western countries, but within the terms of an interventionist government. Our findings confirm the view that the advent of economic activity based on Web platforms does not disrupt but relies on the corporate organisational structure of the industrial economy (Benkler, 2006; Pascu et al., 2007).

In conclusion, in relation to the two lines of theoretical investigation that we identified from our literature review, our case studies provide evidence that substantiates the way Web platforms support social relations that enable and give particular form to economic activity, and shows the cultural influences and organisational structures that constitute the institutional setting for networks of netrepreneurs. We found that netrepreneurs combine Web platform tools with conventional means of direct communication, such as face-to-face and telephone conversation and rely heavily on local community-based relations. Our research suggests that Web platforms create conditions of possibility for entrepreneurial activity that extend to business relations and markets beyond the entrepreneurs’ community. Such activity takes shape by developing technology uses appropriate to local culture and continues to be supported by organisational actors of the industrial economy. One organisational actor that stands out for its crucial role in the emergence and development of the entrepreneurial networks is the corporation providing the Web platform services.

It would be inappropriate, beyond the abstract theoretical thesis on the nature of social embeddedness that makes Web platform-based entrepreneurial networks possible, to generalise the specific mix of virtual and social relational mechanisms and institutional underpinnings that may be formed to develop successful entrepreneurship in developing countries. More research is required to establish the significance of community and co-location. In China, the Taobao platform may be used by isolated entrepreneurs that do not have the benefits of dense local networks. The heroic individual netrepreneur remains a possibility, as entrepreneurs have always been, and we would expect to find a different multiplex of virtual and social embeddedness mechanisms of business and trade exchanges for netrepreneurs that are not part of geographically co-located networks.

Caution is also required in drawing lessons for other countries. The corporate actor, keen to do business with domestic customers and so crucial in the creation of entrepreneurial activity in the Chinese networks, is not replicable on other developing countries. In countries which lack domestic corporate actors for digital services and have historically developed different cultures and organisational forms of business and government, economic agents may coalesce in different ways to exploit the potential of Web platforms for supporting economic activity networks. For example, local companies may adapt, moderate or enhance the services of global corporations such as Google and eBay.

Our research did not address the question of whether and under what conditions the networks of netrepreneurs are sustainable or may grow. Longitudinal research in these two cases wil allow us to examine the risk of too much relational embeddedness, which Uzzi (1997) has cautioned against. In the case of Dongfeng, reliance on the government may breed compla cency and corruption. In both cases, reliance on Taobao may have undesirable lock-in effects.

The significance of our contribution is twofold. First, in relation to the ongoing research on the social embeddedness of internet-based business practices and economic exchanges, we show that Web platform communication tools and services can complement traditional trust, knowl edge sharing and problem-sharing mechanisms thus enabling conditions of social embeddedness of economic activity beyond co-locational relations. Second, we broaden the notion of social embeddedness beyond the micro-situated interpersonal relations of IT-based economic activity, which is the notion that has have so far been studied in information systems research, and we develop a notion of embeddedness that considers also macro-institutional aspects.
