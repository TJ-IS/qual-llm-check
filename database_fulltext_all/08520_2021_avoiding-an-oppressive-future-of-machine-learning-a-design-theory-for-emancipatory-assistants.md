# AVOIDING AN OPPRESSIVE FUTURE OF MACHINE LEARNING:A DESIGN THEORY FOR EMANCIPATORY ASSISTANTS<sup>1</sup>

Gerald C. Kane Carroll School of Management, Boston College, 140 Commonwealth Avenue, Chestnut Hill, MA 02467 U.S.A. {gerald.kane@bc.edu}

Amber G. Young Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, 220 N. McIlroy Avenue, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {ayoung@walton.uark.edu}

Ann Majchrzak Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA 90089 U.S.A. {majchrza@usc.edu}

Sam Ransbotham Carroll School of Management, Boston College, 140 Commonwealth Avenue, Chestnut Hill, MA 02467 U.S.A. {sam.ransbotham@bc.edu}

Widespread use of machine learning (ML) systems could result in an oppressive future of ubiquitous monitoring and behavior control that, for dialogic purposes, we call “Informania.” This dystopian future results from ML systems’ inherent design based on training data rather than built with code. To avoid this oppressive future, we develop the concept of an emancipatory assistant (EA), an ML system that engages with human users to help them understand and enact emancipatory outcomes amidst the oppressive environment of Informania. Using emancipatory pedagogy as a kernel theory, we develop two sets of design principles: one for the near future and the other for the far-term future. Designers optimize EA on emancipatory outcomes for an individual user, which protects the user from Informania’s oppression by engaging in an adversarial relationship with its oppressive ML platforms when necessary. The principles should encourage IS researchers to enlarge the range of possibilities for responding to the influx of ML systems. Given the fusion of social and technical expertise that IS research embodies, we encourage other IS researchers to theorize boldly about the long-term consequences of emerging technologies on society and potentially change their trajectory

Keywords: Machine learning, artificial intelligence, design theory, critical theory, next generation, oppression, emancipation, pedagogy, emerging technologies, socio-technical systems, affordances, future forecasting, freedom, social inclusion, algorithm, agency, rationality, autonomy

## Introduction

Machine learning (ML) systems represent a considerable step forward in information systems (IS) development. Trained on data rather than developed from code, these systems excel at many tasks previously thought to be difficult, if not insurmountable, for IS—such as image and speech recognition, driving autonomous vehicles, language translation, and automated chatting, to name just a few. Companies now apply ML and other types of artificial intelligence in ways that have brought and will bring a substantial new wave of business innovation (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2017). While organizations may realize considerable benefits from ML, ML’s widespread application may have somewhat different outcomes for individuals. The individuals whom these organizations continually monitor and manipulate face a potentially dystopian future (Demetis and Lee 2018; Zuboff 2019).

Many academics and practitioners predict a scary future that may emerge from the widespread adoption and use of ML. Large-scale platform companies like Facebook and Google may already wield mass behavioral control through ubiquitous mobile devices yielding pervasive “surveillance capitalism” (Zuboff 2019). This widespread behavior control may become significantly more frightening if current trends of ubiquitous data collection through mobile devices, wearables, and other “smart” devices continue. Instead of humans controlling information technology (IT), IT may soon control us (e.g., Demetis and Lee 2018).

To this end, we envision and describe this future and call it “Informania,”<sup>2</sup> a dystopian future state where ubiquitous data collection feeds ML systems that users do not understand, that lack user feedback, and that result in behavioral control of humans using internet-based platforms. In Informania, ML systems optimize on outcomes for millions (or billions) of users, with little regard for individual rights within the collective. Designers select outcomes for the ML systems, with little input from the individuals who inhabit Informania. Informania generally disregards how these ML systems affect the individual’s experiences and outcomes, outside of the outcome variables the platform owners designate to optimize on their objectives.

The potential for Informania is rising. Nation-states intentionally use ML systems to govern and manage people more efficiently. For example, China’s social credit system or the U.S. Justice Department’s COMPAS system could evolve into this dystopian future. The U.S. government recently used location data from mobile devices to monitor individuals participating in the 2020 racial inequality protests sparked by the killing of George Floyd (Biddle 2020), which may expand to facial recognition (Reichert 2020). Free-market capitalism, where technology companies pursue their economic goals of shareholder value with unchecked, rational self-interest, can encourage and enable similar monitoring of individuals. In such a case, multiple organizations could develop ML infrastructures with increasingly pervasive data collection to realize their organizational goals, resulting in a massive behavior control infrastructure that exists mostly outside of the awareness of any single human or organization. Finally, Informania could arise from path dependencies in which technology trajectories gain steam until humans lose agency. This loss of agency can apply to the inhabitants of Informania and also to the developers. The ML infrastructure of Informania could evolve in ways that go beyond or fail to align with the interests of those developing it.

Informania is only one potential future of the current ML trajectory; therefore, its oppressive influence is currently only a potential. In developing a design theory to avoid that potential, it is helpful to speak as if Informania and its oppressive potential already exist. Behavior control in Informania oppresses humans, yet humans are unaware of the extent to which machines control their behavior, and they lack options to exert control if they become aware. The oppression of Informania need not necessarily arise from malicious intent. Indeed, oppression often stems from misguided efforts to enhance social good (Lin et al. 2015), such as contact tracing to reduce coronavirus spread during the COVID-19 pandemic. An ML system may oppress people not out of intent but simply because its designers have not avoided oppression by taking users’ humanity into account.

Oppression dehumanizes when it prevents users from reaching their full potential as human beings. Taking humanity into account requires an unconventional ML design. Such a design needs to continue to take advantage of ML’s basic technical features, including optimized outcomes, pattern matching, large data sets, and the ability to train on and refine the patterns. However, this design also must disrupt the pathdependent trajectory of current ML development that ignores users’ humanity. How to accomplish this objective is the focus of this paper. The purpose of constructing and labeling this potentially oppressive future state of Informania is to design an ML system to counter oppression by supporting human emancipation and protecting users’ humanity. Critical theory and design science can provide some help, and we rely on this previous research when it does. Prior critical research in IS focused on how to code systems that were not oppressive, but the extent to which prescriptions apply to an ML system trained on data is not apparent.

Using emancipatory pedagogy (Freire 1970) as a kernel theory, we offer design principles for ML systems that support human emancipation. Our theory designs a new class of system we call an emancipatory assistant (EA), an ML system that engages with human users to help them understand and enact emancipatory outcomes amidst the oppressive environment that is Informania. It does so by developing a partnership that creates a bidirectional understanding between an individual user and their dedicated EA. We offer two sets of design principles. The first set focuses on the near-term future and the technical parameters necessary to develop EAs. The second set focuses on the far-term future, outlining market and societal factors essential to sustain the emancipatory outcomes that EAs support.

## Oppression and Information Systems

Critical theory challenges “social conditions and institutions and oppressive forms of control, often enabled and supported by IS, which prevent realization of humane, just and free organizations and society” (Cecez-Kecmanovic 2011, p. 442). Emancipation, the “lynchpin” of critical IS research (Richardson and Robinson 2007, p. 262), is a theoretical state in which power dynamics between agents are neutral (Introna 2002) or equal (Brooke 2002). In contrast, oppression is a state in which unequal power dynamics lead to domination or marginalization. In Informania, we envision an imbalance of power between those who manage the behavior control infrastructure and those who inhabit it. Emancipation and oppression are complex, multifaceted constructs with extensive literature (Hirschheim and Klein 1994; Myers and Klein 2011; Stahl 2008). Alvesson and Willmott (1992a) provide an overview of emancipation in the organizational literature, and Young et al. (2021) provide an overview of emancipation in the IS literature.

Oppression constrains users’ freedom in three ways: their freedom to think, their freedom to act, and their freedom to belong. First, oppression constrains users’ freedom to think (e.g., Avgerou and McGrath 2007; Lyytinen and Hirschheim 1988; Lyytinen and Klein 1985; Ngwenyama 1991; Miranda et al. 2016). Rationality is a mental model or way of thinking about the world that shapes values and goals. The role of rationality has a complex history in critical theory. Rationality can emancipate societies when the rationality is robust, accounting for many perspectives (Brubaker 1987); however, rationality can oppress when power holders institutionalize unquestioned, specialized rationalities (e.g., Weber 1958, 1978). People develop specialized rationalities in the context of their field of expertise or limited personal experiences but may fail to test or refine them against competing rationalities.

When people do not synthesize different mental models toward robust rationality, one group’s rationality will dominate (e.g., Weber 1958, 1978). For instance, if developers do not work with users to understand user needs and goals, they will develop systems that privilege the developers’ rationalities and goals. Constraints on rationality may be explicit (e.g., restrictions on information flows) or tacit (e.g., manipulation of socio-cultural meanings) (Miranda and Saunders 2003).

Second, oppression constrains a user’s freedom to act (e.g., Cram et al. 2016; Demetis and Lee 2018; Klein and Huynh 2004; Vieira da Cunha et al. 2015; Zheng and Stahl 2011)— that is, a user’s agency. Initially, humans developed machines as substitutes for human labor (Barrett et al. 2012). The word “bot” comes from the Czech word robota, meaning forced labor (Lanfranco et al. 2004, p. 14). Nevertheless, as societal dependence on autonomous technologies increases, these technologies increasingly subvert and subdue users’ agency (Demetis and Lee 2018). What began as a dynamic of humans implementing IS as tools for efficiency is trending toward a dynamic of IS “implementing” humans as tools (Young et al. 2021). Systems can manipulate or otherwise control individual actions, especially when individuals passively receive the system. Moreover, humans controlling smart systems can implement these systems as tools of power to surveil and control large populations.

Third, oppression constrains the freedom to belong. Information systems affect social, political, financial, and cultural inclusion (Adam 2002; Díaz Andrade and Doolin 2016; Ortiz et al. 2019; Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020a). Constraints on inclusion inhibit meaningful participation in the public sphere, hindering marginalized individuals’ abilities to be heard, gain power, accumulate resources, or otherwise operate effectively in the dominant society. Information systems can reduce or increase marginalization depending on factors including system design (Wilson 1997), users’ abilities to actualize emancipatory affordances (Miranda et al. 2016; Young 2018), user traits and resources (Burtch and Chan 2019; George and Leidner 2018), and macro-political contexts (Avgerou 2008; Lin et al. 2015).

ML systems can and do oppress individuals by constraining each of these types of freedom. For example, many ML algorithms engage in information filtering (e.g., newsfeeds, autoplay, and product recommendation), limiting information based on past behaviors. This application of ML constrains users’ freedom to think by controlling the amount and type of information available for decision making. Other ML applications automate actions (e.g., scheduling systems and stock market trading bots), taking routine actions on behalf of the user, thus constraining users’ freedom to act. Still other ML systems constrain users’ social, political, and cultural inclusion by making decisions based on data embedded with biases (e.g., Amazon developed a hiring ML system biased against women; Dastin 2018). While today’s examples of oppression may be relatively innocuous by historical standards, we expect the oppressive influence to increase as Informania becomes pervasive. Thus, we conceptualize oppression broadly to encompass dehumanizing constraints on each of these three freedoms.

## The Oppressive Nature of Machine Learning

Fundamental differences between code-based and data-based systems undermine many of the insights and strategies for counteracting oppression developed when most systems were code-based. Figure 1 shows a basic model of ML systems that illustrates four fundamental features. First, ML models optimize for a measurable outcome. Second, they use data inputs from multiple sources that require cleaning and are not available to most users. Third, ML models then weight obscure parameters. Finally, feedback helps train ML models. The way these features work together makes it difficult to simply apply insights from previous critical theory to counteract any potential oppressive influence.

In summary, while developers build traditional code-based systems, they only develop processes to train ML algorithms. For example, in code-based systems, the developers directly designed the rules that systems would execute and had complete control over those rules, even if those rules behaved unexpectedly or not as intended. In data-based systems, ML designers control the processes by which systems learn from existing data and audit the model’s outcomes, but they have less ability to make small, incremental adjustments. Instead, they have to retrain the model before determining if the changes result in the desired outcomes. Understanding databased systems is also harder than understanding code-based systems because, for example, designers must interpret model weights instead of text-based code. Feedback loops can also evolve ML systems as input data evolves. As a result, designers may not anticipate all potential outcomes from new data. Faced with unexpected outcomes, designers mayrequire time to adjust the model. Unfortunately, unlike extensive testing of code-based systems before production use, ML systems can adjust in production—both a benefit and a drawback of data-based systems. It is often difficult for developers to know how an ML system will behave when released in a new environment.

Therefore, even when critical theory remains applicable, achieving the design guidance advocated by those theories requires different approaches. Furthermore, these four features make it more difficult to apply lessons from previous critical theory to ML systems, and we argue that they inherently constrain the three freedoms of humans. We discuss each of the basic ML model’s fundamental features in greater depth in the following sections. We then identify a design principle that critical theory literature identifies as essential for avoiding oppression in code-based systems relating to that feature. Finally, we show how that specific feature makes achieving the design principle from critical theory more difficult in data-based systems, potentially leading to oppressive outcomes.

## Oppressive Feature #1: ML Systems Optimize Outcomes for Large Samples at the Expense of Individuals

The basic ML model optimizes on outcome variables. When platform designers select these outcome variables, the designers will almost certainly select outcome variables that benefit the platform above individual users (Zuboff 2019). Thus, ML systems oppress because users do not control the selection of outcome variables.

Some insights from existing critical IS research identify similar oppressive influences in code-based systems when system design subjugates user needs to those of the system or the organization (e.g., Lyytinen and Hirschheim 1988). For example, developers may design systems to privilege objective technical criteria over subjective criteria such as human values and feelings (Oliver and Romm 2002). Even if designers consider user values when developing systems, they may ignore differences between individual users in ways that reinforce existing inequalities by focusing on the needs of the power-holding demographic (Adam and Richardson 2001) or organization (Lyytinen and Hirschheim 1988). As a result, systems perpetuate a status quo of oppression through their design (Lyytinen and Klein 1985). Although the oppressed sometimes harness IS’s emancipatory potential to achieve gradients of emancipation, this process is fraught with complications (Young 2018). For instance, even when marginalized individuals acquire potentially emancipatory systems, they are less likely to use them in emancipatory ways than nonmarginalized users (Burtch and Chan 2019; George and Leidner 2018). Likewise, managers may empower employees by providing them with tools that give them more freedom in their work but reduce the employees’ freedom by forcing the tools, and associated outcomes, on them (Trusson et al. 2014). Forcing ill-fitted systems on developing communities is unfortunately common (Heeks 2002; Lin et al. 2015; Young 2018). Even when platform designers seek to develop ML algorithms that optimize on individual benefits, they are unlikely to achieve this goal with current ML designs. ## Oppressive Feature #2: Data Inputs Face Backward, Limiting Future Possibilities

The basic ML model oppresses individuals through input data. Input data sets have several drawbacks (Newell and Marabelli 2015), such as data coverage and bias. Data to train ML systems should reflect the entire scope of the decision-making, but it may be difficult to assemble such comprehensive datasets in some cases. Furthermore, the training data may reflect past biases. A hindrance to emancipation is the tapestry of biased socio-cultural meanings that the oppressed must first recognize and then substitute with unbiased meanings. Various types of bias can hinder emancipation, such as authoritarian bias (Alvesson and Willmott 1992b; Deetz 1992), modernity bias (Díaz Andrade and Urqhart 2012), and bias against minorities (Payton 2016; Reid et al. 2010; Zahedi et al. 2006). One approach to dealing with bias is through the simple act of questioning past assumptions, which can open up alternative futures (Jackson 1992). This questioning is vital because those preoccupied with the past or present cannot build an emancipatory future (Freire 1970).

Identifying and figuring out how ML systems can overcome these biases embedded in existing data sets involves a fundamentally different process than eliminating bias from the traditional development life cycle of code-based systems. Indeed, prior generations of designers and developers built biases into their code, but carefully scrutinizing that code could have uncovered them. Data-based biases are harder to detect (Ajunwa 2020; Chen et al. 2018; Datta et al. 2017; Mann and O’Neil 2016). Since code embodied the designers prior experiences, general knowledge, and expectations, programmers could embed aspirations for how IS should work against biases and promote fairness (Cowgill and Tucker 2019). ML systems, however, pick up on and reinforce existing societal biases embedded in the data, even when the data only implicitly represents them. Even if the input data sets are relatively complete and free of bias, ML systems fundamentally face backward and struggle to reflect future conditions. ML systems based solely on historical data with limited causal inference (Kilbertus et al. 2017) will struggle with longer horizons or more general contexts. While MLbased algorithms are effective on specific data sets, generalizability suffers in slightly different data sets in related settings (Hutson 2018).

## Oppressive Feature #3: Obscure Parameters and Model Weights Make ML Systems Difficult to Understand

The basic ML model obscures model weights making it challenging to understand how ML systems constrain user agency. Transparency is a key design factor for avoiding oppressive systems (Ehsan and Riedl 2020). Even before ML systems, lack of transparency in systems (Goodspeed 2016) and lack of forthrightness from managers raise concerns about hidden agendas (Myers and Young 1997). A system that renders information less observable may become nearly invisible to users whose lives it affects. When a system becomes exceptionally efficient, it may become “too good” at blending into social processes and remain largely unnoticed and unchallenged as it accumulates power over users (Beer 2017, pp. 1-2). In such cases, the system is mostly hidden from users, yet leaves users feeling vulnerable, as though there is nowhere that they could hide from the system (Schlagwein et al. 2019).

Software has always been opaque, but ML systems exacerbate this transparency problem between computers and humans (Burrell 2016; Castelvecchi 2016). Prior generations of software emphasized code, which embodied designers’ and developers’ expectations of how the system would function. Now, ML-based agents benefit from the copious data available (Chen et al. 2012), and the algorithms are secondary (Cheney-Lippold 2018). For example, supervised training of a neural network generates various weightings of multiple layers of nodes (Michalski et al. 2013). While a programmer can evaluate the coded rules and comments that prior designers and developers leave, interpreting the weights and nodes of a trained neural network is far more inscrutable (Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020b). As a result, even if developers and designers identify that an ML system is acting in oppressive or other undesirable ways through auditing processes, they may not know why. They may be relatively powerless to fix the oppressive tendencies even when identified because it may not be obvious what part of the training process—the data, the training algorithm, or other factors— led to the oppressive tendencies. Furthermore, the speed with which humans can audit ML systems will either be insufficient to keep up with developments or hinder the ML systems’ development.

## Oppressive Feature #4: Specialists Train and Update ML Systems with Limited Feedback from Users

Current feedback mechanisms make it difficult for users to provide useful feedback to the basic ML model. Iterative dialogue and feedback are essential in the emancipatory process (Freire 1970). In particular, the oppressed have unique insights into the nature and consequences of oppression. Unfortunately, oppressors rarely glean these insights because they largely ignore oppressed individuals’ voices (e.g., microtask workers, Deng et al. 2016) and groups (e.g., Indigenous peoples, Myers et al. 2020). Many IT projects lack input from those who will receive the technologies (Bidwell 2016; Díaz Andrade and Urquhart 2012; Young 2018). As a result, projects that could have been emancipatory oppress instead (Lin et al. 2015). In the workforce, even when those in power solicit feedback from those in a less powerful position, fear of retribution for negative feedback may inhibit participation in and effectiveness of feedback processes (Schlagwein et al. 2019). If this fear is left unaddressed, it may even motivate the falsification of feedback data (Shaw and Stahl 2011).

Testing systems to ensure desired behavior has always been difficult for system designers and developers. Although testing was difficult and costly in deterministic code-based systems, it was achievable with human effort. Furthermore, designers could change code in response to user feedback. However, training systems that continuously change based on emerging data will be more challenging to alter in a manner intended by the user feedback. ML systems use new data to learn and refine their behavior. However, designers may not expect some underlying data patterns, and real-world data may differ widely from training data (de Cuerto 2012). Thus, even when designers and developers test ML systems before implementation, these systems may behave quite differently in production. Furthermore, data-based systems can result in counterproductive outcomes, such as feedback loops, that can lead to unpredictable results or instability of the ML system (Faraj et al. 2018). While these feedback mechanisms allow ML systems to excel in areas requiring quick decisions (Agarwal et al. 2020; Grover and Lyytinen 2015), when this speed of response combines with rapidly changing data, this feature may become a bug as ML systems make decisions quickly in untested scenarios.

In summary, differences between ML systems and more traditional code-based systems make it more difficult to design these systems in a way that is consistent with principles that critical theory identifies as crucial for emancipation. Therefore, these features inherently demonstrate oppressive potential. Figure 2 summarizes these oppressive features and places them on the figure of the basic ML model we outlined earlier.

## The Need for a New Design Theory

Organizations currently using ML may create a dystopian state for humans that we have called Informania. To counter this trend, we need a way to design ML systems for a more emancipatory future. In the remainder of the paper, we provide specifics of a design theory for an ML system that protects humans from oppression by Informania. Design science (Hevner et al. 2004; Kuechler and Vaishnavi 2012) is an essential paradigm in IS research (Goes 2014). Initially, a design theory was “a prescriptive theory based on theoretical underpinnings which says how a design process can be carried out in a way which is both effective and feasible” (Walls et al. 1992, p. 37). More recently, the expanded definition includes knowledge about why an IT artifact behaves as it does (Gregor and Jones 2007). While some design science research focuses on theory development, other research emphasizes knowledge of the artifact (Baskerville et al. 2018; Gregor and Hevner 2013). Across the different approaches, systems do not operate alone but instead interact with humans (Rai 2017). Thus, designs include both the system and the system-in-use by the human. Gregor and Jones (2007) outline a process for “specifying design theory so that it can be communicated, justified, and developed cumulatively” (p. 312). Table 1 provides a summary of this process and shows how our theorizing generally follows that process.

The development of new systems relies not only on prototyping and user participation but also on a kernel theory (Walls et al. 1992, 2004). A kernel theory is a theory based in natural or social sciences, which provides insight into how to solve a problem. Thus, relying on the kernel theory to understand what could be done through design to achieve a particular solution allows design theories to provide normative prescriptions for what should be done through design and how. For example, contingency theory can serve as a kernel theory for a group support system’s design theory (GSS). In this example, contingency theory informs the design of ideal task/technology alignment (Zigurs and Buckland 1998). This theoretical understanding of fit informs GSS strategies for types of tasks.

Identifying suitable kernel theories “is where the creativity and innovativeness of the design scientist come into play” (Wall et al. 2004, p. 56). Design theories may draw from a wide range of theories, or a combination of theories, to address each unique design problem. For example, the emergent knowledge processes theory was instrumental in designing knowledge management systems for organizational change (Markus et al. 2002). The theory of cognitive bias was useful in designing a methodology for developing decision support systems (Arnott 2006). Both theories of software process improvement and risk management theories supported the design of an approach to understanding and managing software process improvement teams (Iversen et al. 2004). The skill-based competence model revealed shortcomings in existing customer competence management systems (Sein et al. 2011). A critical social theory is well-suited to serve as the kernel theory for a design theory involving emancipation.

## Emancipatory Pedagogy as a Kernel Theory

To develop design principles for emancipatory ML, we use emancipatory pedagogy (Freire 1970) as a kernel theory adapted to ML oppression and emancipation’s unique context. This theory provides an integrative framework for understanding how actors can work toward emancipation. While early critical IS research relied almost exclusively on Habermasian theories, three main lineages of critical research emerged (Myers and Klein 2011; see Table 2). Freire’s work is similar to Habermas, with a key difference: Freire provides ready-made pedagogical steps to foster concrete gains of emancipation (Morrow and Torres 2002).

Freire developed emancipatory pedagogy for the unique situation that he found himself: minister of education in postcolonial Brazil (Morrow and Torres 2002). Given that he did not have the tools to undo the damage wrought by a colonial society on the native cultures, he reasoned that the only way to emancipate Indigenous people from the system that oppressed them was for a new form of co-education to occur in which oppressors and oppressed work together to understand each other. Emancipatory pedagogy aligns well with the context of emancipation from ML-based oppression for several reasons.

The first value that emancipatory pedagogy brings to a design theory is that, in much the same way that the powerless of postcolonial Brazil did not know how to rectify the oppression they experienced, most users of online platforms today may not recognize the many ways in which these platforms oppress them today and how to change it (Zuboff 2019). The emergence of Informania will exacerbate the many sources of oppression, creating the potential that most accept oppression, and few can alter it. Like Freire’s practices to help the oppressed and oppressors understand each other, we need processes and features that help create a partnership that includes the oppressive system. Simple awareness and skills will be unlikely to lead to this type of emancipatory environment.

A second value that emancipatory pedagogy brings to a design theory is that Freire did not advocate the oppressed simply rise and overthrow their oppressors. Instead, he advocated that the oppressors and oppressed create new educational processes that would allow them to work together to create a new type of society that was emancipatory for all. Freire conceptualizes a solution to oppression: a partnership where both the oppressed and the oppressors change their language through a deepened appreciation of each other. Through this partnership, both sides feel heard and impactful. Similarly, we envision a design in which an EA learns from an individual user what emancipation would consist for them, allowing the EA to represent these interests when operating within the larger environment of Informania.

A third value that emancipatory pedagogy brings to ML’s design theory is maintaining a consistent focus on continuous learning between the oppressor and the oppressed. Prior critical research in IS focused on how to code systems that were not oppressive, but the extent to which these prescriptions apply to an ML system trained on data is not apparent.

## Applying the Universal Themes of Emancipatory Pedagogy to Modify ML Design

After initial development in postcolonial Brazil, emancipatory pedagogy has served in other oppressive, or potentially oppressive, contexts. For example, communities have applied emancipatory pedagogy to establish public policies to reduce violence in an at-risk community, empower youth, and reorient health services (Monteiro et al. 2015). In information and communication technologies for development, emancipatory pedagogy explains human development and prevent Western domination of agendas and goals (Young 2018). Educators have applied emancipatory pedagogy across disciplines (e.g., music, Chaib 2010; and science, Reigota 2020) and education levels (e.g., higher education, Barnes 1992; and middle school, Larrain et al. 2019).

While emancipatory pedagogy applies broadly, educators also adapt it—indeed, Freire claims that educators should adapt it (Morrow and Torres 2002)—to each setting’s unique characteristics. While each adaptation is unique, most applications of emancipatory pedagogy share several universal themes (Morrow and Torres 2002): (1) learning is a subject-subject relation, in which participants engage in mutual inquiry; (2) this mutual inquiry considers the well-being of others and mutual respect between participants; (3) this process needs to connect with ongoing human experience; (4) participants codify and decodify experience as a strategy of cultural criticism; and (5) the learning process balances the needs for freedom and authority. In this section, we describe how we are contextualizing these themes for our ML context.

The universal theme of subject-subject relation is paramount and encompasses the other themes within it. For Freire, this theme meant that teachers and students (or oppressor and oppressed) co-create emancipatory knowledge; one is not superior to the other. For our theory, this theme means that the unit of analysis is the emancipatory partnership, consisting of an emancipatory assistant and its human partner. While we are primarily theorizing the design of a computerized system (i.e., the EA), the purpose of this system is to engage in an ongoing dialog with its human partner as co-creators, and so we must remain cognizant of the sociotechnical unit as we develop our design theory.

We apply the four remaining universal themes of emancipatory pedagogy to develop four modifications to the basic ML model that will yield distinctive design features of an EA that we will refer to as modifications 1–4. The first universal theme of emancipatory pedagogy modifies outcomes. Emancipatory pedagogy requires mutual care and respect between teacher and student as they begin to recognize each other’s humanity and not regard each other as a dehumanized characterization of oppressed or oppressor, a process Freire calls humanization (Freire 1970). Oppression dehumanizes when a person becomes an object (e.g., a cog in the machine) or a two-dimensional caricature due to a constrained worldview (e.g., the privileged white male archetype). We argued in the previous section that ML algorithms would necessarily oppress people when they optimize on platform-level outcomes because they disregard humanity in their optimization process. Therefore, contextualizing this theme of emancipatory pedagogy within EA development, creating an emancipatory partnership for humanization requires an EA to collect primary data on and optimize for individual emancipatory outcomes. We refer to this contextualization as modification 1 (M1).

The next universal theme modifies data inputs. Emancipatory pedagogy connects to ongoing human experiences. For Freire, education cannot merely involve abstract ideas teachers present in the classroom. Instead, ideas must contextualize the students’ experiences to learn about the world and their place. A challenge of ML systems is their decreased generalizability compared with other forms of IS. ML systems trained on a given data set may not function well in a similar but different environment. Given that the human needs will change over time, the EA must be able to change to account for future conditions that alter the context of the human’s experiences. The EA can automatically depart from the training data to connect to the human’s dynamic experience. Contextualizing this theme of emancipatory pedagogy requires an EA to add or discard training data sets to avoid the backward nature of decisions based primarily on past data. This contextualization is modification 2 (M2).

The third universal theme modifies model weights. Emancipatory pedagogy codifies and decodifies to increase understanding and communication between participants. One reason that traditional pedagogical practices were not effective in Brazil was that teachers used language in the classroom that did not represent the learners’ experience (Freire 2005). Codification and decodification overcome this problem by deconstructing language and learning anew what words to associate with the students’ experiences. To contextualize this theme of emancipatory pedagogy, an EA should codify and decodify to overcome the fundamental opacity of model weights. The purpose of this modification is to enable understandable communications between ML systems and users to overcome the fundamental opacity of decision-making criteria. We recognize that the problem of opacity is one on which ML researchers are actively working (e.g., Cramer et al. 2008; Gunning 2017), but emancipatory pedagogy suggests a novel way to approach it. This process of codification and decodification seeks not generalized explainability in which anyone can understand the model weights, a common goal for many current ML explainability efforts. Instead, it is a specialized explainability in which only the EA and its human user need to understand each other. Attempting to explain their emancipatory perspective to their EA partner may also trigger critical consciousness in human users. This contextualization is modification 3 (M3).

The final universal theme modifies the feedback mechanisms. Emancipatory pedagogy balances freedom and authority (Freire 2005). Emancipation involves freedoms of thought, action, and belonging. Nevertheless, abuse of these freedoms, such that freedom gives way to licentiousness, does not result in emancipatory outcomes. While emancipatory pedagogy treats students as co-equal subjects in the process of mutual inquiry, it recognizes the need for emergent rules and accountability—to oneself and the partnership. However, accountability, when abused, is likewise oppressive, as it turns to authoritarianism. Thus, an EA must balance between freedom and authority in feedback mechanisms such that both the EA and the human user maintain continual awareness of the amount of constraint the EA is exercising over the human, and both sides can propose adjustments in response to changing conditions. This contextualization is modification 4 (M4).

## Near-Term Principle #1: Design for Richness of Preferences

Informania works with incomplete information about users. EAs could help users by providing Informania with more details in the user’s interest. EAs can further help users articulate information that Informania might need. However, individuals cannot control how Informania will use this information and the consequences of revealing information. EAs can further help users understand the potential implications of disclosing information within the Informania environment’s power dynamics.

Consider a hypothetical person, Alice, and her career. Upon graduating, Alice took a job that fits well with her education and goals. Alice progressed well in her career, earning promotions and recognition. However, now that she has worked for a few years, Alice feels increasingly discontent and has been rethinking her aspirations. Job activities that were once a challenge are now dull and routine. The next promotions will require more working time, and Alice is thinking about spending more time enjoying the rewards of prior hard work and perhaps even starting a family. Maybe it was always this way, but Alice has the feeling that the company hires only young people just like Alice, who spend little time with family. While this feeling is hard to express explicitly, it is unsettling. Testing the job market, Alice notices that the jobs that Informania offers are similar to her current position— omething she no longer wants. It is frustrating that she does not know how to inform the algorithm to help with a preferred identity—not the one she has used in the past. At the same time, Alice does not want to reveal too much about herself. Having done the same thing for so many years, Alice does not know if her aspirations are realistic or if different jobs would be better or just different.

A similar sort of algorithmic control already exists in online labor markets (Rahman 2019). Informania would extend this type of algorithmic control to the entire labor market, making it difficult, if not impossible, to make independent career decisions. Furthermore, presuming these platforms would also extend to collect data from schooling; Informania’s ML system may shape the option for Alice’s first job as well, giving her little control over her career trajectory. Because Informania tightly controls Alice’s information about available jobs, it oppresses her—not in a manner that forces her to work a particular job, but in a manner in which she has inaccurate or strategically incomplete information about her environment. Oppression suppresses consciousness, preventing the oppressed from accurately perceiving the world around them. In the scenario, Informania restrains Alice’s consciousness such that she cannot recognize trade-offs, opportunities, risks, forces working against her, or power structures and institutions that uphold the status quo of oppressive power dynamics. We conceptualize three ways to design a system to support richer preferences.

## An EA Should Allow the User to Reveal Existing Preferences Not Present in Past Data

In the scenario, Informania assumes that Alice’s history indicates her future job preferences. Without additional information, Alice has few alternatives, severely constraining her rationality in achieving her objectives. When looking for recruits, background information such as past performance in similar jobs is accessible, quantifiable, and highly salient to Informania. Conversely, the changes in Alice’s aspirations will be abstract and more challenging for Informania to incorporate. However, a person who has just had a baby may have different job aspirations than someone trying to make enough money to save up for a house. Alternatively, perhaps the user is rethinking their impact on society and might want a job that supports social good. Informania further constraining humans to past roles is oppressive.

An EA could learn and refine a user’s preferences, even subconscious preferences, and vet these preferences with the user to combat this outcome. If an EA could help make information about these preferences available, Informania could incorporate forward-looking information, such as aspirations or expected changes. While an EA cannot force Informania to use this information, EAs can make it less costly and effortful for Informania to incorporate this richer preference data.

## An EAShould ProactivelySuggest New Preferences, Which May Lead to More Emancipatory Outcomes

In the scenario, Alice’s job dissatisfaction may have been growing, unrecognized, for some time. She may lack awareness of alternatives that may lead to greater agency. Oppression looms when the user lacks access to information about other options. Working conditions or company practices may have changed unbeknownst to Alice. Alternatively, despite past excellent performance in one area, Alice might desire a position in a new, emerging field—but her history will not convey that aspiration. As a result, Informania will not provide options that reflect these preferences, constraining the user.

While users know some preferences and expect some changes, they have not likely formed preferences in unknown areas. An EA can monitor for changes that may affect a user’s preferences. With that information, an EA could even develop scenarios to elicit user preferences for probable situations the user has not yet faced. For example, an EA could gather information about other potential jobs and take the user through simulations to understand what different careers entail.

## An EA Should Inform Users of Possible Personal Tradeoffs

In the scenario, Alice needs more information to know how alternative careers may suit her. It is not feasible to job-hop for years to gather this information. When considering specific new opportunities, Alice will need information about aspects that the new opportunities may deemphasize. Avoiding one form of oppression may unwittingly lead Alice to another type of oppression. Her inherently limited understanding of complex relationships will constrain her options. Worse, Informania will prioritize short-term, quantifiable outcomes (such as filling an open position) with less concern about Alice’s preferences.

While some suggested jobs might appeal to one preference, such as income or vacation time, an EA could help the user understand how the job might affect other preferences. Alternatively, an EA could recognize that a different role requires further certification or training; these might require compromise in short-term preferences to improve longer-term prospects. These compromises would not oppress if part of a longer-term plan, allowing a user to make intentional choices. The EA provides credible information about tradeoffs and makes recommendations while balancing the EA’s authority on outcomes with the user’s freedom to author her own life.

## Near-Term Principle #2: Design for Recognizing and Resolving Conflict

Even if an EA clarifies and communicates preferences, conflict is inevitable. Users are unlikely to have the same objective as Informania. Instead, an EA can help users recognize when their goals conflict with the goals of Informania. Once consciousness emerges, users can work to reduce their oppression. Praxis, critical reflection that informs action, begins this struggle against oppression (Freire 1970). EAs can help users by promoting awareness of the user, Informania, the conflict, and the power dynamics at play. The “adversarial” iteration between the EA and Informania’s ML system protects the user during this struggle and helps resolve the conflict in collaborative ways.

Consider another hypothetical person, Bob. Buying a house seemed like a fun and savvy move. However, Bob quickly learns that getting a loan is exhausting. Despite all the online options, he cannot help but feel like he is missing alternatives and paying more than everyone else. The consolidated information on aggregated platforms saves time and seems complete, and the platform ads are easy enough to ignore. After all, financing options should be fairly standard—isn’t that what market competition does? Still, Bob suspects he could get more options and better deals by contacting individual lenders directly. Notably, smaller businesses do not seem to be listed. Some options that look interesting evaporate when Bob investigates further. Were the ML systems tracking his keystrokes just testing him to see what his interests were? Bob has also heard rumors of better deals by clearing browser cookies, using alternative browsers, or even disguising location using a virtual private network (VPN). However, it is not clear how much difference that would make, and who has time for all that? Bob wants to get back to the fun part of buying a house!

This example only applies to a single home buying decision, but this type of control would permeate virtually all aspects of life in Informania, making it virtually impossible to escape this type of reality that steers Bob to make the choices Informania wants him to make. Once his consciousness begins to emerge, Bob realizes he has been or will be vulnerable to being manipulated and taken advantage. This awareness initiates his struggle to evolve from a pawn to an agent. Nevertheless, it is in the interest of power holders, such as aggregated platforms, to keep critical consciousness submerged. Those in power will not surrender control without a fight. Bob alone does not have the tools to navigate this situation. Together, Bob and his EA can identify strategic action opportunities to neutralize the aggregated platforms’ oppressive power dynamics. We envision three ways to design an EA to recognize and resolve this conflict.

## An EA Should Identify Conflict When the User’s Preferences for Outcomes Conflict with Informania

While buyers like Bob want lower prices and sellers desire higher prices, aggregators’ preferences are less clear. Pricing disparity is not inherently oppressive, but a necessary step to understanding potential oppression is recognizing it. As Informania filters overwhelming amounts of information, it filters to meet its objectives, not the user’s objectives. To know how much the objectives of Informania differ from the user’s objectives requires considerable information.

To help, EAs could aggregate information for users. Informania operates at scales that spread information costs across many users. EAs could make information-gathering less costly for users. Additionally, an EA could help assess whether Informania adequately compensates for non-price factors, such as the sale of data or advertising, which it may or may not. An EA could gather information independently so that the user controls the selection, not Informania.

## An EA Should Support Resolving Conflicts with Informania

Bob suspects that he can get better outcomes through more effort (e.g., directly contacting many providers, learning from others’ experience). Bob also wonders if the platform is not negotiating in good faith but making him a guinea pig for testing that he never agreed to. Even with information about alternatives, it would take Bob considerable effort to present that information to Informania and resolve the conflicting objectives. Worse, every decision Bob makes reveals information such as pricing preferences that Informania can use.

Alternatively, EAs could help users investigate options and execute conflict resolution strategies (such as resignation, titfor-tat, compromise, or overthrow). EAs could help users with the effortful tasks of investigating many options to reverse any experiments, getting better information about the preferences of Informania. If Informania offers better prices based on attributes, the EA could help users present those attributes that lead to better pricing. The EA could interfere with segmentation strategies and reduce the effectiveness of Informania information gathering. To the degree these actions might have consequences, the EA can help the user assess their options.

## An EA Should Allow Users to Communicate with Others for Organized Action

Working alone, Bob cannot tell how the prices he gets compare with others. A grocery store cannot have custom pricing for everyone who walks down the aisles. A restaurant does not print different menus for each customer. With Informania, much is hidden. Bob is not aware of how his experience differs from others. Bob is alone, and individuals have less power than collectives. Additionally, Bob lacks ways to provide feedback to Informania about his decisions—all Informania observes is shopping and decisions.

An EA can help restore balance by communicating with Informania and other users, informing them of Informania’s practices. For example, users could then coordinate through their EA to boycott Informania as necessary. However, it may not have to come to that result. Less gladiatorial collective action can likely influence Informania practices rendering escalation to a boycott unnecessary.

## Near-Term Principle #3: Design for Personalized Storytelling

Humans need to preserve their inherent dignity. Storytelling allows users to present themselves in ways that align with their intended impressions. Additionally, this need is highly contextualized. Users need the ability to share and adjust their information for the situation. Consider Carol. Despite enthusiastically agreeing with her friends to install a contact tracing app to help track the spread of COVID-19, Carol is worried. Most of her friends do not know her underlying medical conditions. With her new job not yet sure, she hates to consider what would happen if her potential employer and insurance company knew. The app claims it will anonymize her data, but the headlines seem to have data breaches every day. Still, perhaps the societal needs make it worth the risk; and by failing to install the app, some might suspect she had something to hide.

Extending this example, if Informania has access to all available data, it could make it available to any party Informania deemed worthy. Furthermore, with ubiquitous and varied types of data collection mechanisms—cameras, sensors, mobile devices—Informania could engage in near-constant data collection on the individual, regardless of whether they would like that information collected. Emancipation requires that individuals have a voice in their identity narratives rather than being subject to dehumanizing identity labels (Díaz Andrade and Urquhart 2012; Young 2018). Emancipation entails conceptualizing humans as humans, not data, objects, or caricatures (Freire 1970). One goal of the emancipatory struggle is “to recapture the loss of our dignity as human beings” (Freire 1970). Humans cannot accomplish this without human agency in digital identity representations. Designing for personalized storytelling can help in four ways.

## An Ea Should Tailor Which User Data to Share in Different Scenarios

Carol is losing control of her personal information. Unchecked, humans and systems leak information to Informania. Once Informania data is out, potential employers, insurers, and government institutions can use that data against individuals. Corporations can also exploit Carol’s data for monetary gain while excluding her from the financial benefits of the data she creates. Carol does not understand how companies use this data or who uses it. She benefits from sharing some information with some, but she is wary of sharing some information with other entities. Forcing disclosure is oppression because, once disclosed, information can lead to social marginalization. Excluding vulnerable users from the monetary benefits of their data can lead to financial marginalization.

Instead, EAs can share information about users at levels of completeness and authenticity, depending on the context. For instance, users might be more comfortable with complete information to a spouse but might restrict slightly to children and restrict even further to potential employers. Users might also be willing to share different information for compensation. EA can help users manage information sharing in diverse settings.

## An EA Should Learn from How the User Tailors Their Impressions

Carol presents herself in different ways in different contexts. These depend upon the choices that she makes and aspects of her personality. However, it is tedious for Carol to keep up with whom she is sharing what. EAs can recognize over time how users choose to present themselves.

Some contexts may be learning opportunities for the EA, while others may be one-off impression management. An EA should learn to handle everyday situations without requiring user input. Other situations, such as a rare job interview, may need more user governance.

## An EA Should Strategically Obscure Information

Carol worries that Informania could use information from the contact-tracing app against her. Despite the app’s potentially positive intent in one context, Informania will use that information to oppress Carol in other contexts. Avoiding the app may not help either. Carol may inadvertently reveal that she has something to hide.

An EA can help by obscuring certain user behaviors through irrelevant behaviors, creating noise. For example, by downloading numerous other apps, a user’s underlying preference is obfuscated. EAs could search for irrelevant information or encrypt communication for mundane activities. Agents could also engage in adversarial tactics to circumvent Informania’s data collection efforts when users do not want that information collected.

## An EA Should Help Correct Inaccuracies

Carol is worried about what her potential employer might think about her from her app data. Her concern is more extensive than just the discovery of accurate information. Given the nature of the internet, potential employers and others may find false or misleading information about Carol online. Informania may inadvertently oppress when it uses suspect data.

As an initial step, EAs could help users identify information about themselves that others may find. Going further, EAs could help by making it easy for Informania to verify personal information. Currently, users abdicate responsibility for verification to platform badges (e.g., verified accounts, ORIC IDs), but an EA could be a source of authentication instead.

## Near Term Principle #4: Design to Educate about Alternative Perspectives

Human consciousness is a goal of emancipation. For emancipatory pedagogy to succeed, actors must consider alternative perspectives with an open mind. Through exposure to multiple perspectives, humans can synergize the strengths and weaknesses of each. EAs can improve users’ ability to develop robust, unconstrained rationality.

Consider David. David loves social media. Who knew the world had so many people just like him? It is easy to lose time reading the articles and watching the videos. Social media platforms present content in a nice list so that once he finishes consuming one, even more sensational content seems to call for him to read next. It is much easier for David to click the next link than spend time searching for different content. Still, the world is not perfect. While some people are as informed as he is, David cannot help but wonder how others can be so crazy and uninformed.

As with our previous examples, imagine this type of behavior control writ large across all interactions within society as it would be in Informania, where the only people one interacts with are those who are most like them. By controlling the information flow, Informania controls the types of people with whom David will interact and have extended relationships. For emancipatory pedagogy to succeed, actors must be humble and consider alternative perspectives with an open mind (Freire 1970). As Informania limits David’s exposure to diverse frames of meaning, it diminishes his ability to develop robust ideologies. David becomes entrenched in extreme views to the extent that he cannot even perceive or relate to counterarguments. Exposure to multiple and diverse frames of meaning reduces each frame’s persuasive power (Ball-Rokeach 1985). When an EA exposes an individual to multiple perspectives, individuals can synergize each’s strengths and weaknesses. By increasing David’s exposure to robust frames of meaning, an EA can help David develop robust, unconstrained rationality. Education about alternative perspectives can help in three ways.

## An EA Should Promote a Diversity of Perspectives

As Informania learns David’s preferences, it continues to recommend content that maintains engagement with the platform. Oppression experienced by David manifests in that he does not achieve critical consciousness. Any consciousness he reaches is at the whim of Informania. It is difficult for Informania to know when to expand David’s preferences and how much expansion David is willing to consider. If Informania receives revenue based on article clicks, showing broader content than his preferences may result in fewer clicks—and less revenue.

Instead, EAs can inform users that the information is not valid or that platform objectives conflict with the user’s other objectives. An EA could inform others in the user’s social circle who look at reputable news outlets and those who do not. Critical consciousness is a challenging state for anyone to stay in, so the EA must continuously encourage critical consciousness. An EA could inform Informania of the value of expanding David’s horizon of content to consume.

## An EA Should Learn Modes That Facilitate User Education

In the scenario, David does not know about content that he does not view. He does not know about content consumed by those who might influence him, particularly those who have different views. There is little information about how David might react outside of his comfort zone.

The EA can search for and present information sources with diverse perspectives and gauge its reaction. With that information, the EA can provide a richer article landscape and indirectly encourage critical consciousness. An agent could monitor the information environment, ensuring that David receives different perspectives in that information environment to be aware of these alternative perspectives.

## An EA Should Distinguish Between Data and Interpretation of Data

People, David included, like sensational content. Informania knows that well. The problem is that David may interpret sensational content as fact, failing to distinguish between the data and the data interpretation. Consequently, David’s consciousness about his society may end up being driven by an Informania sensationalization algorithm.

The EA can help assess the validity of data and the many alternative perspectives possible. Instead, David may want to explore shocking content to learn about others’ perspectives or understand propaganda techniques. In such cases, David can provide feedback to the EA that he views the content out of curiosity but does not interpret them as factual. This feedback will train the EA to distinguish between exploration of problematic content versus domination of an Informania ML system that optimizes on oppressive outcomes.

## Far-Term Principles: Design Organizing Principles for the Far Future State

Complete emancipation is elusive; oppressive threats require constant, enduring vigilance (Young 2018). Short-term gains in emancipation can lead to greater emancipation in the distant future. We may be unlikely to avoid Informania entirely, but EAs can help make it less oppressive. The critical difference between less oppressive Informania and the dystopian future we imagined at the outset of the paper is that the presence of powerful and widespread EAs provide individuals greater freedom to think critically, assert their agency, and experience inclusion. If EA can make Informania less oppressive, it will be in Informania’s best interests to work with the EA through various application programming interfaces (APIs) to negotiate Pareto-optimal outcomes for parties at both levels.

Nevertheless, societies tend toward oppression (Freire 1970), and this far-future state will not necessarily maintain a permanent state of emancipation on its own. Three organizing principles can help ensure that this less oppressive Informania remains robust and healthy. These organizing principles simply intend to provoke further thought, but a more exhaustive treatment is beyond the scope of what is possible here.

## Establish a Certification Body for the Development of EAs

The market forces that emerge to support the development of EAs could devolve to prioritize non-emancipatory outcomes such as ease of use or EAs sales to serve the interests of the EA developers, instead of individual emancipation. In this case, the oppressive influence simply moves from one ML algorithm to another. Certification bodies could ensure that EAs have certain essential features and outcome standards. Certifications already promote trust in a variety of societal components from electrical parts to public accountants. Certification bodies could also establish a professional ethics system, legally requiring EA developers to support an individual’s emancipatory goals, much like fiduciaries must act in their client’s best interest. Certification bodies could also validate new features as EAs evolve. EAs that conform to these bodies’ standards could display this certification, such as the Trust-e badge for internet privacy or the LEED certification for sustainable construction.

## User Certification Programs

Human partners to an EA must also participate in their emancipation. Users in Informania must first learn general EA literacy, understanding why these systems exist and the emancipatory processes they enable, before they can effectively use these tools. This process could involve interpersonal training with human “emancipation coaches” or be available through some sort of online training module that the human and the EA engage in together, much like a gaming tutorial. Traditional educational systems (K-12, university) could also develop various curricula to understand, use, and develop EAs.

## Emancipatory Audit Committees

We envision that this less oppressive Informania will represent a widespread ecosystem of ML algorithms at the individual and platform levels (e.g., government, platform providers, API providers, data sources, individual users, social networks of users, etc.). Nevertheless, different segments of this ecosystem may have very different goals, objectives, and norms from one another. Emancipatory efforts often have paradoxical effects (Lin et al. 2015; Miranda et al. 2016), making it difficult to gauge the net emancipatory or oppressive impact of solutions and governance policies. Governing bodies may establish norms and standards within each particular segment of Informania. Audit committees (groups of humans and dedicated ML systems to monitor the health of the collective) can promote adherence to the collective’s stated standards. For example, if diversity and inclusion is a stated goal of a particular collective, the audit committee could monitor trends along multiple dimensions, notifying the committee and individuals when trends violating that goal emerge. This committee might also identify best practices that support the stated goals of the community.

This research fundamentally challenges familiar notions of how ML systems and users can and should interact. In doing so, we open new paths of inquiry for a broad range of IS subdisciplines. Table 4 provides just a few of the many research questions raised by this theory. We encourage researchers to pursue these questions and others related to emancipatory information systems.

## Discussion: Emancipatory Assistants Yield an Adversarial Ecosystem

This paper argues that ML algorithms’ current design is inherently oppressive to humans, and this oppressive influence will only grow as ML systems become widespread. The core intuition behind our assertion is simple. If ML systems do not optimize on human well-being, they will ultimately oppress humans and subvert their free will once it approaches the scale and scope of Informania as it seeks to optimize on other factors. We do not ascribe nefarious intent on behalf of those who develop and implement ML systems. We simply argue that such an outcome represents the logical conclusion of our current trajectory.

## The Dose of ML Makes the Oppression

The Swiss philosopher Paracelsus is credited with the phrase “the dose makes the poison,” meaning that many common edibles that are harmless or beneficial in small doses— caffeine, acetaminophen, water—can become toxic when taken in too significant an amount. That perspective was our rationale for creating Informania in this paper. It unifies small oppressive examples into a singular experience that articulates an extrapolated current state’s oppressive potential. For example, Informania allows us to extrapolate a simple current example of algorithmic job matching (e.g., Rahman 2019) into one where the ML platform controls access to all job listings, job searches providing only listings for which an oppressive society decides someone is qualified. Informania begins to make these decisions for the individual early in one’s education, controlling its trajectory from the outset. While the current instantiation of ML systems in this instance does not seem oppressive, the extrapolated one certainly does. It is the dose of ML control that makes the oppression.

This extrapolation also allows us to consider the oppressive potential of innocuous applications of ML that seem unlikely to oppress today but might grow oppressive as the scope and scale of ML systems increase to the level of Informania. For example, it is not readily apparent how ML used in current navigation systems might become oppressive. As they grow to dominate navigation for autonomous vehicles, connect with other data sources, and become associated with other goals of Informania, however, imagining this oppressive influence becomes easier. For example, the ML system could choose routes to keep information from the passenger, such as intentionally avoiding areas that exhibit extreme poverty or extreme wealth in one’s community. Informania could combine navigation data with purchasing data, selecting a route that would take the passenger by a frequented restaurant because Informania knows it might persuade the passenger to stop and make a purchase at certain times of the day. It could intentionally create traffic slowdowns or ride scarcity if it wanted to keep people from joining a protest against Informania.

Users may initially regard many of these oppressive aspects of Informania as beneficial, and they only become oppressive as they begin to control an increasingly more significant portion of our lives. For example, even when today’s ML systems attempt to optimize on human desires, such as YouTube autoplay or Facebook newsfeed serving up content that it thinks users want to see, these algorithms simply use metrics that proxy for human preference (e.g., viewing time). These proxies can become distorted at scale because the current ML systems cannot know or consider whether the user might not want to watch another video. What begins as a good faith attempt to provide a helpful service to users eventually may become an addictive mechanism trying to control their attention.

## EA as a Type of Adversarial ML System

Emancipatory pedagogy avoids calls for the oppressed to simply rise and overthrow or directly resist the oppressors, which simply destroys the oppressor or the oppression of a different group. Instead, emancipatory pedagogy advocates for ways to change the conversation, language, and interactions between oppressor and oppressed to create more emancipatory outcomes for all. Thus, our design theory does not directly resist, regulate, or boycott ML systems, common strategies advocated by others (e.g., Zuboff 2019). Instead, we outline the blueprint of a system that could enable new types of interactions between humans and ML systems that could allow organizations to continue to harness the considerable power of ML while also avoiding the oppressive future that the current trajectory portends.

The fundamental universal theme of emancipatory pedagogy is subject-subject relation, a mutual inquiry process where people see one another as authentic individuals and not just as cogs in a societal machine. This theme guided our design theory. We design an intermediary that could facilitate such a process of mutual inquiry between humans and ML systems, first by helping an individual uncover his or her authentic preferences and desires and then by providing Informania with a mechanism to factor those desires into its optimization function. Humans cannot view ML systems as authentic individuals, but they can learn to recognize when ML systems’ objectives might align with their own or lead to greater societal good, including companies providing desirable products or services that meet individuals’ needs or contribute to their well-being. This intermediary can then provide individuals with tools to resist those objectives when they do not align with their own and support and reinforce those objectives when they do. If both individuals and Informania can begin to understand the other’s objectives, they can learn to act in ways that optimize on shared objectives.

One might think of our EA as a type of adversarial ML system, which can occur in two different forms. The first form of adversarial ML is mostly destructive—like a cyberattack—that subverts ML models by providing deceptive input (Biggio and Roli 2018). For example, humans barely notice defects in an image, but strategic defects can cause ML systems to misclassify that image (Szegedy et al. 2013). Additionally, showing a split-second image of a stop sign on a digital billboard can fool Tesla’s Autopilot into stopping (Nassi et al. 2020). Similarly, slightly altering a physical object can make it undetectable to surveillance systems (Thys et al. 2019). An EA could provide analogous adversarial countermeasures to protect the individual from Informania’s influence. However, the purpose of these countermeasures is not sustained, direct resistance. Developers can train ML systems to be robust to these disruptions (Kruakin et al. 2016), and we expect that large organizations have more significant resources and capabilities than individuals to prevail in any arms race of direct resistance. Furthermore, sustained direct resistance is also not consistent with the ideals of emancipatory pedagogy. Instead, the purpose is for the EA to provide selective resistance, incentivizing the relationship between the EA and Informania’s ML systems to evolve into a second, more constructive form of an adversarial ML system.

This second approach to adversarial ML is known as generative adversarial networks (e.g., Mao et al. 2017; Zhang 2019). In this approach, two collaborating ML systems work iteratively to achieve a shared goal. They work together like sparring or training partners in sports, practicing against one another to improve their abilities, allowing each one to get closer to their goal as a result. For example, generative adversarial networks can generate realistic fake images. One producer ML system generates a realistic fake image; a second detector ML system attempts to classify which images are fake. The ML systems work together; the producer ML system keeps refining its output with feedback from the detector ML system until it can produce a realistic enough image to fool the detector.

An EA could engage in a similar generative adversarial relationship with Informania’s ML systems. When Informania performs an action, behavior, or outcome that is oppressive to the individual, the EA could provide sufficient countermeasures to thwart the interests of Informania actively. Nevertheless, this resistance is not applied uniformly. When Informania is not acting oppressively, the EA does not resist Informania’s actions. Perhaps most importantly, when Informania is acting to support or align with the individual’s goals, the EA may actively support Informania’s actions. In doing so, the EA provides feedback to Infomania’s ML systems, incentivizing it to act in non-oppressive or emancipatory ways. The EA’s objective is to provide just enough feedback through selective resistance to render working with the EA (or at least not against them) the functionally optimal route for Informania to accomplish its goals. This two-stage approach of adversarial ML differs somewhat from traditional generative adversarial networks in that the designers do not program the ML systems to work together toward a common goal from the outset. Instead, the designers program the EA with mechanisms that seek to bring about such collaborative interaction.

## Incentivizing a Different Trajectory for ML Development

A critical feature of this adversarial approach to our design theory is that it does not require current ML designers to do anything differently in the short term to alter the trajectory of ML. We do not expect that large organizations currently developing and implementing ML systems have any interest in preventing Informania; the current ML trajectory serves their interests quite well. Instead, we hope to achieve a more modest goal of convincing a small set of ML designers and researchers that a different approach to ML is needed, desirable, and possible, and to inspire these designers and researchers to design an EA that can negotiate with existing ML platforms to achieve more emancipatory outcomes. We also hope our design theory might convince them that our approach could also enjoy a strong business case because it involves developing a fundamentally new product that individuals would want. Indeed, we see EAs as a simple extension of the existing strategy of competing to meet users’ intangible demands. For example, companies like Apple and Firefox have already adopted strategies to be the company that cares most about users’ privacy.

The widespread availability of EAs would then fundamentally change the ML environment to incentivize large platform companies to adopt a different ML development approach. Traditional ML designers would face a fundamentally different choice for how to develop their systems. Designers who choose not to incorporate the feedback of EAs and continue to ignore human freedom in ML model optimization may begin to encounter considerable and varied resistance from an entire population of adversarial ML systems. In contrast, ML designers that do begin to incorporate feedback from EAs and align the objectives of ML models in a way that does not oppress human freedom may find that they have access to vast new datasets and ubiquitous feedback mech anisms that allow them to accomplish their objectives better. When ML systems begin to cooperate with EAs, the EAs becomes a vast and rich sensor network that provides the ML systems with immense amounts of data for optimization, far greater than they could obtain without cooperating.

If the population of EAs does become an asset for accomplishing ML objectives, then designers will proactively seek ways to incorporate feedback from EAs, even when it might not be strictly necessary. For example, in our earlier navi gation example, ML systems can chart an efficient route without EA feedback. Nevertheless, people may be more eager to use a navigation system that allows them to optimize on additional goals, such as avoiding unsafe bridges, having a more scenic view, or plotting a route that takes them by their favorite restaurant for takeout. The critical difference between this example and our earlier oppressive one is that the individual and their EA determine these other goals, not Informania exclusively.

Our design theory seeks to enable both the individual and Informania to be better off because of this partnership. The EA provides the individual user with a type of enlightened free will. The EA can work with the individual to help uncover and articulate their authentic goals and objectives and guide them toward taking actions or making decisions that support those goals. The EA can then work with Informania to help the individual align their goals with the overall societal good when consistent with their personal goals and objectives. The EA could also help Informania achieve a type of enlightened or ethical optimization that seeks to achieve platform level objectives while minimizing oppression for the individuals subject to those decisions. This enlightened optimization could also result in better overall outcomes for Informania because Informania would have a far greater number of empowered individuals seeking to achieve these objectives, rather than billions of cogs merely being subject to and potentially resisting its influence. In this paper, we have only raised how such enlightened free will and enlightened optimization might look. More research would be necessary before developers could fully implement these ideas and become a reality. We hope this paper inspires such research.

## Conclusion

We hope that this paper’s lasting implication is that other IS researchers incorporate similar far-term horizons into IS research. One modest way of doing so is to consider far-term horizons in the theoretical implications of research papers. While sections of research papers already theorize about short-term futures when dealing with theoretical and practical implications, an additional section that explores the potential long-term disruptive influence of their research may stimulate thinking. These sections will need to, by design, extrapolate beyond the focal findings. We should encourage this welllabeled extrapolation. If researchers engage in a similar type of thought exercise for every paper they publish, even in a small way, it could open our minds up to thinking more expansively about the future possibilities of IS in multiple settings. Perhaps our field needs a little more thinking and theorizing that accepts the risks involved in constructing a more emancipatory future of IS.

Expanding on this idea, IS researchers should engage in similar thought and design science exercises for other emerging issues. Historically, the capabilities of technology limited what society could do. Now, as rapidly developing technologies erode prior limits, questions shift from “What can we do?” to “What should we do?” Society does not yet have tools to handle emerging complex issues such as the blurred reality from video generation, pervasive influence of ubiquitous computing, or cascaded vulnerabilities from device interdependence. IS research can help. However, limiting ourselves to investigating existing phenomena or theorizing about near-term implications will mute any potential impact. Given the fusion of social and technical expertise that IS research embodies, IS researchers can theorize boldly about the far-term consequences of emerging technologies on society. If we do so, our research can potentially have a more significant impact on business and society, possibly affecting those technologies’ trajectory as they emerge.

## About the Authors

Gerald C. (Jerry) Kane is a professor of Information Systems and Faculty Director of the Edmund H. Shea, Jr. Center for Entrepreneurship at Boston College’s Carroll School of Management. He researches and teaches about how companies can understand and respond to digital disruption to undergraduate, graduate, and executive education students worldwide. He has published over 100 papers, articles, and reports on these topics in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Organization Science, Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Harvard Business Review, and MIT-Sloan Management Review. He is lead author of two books The Technology Fallacy: How People are the Real Key to Digital Transformation and The Transformation Myth: Leading Your Organization Through Uncertain Times, both published by MIT Press.

Amber G. Young is director of the Information Systems Ph.D. Program and an assistant professor of Information Systems at the Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas. Her research focuses on how organizations can design and implement technology for social good and human empowerment. She is concerned with the effects of technology on individuals’ humanity and dignity. Amber’s work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information & Organization, Communications of the AIS, The London School of Economics Business Review, and various conferences. She is on the editorial board of Information & Organization.

Ann Majchrzak is the Associates’ of USC Chair of Business Administration for the Marshall School of Business at the University of Southern California. She is a professor of Digital Innovation in the Department of Data Sciences and Operations. Ann is a Senior Scholar and Fellow of the Association for Information Systems, awarded for “making an outstanding contribution to the I.S. discipline in research, teaching, and service.” She has been a member of three National Research Council Committees. She publishes in top academic (Management Science, Organization Science, Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly) as well as top practitioner (Harvard Business Review, Sloan Management Review, California Management Review) journals. She has held concurrent appointments as a research mentor and visiting professor at Esade Business School, Ramon Llull University, Barcelona; Department of Business and Management at LUISS University,

Rome, in the areas of Innovation and Organization; and Stevens Institute of Technology. She is also an external expert for the Information Systems and Innovation Group, Department of Management at the London School of Economics. She has a recent book (Palgrave, 2020) Unleashing the Crowds: Collaborative Solutions to Wicked Business and Societal Problems describing the results of a 5-year study of identifying success factors for innovation.

Sam Ransbotham is a professor of information systems and McKiernan Distinguished Fellow at Boston College. Sam earned a bachelor’s degree in chemical engineering, an MBA, and a Ph.D. all from the Georgia Institute of Technology.
