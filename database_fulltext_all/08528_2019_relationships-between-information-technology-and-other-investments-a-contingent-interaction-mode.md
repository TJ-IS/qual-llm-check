# Relationships Between Information Technology and Other Investments: A Contingent Interaction Model

Abstract. Recent studies on the business value of information technology (BVIT) investments have focused on understanding IT’s impact on firm performance, either in the context of other organizational resources or in the light of contingency factors. This study extends the prior literature by integrating these two perspectives and examining the BVIT while simultaneously considering other organizational resources and one importan contingency factor—environmental turbulence. We develop a contingent interaction model, proposing that a firm’s IT interacts differently with a resource focused on fostering innovation (i.e., research and development [R&D]) and a resource focused on enhancing the competitive gains in the market (i.e., advertising), depending on the extent of environmental turbulence in its industry. Specifically, we hypothesize that as the extent of environmental turbulence in a firm’s industry increases, the positive effect of the interaction between IT and R&D investments on firm performance increases, but the positive effect of the interaction between IT and advertising investments on firm performance decreases. Our findings, based on a longitudinal sample of 294 firms observed from 1999 to 2008 and collected from three archival sources (InformationWeek, COMPUSTAT, and CRSP) provide evidence that supports the proposed hypotheses. Additionally, the results of a split sample analysis reveal that the interaction between IT and R&D has a positive effect on firm performance in turbulent environments but a negative effect in stable environments Conversely, the interaction between IT and advertising has a negative effect on firm per formance in turbulent environments but a positive effect in stable environments. Put together, these findings indicate that a firm’s IT interacts differently with other organizationa resources depending on (a) the resource’s focus on value creation through innovation or value appropriation in the market; and (b) the extent of turbulence in the firm’s industry. This paper discusses the theoretical and empirical implications of these findings in detail.

## Introduction

The business value of information technology (BVIT) has received considerable attention in the information systems (IS) literature (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Bharadwaj 2000). Initial studies on the BVIT focused on the direct effects of information technology (IT) investments on a range of financial performance measures (e.g., Barua et al. 1995, Hitt et al. 2002). These studies generally support the positive role of IT in enhancing firm performance (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Melville et. al. 2004). However, the recent BVIT literature has moved beyond the direct effects of IT investments to examine the interactions either across various types of IT resources (e.g., Aral et al. 2012) or between investments in IT and other organizational resources (e.g., research and development [R&D]; Bardhan et al. 2013).

Despite their observed direct effects on firm performance, IT investments do not work in isolation. Instead, they work (a) along with other resources; and (b) within the context of the firm’s industry environment. The first of these aspects relates to the interactions among resources in generating value for a firm. Prior literature on this aspect examines how IT investments operate with other investments while affecting firm performance (Aral et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013). The second aspect reflects a contingency view, which argues that there is no universal best way to manage resources, and resources should instead be managed according to the context (e.g., Lawrence and Lorsch 1967, Thompson 1967). The contingency view has been used to examine how the industry environment moderates the effects of IT investments on firm performance (Stoel and Muhanna 2009, Xue et al. 2012, Steelman et al. 2019).

Both resource interactions and contingencies are important in considering the BVIT but have typically been examined independently. Despite the recognition that the impacts of IT and other resources on firm performance may vary across different environments, our knowledge of IT’s interactions with other resources under differing environmental conditions is preliminary (Nevo and Wade 2010). Thus, prior literature recommends the consideration of three-way interactions among IT, other key resources that enhance key capabilities, and environmental uncertainty (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008, Rai and Tang 2013) and uses the analogy of a “threesome tango” (El Sawy et al. 2010, p. 835) to refer to the simultaneous consideration of these interconnected concepts. Following the above recommendation, this paper combines the interactions between investments in IT and other resources with environmental uncertainty to develop a contingent interaction model of the impact of IT investments on firm performance. More specifically, it examines the influence of environmental turbulence (i.e., the rate at which a firm’s environment changes over time) (Vitale et al. 1986) on the performance outcomes of interdependencies between IT and other organizational resources. To understand the contingent interactions of IT and other organizationa resources under differing environmental conditions, we focus on two important organizational resources: R&D and advertising. These investments have previously been examined as important production inputs, along with IT, in the BVIT literature (Mithas et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013, Mithas et al. 2017). However, they differ in that R&D helps the firm in creating value by advancing innovations and new products, whereas advertising helps the firm in appropriating value by differentiating itself from its competitors (Mizik and Jacobson 2003, Mithas et al. 2012). Thus, this paper examines the following question: How does the turbulence of the industry environment affect the way in which interactions of investment in IT with investments in (a) R&D and (b) advertising resources influence firm performance?

We argue that IT investments contribute to firm performance through both value-creating and valueappropriating effects. Moreover, these two effects depend on the R&D and advertising resources available to the firm. In particular, investing in R&D (which increases the firm’s value-creating capability [Dutta et al. 1999]) enhances the value-creating effects of IT. By contrast, investing in advertising (which increases the firm’s valueappropriating capability [Dutta et al. 1999]) enhances the value-appropriating effects of IT. Thus, the interactions between IT investments and these two types of resources contribute to overall firm performance. Building on the extant literature that characterizes appropriate firm activities depending on the environmental condi tions (e.g., Xue et al. 2012), and as discussed further in the Theoretical Development section, we propose that environmental turbulence moderates the interactions of IT with these resources differently. Specifically, we argue that environmental turbulence enhances the positive effect of resources that enhance value-creating capabilities (i.e., R&D resources) in reaping benefits from IT investments but reduces the positive effect of resources that enhance value-appropriating capabilities (i.e., advertising resources) in reaping benefits from IT investments. Thus, although an increase in environmental turbulence increases the effect of the interaction between IT and R&D on firm performance, it reduces the effect of the interaction between IT and advertising on firm performance. Results based on panel data on 294 firms for 1999–2008 support these expectations. Further, split-sample analyses reveal that the interaction between IT and R&D has a positive effect on firm performance in turbulent environments but a negative effect in stable environments. Conversely, the interaction between IT and advertising has a negative effect on firm performance in turbulent environments but a positive effect in stable environments. The subsequent sections examine these contingent interactions and their implications for organizations allocating resources under varying environmental conditions.

## Theoretical Development

## Different Types of Organizational Investments

As mentioned above, prior literature examines the impact of IT investments on firm performance along with other investments. Prior literature also distinguishes between value-creating resources, which enable a firm to generate value in the market through innovations, and value-appropriating resources, which help appropriate value from the marketplace and existing resources (Mizik and Jacobson 2003, Joshi and Hanssens, 2010). This distinction relates to the difference between two specific non-IT resources—R&D and advertising—that seem to have received the greatest attention in the prior literature as important determinants of firm productivity (McAlister et al. 2007, Srinivasan et al. 2011). Investments in these two resources have been used as either control variables (Bharadwaj et al. 1999) or focal constructs (Mithas et al. 2012, 2017) in the BVIT literature. For example, Mithas et al. (2012) find that IT investments have greater effects on sales and profitability than R&D and advertising investments.

Prior literature indicates that R&D and advertising differ in the ways that they generate benefits for firms. R&D increases the firm’s ability to create value, because it enables the firm to innovate and create new opportunities through the discovery and creation of new methods and products (Mizik and Jacobson 2003). By contrast, advertising increases the firm’s ability to appropriate value, because it enables the firm to protect its competitive advantage by differentiating itself from its competitors (e.g., McAlister et al. 2007, Srinivasan et al. 2011) and directing advertising efforts to markets where it can best leverage its advantage (Mizik and Jacobson 2003, Lee and Bradlow 2011). Realizing the importance of both R&D and advertising and the difference in their strategic effects, we consider both of these resources while analyzing how IT generates business value in combination with other organizational resources.

## Resource Interactions

To examine the impacts of IT investments along with other resources that enhance value-creating and valueappropriating effects, we draw upon and extend the prior literature on resource interactions. A resource interaction refers to “how one resource may influence another and how the relationship between them affects competitive position or performance” (Teece 1986, p. 285). Resource interactions are usually viewed in terms of the marginal effects of the resources. The marginal effect of a resource is the change in performance as a result of investing in one unit of that resource as a production input (Brewer 2010). A positive interaction exists between two resources when the marginal effect of investing in a resource on performance increases as a result of investing in the other resource, whereas a negative interaction exists when the marginal effect of investing in a resource decreases as a result of investing in the other (Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2012, Tambe 2014).

Resource interaction is an important notion in IS and the resource-based view (RBV) literature (Bharadwaj et al. 2007, Bardhan et al. 2013). The RBV suggests that IT may interact differently with alternative organizational resources (Black and Boal 1994), possibly with a positive interaction with some resources and a negative interaction with others. Prior literature has examined the interaction between integrated IS capabilities and cross-departmental coordination (Bharadwaj et al. 2007) and between IT and other organizational resources, such as workplace organization (Bresnahan et al. 2002), human resources (Jeffers et al. 2008), and work practices (Jeffers et al. 2008).

Although prior literature on the performance consequences of IT investments has given considerable attention to R&D and advertising investments, the interactions among these investments have received less attention. Bardhan et al. (2013) propose, and find empirical support for, a positive interaction between IT and R&D investments in terms of the effects on firm performance (measured using Tobin’s Q). However, we are not aware of any prior empirical study that examines the interaction between IT and advertising investments.

To understand the effect of the interaction of IT investments with R&D and advertising investments, we use the above notion of the marginal effects of resources and the distinction between R&D and advertising investments as enhancing the firm’s value-creation and value-appropriation capabilities, respectively. IT resources, including hardware, software, and labor (or human capital), are versatile and can be utilized for different purposes. This is evident in the prior findings that IT investments enhance both revenue generation and cost reduction (e.g., Mithas et al. 2012); the strategic alignment literature (e.g., Sabherwal and Chan 2001 Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011) that indicates that IT can be used to support any business strategy; and prior suggestions that IT can be used to support any core competence (Ravichandran and Lertwingsatien 2005). Consistent with the prior literature, the overall IT investment can be used to pursue either value creation or value appropriation. In addition, the versatility of IT can be seen with specific technologies. For instance, consider business analytics technologies, including the ware house infrastructure, the online analytical processing applications, and analysts and developers interacting with them. These technologies and the individuals interacting with them can support either value creation, such as through new product development; or value appropriation, such as through promoting the existing products.<sup>1</sup> Through analyzing the data pertaining to moves made by competitors and analyzing the customer needs that are not currently met in a market, business analytics technologies can contribute to the recognition of future market opportunities and the identification and development of new products (i.e., the value-creating role of business analytics). On the other hand, through analyzing the areas of the marke that the current products have not permeated, analytics technologies can provide the firm with information about groups that could be targeted, their demographics, and the suitable means to attract them (i.e., the value-appropriating role of business analytics).

Thus, IT leads to firm performance through value creation and value appropriation. R&D and advertising influence these effects of IT. More specifically, R&D resources, and the capabilities they enhance, increase the value-creating effects of IT. For example, business analytics technology affects firm performance by creating value through identifying new market opportunities and prod ucts. This effect is likely to be greater if the firm possesses R&D resources that support ideation and development of new products. Conversely, advertising resources, and the capabilities they enhance, increase the value-appropriating effects of IT. For example, business analytics technology affects firm performance by appropriating value through new information about the target market. This effect is likely to be greater if the firm possesses advertising resources and capabilities to effectively influence the target audience.

Although investments in resources that enhance value-creating and value-appropriating capabilities increase the value-creating and value-appropriating effects of IT, respectively, a firm’s operating context can influence the extent to which this happens. Therefore, we use a contingency view to develop further insights into the heterogeneity of benefits gained from the interactions of IT with value-creating and value-appropriating resources.

## Contingency View and the Role of Environmental Turbulence

Contingency theory argues that resources should be managed depending on the context (e.g., Lawrence and Lorsch 1967, Thompson 1967). In the IS literature, a contingency view has been used to examine the effects of the industry environment and other factors on several aspects, including IS planning (Vitale et al. 1986), strategic IT decision making (Sabherwal and King 1995), and IT outsourcing (Lee et al. 2004). One contingency variable that has received considerable attention in IS literature is the turbulence, or dynamism, of the industry environment in which the firm operates (e.g., Vitale et al. 1986). The following definition is representative:

Environmental turbulence (dynamism) is the rate and instability of the environment, which is the result of changes in customer preference, development of new products, new technology, or the competition. (Stoel and Muhanna 2009, p. 182)

Literature on both strategy (e.g., Brown and Eisenhardt 1997) and the BVIT (e.g., Stoel and Muhanna 2009) argue and empirically find the impact of investments to be contingent upon the nature of the industry environment (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Xue et al. 2012). For instance, Tallon and Pinsonneault (2011) argue that IT-enabled agility is more beneficial in turbulent environments than in stable environments. Likewise, Barney (1991, p. 106) note:

That firm attributes must be valuable in order to be considered resources (and thus as possible sources of sustained competitive advantage) points to an important complementarity between environmental models of competitive advantage and the resource-based model. These environmental models help isolate those firm attributes that exploit opportunities and/or neutralize threats, and thus specify which firm attributes can be considered as resources.

Stable environments are characterized by the presence of sustained opportunities and enduring advantages (Xue et al. 2012). As a result, activities pertaining to the exploitation of existing opportunities, or value appropriation, are considered beneficial. Specifically, activities pertaining to the current products bear more fruit owing to the slower clock speed of changes in stable environments. Conversely, in turbulent environments, opportunities are not sustained and advantages are short-lived (Xue et al. 2012), requiring firms to explore new opportunities (Wang et al. 2012) and innovate.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, activities for exploring new opportunities are considered beneficial. Previous studies suggest that turbulent environments cause “unlearning” of current processes and routines (Miller 1987) while also being fertile for new opportunities when innovating (e.g., Li and Atuahene-Gima 2001). Thus, firms in turbulent environments may benefit more from the inception, innovation, and creation of new products and ideas than from marketing existing products. This argument is consistent with Xue et al.’s (2012, p. 511) conclusion that

in more stable environments, the pursuit of exploitation leads to superior financial performance, whereas in more unstable environments, the pursuit of exploration leads to superior financial performance.

Therefore, an increase in environmental turbulence increases the benefits from value creation, whereas a decrease in environmental turbulence increases the benefits from value appropriation. This conclusion can also be seen in terms of the specific investments of each kind (i.e., R&D and advertising, respectively): an increase in environmental turbulence enhances the effect of R&D investments through greater benefits from product innovation (e.g., Li and Atuahene-Gima 2001), whereas a decrease in environmental turbulence enhances the effect of advertising by lengthening the period during which the firm can appropriate value (Mizik and Jacobson 2003).

## The Contingent Interactions Mode

On the basis of the above discussion, firms need different resources to succeed in turbulent environments relative to stable environments (Xue et al. 2011). The RBV also suggests, “a combination of resources may not be of major benefit if a firm is unable to appropriate its returns” (Wade and Hulland 2004, p. 116). Thus, the industry environment affects the appropriability of resource combinations, and more specifically, how IT investments add to business value in conjunction with investments in other resources that enhance key capabilities (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008, El Sawy et al. 2010). This paper builds on the above conceptualization of resource interactions to develop the theoretical model of contingent interactions, including environmental turbulence as a contingency factor that moderates the interactions between IT and other investments on firm performance.

In our discussion of resource interactions, we concluded that R&D resources and the capabilities they enhance (i.e., value creation) increase the marginal effects of IT in value-creating activities. Moreover, in our above discussion of the role of environment, we concluded that an increase in environmental turbulence increases the benefits from value creation. A combination of these conclusions suggests that an increase in environmental turbulence would increase the marginal effect of the IT–R&D interaction on firm performance. This may also be seen in the context of the business analytics example. As the environmental turbulence increases, the higher rate of change in the market and the lack of enduring opportunities provide greater rewards for the IT-enabled and R&D-supported innovations. Therefore, when the environment is more turbulent, the marginal effect of R&D resources (e.g., better means of prototyping and the ability to turn concepts into products) in leveraging IT’s potential ability to identify new opportunities would be higher. Therefore, we posit the following:

## Hypothesis 1. The magnitude of the positive interaction between IT and R&D investments in their effects on firm performance increases as the environmental turbulence increases.

By contrast, in our discussion of resource interactions, we concluded that advertising resources and the capabilities they enhance (i.e., value appropriation) increase the marginal effects of IT in valueappropriating activities. We also concluded above that a decrease in environmental turbulence enhances the benefits from value appropriation. Thus, a decrease in environmental turbulence would enhance the marginal effect of the IT–advertising interaction on firm performance. This may also be seen in the business analytics example. The use of analytics technologies to identify and target nonadopters of existing products, supported by effective advertising channels in the firm, would foster exploitation of existing opportunities $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ value appropriation) and produce greater benefits in more stable environments. However, the rapid changes in more turbulent environments and the rapidly changing customer needs imply that the benefits from better advertising of existing products only endure for a short period and are unlikely to produce sustained competitive gains. Hence, we expect the following:

Hypothesis 2. The magnitude of the positive interaction between IT and advertising investments in their effects on firm performance decreases as the environmental turbulence increases.

## Research Methods

## Data Sources

Data for the study were collected from three sources: InformationWeek, COMPUSTAT, and CRSP. Our initial data set consists of information we were able to obtain from InformationWeek (which annually provides rankings of the 500 most innovative users of IT), on survey responses from the firms for 1999–2007. We supplemented these data with COMPUSTAT and CRSP data from 1999 to 2008, the additional year being needed owing to lagged variables in the analyses. Following prior research (Fama and French 2001, Von Eije and Megginson 2008), we excluded financial firms (Standard Industrial Classification [SIC] codes 6000 to 6999) because some of the variables included in our analyses differ largely between financial and nonfinancial firms. We also excluded foreign firms,<sup>3</sup> subsidiaries and divisions, and private firms, because stock market information is not available for them. Finally, because we needed segment data to compute a firm’s market share, we excluded firms whose business segment data are not available in COMPU-STAT. This produced an initial sample of 657 firms spread over one or more years, for a total of 2,224 firmyear observations. We only retained those observations for which all variables of interest can be calculated, reducing the sample for our analyses to 294 firms and 1,042 firm-year observations. Industries with 10 or more firms in the sample include manufacturing (170), information (34), utilities (24), wholesale trade (18), retailing (18), and transportation and warehousing (16). The sample firms have mean annual sales of \$6.1 billion (standard deviation [SD] = \$18.7 billion); mean number of employees of 20,890 (SD = 5,910); mean market share of 6.22% (SD = 11.21%); and the mean ratio of investments in R&D, advertising, and IT to annual sales is 13.82%, 2.62%, and 3.31%, respectively.

## Measures

We measure firm output using the Tobin’s Q ratio (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Chari et al. 2008). Tobin’s Q is a forward-looking market-based measure of firm value. It is risk-adjusted and less vulnerable to changes in accounting practices (Chari et al. 2008). A Tobin’s Q greater than one indicates that the long-run equilibrium market value of the firm is greater than the replacement value of its assets, signifying an unmeasured source of value (Bharadwaj et al. 1999). We use the Chung and Pruitt (1994) measure of Tobin’s Q. In this measure, Q = (market value of equity + book value of inventories + liquidating value of preferred stock + long-term debt + net short-term debt)/total assets. Net short-term debt is measured as the difference between current assets and current liabilities. R&D investment and advertising investment are computed using COMPUSTAT data as ratios of R&D to sales and advertising to sales, respectively.<sup>5</sup> Responses from InformationWeek surveys indicate the overall ratio of the firm’s annual worldwide IT budget to the firm’s annual sales. We use these data to calculate investments in IT. Finally, we measure environmental turbulence as the industry’s sales volatility following Xue et al. (2011). We regress the natural log of total sales of the four-digit SIC industry to which a firm belongs against an index variable of years, for a period of five years [t, t-4] and use the antilog of the standard error of the regression coefficient to measure sales volatility.

We compute assets<sup>6</sup> as the ratio of tangible assets to sales. We compute industry concentration as the Herfindahl index of the market shares of the top four firms in that industry (i.e., the sum of the squares of the market shares of the four firms with the highest sales in that industry). ## Estimation

We standardize all variables before computing interactions to reduce multicollinearity (Cohen et al. 1975). Because of the use of panel data, we evaluate heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. The variance of the error term for each industry may fluctuate over time and across firms, resulting in panel-level heteroskedasticity. The likelihood ratio test $[ \chi ^ { 2 } ( 5 2 ) = 9 6 . 3 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ]$ indicates heteroskedasticity. The Wooldridge (2010) test [F(1,167) = 12.44, p < 0.001] indicates the presence of AutoRegression(1) [AR(1)] autocorrelation in the data. Because autocorrelation could differ in magnitude across firms, we test for panel-specific AR(1) (PSAR1) through a likelihood test of similar correlations across firms. On the basis of the results of this test [LR χ<sup>2</sup>(52) = 10.316, p < 0.001], we use feasible generalized least square (GLS) regression with corrections for PSAR1 autocorrelation and panel-specific heteroskedasticity, which is a random-effects estimation that also removes firmspecific time-invariant unobserved heterogeneities.

## Split-Sample Analysis

The hypotheses tested in the three-way interaction models focus on how relationships change with an increase in turbulence. To better interpret the results, we conduct split-sample analyses in stable and turbulent environments. Although the three-way interaction analyses help in understanding whether the extents of the interactions between IT and R&D and between IT and advertising change under differing levels of environmental turbulence, it does not clearly show whether the change in the extent of the interaction changes the sign of the interaction. In other words, a positive three-way interaction between environmental turbulence, IT, and R&D does not distinguish between the following two situations: (a) there is a positive twoway interaction between IT and R&D in both low environmental turbulence and high environmenta turbulence contexts, but the interaction increases significantly as turbulence increases; and (b) there is a negative two-way interaction between IT and R&D in a low environmental turbulence context but a positive two-way interaction between IT and R&D in a high turbulence context, with the difference between the interactions in the two environmental contexts being significant. A split-sample analysis differentiates between these two situations.

Alternative Test of Resource Interactions. The above split-sample analysis is based on interaction effects. Milgrom and Roberts (1995) and Brynjolfsson and Milgrom (2012) provide an alternative way of examining positive and negative interactions. Milgrom and Roberts represent two resources that have a positive interaction<sup>10</sup> as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\Pi (\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{H}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) > [ \Pi (\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{L}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) ]} \\ & {\quad + [ \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{H}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) ],} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where Π(x, y) represents firm performance as a result of investment at different levels (indicated through H and L, for high and low levels, respectively) of the two investments of interest. For example, Π(H, L) indicates firm performance at a high level of investment in the first resource and a low level of investment in the second resource. Similarly, for resources with a negative interaction, Equation (3) takes the following form:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\Pi (\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{H}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) <   [ \Pi (\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{L}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) ]} \\ & {+ [ \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{H}) - \Pi (\mathrm{L}, \mathrm{L}) ].} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Brynjolfsson and Milgrom (2012) suggest an alternative test of positive and negative interactions, in which the sample is divided into four quadrants based on high and low levels of investment in the two resources involved. Then, the mean difference in firm output is computed between each of the three other quadrants and the low–low quadrant, while controlling for the levels of production inputs. The mean difference for each quadrant reflects $\mathrm { \dot { \Pi } } \Pi ( . , ~ . ) \mathrm { ~ - ~ } \Pi ( \mathrm { L } , ~ \mathrm { L } )$ . Addressing the Issue of Endogeneity. The BVIT research is cognizant of the potential for reverse causality between a firm’s performance and its investments in IT. This can make the residuals of our model correlate with the independent variables and bias the coefficient estimates (Greene 2002). One approach to address this issue is to search for proper instrumental variables to estimate a two-stage least squares analysis. Although instrumental variable candidates are limited (Bardhan et al. 2013), we have used several instrumental variable approaches (see Online Appendix B) based on prior literature. An alternative is to use the Arellano-Bover/ Blundell-Bond system of generalized method of moments (system GMM, hereafter) estimator (Arellano and Bond 1991, Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998).

This method is suitable for an unbalanced panel with a relatively large number of observations, independent variables that are not strictly exogenous, and the presence of serial correlation and heteroskedasticity (Roodman 2009). The estimations based on this method are nonbiased (e.g., consistent) and efficient in the presence of endogenous variables as well as fixed effects Following prior literature (Aral et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013, Sabherwal et al. 2019), we treat IT and all its interactions terms as endogenous.<sup>11</sup> We then use a twostep system GMM model to obtain estimates that are robust to both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation and use robust standard errors to correct for possible bias in estimating standard errors. Specifically, we estimate robust standard errors through Windmeijer correction that adjusts for downward biases in estimating the standard errors (Roodman 2009). The estimator uses a system of two equations. One equation is a transformation obtained by first-differencing variables in the level equation, whereas the second equation uses the original level. After removing the fixed effects through first-differencing (in the first equation), the system GMM estimator uses lagged values of the endogenous variables and their differences as instrumental variables to control for endogeneity.

For the accuracy of such a specification, the instruments and error terms should be exogenous, implying that the error terms should not be autocorrelated. If the error terms are autocorrelated, longer lags should be used in the transformed equation. The results of AR(2) tests indicate that there is no serial correlation in the second differences of residuals (Arellano-Bond test: z = $- 0 . 7 4 , p > 0 . 1 0 )$ , the Hansen test of over-identification fails to reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are orthogonal to errors $[ \dot { \chi } ^ { 2 } ( 2 6 9 ) = 1 5 . 5 7 , p > 0 . 1 0 ] ,$ , and the difference-in-Hansen test of exogeneity fails to reject the null hypothesis that the subsets of instruments used in the level equations are exogenous $[ \chi ^ { 2 } ( 2 4 4 ) = 3 3 . 4 5 ,$ $p > 0 . 1 0 ]$ . Model 6 (in Table 3) presents the results of our GMM estimation, indicating that after accounting for the threat of endogeneity, the results remain qualitatively robust.

We conduct seven additional endogeneity tests to assess the robustness of the main results. Online Appendix B describes them, along with the associated results. First, while still using the system GMM estimation, we consider R&D and advertising, in addition to IT investment, and their interaction terms as endogenous variables instead of instrumenting only IT investment. Second, whereas in the main analysis we use only the second lags of first-differences as instruments, we test a model in which second and longer lags of first-differences are included as instruments. Next, we follow three additional approaches to instrument IT investment, based on Mithas and Rust (2016), Lewbel (2012), and Xue et al. (2012). Our sixth additional test to attenuate concerns about endogeneity focuses on the approach introduced by Garen (1984). This approach, implemented by Ho et al. (2017) in the BVIT literature, corrects for the selectivity of continuous endogenous variables through a two-stage procedure. Finally, we conduct a Granger’s causality test, which is better suited to address concerns that may arise owing to the simultaneity of independent and dependent variables. Online Appendix B describes these additional tests and presents their results. These results are consistent with the main results in Table 3, providing evidence that the key three-way interactions in the study remain significant after using a myriad of techniques and specifications that intend to reduce the threats of endogeneity.

## Discussion

The BVIT literature has evolved considerably over time, especially in (a) identifying the contingency factors, such as environmental uncertainty, that affect the BVIT; and (b) considering the effects of IT relative to other investments. However, the performance impacts of IT resources have generally been considered only through two-way interactions such as in conjunction with either environmental uncertainty or other resources. This has led to calls for examining the intertwined relationship among the three components: (a) IT, (b) other resources that enhance key capabilities, and (c) environmental uncertainty (El Sawy and Pavlou 2008, El Sawy et al. 2010). We address this need by developing and testing a contingent interaction model of the BVIT that combines all three aspects. Specifically, we show that IT interacts with R&D and advertising in very different ways. In the strategic management literature, R&D and advertising are conceptualized in contrasting ways—enhancing value-creating and valueappropriating capabilities (Mizik and Jacobson 2003), respectively. Our findings extend these arguments by proposing and empirically showing that these resources interact with IT in opposing ways in different environmental conditions. Consistent with our expectations, the results show that the marginal effect of the interaction between IT and R&D (advertising) on firm performance declines as the environmental turbulence decreases (increases). Furthermore, the split sample results suggest that the interaction between IT and R&D (advertising) has a positive effect on firm performance in turbulent (stable) environments. Our theoretical development explains the moderating effect of environmental turbulence on the interaction of IT with R&D and advertising in influencing firm performance.<sup>12</sup> However, the results also indicate a negative interaction between IT with R&D investments in stable environments and a negative interaction between IT with advertising investments in turbulent environments. These negative interactions, about which we had not hypothesized, may be interpreted as follows.

As discussed earlier, a decrease in environmental turbulence reduces the benefits from value creation. Because R&D enables value creation, if R&D investment is high in stable environments, it would enhance the less beneficial (in stable environments) value-creating effect of IT rather than the more beneficial valueappropriating effect of IT. More specifically, R&D focuses on creating value by identifying potential new markets, developing innovative products quickly, and launching the products before competitors catch up. IT can support several of these activities, but if used to pursue them in a stable market, where products do not change much, an increase in IT investment accompanied with an increase in R&D investment would lead to the firm using IT to try too many new things, thereby wasting resources. As a result, the marginal benefits from additional investment in IT would not match the associated costs, leading to inefficiencies in utilizing IT and depressing the economic performance. This argument finds support in the literature, indicating that explorative initiatives (such as IT initiatives supporting R&D) do not match stable environments, lead to too many directions, and cause economic loss (e.g., Bourgeois 1985).

Moreover, as also discussed earlier, an increase in environmental turbulence reduces the benefits from value appropriation. Because advertising investment enables value appropriation, if advertising investment is high in turbulent environments, it would enhance the less beneficial (in turbulent environments) valueappropriating effect of IT rather than the more beneficial value-creating effect of IT, such as improvisation (Pavlou and El Sawy 2010). More specifically, advertising focuses on appropriating value from existing products by identifying the target audience for those products, understanding the behavior of the target market, and providing potential customers with the appropriate message to buy. IT can support such activities, but if used to pursue them in a fast-changing market, the insights obtained from IT might lag behind the market changes. Thus, the marginal benefits from additional investments in IT would not match the associated costs, adversely affecting the economic performance.

The study’s results need to be examined in the light of its potential limitations. First, our knowledge of the specific components of IT investments is limited owing to the archival nature of the data. Greater insights may be gained if IT investment is further granulized through alternative methods of data collection, such as surveys of senior IT executives or firm announcements. Second, the effects of different investments—including IT, R&D, and advertising—on firm performance may occur over varying time periods. Finally, we use data on publicly traded firms that are listed in the InformationWeek surveys, which implies that our sample firms are among the more successful firms in terms of IT. This limits the generalization of our results to other, less successful, firms and suggests pursuing further research through data collected using other methods or from other sources.

Despite these limitations, the paper has several theoretical contributions. Three elements have previously been emphasized as affecting firm performance (e.g., El Sawy et al. 2010): (a) IT investments; (b) a firm’s value-creating/appropriating capabilities (as reflected by investments in R&D and advertising, which contribute to these capabilities); and (c) environmental turbulence. These three elements and their roles in generating value for firms have previously been studied either in isolation or in pairs (including through their two-way interactions) (e.g., Xue et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013). By contrast, we consider and empirically find that their triadic interactions provide additional value and insights. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to find empirical evidence that supports integrating the previously separate resource interaction and resource contingency views of IT, in accordance with the digital ecodynamics framework proposed by El Sawy et al. (2010).

Further, this study extends the previous literature on IT interactions and their impact on firm performance by showing that IT interacts differently with organizational resources, depending on the ways in which those resources enable firm value. Specifically, and as the previous literature (e.g., Mizik and Jacobson 2003) indicates, R&D and advertising differ markedly in their organizational roles. Whereas R&D is geared toward value creation, advertising is more concerned with value appropriation. Previous literature has discussed the interaction of IT with resources that are geared toward value creation, such as R&D. However, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to examine the interaction of IT with resources geared toward value appropriation, such as advertising, and compare it with IT’s better-known interaction with resources that are focused on value creation. A concurrent consideration of the interactions of IT with value-creating (i.e., R&D) and value-appropriating (i.e., advertising) resources unravels a sharp contrast between these interactions. Thus, this study highlights the importance of considering the underlying mechanisms through which IT and other key resources generate value, when investigating resource interactions.

The results also contribute to the literature on the BVIT and the impact of resource interactions on firm performance by highlighting the importance of viewing interactions between IT and other investments as contingent upon the context. This paper has focused on one important contingency: environmental turbulence. Not only do the paper’s findings provide evidence that environmental turbulence moderates the effect of the in teractions between IT and other organizational resources on firm performance, but they also show that the nature of those interactions differs substantially between stable and turbulent environments. Environmental factors ex plain the heterogeneity in how organizational resources, including IT, interact with one another. Thus, considering the firm’s environmental conditions facilitates the interpretation of the mechanisms that underlie the ef fectiveness of IT, other resources, and their interactions in generating value for the firm.

The paper also has some potentially important implications for practice. First, the interdependent nature of IT resources with other resources suggests that IT investments should be made while considering such interactions. This provides managers with actionable insights on investments in IT, while considering other investments. Furthermore, the paper provides empirical evidence that the interaction of IT with other organizational resources in generating value is dependent on the pace of change in an organization’s environment. More specifically, the results suggest that IT investments have a positive impact when combined with R&D investments in turbulent environments, and when combined with advertising investments in stable environments. Thus, in turbulent environments, managers should seek ways to use IT to complement R&D investments by investing in systems that enable the creation of knowledge and the identification and pursuit of new opportunities. By contrast, in stable environments, managers should seek ways to use IT to complement advertising investments by investing in systems that enable learning from past market events to appropriately target advertising efforts. Finally, IT may erode some of the marginal benefits of R&D and advertising investments in stable and turbulent environments, respectively. Therefore, managers should make cautious decisions when making concurrent investments in IT and R&D in stable environments, and exercise similar caution in coordinating investments in IT and advertising in turbulent environments.
