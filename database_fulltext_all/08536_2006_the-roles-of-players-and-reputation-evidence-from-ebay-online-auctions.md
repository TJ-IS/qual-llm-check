# The roles of players and reputation: Evidence from eBay online auctions<sup>☆</sup>

Jie Zhang

College of Business Administration, University of Toledo, MS #103, 2801 W. Bancroft St., Toledo, OH, 43606, United States

Received 21 May 2005; received in revised form 1 March 2006; accepted 8 March 2006

Available online 11 May 2006

## Abstract

The uncertainty about product quality and the traders' credibility may impede the liquidity and efficiency of online auctions. In contrast to the literature on online feedback mechanisms that evaluate sellers' reputation without distinguishing their roles in the transactions, we claim that “not all reputation is created equal” — bidders in online auctions assess sellers' reputation based on their feedback history gained from sales solely. This paper explores the impact of the consumers' above behavior on the reputation system with an analytical model and empirical evidence from a popular online auction website eBay. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Online auction; Asymmetric Information; Reputation

## 1. Introduction

Electronic business has grown dramatically over the past decade, represented by more than 25 % growth rate in total retail e-commerce revenue (US Census Bureau 2004). Consumer-to-consumer online auction, for example, eBay.com, Yahoo! Auction, and Amazon auction etc., has become one of the most successful business models of electronic commerce. Founded in 1995, eBay has become the most popular online auction marketplace: with 135.5 million registered users, eBay reported 1.4 billion listed items and \$34.2 billion worth of sold items in 2004. The fast growing online auction business indicates a promising future of e-commerce.

The success of the auction business comes from its several advantages. Internet provides a convenient and efficient marketplace for the consumers to trade among each other. In contrast with physical auction houses, online auction web sites have massive content storage capacity and hold no inventory, which puts minimum restriction on the number and variety of listings. They provide accessibility, anonymity and interactiveness to the customers. Easy access, handy searching, instantaneous information update, as well as the excitement involved in bidding attract many users to participate. More importantly, online auction has very low entry cost for sellers. Any ordinary individual can bring her goods to millions of potentially interested buyers worldwide without setting up any storefronts or bearing the high cost incurred in transactions. Therefore online auction web sites offer a virtual “flea market” where everyone can become a seller as easily as being a buyer.

However, the ideal “frictionless” marketplace cannot exist without trust among online traders. The virtuality and anonymity of this global marketplace challenges trust building in the Internet community, especially on the consumer-to-consumer online auction web sites. Buyers cannot inspect the goods or look at the face of a seller when trading in the online world. They have to rely on text description and images the seller posted on the web for product information. On the other hand, traders exist in the name of a faceless user ID, which could be created and changed easily. A trader could possibly have as many identities on the Internet as she has “eyebrows” in the real life. Hence, it is difficult for the buyers to tell the difference between a reputable online seller and a dishonest one who uses the Internet to “rob” people. Deterioration of this problem is evidenced by the growing number of frauds happening in online auctions. Internet Fraud Watch operated by the National Consumers League reported that online auction frauds accounted for nearly half of the complaints the agency received in 2003, a 130 % increase from 2001, and the total loss to online fraud reached \$200 million in 2003. While the FTC does not break out figures by companies, the vast majority of online auctions are conducted on eBay [9].

Reputation theory ([26,13,20]) and auction theory ([24,21,19]) indicate that the uncertainty about the sellers' honesty will affect the buyers' bidding behavior by lowering their bids and thus reduce market liquidity. To mitigate this negative effect, many auction web sites have set up some public feedback forums (Fig. 1) where bidders can comment on their trading partners based on their experiences. The basic idea is to use the voluntary inputs of anecdotal evidence about a user ID to hint the others about that user's credibility and help them make more informed bidding decisions. This feedback mechanism is expected to reflect the true honesty of the users and to identify the fraudulent ones. On the other hand, the downside effect of getting negative ratings will presumably threaten the participants to behave well in the transactions on those web sites.

The feedback system adopted by eBay and other auction sites quantifies the reputation measures of the bidders, making it possible to empirically examine the effect of the reputation on bidders' behavior. Hence, a serial of research took the transaction and reputation data from eBay and examined the effect of online feedback profiles on bidding outcomes: Ba and Pavlou [3], Bajari and Hortacsu [4], Dewan and Hsu [7], Houser and Wooders [10], McDonald and Slawson [17], Melnik and Alm [18], to name a few. As shown in Section 2 of the paper, fruitful results have been achieved in the literature. However, the conclusions are mixed and this paper provides some explanation for that.

All the prior research mentioned above assume that sellers only sell, while buyers only purchase from the market. In our view, one of the most important features of online auction marketplace, or more generally, most e-commerce markets, is the greatly reduced cost to become a seller. This means that sellers are no longer sellers in the traditional sense — a merchandiser or a retail store, which sells large quantity of products in the market with almost no purchase from the downstream market. Now anyone can sell their goods in the market for a very low cost, for example, sellers on eBay only pay a maximum of \$4.80 insertion fee plus a final value fee for a successful sale, which is 5.25 % of the final sales price for the first \$25 and discounted rate for higher sale prices. A user can take the role as a buyer at some time, and as a seller at other times. We claim that this is the defining feature, and the basic idea behind eBay — the multi-billion dollar business — a “flea market”. Unfortunately, the important implication of this feature has been missing in all the previous literature.

As far as we know, this paper is the first attempt to investigate the reputation effect of the double-role feature. Specifically, a user has dual roles as a buyer or as a seller in the online marketplace and can always changes the role at any time. Therefore the reputation has two components corresponding to each of the roles, respectively: “selling reputation”, coming from the previous selling records and “buying reputation”, coming from the previous buying records. Given the current feedback mechanism design of those online auction markets, the cost and benefit relationship for the users to build the two types of reputations varies, which gives incentive for the users to strategically manipulate their reputations. If the buyers do not distinguish the feedback from the two different sources, they may end up overbidding. However, rational buyers should be able to comprehend this and separate a seller's selling reputation from buying reputation when trying to infer the true probability of receiving the right product. As a result, the two types of reputation should have very different effects on buyers' willingness-to-pay.

The purpose of this research is to explore the following questions:

• Is all reputation created equal? Do bidders view the sellers' buying and selling reputation equally?

• For sellers, does their buying reputation affect the auction outcome to the same extent as their selling reputation?

• Do sellers strategically manipulate their reputation through buying on eBay and earning positive feedback easily?

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature of theoretical conclusions on reputation and empirical results on the effect of online feedback profiles. Section 3 describes the data collection process and some findings from our data sample. We theoretically model the seller and buyer behaviors and derive the hypotheses in Section 4. ## 2. Literature review

Reputation formation has been extensively studied in economics using game theory (See [6] for a detailed review). Uncertainty about product quality may lead to market failure. For example, in the used car market, with the absence of any signals about quality (dealers' reputation, credible guarantees, warranties, or certification), “lemons” will drive out “gems”, and eventually no one would like to buy and as a result no one would like to sell [1]. Reputation comes as a way to alleviate the information asymmetry by signaling the sellers' credibility based on the previous transactions. In a repeated game with asymmetric information, building reputation can improve a player's long-term payoff by achieving an outcome more efficient than stage-game Nash equilibrium ([26]). The above results were explained in [13] and [20], wherein the players were classified into two types: honest sellers who never cheat and opportunistic sellers who cheat whenever advantageous for them. In their models, the opportunistic sellers will choose to build reputation through cooperation (delivering the good with promised quality) when the long-run premium of being an honest seller outweighs the short-run profit from cheating.

An important assumption made in the above reputation literature is that a trader's identity as a buyer or a seller in the repeated game is exclusive and static. That is, a seller will not buy at any period of the game while a buyer will never sell either. The Internet and electronic commerce foster the interactive communication and greatly reduce the entry barrier for any buyer as well as for any seller. Thus the online auction websites have indeed become online “flea-markets”, where unlike in traditional commerce venues, participants can sell goods as conveniently as they buy stuffs.

A number of works have empirically studied the feedback mechanism on eBay. Some principal empirical research results on online feedback forums are summarized in Table 1 (Adapted from [23]). Those papers studied the impact of feedback ratings on the closing prices and/or probability of sales with different focuses. A trader's feedback comments from her previous trading partners are publicly observed. Actions of either a buyer or a seller, such as willingness to bid, bid amount, determination to complete the transactions, will be influenced by those ratings. They made assumptions based on the traditional reputation theory that a customer is either a buyer or a seller and that character, once formed, will never change over time. The findings of previous studies on the relationship between sellers' reputation and the bid prices are inconclusive or even conflicting. For example, [3,4,8,14] found that negative feedback has no significant impact on closing price for the whole or some of the sample; [16] found that positive feedback has no effect on prices; and [11] and [22] found that both forms of feedback have no significant impact on closing prices. Our study contributes by giving a reason for the mixed results, and moreover, by presenting our results with new managerial implications.

The uniqueness of our paper is that it looks at the dual-role of online traders in an auction environment. Therefore online auction users may behave differently from what the literature presented. For example, they may have incentive to manipulate their feedback ratings by acting differently as a buyer from as a seller. Anticipating this, rational buyers will not infer a seller's true reputation from all of his feedback comments, but care only about his feedback gained as a seller exclusively.

To our best knowledge, there are a couple recent papers, [25], [27], and [5], relevant to the idea of this paper. Nonetheless, their focuses are very different from ours.

Wang, Hidvegi, and Whinston [25] studied how to use a market mechanism to prevent shilling, which is the use of insincere bids on the seller's behalf to artificially drive up the price of the listing. Shilling is a type of online auction fraud. A shill seller may pretend to be a buyer to bid on her own items, however she aims to increase her reserve price secretly but not to receive positive feedback as a buyer. We focus on the reputation effect of the role switching enabled by the online market, which differs from concept of shilling.

Wood and Ow [27] examined how selling and buying experiences of buyers affect selling prices. They found that both selling and buying experiences increase selling prices. Our paper differs from theirs in that we examine how the roles of a seller in the previous transactions are taken into account when buyers assess the seller's reputation to decide their bids. In our empirical model, the experience effects have been controlled by other variables such as product and auction characteristics. We find the two types of reputation do have very different effects on prices: selling reputation significantly affects the closing prices but buying reputation does not.

Cabral and Hortacsu [5] noticed the phenomenon that some sellers not only sell, but also buy on eBay.

They found in their dataset that about one third of the sellers followed the “buy first, sell later” strategy. So they concluded that “buy first and sell later” is a widespread phenomenon on eBay. They interpreted this as evidence of their hypothesis that some sellers start off by investing (as a buyer) on reputation history, assuming that buyers would take the initial record of a seller as a genuine selling record although it contains selling as well as buying. Unsurprisingly, their empirical test did not support their theoretical prediction that sellers with bad reputation should engage in this activity. Essentially, we are asking what the effect of a seller's buying record on his reputation as a seller is. So we are not limiting our attention to this particular “buy first, sell later” phenomenon. If a good purchasing record does improve the seller's “reputation” and thus increases the selling prices, there is nothing to prevent the seller from “buying the reputation” (building good reputation through buying on eBay and receiving positive feedback) at any time after the initial period, although the seller may be more eager to do so at the beginning. But if buyers are capable of distinguishing a seller's selling behavior from his buying records, then the buying transactions would not affect the seller's “selling reputation”, hence would not affect the auction price.

Since all of the above literature overlooked the special characteristics of online traders and viewed equally a trader's feedback for the two different roles, we expect that our theoretical model and empirical study under this new assumption can lead to a better understanding of consumers' bidding behavior in online auctions and can explain the mixed results obtained in the prior research. If our hypotheses are proved, new suggestions can be provided to the online auction companies to improve their feedback mechanism.

## 3. Data collection and description

We wrote an intelligent web agent (“Spider”) program to collect transaction and feedback data of auctions of 15G and 20G Apple iPod MP3 players from eBay's website. The closing date of the auctions ranged from February 14 to May 27, 2004, during which period the market prices of the items remained stable, as reflected from web sites of iPod.com and such major electronic product web sites as CNET.com and PCMag. com. The spider program automatically downloaded the relevant transaction data including trading item, the seller's user ID and the winning buyer's ID, starting and end date/time, starting bid prices, end prices, number of bidders participated, description of the auction items and the whole feedback history that the seller had received from his previous transactions.

Our primary data set, Sample I, has 1768 valid auction transactions and is used to study the effect of reputation on probability of sale, which will be elaborated in Section 5.2. To study the effect of reputation on the auction's closing price (Section 5.1), we removed those auctions that received no bids or ended with a Buy-it-now price because those auctions do no have valid bids to reflect the buyers' true values. Then we were left with 1276 transactions, all of which ended with a successful bid. They constitute our Sample II.

## 3.1. Variables used in empirical analysis

The dependent variables in our two empirical models are denoted as ClosingPrice and Sold, respectively. ClosingPrice is the final price shown on the webpage when the auction is completed, while Sold is a dummy variable whose value is one when the auction ends with a sale and zero otherwise.

The seller's reputation is measured by the unique number of positive/negative feedbacks, which are derived from the seller's feedback history up to the closing time of that particular auction. Each buyer or seller might get multiple comments from a unique trading partner. In order to eliminate the bias of the comments from a particular customer and also to prevent reputation manipulation (e.g. shilling), we counted only the first comment of each type (e.g. positive or negative) left by a unique user ID. This is the way eBay calculates and reports users' reputation scores, and is also commonly used in the literature. Since any trader can earn feedback from either a sale or a purchase transaction, we separated the indices into two groups — from unique sellers and from unique buyers — according to the role the user played in the auction. We measure the seller's reputation with variables SellPos, SellNeg, BuyPos, BuyNeg, AllPos, and AllNeg, which are the numbers of positive and negative comments from unique buyers, from unique sellers, and from all unique registered users, respectively.

Characteristics of auction items are Size, Generation, NIB and Accessories. The auction items in our sample are homogeneous except for size (15G or 20G), generation (the 1st to the 3rd), degree of usage, and value of accessories. A greater capacity or later generation iPod MP3 player is valued more than a smaller or an earlier generation one. We control the properties with variables Size (0 as 15G and 1 as 20G) and Generation (1 to 3). A brand new iPod is typically valued more than a used one. Therefore we use a dummy variable NIB to represent the new or used property of the auction items. A standard iPod MP3 player comes with accompanying accessories like Apple earphones, AC adapter, firewire cable, PC firewire adapter, etc. Extra accessories increase the value of an item and missing ones reduce the value. Thus we collected the market prices of accessories from Apple Inc.'s online store and controlled the value of extra or missing accessories with the variable Accessories, which represents the market value of extra accessories (a positive value) or the missing ones (a negative value).

Other variables capture the auction's characteristics: NumofBids, Length, StartingBid, Shipping, Paypal, Gallery, Bold, Reserve, and BIN. NumofBids refers to the total number of bids appearing in the bidding history of an auction. Length is the number of days an auction listing lasts. StartingBid is the starting bid amount set by the seller. Shipping is the shipping and handling cost specified by the seller in the auction description page. Paypal, Gallery, Bold, Reserve and BIN are all dummy variables. Paypal takes one if the seller accepts payment through Paypal or credit cards, which provides certain degree of protection to buyers. Gallery and Bold, respectively, refer to whether the seller adds a thumbnail photo, or boldfaces the listing on search and listing pages. These two variables control the sellers' effort to grab the buyers' eyeballs. We use variables Reserve and BIN to indicate whether the seller sets a secrete reserve price and whether the seller adopts the Buy-it-now option in the auction.

## 4. The model

We develop a theoretical model based on traditional reputation theory [13,20,26], Ba [2] and Houser and

Wooders [10] to study how the role of a player in the previous auction transactions influences his reputation and the bidding outcome. Assume there are two types of players: honest (H) and dishonest (D). This honesty characteristic is determined exogenously by nature. The fraction of honest players is $\theta \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , which is common knowledge to all players. But the exact type of a player is private information to that player only. An honest player will always choose to cooperate, that is, delivering the right good if he is a seller or fulfilling the payment if he is a buyer. A dishonest player, however, will act strategically by choosing to cooperate or noncooperate, whichever brings him the optimal long-run profit.

All players live infinite periods. A player, or an online auction user, can sell or purchase a unit of indivisible product at any period. We assume that the sell or buy decision in the current period is determined exogenously. That is, the role of a player as either a seller or a buyer in each period is determined by a random shock. If a user sells a unit of the product in the current period, he becomes a seller. The seller lists the product on eBay by providing product description, starting bid price, auction duration, shipping, payment and other related information. Other users who want to buy the product then bid on the product and become buyers.

Suppose that buyer i's value of the good is a random variable $\nu _ { i } ,$ which is randomly drawn from a continuous distribution F(v). All the buyers observe the listing information as well as the seller's feedback ratings from previous transactions and place their bids. We use $P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ $P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } }$ to represent the number of positive and negative feedbacks received by an eBay user as a seller and as a buyer, respectively. $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ is the probability of the seller with history $( P _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , N _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , P _ { \mathrm { { b } } } , N _ { \mathrm { { b } } } )$ cooperating by delivering the good as described.<sup>1</sup> Buyer i's bid for the good is $b _ { i \cdot }$ The highest bid wins, and the winner pays the second highest bid plus the minimum increment. We use m to denote the expected amount of this payment. Since the common practice of completing a transaction on eBay and other online auction sites is that sellers will not ship products until payment is received, the winning bidder i's expected profit is $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \cdot \nu _ { i } - m$ if she makes the payment, and zero if she defaults on the payment. If we ignore the cost of participating in an auction, those buyers who lose get zero payoff. Hence we express the single period value function of buyer $i ,$ who is considering bidding on an item listed by a seller with reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} V (v _ {i}; P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \\ = \operatorname{Prob} (\text {Win}) \cdot \max \bigl \{p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \cdot v _ {i} - m, 0 \bigr \}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The auction mechanism of eBay can be modeled as a variation of the second price sealed bid auction (Vickrey auction) as in Bajari and Hortacsu [4]. Given the value function (1) and assuming the minimum increment is negligible, we obtain the following conclusions:

## Proposition 1.

(i) There exists an equilibrium in which each buyer bids

$$
b _ {i} = p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \cdot v _ {i} \quad \forall i.\tag{2}
$$

(ii) Buyer i's expected payment if winning is

$$
m = p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) E (v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i}).\tag{3}
$$

(iii) The weakly dominant strategy for the winning buyer is to cooperate.

(iv) A player's value function of participating in an auction as a buyer is

$$
\begin{array}{l} V (v _ {i}; P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \\ = F (v _ {i}) ^ {n - 1} p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) (v _ {i} - E (v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i})). \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Proof.

(i) If bidder i bids $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ , she will win if $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } { \mathrm { > } } \mathrm { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j }$ and lose if $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } ,$ $N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } { < } \mathrm { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j }$ . If $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } { = } \mathrm { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j } ,$ bidder i is indifferent between winning and losing. Suppose that she bids an amount $x _ { i } { < } p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ $\nu _ { i } .$ . If $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname* { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j } < x _ { i } < p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ P _ { \mathrm { b } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } , } \end{array}$ then she still wins. If $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { i } { < } p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } { < } \mathrm { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j } ; } \end{array}$ , then she still loses. And in the above two cases, bidder i gets the same profit as when she bids $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ P _ { \mathrm { b } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i }$ However, $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname { i f } x _ { i } < \operatorname* { m a x } _ { j \neq i } b _ { j } < p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i } , } \end{array}$ then she loses whereas if she had bid $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ P _ { \mathrm { b } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i }$ she would have made a positive profit. Thus, bidding less than $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i }$ can never increases her profit but in some circumstances may actually decrease it. A similar argument shows that it is not profitable to bid more than $p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu _ { i }$

(ii) If a buyer with value $\nu _ { i }$ wins, his expected payment is the second highest bid plus the minimum increment, that is, m = E(2nd highest bid + minimum increment $| { b } _ { i }$ is the highest bid). When the minimum increment is negligible, we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} m = E (p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) v _ {i}) \\ \quad = E (p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i}) \\ \quad = p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) E (v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | <   v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i}) \end{array}
$$

where $\nu _ { - 1 } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ represents the 2nd highest value among all the buyers' values if buyer i has the highest value.

(iii) The expected gain for the winning buyer i is

$$
\begin{array}{l} p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \cdot v _ {i} - m \\ = p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \cdot (v _ {i} - E (v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i})) \geq 0. \end{array}
$$

So a winning buyer will always choose to cooperate (make the payment), which is the weakly dominant strategy.

(iv) Buyer i's probability of winning is

Prob $( p \nu _ { i }$ is the highest bid among all n buyers) = Prob $( \nu _ { i }$ is the highest value among all n buyers) $= F ( \nu _ { i } ) ^ { n - 1 }$

Given (i) to (iii), player $i ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ expected gain from participating in an auction as a buyer is

$$
\begin{array}{l} V (v _ {i}; P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) \\ = F (v _ {i}) ^ {n - 1} p (P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}) (v _ {i} - E (v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} | v _ {- i} ^ {(1)} <   v _ {i})). \end{array}
$$

Proposition 1 suggests that each bidder is bidding on her true willingness-to-pay adjusted by the probability of receiving the good. The bidder with the highest value wins and pays the second highest bid plus the minimum increment. The belief of a seller's honesty acts as a discounter to the buyers' willingness-to-pay: the belief of an honest seller will boost up bids, while that of a dishonest seller will lower bids. A rational buyer will not bid more than $p \nu _ { i } ,$ and a winning bidder will always choose to cooperate (make payment) because she cannot gain more through defaulting payment. Hence, cooperate (make a payment) is the weakly dominant strategy for buyers regardless of their types. This result is consistent with the empirical evidence from our data set that a very low percentage of the buying reputation is negative and that sellers are over three times more likely to receive negative ratings than buyers. The fact that there are still a small number of negative ratings for buyers could be explained by the imperfect monitoring and noise in score reporting to the review system. Consequently, the buying feedback of a player does not signal his true type.

On receiving the payment, a seller can choose to cooperate (deliver the right good), or cheat (not deliver the good or deliver a good of inferior quality). An honest seller always cooperates, while a dishonest seller may not. Cheating brings in an immediate profit but may hurt reputation and lower future gains. This is a complicated dynamic problem and the optimal strategy is intractable in a general setting. Since this paper focuses on how the buyers bid based on the sellers' buying and selling reputation, we ignore the dynamics of the cooperation or cheating decisions in the selling transactions. To simplify our model, we assume that a dishonest seller will choose to cheat all the time. This simplification does not affect our theoretical model about the buyers strategy, or the following empirical results.

After receiving the product, or not receiving the product for a reasonably long period, the buyer can report her feedback regarding the transaction process to the system so that everybody can view it. In addition to any text comments, the buyer explicitly gives a numeric score + 1 (positive) to a satisfied transaction, − 1 (negative) to an unsatisfied transaction, and 0 (neutral) otherwise. In a perfect monitoring environment, a dishonest seller can be easily detected with a single negative rating, after which no buyers will bid on his listings any more. However, the ideal perfect monitoring does not exist in real online auction marketplaces. There are various reasons why a seller may receive negative feedbacks even if he delivers the right good. The product may be delayed or damaged during shipping, or the buyer may feel the good not as described. We model this imperfection as the noise of the feedback rating system: there is a probability α that a buyer will report a negative feedback even when the seller cooperates, and a probability β that a buyer reports a positive feedback when the seller cheats. It is valid to assume that the noises are small enough not to overshadow the true nature of the players, that is, the probability of a distorted report α (or β) is less than that of a true report 1 $- \alpha ( \mathrm { o r } 1 - \beta )$ . Therefore we have $0 < \alpha < 0 . 5$ and $0 < \beta < 0 . 5$ We assume that the same types of systematic noises also exist in the buyers' feedback reports.

Proposition 1 has solved the buyers' decision problem in one auction transaction, however, another question remains: how is the buyers' belief of the probability that the seller cooperates $p$ related to the seller's reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , \ P _ { \mathrm { { b } } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { { b } } } ) ?$ We now propose the linkage between them. Assume a consumer's prior belief of the probability of the seller's honesty θ is $p _ { 0 }$ . The buyer will update her belief of $p$ based on the seller's current reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } ,$ $P _ { \mathrm { b } } , \ N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ , or to be exactly, current selling reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , \ : N _ { \mathrm { s } } )$ . Because by Proposition 1, a players buying reputation does not signal his honesty, a buyer can only learn the seller's honesty from his selling reputation.

Proposition 2. Both the closing price and the probability of sale increase with the seller's number of positive feedbacks in his selling reputation $P _ { s } ,$ and decrease with the seller's number of negative feedbacks in his selling reputation $N _ { s } ,$ but are unrelated with the buying reputation $P _ { b }$ and $N _ { b }$ .

Proof. Since a buyer distinguishes the seller's “selling reputation” and “buying reputation”, the posterior beliefs will be formed solely based on the seller's selling reputation. Applying the Baye's rule, we have the posterior consumer belief of the probability that the seller cooperates p given the sellers' reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , N _ { \mathrm { { s } } } , P _ { \mathrm { { b } } } , N _ { \mathrm { { b } } } )$ is

$$
\begin{array}{l} p \left(P _ {\mathrm{s}}, N _ {\mathrm{s}}, P _ {\mathrm{b}}, N _ {\mathrm{b}}\right) \\ = \frac {\operatorname * {P r} \left(P _ {\mathrm{s}} , N _ {\mathrm{s}} , P _ {\mathrm{b}} , N _ {\mathrm{b}} \mid H\right) p _ {0}}{\operatorname * {P r} \left(P _ {\mathrm{s}} , N _ {\mathrm{s}} , P _ {\mathrm{b}} , N _ {\mathrm{b}} \mid H\right) p _ {0} + \operatorname * {P r} \left(P _ {\mathrm{s}} , N _ {\mathrm{s}} , P _ {\mathrm{b}} , N _ {\mathrm{b}} \mid D\right) \left(1 - p _ {0}\right)} \\ = \frac {(1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} \alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} p _ {0}}{(1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} \alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} p _ {0} + \beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - p _ {0})}. \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

The above equation suggests the way a buyer evaluates the seller's reputation, that is, a buyer looks at the seller's reputation from his prior selling transactions exclusively. The seller's feedbacks from prior buying transactions do not play any role in the buyer's bid.

The winning policy on eBay is that the buyer with the highest value wins the auction and pay the second highest bid price plus the minimum increment. If we use $\mathbf { \Sigma } _ { \nu } ^ { \bigstar ( 2 ) }$ to represent the second highest value among all buyers', then by Proposition 1, the closing price should be

ClosingPrice $= p ( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } ) \nu ^ { ( 2 ) }$

If we take derivative of this closing price with respect to the seller's ratings $P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } }$ and $N _ { \mathrm { b } } ,$ , given $0 < \alpha < 0 . 5$ and $0 < \beta { < } 0 . 5$ , we have

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial \text {ClosingPrice}}{\partial P _ {\mathrm{s}}} = \frac {\partial p}{\partial P _ {\mathrm{s}}} = - \frac {\beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - p _ {0})}{(\alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} p _ {0} + \beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - p _ {0})) ^ {2}} \ln \left(\frac {\beta}{1 - \alpha}\right) > 0 \\ \frac {\partial \text {ClosingPrice}}{\partial N _ {\mathrm{s}}} = \frac {\partial p}{\partial N _ {\mathrm{s}}} = - \frac {\beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - p _ {0})}{(\alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} p _ {0} + \beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}}} (1 - P _ {0})) ^ {2}} \ln \left(\frac {1 - \beta}{\alpha}\right) <   0 \\ \frac {\partial \text {ClosingPrice}}{\partial P _ {\mathrm{b}}} = \frac {\partial p}{\partial P _ {\mathrm{b}}} = 0 \\ \frac {\partial \text {ClosingPrice}}{\partial N _ {\mathrm{b}}} = \frac {\partial p}{\partial N _ {\mathrm{b}}} = 0. \end{array}
$$

Since Prob (sale) = Prob (closing price ≥ seller's reserve price), it increases with a higher closing price given a seller's reserve price. Therefore, the seller's reputation profile $( P _ { \mathrm { s } } , N _ { \mathrm { s } } , P _ { \mathrm { b } } , N _ { \mathrm { b } } )$ has the same directions of influence on the probability of sale as on the closing price.

Proposition 2 posits that the sellers' selling reputation affects the buyers' perceived risk of receiving the correct item, therefore affects buyers' bid prices indirectly. However, the sellers' buying reputation has no effect on the bid prices. The sellers' selling reputation consists of positive feedbacks and negative feedbacks. Presumably, positive feedback increases buyers' belief about the honesty of the sellers and induce them to discount less of their willingness-to-pay. Thus, positive feedback increases buyers' bid prices, and therefore increases the closing price. On the contrary, negative feedback reduces it. The above analysis leads to our hypothesis about the correlation of the closing price with the seller's selling reputation and buying reputation, respectively. □

Hypothesis 1. The closing price is increasing with the number of seller's positive selling ratings and decreasing with the number of negative selling ratings, but uncorrelated with the seller's buying reputation.

Presumably, a high closing price will increase the probability of a bid exceeding the seller's reserve price, thus increase the chance the good is sold. Hence we also conjecture that the probability of sale has the same correlation relationship with the seller's reputation as a closing price does. Thus we come up with another hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. The probability of sale is increasing with the number of seller's positive selling ratings and decreasing with the number of negative selling ratings, but uncorrelated with the seller's buying reputation.

Prior research on online auctions assumes buyers take all feedback a seller obtained before as the “selling record”, then the posterior expected consumer belief of p will be:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p (P, N) = \frac {\alpha^ {N} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P} p _ {0}}{\alpha^ {N} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P} p _ {0} + \beta^ {P} (1 - \beta) ^ {N} (1 - p _ {0})} \\ = \frac {\alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}} + N _ {\mathrm{b}}} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}} + P _ {\mathrm{b}}} p _ {0}}{\alpha^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}} + N _ {\mathrm{b}}} (1 - \alpha) ^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}} + P _ {\mathrm{b}}} p _ {0} + \beta^ {P _ {\mathrm{s}} + P _ {\mathrm{b}}} (1 - \beta) ^ {N _ {\mathrm{s}} + N _ {\mathrm{b}}} (1 - p _ {0})}. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

If (6) is true, both selling reputation and buying reputation will influence the buyers' bids, which increase with $P _ { \mathrm { { s } } }$ and $P _ { \mathrm { b } } ,$ , and decrease with $N _ { \mathrm { s } }$ and $N _ { \mathfrak { b } }$ In this case, there would be extra incentive for a buyer who wants to improve her reputation to bid a higher price than the expected value. If such a buyer wins the auction, the price would also reflect her “reputation motives” besides the seller's reputation premium, that is, it is correlated with buyer's reputation. For example, we would expect a buyer with lower reputation to bid higher price. But if the buyers can distinguish the seller's “selling reputation” and “buying reputation” as we predicted, there is no “reputation motives”, and buyers' bid function will follow Proposition 1. The hypotheses will be investigated with the auction data collected from eBay in Section 5.

## 5. Evidence from eBay

The comprehensive information available in the online auction market allows us to investigate the bidders' behavior and verify the hypothesized relationship between auction outcome and sellers' two types of reputation. This section bridges the above theoretical model to the real auction practices through data collected from the most popular online auction website eBay.com.

## 5.1. The hedonic regression model (Hypothesis 1)

Our model predicts that the two types of reputation impact differently on the buyers' bidding behavior and that only selling reputation affects the seller's profit. The above results empirically cannot reject Hypothesis 1 that buying reputation does not affect the seller's goodwill in a selling transaction. Buyers act rationally in bidding on eBay auctions: they differentiate the sellers selling and buying ratings and ignore those ratings, positive or negative, which the sellers gained from their previous purchases. Knowing that, the sellers have no incentive to build up their reputation through purchasing on eBay.

The closing price increases significantly with the product quality, for example, capacity and generation properties of the player, value of accessories and whether the product is brand new. Our result also shows that an iPod MP3 seller on eBay may increase the closing price more than the cost by bold-facing the item description to make the listing stand out from the competing ones or by putting the item picture into eBay gallery to attract more eyeballs.

Moreover, a relatively high starting price, and a hidden reserve price screen out the low bids from passing through and therefore increase the closing price. They will, however, deter low value buyers and therefore lower the chance that the good is sold (as demonstrated in Section 5.2). A longer bidding period and more bids will increase the chance of receiving high bids and therefore boost up the closing price.

So the two types of reputation are viewed differently by the online auction users. If the sellers' selling and buying reputations are not separated and are taken as a whole to examine the relationship of reputation and bidding results, as we observed in most common online auction literature, a problem of measurement error will arise and misleading results may be obtained. That is a potential reason for the mixed results demonstrated in the literature (Table 1). To illustrate this point, we test model (7) again with the mixed reputation variables AllPos (the sum of SellPos and BuyPos) and AllNeg (the sum of SellNeg and BuyNeg) instead of SellPos, SellNeg, BuyPos and BuyNeg. The results are exhibited in Table 6. AllPos is positively influencing the bid price and is significant, while AllNeg is not robustly significant. Those results are not consistent with the theory and our above results, but fall in with the conclusions of Ba and Pavlou [3], Bajari and Hortacsu [4], and Eaton [8], as well as part of the conclusions in Jin and Kato [11]. Hence, the above example can serve as a replication of the work done by the literature. It explains the mixed results obtained in the prior literature on online auctions and demonstrates the robustness of the model proposed in this paper.

## 5.2. The logit model

We then test Hypothesis 2 by looking into how selling and buying reputation influence the liquidity of the auction transactions differently. Still based on Proposition 2 and by modifying model (7), a logit model (9) is used to examine the effect of reputation on probability of good sold:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{logit} (\operatorname {P r o b \_ S o l d} _ {i}) \\ = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln (\operatorname{SellPos} _ {i} + 1) + \beta_ {2} \ln (\operatorname{SellNeg} _ {i} + 1) \\ \quad + \beta_ {3} \ln (\operatorname{BuyPos} _ {i} + 1) + \beta_ {4} \ln (\operatorname{BuyNeg} _ {i} + 1) \\ \quad + \sum_ {j} \alpha_ {i j} X _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

Applying the model to our data sample I, we get the results as shown in Table 7. Similar to the regression model in the last section, multi-collinearity is also excluded by checking the condition indices and the tolerances of the variables. Hypothesis 2 is strongly supported. We find that the positive and negative ratings from selling transactions consistently significantly affect the probability of good sold, but not the ratings from buying transactions. So the buyers tend to bid on the auctions sold by more trustable sellers, and they are also more likely to bid above the starting prices or reservation prices. Buyers put more weight on negative feedbacks in evaluating a seller's selling reputation, reflected by the fact that the number of negative feedbacks has a greater impact on the probability of good (estimated odds ratio 0.60) sold than that of positive feedbacks (estimated odds ratio 1.28). This result implies that buyers tend to avoid bidding for a seller with too many negative ratings, so sellers of that kind will have to withdraw from selling on eBay to avoid the listing fee, or they will discard the bad reputed ID and change a new user ID. This buyers' selection bias can explain the phenomenon that the majority of the feedbacks on eBay are positive (the negative rating ratio is less than 3% in our sample).

Quality variables such as Generation and Accessories are shown to be significantly increasing the probability of item sold. Putting the item into eBay gallery list attracts the buyers' attention more easily; therefore, it increases the chance that the item is sold. Making the auction last longer gives more buyers the chance to see and to bid on it. It is also proved to increases the liquidity of the auctions.

Setting a Buy-it-now price provides the interested buyers with the option to finish the auction earlier if their valuation is higher than the Buy-it-now price and if the value of waiting does not justify the cost. Presumably the Buy-it-now option expedites the auction process and can increase the liquidity of transactions under the condition that the Buy-it-now price is set properly. Our data sample shows that most eBay sellers adopting the Buy-it-now option did finish the auction earlier than the closing time. Our results from the logit model also support this conjecture.

Longer auction duration not only boosts up bid prices, but also increases probability of sale. A high starting price and a hidden reserve price, even though increase bid prices, reduce the probability of good sold.

To inspect the robustness of the results, we also test models (7) and (9) with alternative functional forms, for example, replacing the ClosingPrice with ln(Closing-Price), and with subsets of the Sample I and II — 15G and 20G iPod players individually. We find that our conclusions about the significance of the variables and the signs and magnitudes of estimation results still hold in all of these tests.

EBay currently reports the net score, which is the difference of the number of positive and negative feedback comments, as one of the primary indices for the sellers' reputation. Based on the conclusions of our analysis, there is room to improve on the reputation system.

The buyers consider the sellers' selling reputation when evaluating their honesty. So the reputation index should come from the sellers' feedback comments from their selling history only.

The results of both of our models shows that negative feedbacks are paid more attention and have greater impact on the bid prices of the items. Therefore, the net score, which weighs both types of feedback equally, is not a good proxy of the users' reputation. Number of negative scores from the selling history should be emphasized more so that the users can easily read and interpret it.

## 6. Conclusions

Trust building in the online community is a crucial issue that relates to the survivability of the Internet market. We build a simple model to illustrate how sellers' reputation is evaluated under the online feedback mechanism adopted by major online auction websites. That is, given the flexibility in identity creation and role switching in the Internet environment, a bidder will separate and weight differently a seller's selling reputation from his buying reputation.

This study raises the changing role problem on the Internet marketplace and discusses its implication on reputation building beyond the conclusions of the previous reputation literature. The different cost and benefit relationships of building the two types of reputation may induce the users to strategically manipulate their reputation. Anticipating this, rational buyers should separate a seller's selling reputation from buying reputation when trying to infer the sellers' true honesty. In our model, with the assumption that sellers only ship the product when payments are received, we show that sellers' good selling reputation increases closing prices and probability of sale, bad selling reputation reduces them, while sellers' buying reputation has no effect on them.

Using actual auction data collected from eBay, we empirically show that first, sellers do make purchases as well as sales intermittently; second, the effects of the two types of reputation confirm our above theoretical prediction about the sellers' selling feedback and probability of sale and closing prices. We also find that negative selling comments are weighted more heavily than positive ones. Moreover, some other variables, such as product condition, auction characteristics such as starting bids and whether to put a secret reserve price, are found to affect the probability of sale and closing prices.

The paper contributes to the literature by clarifying the meaning of reputation in online auction markets theoretically and empirically. The work changes and explains some of the mixed results obtained in the prior literature [3,4,8,11]. That is possibly because the inclusion of sellers' buying feedback “dampens” the effect of reputation. More importantly, our results call for more attention to the strategic aspects of reputation and reconsideration of the long-short run player reputation framework frequently used in the literature. The existence of such a phenomenon is enough to serve as a first call for further studies, both theoretical and empirical ones, to generalize the results and go beyond the current research.

EBay has started to indicate the types of transactions in the feedback history pages since June 2001. Starting from January 2004, it further grouped the ratings based on the sellers' identity in the transactions (seller/buyer) with tabs on the feedback history pages. By clicking the tabs, users can easily view the comments on the seller's selling reputation as well as his buying reputation separately. These practices show that eBay has realized the importance of separating the selling feedback from the buying feedback. But according to our research results, the feedback scores are expected to be reported by separating selling reputation and buying reputation. Since negative feedbacks are viewed more crucially than positive ones, the net score should be replaced with the exact amount of feedback comments, typically, the number of negative selling ratings, from the users' feedback history.
