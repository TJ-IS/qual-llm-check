# Timing of Adaptive Web Personalization and Its Effects on Online Consumer Behavior

Shuk Ying Ho

School of Accounting and Business Information Systems, College of Business and Economics, The Australian National University, Canberra, ACT 0200, Australia, susanna.ho@anu.edu.au

David Bodoff

Graduate School of Management, University of Haifa, Haifa 31905, Israel, dbodoff@gsb.haifa.ac.il

Kar Yan Tam

Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics, and Operations Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong, kytam@ust.hk

W<sup>eb</sup> <sup>personalization</sup> <sup>allows</sup> <sup>online</sup> <sup>merchants</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>customize</sup> <sup>Web</sup> <sup>content</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>serve</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>needs</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>individual</sup> customers. Using data mining and clickstream analysis techniques, merchants can now adapt website content in real time to capture the current preferences of online customers. Though the ability to offer adaptive content in real time opens up new business opportunities for online merchants, it also raises questions of timing. One question is when to present personalized content to consumers. Consumers prefer early presentation that eases their selection process, whereas adaptive systems can make better personalized content if they are allowed to collect more consumers’ clicks over time. A review of personalization research confirms that little work has been done on these timing issues in the context of personalized services. The current study aims to fill that gap. Drawing on consumer search theory, we develop hypotheses about consumer responses to differences in presentation timing and recommendation type and the interaction between the two. The findings establish that quality improves over the course of an online session but the probability of considering and accepting a given recommendation diminishes over the course of the session. These effects are also shown to interact with consumer expertise, providing insights on the interplay between the different design elements of a personalization strategy.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of Web-based applications has turned the Web into an essential “touch point” of corporate services (Rayport and Jaworski 2005). As such, rendering services over the Web provides a firm with valuable promotion and sales opportunities.

Personalization technologies such as data mining and collaborative filtering allow online merchants to realize these opportunities by profiling their customers and revealing their preferences and tastes based on past transactions (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Mobasher et al. 2002, Xiao and Benbasat 2007). An often-cited example is Amazon.com, which generates book recommendations based on collaborative filtering. Recent advances in behavioral targeting further empower merchants to adapt website content in real time to maximize business opportunities. Not only are online merchants now able to retrieve the profile of a customer when he accesses a Web service, they are also able to change the Web content and its format adaptively according to the context of interaction. We refer to the process of profiling consumers and generating content that matches their preferences and tastes as Web personalization, the suite of software<sup>1</sup> used to generate the content as the personalization agent, and the content thus generated as personalized content.<sup>2</sup>

The stored profile of a consumer represents only a baseline for Web personalization. Increasingly, the profile is combined with clickstream and other realtime data to create a context of interaction on which adaptive content will be generated. The concept of context-driven personalization suggests a process of observing a consumer’s dynamic Web behavior to augment the static baseline profile in each interaction. It also suggests that the ability to capture the richness of a context is positively related to the extent of the observation. The longer the observation, the more knowledge gathered about the customer’s spontaneous preferences and tastes. This is shown as the upward sloping line in Figure 1.<sup>3</sup>

The acquisition of spontaneous preferences, however, cannot consume a prolonged period of time for two reasons. First, a Web session is typically very short (Jansen et al. 2005). As a result, Web personalization has to take place in real time and personalized content must be presented before a consumer exits the Web service. Second, for Web services involving choice decisions, the consumer’s interests in personalized recommendations may diminish as he may have identified a good product before the personalized items are presented. This is represented as the downward sloping line in Figure 1.

The result is two competing effects. On one hand, if a personalization agent is designed effectively, adaptive personalized recommendations that are presented later are more likely to match a consumer’s interests. This is because the recommendations are based on a longer period of observing the consumer. On the other hand, all else being equal, the effectiveness of offering personalized items is likely to diminish over time. The consumer may feel that he has already found something satisfactory and decide that it is not worth the effort to search any further. These two effects can be viewed as a trade-off between quality and timeliness of the recommendations. The purpose of our research is to delineate each of these two effects by conducting carefully designed experiments. In addition to the two main effects, this work explores expertise as a moderating factor. Although we write of “timing,” the operative factor in both effects is not time elapsed on the clock per se but how far into the search the consumer has progressed when the personalized items are presented. Little work has been done on the time dimension of personalization (Murthi and Sarkar 2003). In fact, timing is the least-studied design element in human computer interaction (HCI) research (Zhang et al. 2002).

Our work contributes to the literature on personalization by addressing the following research questions: (1) Are consumers more likely to sample and select personalized recommendations that are adaptive instead of static? (2) Are consumers more likely to sample and select personalized recommendations if they are presented early in the session? (3) What factors moderate these two main effects? To address these questions of timing, we develop a series of hypotheses inspired by the normative analytical literature of consumer search theory (CST). The theoretical contributions of this paper are in theorizing and isolating each of the main effects and assessing their combined effect on consumers’ responses to personalized recommendations.

The paper is organized as follows. In §2, we review the literature of timing effects and CST. In §3, we adopt one CST model and develop hypotheses for the main upward and downward effects of time as depicted in Figure 1. We also discuss the combined result of these competing main effects. Section 4 predicts the moderating effect of the consumer’s level of domain expertise. Section 5 outlines two experiments. Sections 6 and 7 present and discuss the findings. Section 8 highlights the theoretical and practical implications of the work. The last section concludes the paper.

## 2. Consumer Behavior Literature

Our research is about the effects of time on the amount of consumer search. Within the consumer behavior literature, the first place to look for relevant work is research that investigates timing as an independent variable. The consumer behavior literature has concerned itself with a variety of timerelated issues such as consumers’ concern with the time expended on search (Jacoby et al. 1976), their valuation of costs and benefits that accrue over time (Mowen and Mowen 1991), and whether consumers will postpone their purchases in order to await a promotion (Mela et al. 1998). By far, the most prominent time-related issue in the literature of consumer behavior is the stability of an advertisement’s persuasiveness over time; in particular, whether the advertisement’s effect fades or persists when it is seen days or weeks before the consumer goes shopping (Alba et al. 1991, Naik 1999, Pratkanis and Greenwald 1993). By contrast, in our context, the personalized content is displayed to the consumer while he is shopping so there is no question of whether the effect has faded. Ho and Tam (2005) address the role of personalization that appears during the consumer decision-making process. However, they focus on static personalization without adaptiveness. To our knowledge, little work has been done to study the microinteraction between a consumer and an online shop in the context of adaptive personalization. The current work attempts to fill this gap.

The second place to look for relevant work is research that studies our dependent variable, the amount of search. This is the literature that is more relevant to our case. Of the widely accepted five stages of consumer decision processes—recognition, search, evaluation, choice, and outcome (Engel et al. 1978)—a substantial body of work has emerged on the search stage (Moorthy et al. 1997, Pham and Higgins 2005). During this stage, the consumer chooses a subset of items and collects information. Examples of information collection include reading product information, asking for references, and inspecting or sampling the product. There is a long tradition of empirical research on consumer information search (Moorthy et al. 1997) under the CST framework.

One particular area of research explores a type of search task called sequential search or the “secretary problem” (Freeman 1983), which is also the type of problem that applies to our context. The original secretary problem envisions a manager searching for a secretary to hire and he interviews one by one until finally offering the job to a candidate. The secretary problem was subsequently applied to the case of consumer search in which a consumer inspects items one by one until deciding which item to choose. This type of problem has a long history in economics (e.g., DeGroot 1968, Stigler 1961, Telser 1973) and has been adopted in consumer behavior and marketing research (e.g., Zwick et al. 2003). The primary research focuses on the optimal “stopping rule,” i.e., when should the consumer stop searching and select the best item found so far? The problem has many variants and the optimal stopping rule depends on the setup of each model. For example, Bearden et al. (2006) and Seale and Rapoport (1997) adopt a variant in which the consumer cannot return later to an item that he considered earlier (“without recall”). Zwick et al. (2003) adopt a model in which a consumer might be able to return to an earlier item (probabilistic recall) while Rapoport and Tversky (1970) consider models in which the consumer can definitely go back (“with recall”). Models with recall typically introduce a search “cost.” This is an abstract representation of all the expenditures of time, money, energy, and other resources that search entails, for which reason people do not conduct exhaustive searches even though recall is allowed. Another aspect that differentiates the variants of the secretary problem pertains to the nature of the reward. In Zwick et al. (2003), the consumer is rewarded only if he finds the very best item (“nothing but the best”). In other models, the reward function is proportional to the rank (Bearden et al. 2006, Seale and Rapoport 1997) or value (Rapoport and Tversky 1970) of the chosen item. Another dimension focuses on a consumer’s knowledge of the distribution of items. Rapoport and Tversky (1970) adopt a model in which the consumer knows the precise distribution of items while Seale and Rapoport (1997) and Zwick et al. (2003) assume only that a person can relatively rank the various options. Sen et al. (2006) adopt a qualitative approach in which they derive qualitative hypotheses based on some common findings across models. In Browne et al. (2007), the authors propose a cognitive psychology approach to hypothesize stopping rules that people might employ as a function of psychological effects.

Drawing from the above literature, we adopt an analytical framework that is most appropriate to our context. It is not our intention to develop another analytical model. Rather, we would like to follow the logic of the adopted framework to predict how the timing of personalized recommendations will affect consumer search behavior. In other words, our research question is: Given the optimal stopping rule as prescribed in the literature, what is the effect of timing on the number of personalized items that the consumer will sample and eventually select? In the following section, we first introduce the stopping rule model that we adopt. We will then explain how, given that rule, timing is predicted to influence consumer sampling and choice.

## 3. Stopping Rule Model and Application to Personalization Timing

As outlined above, the literature includes many variants of the secretary problem that differ regarding recall, reward, and other dimensions. We adopt a model with recall because it most resembles our context in which a consumer is free to choose any of the product items sampled during the shopping process without any restriction. The model includes a search cost associated with each sampling, as explained earlier. We also assume that the value of a product item reflects how good the item is to the consumer, i.e., product value is a subjective measure. Furthermore, the search process is framed as a trade-off between expected gain in value and the cost of searching for an additional product item. The most prominent models that fit these requirements are those of DeGroot (1968) and Rapoport and Tversky (1970). These early models have served as the basis for subsequent elaboration and applications. For example, Ben Abdelaziz and Krichen (2005) consider a model similar to DeGroot (1968) but with two decision makers, and Engen and Seim (1987) derive the same result as DeGroot (1968) using a different mathematical approach. We use the original models of DeGroot (1968) and Rapoport and Tversky (1970) because they are simple and intuitive. They provide the overarching logic that suits our needs.

Given a model with recall and search cost together with a value that a consumer wants to maximize in a sequential search process, when should the consumer stop searching? The premise of the solution is that the person should stop searching when the expected gain in value of sampling one more item is no longer larger than its marginal cost. Applying this rule to the situation where the consumer knows the distribution of item values, the optimal stopping rule has the form “the consumer should search until he finds an item of value $x ,$ then the consumer should stop and choose $\mathrm { i t ^ { \prime \prime } }$ (Rapoport and Tversky 1970). Theoretically, if the person knows the distribution of item values, he can precompute an optimal value x and employ the rule “keep searching until finding an item at least as good as $x . ^ { \prime \prime }$ In our context, “knowing the distribution of values” means knowing the mean item value<sup>4</sup> (i.e., how good the items are on average) and variance (i.e., how varied the item values are).

If the consumer does not know in advance how the items are distributed, then a Bayesian approach is more appropriate. The intuition of the Bayesian model is that the consumer learns about the distribution of available items while he is searching. This is an apt description of the nature of consumer search in our case. The stopping rule has the same form as above, except that the value x, which will terminate the search, is not fixed in advance, but is constantly updated in response to the consumer’s latest knowledge about how good and how varied the available items are. At the outset, based on prior beliefs on the mean and variance of the item values, the person may set for himself a particular target value, but as the person searches and learns that the sampled items are actually better (worse) or more (less) varied than he thought, the person may adjust the target upward (downward) accordingly.

Following the Bayesian approach of DeGroot (1968), the actual distribution of personalized item values is $N ( \theta , 1 )$ , where $\theta$ is the mean and the number 1 denotes the inverse of variance called precision, which is assigned the value 1 without loss of generality. However, $\theta$ is unknown to the consumer. Although the mean is unknown, the consumer has an initial estimate of $\theta ,$ which is modeled as a prior distribution $\theta \sim N ( \mu , \tau )$ where  denotes the precision of this estimate. In other words, the consumer estimates the true mean $\theta$ as lying within a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and precision . Note that  denotes the precision of the consumer’s estimate of $\theta ,$ the personalized items’ true mean value; $\tau$ does not denote the precision of the item values, which as already stated is assigned the value 1. When a personalized item with value of x is sampled, the consumer updates his estimate of the distribution of $\theta$ so that the posterior is $\theta \sim N ( [ ( \tau \mu + x ) / ( \tau + 1 ) ] , \tau + 1 )$ 5. Note that the precision of the consumer’s estimate of $\theta$ will increase from  to $\tau + 1$ with each item sampled. The general form of the stopping rule under this approach, which will form the basis for our theoretical perspective and predictions, is as follows:

The Bayesian search rule: The consumer should continue searching (i.e., sampling) until the consumer is holding an item whose value v satisfies

$$
v > \mu + f \left(\frac {\tau}{\tau + 1}, c\right),\tag{1}
$$

where $v$ is the value of the highest-valued item sampled so far, $\mu$ is the consumer’s most up-to-date estimate of the mean value of the items,  is the precision of that estimate, the number $^ { \prime \prime } { 1 ^ { \prime \prime } }$ in the expression $\tau + 1$ is the precision of the personalized items, $c$ is the cost of sampling one more item, and $f ( )$ is a function that is decreasing in $c$ and $\tau / ( \tau + 1 )$ . Each time the person samples an item, he will update both $\mu$ and  as described above, and if the item is the best sampled so $\operatorname { f a r } ,$ he will also update $v$ with the new value. $\mathtt { B y }$ definition, $v$ can only increase with each item sampled while the right-hand side of (1) tends to decrease with each item sampled because sampling increases the precision $\tau$ of the consumer’s estimate of the item mean and $f ( )$ decreases with $\tau / ( \tau + 1 )$ . The search will continue until the right-hand side is no longer larger than the left-hand side.

The decision rule is simple and intuitive. It is premised on the idea that one should search until such time that the expected marginal benefit of sampling one more item is no longer greater than the marginal cost of doing so. Given a search cost $c ,$ if the items have a high estimated mean $\mu$ compared to the best item encountered so far $( \mathbf { i . e . , \textit { v } } )$ , this favors continued sampling. On the other hand, even if $\mu$ is not high, the consumer may still want to search further if the estimate of the mean personalized values is uncertain $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \tau$ is small) because of the potential upside of encountering a better item down the road. Whether to continue searching also depends on the constant marginal cost c. The larger the cost, the less incentive there is to search further and vice versa.

It is important to note that in the current context, the parameters on the right side of Equation (1) refer only to the distribution of personalized items. Though our model allows consumers to sample items from the entire catalog of items (stock items) before the personalized items are presented, it does not make reference to distribution parameters of the stock items as our focus lies in the search process and amount of sampling of personalized items. In developing our hypotheses, $\mu$ and  refer to the distribution parameters of the personalized list only. However, the value v could be either from the stock or personalized list. The basic idea of Equation (1) in our context is that whether to stop or continue sampling from the personalized distribution depends on the parameters $\mu$ and  and on the best item found so far from any source. If the consumer’s estimate of the personalized distribution has a high enough mean or variance relative to the best item found so far from any source, then sampling from the personalized list should commence or continue; otherwise, it should stop. In the following section, we apply the basic model of Equation (1) to predict the upward and downward effects of delaying the presentation of the personalized items on the amount of sampling and choice of personalized recommendations.

## 3.1. Delayed Presentation Means Less Timely Recommendations

To put the timing issue of Web personalization into perspective, we conceptualize an online shopping session as a finite period of time starting with the logon to an online shop<sup>5</sup> and ending with a purchase. The task for the consumer is to make a choice among the many items encountered in a session. In the beginning, the consumer has access to a set of stock (nonpersonalized) items to choose from. At some point, the system presents the consumer with additional personalized items for his consideration. To facilitate our analysis, we focus on two points in a shopping session at which a personalization agent can present its recommendations: (1) personalized content is presented when the consumer first accesses the online shop, and (2) personalized content is presented only after the consumer has considered a number of nonpersonalized stock items. We now explain how the proposed Bayesian stopping rule leads to the prediction of less sampling from the personalized list when it is presented later.

The key insight that allows us to apply Equation (1) to the effects of delay is that, though $\mu$ and  are parameters of the estimated mean of the personalized item distribution, the value v represents the best item sampled so far from either the stock or the personalized list. Suppose the presentation of personalized items is delayed while the consumer samples stock items. During this time, most of the parameters of Equation (1) are unaffected because the consumer is learning nothing about the parameters of the personalized list. However, the value of v may change as the number of items sampled increases. The longer the delay, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the more stock items the consumer has sampled before the personalized items are presented, the higher the starting value of v will be. We use the term starting value of v to refer to the best item that the consumer had found when the personalized items are first presented. When the personalized items are finally presented, the consumer will compare the right-hand side of Equation (1) against the starting value of v. It might be that the starting value of v is already higher than $\mu + f ( \tau / ( \tau + 1 ) , c )$ , in which case the person will not sample any personalized item. Otherwise, the consumer may begin to sample personalized items, each time updating the values of $\mu$ and  and possibly also v, and then checking again the stopping rule. The higher the starting value of $v ,$ the earlier the expected invocation of the stopping rule will be. This accounts for the “downward” effect of time.

Based on the above, we hypothesize that given a set of personalized items, if they are presented later, then the personalized items are less likely to be attractive compared with the best item encountered so far. To isolate this effect—the downward sloping line of Figure 1—we will test whether personalized items of a fixed level of quality are more likely to be sampled when they are presented earlier in the search process.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Controlling for the quality of the recommendations, a consumer will sample more personalized recommendations if they are presented at an earlier stage of an online session.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> Controlling for the quality of the recommendations, the probability that a consumer will choose personalized recommendations is higher if they are presented at an earlier stage of an online session.

Satisfaction has shown to be an antecedent of repeated purchase (Hoyer 1984), loyalty (Devaraj et al. 2002), and repeated system usage (Meuter et al. 2000). Therefore, upholding or even enhancing the satisfaction level of a consumer during the microinteraction of an online shopping session is of paramount importance. The analytical model does not explicitly include satisfaction, a latent variable, but it allows analysis of the expected value of the shopping session, defined as the expected value of v—the value of the best item found in the session—minus all search costs. We have already shown that consumers are more likely to sample and select personalized items when they are presented earlier, as this raises the expected value of their session. Because satisfaction is closely related to value and utility (Clark et al. 2008), it is expected that satisfaction with the shopping experience should also increase. Given that the expected value of the shopping session will increase with less sampling (less effort), it follows that satisfaction is predicted to be higher when the items are presented earlier, all else being equal. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1C</sup> <sup>(H1C).</sup> Controlling for the quality of the recommendations, consumer satisfaction will be higher if the recommendations are presented at an earlier stage of an online session.

## 3.2. Delayed Presentation Means Better Personalized Recommendations

Success in personalization hinges on the ability to understand customers’ preferences and translate these understandings into product offerings. Personalization strategies take various forms and require different levels of complexity and investment (Kumar and Benbasat 2006, Tam and Ho 2005). The kind of recommendation system we consider here is known as content-based recommendation, in which the consumer is recommended items similar to ones he has preferred in the past as opposed to collaborative recommendations in which the consumer is recommended items that appeal to people with similar tastes and preferences (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005). Here, we consider two different kinds of content-based recommendations: static personalization and real-time adaptive personalization.

Static personalization corresponds to situations whereby the online merchant only has basic knowledge about the preferences and needs of its customers. Preferences are usually collected when customers join the service by asking them to report their demographics, interests, and needs. This information forms the basic customer profile. The assumption that underscores this approach is that preference is static and does not change frequently.

An emerging personalization strategy is real-time adaptive personalization, which infers the current preferences of consumers and generates content congruent with their preferences. Previous research has shown that past preferences may not be a good predictor of future preferences because of possible changes of mind and taste (Simonson 1990). Generally, consumers’ preference for different alternatives in a choice setting is not known in advance but is formed during the elicitation process (Slovic 1995).

Therefore, to capture the dynamics of preference change, the personalization agent needs to understand the context of the interaction and adapt its content accordingly. For example, click analysis attempts to infer the interests of a consumer from his browsing behavior and to generate product offerings that match the interests that are implicit in this behavior. Unlike static personalization, the adaptive system can adapt product recommendations by analyzing which items the consumer has sampled so far. There is increasing evidence that in many situations, preference structures are not static and are retrieved from memory in a last-in, first-out manner and are even constructed on the fly (Bettman et al. 1998, Payne et al. 1992). The ability to update customers’ preference profiles is particularly relevant to hedonic products, which are often trendy and consumer tastes change from time to time. We expect that as an adaptive personalization system observes user behavior, it can generate recommendations that can better match the user’s spontaneous preferences. We define the terms “late adaptive” (and “early adaptive”) as the recommendations of an adaptive system after the user has sampled more (or fewer) items. We similarly define the terms “late static” and “early static,” though these refer to identical recommendations because the static systems do not update. Figure 2 depicts the relationship between personalization strategy and preference matching over time.

As shown in Figure 2, there are two equivalent ways to demonstrate the beneficial effect of adaptive systems over time, comparing late adaptive to early adaptive and comparing late adaptive to late static as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Late-adaptive recommendations will have a higher level of match with the consumer’s current preferences than early-adaptive recommendations.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Late-adaptive recommendations will have a higher level of match with the consumer’s current preferences than late-static recommendations.

Based on Equation (1), we further hypothesize that consumers will sample more late-adaptive recommendations than either late-static or early-adaptive recommendations. The reason is that if the items are generated by an adaptive agent and are better matches with the spontaneous preferences of the consumer, then the consumer will have a higher estimate  relative to the starting value of v. To isolate this upward quality effect from the downward timing effect (H1A–H1B), we compare late adaptive to late static rather than comparing late adaptive to early adaptive. The reason is that if we simply compare lateversus early-adaptive recommendations, this would also conflate the opposite downward effect of time discussed in §2.3. ## 3.3. Two Competing Effects

We have presented two main effects: (1) all else being equal, a consumer can derive more benefit from recommendations that are presented earlier in the search process, and (2) real-time adaptive recommendations that consider the consumer’s recent behavior are more likely to match the consumer’s spontaneous preferences. In terms of Equation (1), the first effect is due to a higher starting value of v when the presentation of personalized items is delayed, while the second effect is due to the relatively higher value of  when presentation is delayed (i.e., the delay allows the agent to learn about the preference of the consumer in real time so as to generate preference-matching recommendations). As we explained earlier, these two main effects are competing in the case of adaptive recommendations<sup>6</sup> because the first effect argues for earlier presentation while the second effect argues for later presentation. Another way to look at this is that the first effect argues in favor of timely intervention (an issue of “when”) while the second effect is concerned with quality (an issue of “what”).

The combined effect—will consumers sample and choose more of the personalized items if we present highly adaptive items later or less adaptive items earlier?—is difficult to generalize. This is because it depends on how strong each of the main effects is in the given setting, e.g., how adaptive the system’s algorithms are.

Though Equation (1) does not make a general prediction about which effect will dominate, it offers a prediction in the following specific case: According to the model, the combined effect of time will be more detrimental in the case of static personalization than in the case of adaptive personalization. The simple reason is that in the case of static personalization, there is no upward sloping effect—only a downward one—whereas in the case of adaptive personalization, there are both. We therefore hypothesize the following.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5A</sup> <sup>(H5A).</sup> The combined effect of time on the amount of sampling will be more negative for static personalization than for adaptive personalization.<sup>7</sup>

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5B</sup> <sup>(H5B).</sup> The combined effect of time on the probability of choosing a personalized item will be more negative for static personalization than for adaptive personalization.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5C</sup> <sup>(H5C).</sup> The combined effect of time on consumer satisfaction will be more negative for static personalization than for adaptive personalization.

Although we do not make general predictions about which effect will dominate, in the next section we explore a moderating factor that gives practitioners an indication of when the downward-sloping effect is stronger and of more concern and when it is less.

## 4. Expertise as a Moderating Factor

Consumer expertise refers to the extent to which a consumer possesses relevant knowledge about a product domain (Ericsson and Kintsch 1995). Prior literature has shown that subjective knowledge of experts influences their search behavior (Moorman et al. 2004). Consumers with high expertise (experts) are those who are familiar with products in the domain and who have an in-depth understanding of the interrelations between products and relevant domain information (Nah and Benbasat 2004). Expertise is hypothesized as a moderator because it is related to the learning process and effort of a consumer as reflected by  and c in our model, respectively. In terms of learning ability, what differentiates novices and experts is that experts begin with a higher value of $\tau ,$ which is the precision of their estimate of the mean value of the personalized items. Given the same number of sampled items, an expert can more readily determine the mean value of the available items than a novice. Experts are also likely to have a lower search cost $^ { c , }$ reflecting their lower cognitive strain in sampling as compared with novices. Finally, we will see that the user’s expertise affects the system’s ability to benefit from the delay to deliver recommendations with a higher $\mu .$ . Thus, though the main upward and downward effects depend on variables $\mu$ and $v$ from Equation (1), expertise is related to $\mu$ and also the remaining variables  and c. In this section, we separately consider how consumer expertise moderates each of the two main effects of time.

## 4.1. Interaction Between Consumer Expertise and Downward Sloping Line

A delay in the presentation of personalized items means a higher starting value of v and an earlier invocation of the stopping rule according to the main downward effect of §3.1. The magnitude of the downward effect—how much earlier the stopping rule is invoked—depends on the magnitude of the expected change in $f ( )$ when the consumer samples an item (recall from $\ S 3$ that $f ( )$ tends to decrease as the consumer samples more items and  gets larger). As shown in Figure $^ { 3 , }$ if the reduction in $f ( )$ is larger, then the effect of delay—the downward effect of time—will be smaller. Thus, any variable that results in a systematically larger or smaller rate of change in $f ( )$ will interact with the main downward effect of time. The question is whether expertise has such a systematic effect.

Though experts have a higher level of  and possibly also a lower c than novices, it is inconclusive to say whether the expected rate of change in $f ( )$ is always higher (or lower) with higher levels of  or c. Therefore, we cannot make an unambiguous prediction regarding the interaction of expertise with the downward effect of time. In summary, level of expertise plays a central and complex role in our model of search, but an unambiguous prediction regarding its moderating effect on the downward effect of time cannot be reached within the scope of the current study.

## 4.2. Interaction Between Consumer Expertise and Upward Sloping Line

The “upward” effect of Figure 1, as reflected in H2 to $\mathrm { H } 3 ,$ means that if the system has the benefit of first observing current consumer behavior before making recommendations, then it will make better recommendations and the consumer will engage in more sampling. However, this assumes that the consumer’s behavior clearly and accurately reflects his current interest so that the system can learn it through observation. A novice, who is not familiar with the product domain, may not know exactly what he likes. The novice will be engaged in more diffuse browsing than an expert (Wood and Lynch 2002). In such a case, an adaptive system will not do as good a job predicting what the consumer wants. Thus, the effect hypothesized in H2 and H3 will be stronger with experts than with novices.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> The increase in level of match between late-adaptive recommendations and early-adaptive recommendations will increase with the consumer’s level of expertise.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>7</sup> <sup>(H7).</sup> The increase in level of match between late-adaptive recommendations and late-static recommendations will increase with the consumer’s level $o f$ expertise.

In terms of Equation (1), the system will generate items that lead to a higher value of $\mu$ for experts than for novices, relative to the starting value of v. We therefore expect that the isolated “upward” effect of time as hypothesized in the hypotheses H4A–H4B will be greater for experts.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>8A</sup> <sup>(H8A).</sup> The increase in the amount of sampling between late-adaptive recommendations and latestatic recommendations will increase with the consumer’s level of expertise.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>8B</sup> <sup>(H8B).</sup> The increase in the probability that a consumer will choose a late-adaptive recommendation versus a late-static recommendation will increase with the consumer’s level of expertise.

We do not make predictions regarding satisfaction level for this interaction because this would require a very different theoretical lens to investigate the processes through which novices and experts develop attitudes. Previous work shows that novices build their attitudes during the interaction (Ericsson and Kintsch 1995), and thus, it may be easier for the personalization agent to impress novices within one single session. Though interaction effects on satisfaction are beyond the scope of CST, they are interesting topics for future research.

We conducted a lab experiment and a field experiment to test these above hypotheses. An online bookstore and a music website were created. We chose to use downloading of books and music as our decisionmaking task because both are hedonic and experienced products. As such, item sampling becomes an important step in decision making and this allows CST to fit well with the experimental manipulations. In the experiments, subjects viewed their book details or sampled their preferred music, which was an act of information search. This resembles the critical features in the study by Hauser and Wernerfelt (1990), who suggested that tasting a bottle of wine is an act of information search. The design and manipulations of the variables are defined in the next section.

## 5. Research Design

This section outlines the two experimental designs and the next section presents the findings.

## 5.1. Study 1—Lab Study

5.1.1. Subjects. We advertised the lab study via university emails. A total of 314 students from the school of business of a major university in Asia participated in this study. There were 143 males and 171 females, and their average age was 20. They were randomly assigned to different lab sessions, and each session consisted of 8 to 15 subjects.

5.1.2. Experimental Procedures. Subjects first filled in a questionnaire about their demographic information and their book preferences. Then, they were asked to enter the experimental website to select a book they liked.

We developed a personalized online bookstore with Amazon Web services application programming interfaces. The bookstore contained nearly 550,000 books, which were grouped into six major categories predefined by Amazon. The Web layout is shown in Figure 4. The icons of book categories were randomly displayed to eliminate position biases. After the subjects selected a book category, a list of books was then displayed in the central area of the window. The label of each book showed the book title and cover image, and there was also a hyperlink. By clicking the link, subjects could view the book’s details (e.g., book description and Amazon book ranking). If a subject liked the book, he could add it to his basket by clicking the “add book to basket” button. When the subject clicked the “basket” button on the left menu, items inside the basket were displayed. The subject then picked one of the books in his basket, confirmed the final choice, and checked out. At the end of the study, a lucky draw was conducted to select 10% of the subjects and they were given the books they chose in the experiment. A pretest with 11 postgraduate students was administered to test the performance of the personalized bookstore website.

5.1.3. Design and Manipulation. The study employed a 2 (recommendation type) × 6 (presentation timing) between-subject design. Regarding the presentation timing manipulation, there were six timing groups altogether. The first group of subjects received personalized recommendations immediately after logon. The second group of subjects received recommendations after they had sampled one (nonpersonalized) book. The other groups received recommendations after two, three, four, or five samples.

There were two levels of manipulations for recommendation type: static and adaptive. In the adaptive group, subjects were given personalized offers that matched the subjects’ current preferences as revealed in their sampling behaviors. A personalization software was developed to study the real-time subjects clickstream, and infer the subjects’ current preferences from the chosen items in their baskets or sampled items. Items in the subjects’ baskets were weighted more heavily than sampled items because these items matched better with their spontaneous preferences. The software then generated book recommendations that were congruent to the inferred preferences.<sup>8</sup> For the sake of experimental control, once the personalized items were presented, the algorithm did not continue to adapt. In the static group, subjects were given personalized offers from the book categories they had chosen in the pre-task preference questionnaire. Regardless of the adaptability approach, all personalized offers were shown under the area of personalized recommendations, which was located at the bottom of the window.

Regarding the timing factor, subjects were presented with personalized recommendations after having sampled zero, one, two, three, four, or five stock (i.e., nonpersonalized) items. If a subject made his final decisions before the display of personalized recommendations, the data point was considered to be a “drop out” and was discarded from the data analysis.<sup>9</sup> Seventy-eight subjects dropped out from the study. The usable sample was 236.

5.1.4. Operationalization of Constructs. Many of the hypotheses tested in this lab study refer to the amount of “sampling.” This construct is used as an independent variable, where it refers to the amount of stock item sampling before the system introduces the personalized recommendations; and it is also used as a dependent variable, where it refers to the amount of sampling of personalized items. In the context of this lab study, we operationalize this as a consumer’s click on a book’s hyperlink. As described, each hyperlink anchor has basic textual and graphic information about the book and leads the consumer to search on more information about it. Clicking on the hyperlink therefore represents an effort to collect additional information about an item of interest.

The other main dependent variables in this study are the consumer’s final choice and user satisfaction. The final choice was directly observable as the item that each consumer finally selected to download and subjects evaluated the process satisfaction at the end of the experiment. The satisfaction construct was adapted from Bhattacherjee (2001).<sup>10</sup>

The interaction variable was expertise. The theory regards their knowledge of the items they prefer and about which the system offered recommendations. At the end of the experiment, subjects were asked about their expertise on the kind of books that the system recommended.<sup>11</sup> We factor analyzed all items in the questionnaire and computed the composite scores. The composite score of consumer expertise and the two discrete variables (i.e., presentation timing and recommendation type) were the independent variables of the analysis.

The lab study, unlike the field study that we describe below, included a measure for H2 and H3 and the related H6 and H7. The measure was about recommendations’ level of match with the person’s preferences. To explicitly measure level of match, in the lab study, after a subject sampled a personalized recommendation, we measured the subject’s assessment of how well that item matched his preference.<sup>12</sup>

## 5.2. Study 2—Field Study

5.2.1. Subjects. We cooperated with a large digital music retailer, EolAsia, in Asia to conduct a field experiment. EolAsia sent email invitations to its members asking them to participate in the current study. Those who opted to participate could click a web link in the email to start the process. Subjects were randomly streamed to different cells of an array of experimental design. In this study, our data analysis focused on 199 subjects (87 females and 112 males, age = 27), and all of them had experience in downloading digital music. They contributed 793 page browsings, 701 samplings, and 251 track evaluations. As a token of appreciation, each subject was given one free music track and a chance to join a lucky draw for special gifts. They would download their free track from our experimental website.

5.2.2. Design and Manipulation. The study employed a 2 (recommendation type) × 2 (presentation timing) between-subject design. The manipulation of recommendation type was the same as in Study 1. Presentation timing was manipulated as follows: The first group of subjects received early personalized offers that were presented immediately after subjects logged on the website. The second group of subjects received the personalized recommendations after they sampled three tracks<sup>13</sup> or added one track to the basket.

5.2.3. Experimental Procedures. Subjects were asked to fill in a questionnaire about their demographic information and digital music download habits. Subjects had to choose and rank 3 favorite international artists and 3 favorite local artists from a selection list of 15.<sup>14</sup> We used the regular reports from the music billboards to decide which artists to include in the selection lists.

Then, all subjects were requested to enter the experimental website to select and download a track they liked. The website provided more than 173,000 tracks, which were categorized into 9 groups by EolAsia. Each track had a popular index.<sup>15</sup> After a subject logged on to the website, he found a main menu on the left and nine music groups presented in the central area of the window (Figure 5). The icons of nine music groups were randomly displayed to eliminate position biases. After the subject picked a group and then an artist, the tracks of that artist were then presented in the central area of the window. The label of each track was a hyperlink. After clicking the link, subjects could browse the details of the track, such as artist information and track duration. On the same page, two link options were provided with one labeled as “preview,” and the other as “add track to basket.” By clicking the “preview” link, subjects could listen to a 30-second preview track. Then, they could add any number of tracks to their shopping baskets and choose to evaluate the tracks using a 9-point Likert scale. When subjects clicked the “basket” button on the left menu, items inside the basket were displayed. Subjects then picked one of the tracks in their baskets and confirmed the final choice. The selected track would then be sent to the subject’s computer in the form of a 256k bit Windows media audio file. In the whole process, all clickstreams were recorded, and the subjects completed a posttask questionnaires to report their experiences.

A pretest with 42 subjects was administered to test the performance of the digital music download system. Normally, subjects could complete the whole process in 25 minutes. All of them agreed that the navigation process and the selection task were smooth.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Key Findings

The aim of this study was to understand the interplay between the “what” and the “when” of personalization and the mechanism through which their effects are exerted on users during an interactive online shopping session. Table 1 summarizes the results of the tested hypotheses.

The main hypothesized effects are clearly visible by inspection of Figures 6 through 10. Referring to the results of the field study (Figure 9(a)), for example, the isolated upward effect of time on sampling and final choice can be seen by comparing late static (0.28) to late adaptive (0.73); the isolated downward effect of time by comparing early static (0.76) to late static (0.28); and the combined effect of time can be seen comparing early adaptive (0.67) to late adaptive (0.73). The pattern of results over time is even more convincingly visible in the lab study results, where the predicted patterns of increase or decrease are clear and consistent. As explained earlier, we made no general prediction on whether the combined effect would be positive or negative because that would depend on situational factors such as the effectiveness of how good the adaptation software.

Some hypotheses (e.g., H4A and H8A) were significant in the lab study but not in the field experiment. In the field study, there was no significant difference between the means of sampling late-adaptive and late-static music tracks. In other words, even if the late-static recommendations did not totally align with subjects’ current interests, subjects were still happy to sample late-static music tracks. We believe that the insignificant results were due to the product nature and experimental setting. Some subjects might sample music not only for inspection purposes but also because they enjoy listening to it (Mathwick and Rigdon 2004).

A strength of the current study is that we combine a field experiment with a lab study. This helps establish the robustness of results and also combines the external validity of the field study with the experimental control of the lab study. We especially took advantage of the lab study to elicit measures to help establish the link between the predicted behaviors and the mechanisms that we theorized to be behind them. The hypothesized theoretical mechanism behind the upward effect is the increasing level of match between an adaptive system’s recommendations and the consumer’s current preferences. This mechanism was hypothesized as H2–H3 and was supported by the data. The hypothesized theoretical mechanism behind the downward effect is the decreasing expected marginal benefit from sampling personalized items, which results from the increasing expected value of the best nonpersonalized item the consumer already has. We did not develop these mechanisms into formal hypotheses because questions of expected marginal benefit lead us too far from our main topic. However, the lab experiment recorded two pieces of empirical evidence of this mechanism. First, before the personalized recommendations were presented, we elicited the subject’s rating of how much he liked each (nonpersonalized) item that he sampled. This measure allows us to confirm the rather obvious effect that consumers who sampled more items before we introduced the personalized recommendations had a higher starting value of v than consumers who had sampled fewer items when we introduced the personalized recommendations. The results confirm that subjects had a higher maximum score after more samples $( t = 3 . 5 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Second, we elicited a subjective measure of expected marginal benefit. Just before we introduced the personalized recommendations, whether this was immediately or after one or more stock items were sampled, we asked subjects if they felt it would be useful at that point to consider some personalized recommendations.<sup>17</sup> By asking about their interests in seeing hypothetical personalized items, we were eliciting their perception of the expected marginal benefit of receiving the personalized offers, which is proportional to the expected marginal benefit of sampling personalized offers (the difference being the search cost, modeled as a constant for each individual). The results confirmed that the groups who received personalized offers after more sampling of stock items reported that it was not a good time to see personalized items $( t = - 7 . 5 8 , p < \bar { 0 } . 0 1 )$ Taken together, these obtrusive measures capture the essence of the downward effect of time that results because subjects have better and better stock items as time goes on and see less benefit from receiving and sampling personalized recommendations.

## 7.2. Postfinding Analyses on the Interaction Between Consumer Expertise and Timing

Section 4 only provides hypotheses on the interaction between consumer expertise and the upward sloping line. As described in §4.1, CST does not provide an unambiguous prediction on the interaction between consumer expertise and the downward sloping line. Thus, we did not formally develop hypotheses to address this interaction. Here, we conducted some post-finding analyses. First, we explored the interaction between expertise and the downward effect of time on users’ sampling. The dependent variable was the amount of sampling of static personalized items, which was regressed on level of expertise, time of presentation, and the interaction. The interaction term was not significant (lab: $p > 0 . 1 ;$ field: $p > 0 . 1 )$ . Second, we conducted a regression testing on whether the consumer chose a personalized item as his final choice. The interaction term was not significant in the lab experiment but was significant in the field experiment (lab: $p > 0 . 1 ;$ field: $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Consistent with our expectation, the results are either insignificant or inconsistent in the two studies.

It is difficult to make predictions regarding the interaction between consumer expertise and the combined effect of time. Overall, one would expect a tendency toward a more positive combined effect of time for experts, because we predicted and found an interaction of expertise with the upward effect while the interaction between expertise and the downward effect is ambiguous. We conducted regression analyses to examine the interaction between expertise and the combined effect of timing on the amount of sampling and probability of choosing. We considered only adaptive recommendations because adaptive recommendations over time reflect both the downward effect of delay and the possible upward effect of improved recommendations. Regarding the lab experiment, we took all adaptive groups into account and ran a regression on timing, expertise, and the interaction. There were significant interaction effects on users’ sampling $( t = \bar { 2 } . 3 9 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and on users’ choice $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 4 . 4 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Regarding the field experiment, again, the data came from adaptive recommendations. The interaction effect between presentation timing and consumer expertise was significant $( t = - 5 . 1 4 , \stackrel { \cdot } { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Also, a significant interaction effect was found on subjects’ final choice $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 6 . 3 4 .$ $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . In both experimental settings, the data shows that the combined effect of time is more favorable for experts. We interpret this as stemming from the fact that the upward effect of time is stronger for experts while there is no clear interaction between expertise and the downward effect of time.

## 8. Implications and Limitations

## 8.1. Theoretical Contributions

This study contributes to the existing literature in several ways. Personalization of information technology (IT) services is intended to provide the right content in the right format at the right time. Recent research on personalization has focused on content and layout personalization, and explored its effects on attitudinal factors (e.g., Komiak and Benbasat 2006; Tam and Ho 2005, 2006; Wang and Benbasat 2005). Ho and Tam (2005) first raised the issue of personalization over the various decision-making stages of a consumer. However, their work is limited to static personalization using a discrete stage approach. By contrast, our study considers adaptive personalization over a continuous time span. In addition to being more current and interesting technologically, this difference requires modified hypotheses and a completely different methodological approach and data analysis. This is because in adaptive personalization, the upward and downward effects are combined. Thus, the focus of the current paper is on isolating and testing these two main effects in the context of the more realistic and current context of adaptive personalization. Other than Ho and Tam’s (2005) work, little research work has been done on the timing dimension of a personalization strategy and its impact on customerbusiness interaction. As Web personalization increasingly relies on an up-to-date profile of the dynamic consumer, it is imperative to understand how timing affects consumers’ behavior and decision outcomes. Thus, the first contribution of this research is that it addresses a gap in the personalization literature.

Second, Kalyanam and Zweben (2005) have remarked, “In marketing, timing is arguably the most important variable of all” (p. 4). One might expect that marketing literature would have much to say about the timing of persuasive messages such as advertisements, promotions, and recommendations. Indeed, timing is a construct that continues to attract considerable attention in marketing research. However, the marketing literature’s primary concern with the timing of persuasive messages has traditionally regarded the persistence of the message’s influence over time measured in terms of weeks and months. In our context, personalized recommendations are presented to the consumer during the search stage of his shopping task, which typically spans over a very short period of time measured in terms of minutes, if not seconds. To our knowledge, our research addresses questions of timing that have not been the focus in most marketing and HCI studies and that arise from the emergence of a new technology platform (i.e., data mining, clickstream analysis, and dynamic content generation) to support online personalized services. This echoes the point by Kalyanam and Zweben (2005) that technology has introduced new opportunities regarding the timing of persuasive marketing messages.

Third, our study adopts the theoretical framework of CST in a relatively simple form of a single equation to illuminate many of the dynamic aspects of real behavior in a consumer task. Our main contribution in this regard is the identification of the two competing main effects of time that apply to personalization technology within a single CST framework. In terms of Equation (1), the upward effect of time is due to an increase in the value of  and the downward effect of time is due to an increase in the starting value of v. Expertise is hypothesized to affect $\mu , \tau ,$ and c. Our theoretical framework explains the effects of timing personalization within a rational framework. It does not attempt to model non-rational psychological influences, and it views item sampling as an act of information search instead of enjoyment fulfillment.

Fourth, a strength that enhances the theoretical contribution is the use of hedonic and experience goods in the two studies. The choice of goods enables us to eliminate potential biases resulting from decision strategies for multiattribute items that are commonly used in studying human decision making (Johnson and Payne 1985, Lipman 1991). Many decision strategies such as the equal weight heuristic, lexicographic, and elimination by aspect require trade-offs to be made between different attributes of a product. Previous research indicates that human decision makers adopts different strategies at different stages of the decision process and their effects are difficult to control (Payne et al. 1993). The use of hedonic and experience goods as a choice item eliminates many of these potential confounding effects and allows us to assess more accurately the saliency of the hypothesized relationships.

## 8.2. Practical Implications

No longer is the Web exclusive to the IT savvy; it has become a core channel for product distribution and customer support. It also represents a touch point that has opened up new opportunities for how firms can create value through better interactions with their customers (Rayport and Jaworski 2005). Personalization is a key design element for systems that directly interface with customers. For Web services, dynamic content is generated based on the browsing behavior and spontaneous preferences of consumers. However, as pointed out by Datta et al. (2004), dynamic content comes at a cost of additional computation and communications across different channels. The cost to generate a personalized page in real time is likely to increase exponentially as users become more and more demanding on “acceptable response time” (Loosley et al. 2000). In summary, adaptive personalization has the potential to become a technology lever to achieve business objectives, but it is costly. IT managers will benefit from a better understanding of the effects of adaptive personalization on customers and the underlying mechanisms before they make investment decisions on the technologies.

Against this backdrop, our study shows that the benefits of dynamic content are not easily or completely attainable. Specifically, our research shows that quality and timeliness present an inherent trade-off; it is not possible to attain the best of both. Even assuming it is still worth the investment, a manager is left with determining how to weigh between the two competing values of better matches and timely recommendations. Our research frames the trade-off so that practitioners can determine which risk and benefit is more important in the context of their business models and their customers. In some contexts, a typical user session may be shorter in which case there is a higher risk from delay, or consumer preferences may be more long lived in which case there is less benefit from delay. In other contexts, the opposite might be true. Beyond framing the essential trade-off, our work offers some practical guidance by identifying that the upward effect of adaptiveness is enhanced as user expertise increases. This means that if an online shop knows that its users are, on average, experts, the argument is increased in favor of later presentation. Better still, if the online shop is able to segment its users into novices and experts, then the benefits of delay can be expected to be relatively enhanced for the experts and perhaps weaker for the novices. This might lead the online shop to favor earlier presentation for novices and later presentation for experts.

By the definition of our experimental task, the recommended items serve only as potential substitutes to the stock items and not as potential additional sales. However, our findings regard a variety of dependent variables including the amount of sampling of personalized items, the probability that the consumer will choose one of those items as his final choice, and consumer satisfaction with the shopping experience. These findings shed light on the business outcomes as follows. First, the finding on sampling is of practical importance. To the extent that an online business model includes advertising revenue, then any increase in consumer browsing activity can translate into increased revenue. A more fundamental reason why sampling is beneficial to a firm is that consumer satisfaction levels track the amount of sampling of personalized items. This is because, in both main effects, increased sampling results from system features that are beneficial to the consumer. The upward effect says that the consumer samples more when the items are a good match with his interests. The downward effect says that the consumer samples more when the items are presented in time to be relevant. Thus, in our context, increased sampling is a sign that the consumer is receiving better, more timely recommendations. Moreover, our work shows that by choosing the timing of presentation of adaptive recommendations, the online shop is trading off whether to benefit its customers through better recommendations or through timely recommendations. A third benefit is that findings on the likelihood to select personalized items allow online shops to recommended items with better margins to customers and to achieve a higher rate of cross-selling and upselling. Knowing the preference of a consumer enables the shop to select and push high-margin items while serving the needs of the customer. Finally, there is the promise of increased sales. Sampling is a type of promotion that is commonly used to promote sales, though the impact of free samples on sales is an underresearched topic (Heiman et al. 2001). Sampling is particularly important for experienced goods (Shapiro and Varian 1998). Thus, although our experimental setting did not allow us to study this directly, there is reason to expect that increased sampling will result in increased sales.

## 8.3. Limitations

There are two primary avenues through which the presentation of personalized recommendations can increase an online shop’s revenue. One avenue is via increased sales, i.e., a user purchases one of the personalized recommendations when otherwise he would not have purchased any item. This is certainly an important target for research, but it is not easily amenable to the experimental method where subjects are spending “house money.” The other avenue is via substitute sales, which has the variety of possible benefits outlined above including increased user activity on the website. One main limitation is the fact that we could not study the effect of personalization on increased sales.

Also, our results can be generalized only to other applications where the level of user involvement is similar to book or music downloads. In part, this means that the results are limited to other applications with a comparable level of importance to users. The reason is that in consumer search theories, the user’s level of involvement is a contingent factor that affects behavior. More work needs to be done to explore other product categories (e.g., durable goods).

## 9. Future Research and Conclusion

In sum, this work highlights the dilemma between the “what” and the “when” of personalization, and investigates the effects of presentation timing on consumers’ consideration and choice of personalized offers over short online shopping sessions. It also explores how consumer expertise interacts with presentation timing and affects choice consideration and outcomes. Given the increasing level of interactivity of HCI technology and the proliferation of online self-service in recent years, interaction between a service provider and a consumer is becoming richer yet shorter and more salient. To exploit the benefits of increasingly sophisticated personalization technologies, there is a need to understand the dynamics of interaction of this nature. Academic research in this area is lagging behind the development of the industry. Our study aims to fill this gap by investigating the timing effect of adaptive personalization during these microinteractions with an online shop.

There is room for future research. First, the current lab and field experiments should be repeated using different categories of products and varying the level of task complexity to further assess the validity and generalizability of our results. Second, this work explores presentation timing only within a single interaction session. One interesting extension would be to study how often a personalization agent should update its recommendations to match consumers’ tastes over multiple sessions. Some customers are variety seekers who look for new products regularly whereas some customers have brand loyalty and repurchase a brand continually. This study represents a first step toward understanding the timing dimension of Web adaptive personalization in the context of online shopping. Much work needs to be done to understand the interaction dynamics between personalized websites and their users.
