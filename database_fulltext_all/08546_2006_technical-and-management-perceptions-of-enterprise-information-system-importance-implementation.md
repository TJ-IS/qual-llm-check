# Technical and management perceptions of enterprise information system importance, implementation and benefits

Hsin Hsin Chang

Department of Business Administration, National Cheng Kung University, Tainan City, Taiwan, email: easyhhc@mail.ncku.edu.tw

Abstract. This paper compares information systems (IS) integration in high-tech organizations from the information technology (IT) and general management perspectives. All the organizations studied have experience of integrated Enterprise Resource Planning systems, and some with their extension to Supply Chain Management and Customer Relationship Management systems. The operational scope of the sample systems, and senior IT and general management perceptions of the importance of their functions, benefits and implementation success factors, obtained by qualitative interviews with 49 senior managers and a quantitative survey of 219 high-tech companies, are described and compared. Although the opinions expressed indicate some mutual hostility, IT and general management perceptions of IS implementation were very similar. IT managements overall importance assessments of business functions were more strongly correlated with their overall level of implementation, and they tended to rate system benefits and system reliability more highly. The significance of these findings is discussed and some suggestions for further investigation, placing them in a wider context, are made. Arguably, the similarities found are more important than the differences.

Keywords: technical perspective, management perspective, enterprise information systems

## INTRODUCTION

There is a history of poor communication between information technology (IT) and general management which has had a negative impact on the actual and perceived performance of enterprise information systems (IS). As the importance of these systems increases, expanding their scope to the integration of all business functions, so does the potential importance of any disagreement on system goals and performance.

There is an existing literature on the IT and general management ‘divide’, and on the closely related ‘hot’ topic of IT-business alignment. Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) implementations would be expected to be especially sensitive to such misalignment due to their business-wide integrative scope. User attitudes to ERP implementation have been studied (e.g. Akkermans & Van Helden, 2002; Newell et al., 2004), but we are not aware of any contemporary study which compares IT and general management attitudes in this context. This study is intended as contribution towards filling this gap.

The paper revisits the ‘historical’ IT and general management divide in the context of hightech organizations with experience of implementing ERP, and optionally, Supply Chain Management (SCM) and Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems. The literature on the history of the (generally negative) relationship between IT and general management is first reviewed, before a discussion of the importance of shared understanding to ERP implementation. Specific research objectives concerned with a comparison of IT and general management perceptions of ERP system scope, importance, benefits and implementation influences are then identified, and their investigation, by a mixture of qualitative (case study interview) and quantitative (importance ranking postal survey), is described.

While there is some anecdotal evidence (reported from interviews) of disagreement between general and IT management staff, importance rankings for IS importance, benefits and implementation influences are very similar. IT managements overall importance assessments of business functions were more strongly correlated with their overall level of implementation (which we argue is not consistent with a relative lack of business awareness) and they tended to rate system benefits and system reliability more highly. In general, however, the quantitative survey indicated a high degree of consensus between IT and general management in this relatively sophisticated industrial sector. It is suggested that this consensus may have emerged from the ERP implementation process, and that it would be of interest to extend the comparison to organizations with a wider range of IT sophistication.

## THE IMPORTANCE OF THE IT–GENERAL MANAGEMENT RELATIONSHIP TO INFORMATION SYSTEMS INTEGRATION

The relationship between IT and general management has been ‘traditionally’ viewed as fraught, even hostile. According to this view, IT staff consider general managers as penny pinching, and lacking in technological awareness or interest, while corporate managers think IT staff produce systems which, if delivered at all, are late, over budget, and often fail to meet business needs.

## Negative perception of IT effectiveness

## Chief information officer–chief executive officer (CIO–CEO) relationships

The effectiveness of the CIO and their relationship with peers and top management has been discussed as both an indicator and determinant of general IT relationships and status (Stephens et al., 1992; Earl & Feeny, 1994; Enns et al., 2003). Smaltz (2000) cites trade press headlines indicating a problem, such as ‘Hatred: an update (CIO–CEO relationships)’ (Klug, 1996), and ‘Chasm closer: the CIO/CEO gap still dogs IS’ (King, 1995). The Klug (1996) article reported a Forbes magazine survey finding that 70% of managers would scrap their IS departments given the chance.

## Lack of business awareness

Perceived shortcomings of IT management include a failure to demonstrate business benefit, a lack of business focus, and a failure to communicate in business terms (Feeny & Edwards, 1992; Smaltz, 2000), while the general management side may fail to understand IT issues or relevance (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Melville et al., 2004).

These perceptions, along with factors such as a historical lack of power (Saunders & Scamell, 1986; Chang, 2002), a lack of participation in strategic planning (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991; Hult et al., 2004), past implementation failures (Willcocks & Lacity, 1998; Rajagopal, 2002), poor user relations (Ward & Elvin, 1999; Weill & Vita, 2002), and limited user awareness (Hirschheim et al., 1988) have in the past had a significant impact on the general effectiveness of IT in many organizations, and partly motivated the large scale devolution or outsourcing of the IT function which took place in the 1990s (Willcocks & Lacity, 1998; Melville et al., 2004).

Earl & Feeny (1994) found CIOs polarized between those whose CEOs regarded the IT function as adding value and those who regarded it simply as a cost. Lacity & Hirschheim (1995) in a study of 19 US organizations found a misalignment between the ‘strategic’ cost-reduction objectives of senior management, and the service excellence objectives of IT management and business unit stakeholders. Hirschheim et al. (2003) attribute the decline and fall of the central IT function at Texaco, despite a ‘stellar performance’ to a similar dichotomy.

## Lack of macroeconomic productivity

Much of the earlier antipathy is attributed to a failure to demonstrate IT productivity. At the macroeconomic level, the suggestion that IT investment up to the early 1990s had been a significant brake on US national productivity (The widely discussed ‘Solow Paradox’ Solow, 1987) reflected scepticism about IT productivity and concern about the coincident economic slowdown, rather than convincing direct evidence (Brynjolffson, 1993).

## Economic rehabilitation in the 1990s

Following the economic upswing in the mid-1990s, IT is generally regarded as a source of productivity (Dumagan et al., 2003; Price & McKittrick, 2002), accounting for an estimated twothirds of the US productivity growth increase in the second half of the decade (Oliner & Sichel, 2000). The failures of earlier studies to demonstrate a positive effect are now attributed to a lag in investment response and inadequate macroeconomic data and metrics (Brynjolffson, 1993; Triplett, 1999).

At the corporate level, Brynjolffson (1993) and Dewan & Min (1997) using International Data Group (IDG) and Computerworld annual survey data, provided early evidence of IT productivity which had been ‘lost in the noise’ in many earlier industry-level macroeconomic studies. Bharadwaj (2000) in a paired comparison of 56 ‘IT leader’ with non-leader ‘control’ companies, found significantly higher profitability and generally lower costs in the ‘leader’ group, although of course the cause and effect relationship could not be proved. Zhu & Kraemer (2002) found a positive association between metrics of e-commerce capability and performance indicators such as profitability and inventory reduction. Melville et al. (2004) review research on IT business value and find a consensus on an overall positive effect, modified by the possession of complementary resources and the nature of the firms trading environment.

The accumulating evidence of IT’s contribution to business performance, its pervasiveness and the increasing emphasis on its integrating role, now make an absolute denial of its importance untenable, and it is regarded as a, perhaps ‘the’, source of competitive advantage (Mata et al., 1995; Yetton et al., 2000). The low cost of extending its integrating capacity beyond the corporate boundary, to customers, suppliers and collaborators, via the internet, represents a new form of organization (Rayport & Sviokla, 1995) with a significant impact on productivity (Zhu & Kraemer, 2002).

Although there is apparently now general recognition of the economic importance of IT in support of change and competitiveness (McKenney, 1995; Davidson & Movizzo, 1996; Yetton et al., 2000; Choy et al., 2003), there is persistent circumstantial and anecdotal evidence of an apparent divide, even hostility, between general and IT management, ‘You know, we’re delivering things in the Internet environment or on the desktop . . . a lot faster than we were 10 years ago. But still not fast enough . . . it’s still the classic complaints . . . They really haven’t changed . . . They don’t go away. They really don’t’ (Hirschheim et al., 2003). If this divide does indeed persist, it might be expected to inhibit communication and understanding of business needs, limiting the effectiveness of IT and so tending to perpetuate itself (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995).

## Strategic alignment

The divide is now often expressed in terms of (mis)alignment between IT and business objectives. Strategic alignment, broadly concerned with the correspondence and compatibility of IT and business strategy within an organization (Chan et al., 1997; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1999) persistently appears in trade (Stepanek, 2002; Mieritz, 2004) and academic media reports (Reich & Benbasat, 1996; Allnoch, 1997; Ball & Adams, 2003) as a key concern of senior IT and general management. Strategic alignment has been found to be positively associated with IT and organizational performance (Miller, 1992; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Chan, 2002).

Strategy has been variously defined (or not), operationalized, dissected and classified. Earl (1989), to give a relatively simple and concrete example, distinguishes three strategies: IS strategy, focused on the alignment of applications with business needs, IT strategy, focused on technology policies such as technical standards and architectures, and IM strategy, focused on IT management and control issues. Because organizational strategy is often felt to be the province of senior management, the latter area is prominent in the literature (e.g. Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999). While we make no formal assessment or claim for the ‘strategic’ status of the IT and general management perception comparisons reported below (we have found management at any level unwilling to consider themselves ‘tactical’), the present study relates to the business alignment of an application suite of company-wide scope, and so would perhaps come under IS strategy in the Earl classification outlined above.

## Integration through IT: the need for shared understanding in ERP system implementation

Hirschheim & Klein (2000) list ‘comprehensive, cross-functional data availability’ as one of five (until recently, they argue, largely unreasonable and unrealized) demands that senior managers have consistently made of the IT function over the last two decades. ERP systems now provide the technical ability to achieve this within the organization, SCM and CRM systems to extend it beyond the conventional organization boundary. ERP applications suites exploit the integrative capacity of the corporate communications network to link ‘traditional’ business functions like finance, production, warehousing and sales into a single system employing a shared database, eliminating multiple data entry and ensuring, for example, that current stock availability and customer account details are available when processing an incoming order.

The introduction of such a system generally requires considerable business process re-engineering efforts, and derives much of its benefits (and risks) from their success or failure (Holland et al., 1999). The risks are illustrated by a survey (Chang, 2002) in which over 35% of the ERP projects had been cancelled and over half those implemented over-ran budget (average overrun 180%) or shop floor (average overrun 200%). In ‘traditional’ ERP systems data exchange stops at the enterprise boundary. SCM and CRM systems extend data exchange to supplier and partner organizations, and customers, often via the internet with a web interface. This extension has been claimed as a new form of organization, the ‘global virtual corporation (Miller et al., 1993).

Stakeholder involvement would intuitively be especially important for ERP implementations, and is specifically advocated in that context by, for example Mendel (1999).

We consider that the high degree of integration involved in ERP, CRM and SCM systems might make them especially sensitive to the degree of shared understanding between business and IT stakeholders, which we accordingly set out to investigate. This paper reports on a comparison of IT and general management perception of ERP systems (and optionally, CRM and SCM systems, described inclusively as enterprise IS below) in terms of the perceived importance of various business functions, influences on implementation success and benefits. We therefore pose the following research questions:

1 Functional scope? What is the operational scope, in terms of business functions, of the enterprise IS in our sample of organizations? – This was initially investigated by case study interview and followed up with a larger postal survey, with suggested functions derived from the case interviews supplemented by others culled from the literature.

2 Importance of functions? Does the perceived importance of these business functions show statistically significant difference between IT and general management? – This was investigated by postal survey analysis.

3 Implementation success factors? What are the perceived influences on IS implementation success? – This was investigated by case study interview.

4 Importance of implementation success factors? Does the perceived importance of the influences on IS implementation success differ significantly between IT and general management? – This was investigated by postal survey analysis.

5 Benefits? What are the perceived benefits of the Enterprise IS? – This was investigated by case study interview.

6 Importance of benefits? Does the perceived importance of the enterprise IS benefits differ significantly between IT and general management? – This was investigated by postal survey analysis.

## RESEARCH METHOD

## Case organization background

Exploratory fieldwork was conducted with the IT and general management of nine high-tech organizations to characterize the enterprise background, software systems, and experience with IS integration. All the case study organizations had experience with ERP implementation, and some with CRM and SCM. Four computer, three electrical engineering equipment and two semiconductor manufacturers formed the initial case study group and 49 managers (18 IT directors/managers, nine production directors/managers, nine finance/accounting directors, eight marketing directors and five human resource managers) were interviewed. ‘Pure’ IT managers are thus a minority (18/49, or 37%), but production managers also have a relatively high degree of system familiarity and experience, and together they form 55% of the sample.

## Research programme

The following stages of fieldwork were undertaken:

1 Interviews: Exploratory interviews was conducted in the summer of 2003 at IT and genera management departments of the nine case companies.

2 Questionnaire Formulation: An analysis of the interviews, together with published research, provided the initial questionnaire design, in which enterprise IS elements that appeared to be significant to IT and organizational respondents were selected for further comparative study.

3 Questionnaire Pilot Testing: The elements of the enterprise IS identified from the case analysis interviews were compared for their importance to IT and general management, in a pilot test of the questionnaire with the case study group. Feedback from the pilot test group was used to improve the wording of some questions and some additional items were suggested.

4 Postal Questionnaire Survey: In the third stage the final form of the questionnaire was distributed to 400 high-tech companies. A total of 219 questionnaires were completed and returned.

5 Questionnaire Survey Results Analysis: Finally, the questionnaire returns were analysed to reveal any statistically significant differences between the perception of enterprise IS from the technical and management perspectives.

## Interview development

Semi-structured, open-question interviews were conducted by a single investigator (the author) and the interview transcript and any initial inferences drawn by the investigator were shown to the interviewee for comment/verification. The interview guide questions are listed in Appendix 1.

For the question designed to elicit functional scope:

‘For which functions of the organization are software systems (ERP modules/components) used?’

A prompt list for functionality, initially based on the literature, was built up during our initial interviews. This was expanded as the interview programme proceeded, and formed the basis for the functional scope section of the questionnaire. Because the initial interviewee often did not know the functional scope of the system outside their own area (e.g. human resources) they often volunteered other (human or documentary) sources of information. These were followed up if possible, unless the information had already been elicited elsewhere, because the aim was to ensure comprehensive coverage for the questionnaire.

Prompts were avoided for implementation influences and benefits; because it was felt tha here they would inevitably bias the response.

For the two questions intended to elicit implementation influences:

• What do you think were the main influences acting for the success of the implementation?;

• What do you think were the main influences acting against the success of the implementation?.

We found the ‘against’ influences were overwhelmingly expressed as the absence of ‘success factors’. We feel this is often a logical/conceptual difficulty with the ‘facilitators and barriers’ terminology seen in the literature, which frequently (and probably inevitably) seem to be ‘mirror images’ of each other. We accordingly generally avoid these terms in favour of the more general ‘implementation influences’.

Interviewees were encouraged to speak freely ‘outside the script’ if they wished, in the introductory phase of the interview, and by the inclusion of some very open ‘opinion gathering questions, e.g. ‘How would you summarize your experience of your organizations information systems so far?’ The case study interviews were then used as a basis for a comparative investigation of IT and general management perspectives with a wider sample of organizations, via a postal survey.

## Questionnaire development

Case study results were used to develop a questionnaire to compare IT and general management perceptions of ERP operational scope and importance, implementation influences and benefits. This questionnaire was then pilot-tested on the case study group before wider postal circulation. A few additional items, and wording improvements, were suggested by respondents following pilot testing and were added to the questionnaire before circulation.

Three criteria were used in selecting an appropriate population for the survey: Companies had to be high-tech organizations (defined subjectively by market sector), with a turnover of more than US\$50 million, and over 500 employees. The aim of the latter two restrictions is to limit the effects of company size, because there is evidence to suggest that different factors may be important with smaller companies (Chang & Powell, 1998).

## CASE STUDY RESULTS SUMMARY

Case study results consisted of relatively unstructured and free ranging expressions of opinion, which were recorded in the hope that they might illuminate the quantitative data analysis, and a list of functions, implementation influences and system benefits which formed the basis of the questionnaire. The responses are summarized, and to a limited extent interpreted, below.

## General observations

The case study interviews revealed the following general points:

1 Although none of the firms had formally measured the financial benefits of IS, identifiable benefits were claimed from more efficient business processes.

2 Managers at all firms believed that the integration of IS was of primary importance.

3 IT and general managers generally agreed on the most important facilitators and barriers to the successful implementation and integration of IS in their companies.

4 Taken on its own, the level of investment in IS was an unreliable indicator of its successful development because the quality of managerial decisions in system planning, design and operation was also important.

## Factors influencing systems implementation

Information technology respondents mentioned clear objectives, the expertise of the implementation team, and data quality most often as being important prerequisites for system integration success. IT managers were likely to be more satisfied with software systems than organizational managers. Semi-technical problems experienced by IT respondents, included a lack of support from software and hardware vendors, a lack of employee training on integration technologies, a high complexity of business processes, and extra, unbudgeted costs of redesign and change to business structures.

Many of these problems, however, appeared to have their basis in the relationship between IT and general management staff, with some IT respondents reporting a lack of high level management support, which they felt limited the effectiveness of their training and promotional efforts, and meant they lacked the support necessary to push through the redesign of complex business processes, which consequently imposed extra costs on system integration. The IT function often appeared dominated by a corporate culture that did not commonly assign it a strategic role.

General management mentioned strategic vision, an understanding of the relationship between business processes and organizational elements, adequate budgetary provision (many of them having experienced cost-overruns on projects) and the use of appropriate performance measures, as facilitators of ERP implementation.

Both groups mentioned adequate planning, an adequately specified and reliable communications infrastructure, and the willingness to revise business procedures as part of the systems integration process, as facilitators.

Most companies had implemented ERP systems and were considering SCM and CRM techniques. Materials Requirements Planning was the most widely implemented production system (97% of respondent companies), reflecting its status, along with financial control systems, as an ‘ancestor’ of the ERP system (Orlicky, 1975). Corresponding most and least implemented functions for sales subfunctions are order management (100%) and market intelligence (75%), a CRM function and the least implemented function overall.

## Results summary

Table 8 summarizes the research questions posed and the answers obtained. For the individually significantly different function importance ratings, one might speculate that IT staffs relatively high rating of sales management reflects the active or planned development of CRM systems in many companies, but the relatively low rating for ‘after sales’ doesn’t apparently support this. The high rating for ‘career path tracing in human resources modules’ might reflect post Y2K career uncertainties, and the high ‘growth need strength’ noted by Couger & Zawack (1980) for IT personnel career motivations. Overall, IT staff rate the system benefits significantly more highly than do general management. Of the individual benefit ratings, IT staff would be expected to rate overall system reliability highly, and do. It is unclear whether the relatively low rating by general management represents a low valuation of reliability (which would be surprising, in view of its common use as the basis for Service Level Agreements) or a perception that the enterprise IS investments have not increased reliability.

## CONCLUSIONS AND SUGGESTIONS

This section considers some of the opinions expressed during the qualitative interview phase, by IT and general managers, and compares them with the quantitative postal questionnaire survey results. Some limitations of the research approach are identified, and suggestions for further investigation made.

## Information technology and management perception comparison: conclusions

We are left with something of a paradox. In the interview phase of our investigation, there was some evidence of a divergence in views between IT and general management, bordering on hostility in a few, especially vocal cases, although sometimes expressed with humour. One operational network manager said ‘Managers know the cost of everything and the value of nothing, well, actually they don’t know the cost of everything either, but they know everything costs too much’. The same individual observed that in his past experience the devolution of the central IT function to individual business units, for accountability and cost control, was especially inappropriate for an integrating infrastructure such as the communications network. He gave examples of proliferation of ad hoc solutions, and of a ‘last straw’ situation where the lastimplemented project had to fund a general network upgrade, an unanticipated and unwelcome cost to the centre concerned.

Another IT manager noted that management had been very reluctant to invest in switched infrastructure, which was advocated by IT on general capacity, maintenance and flexibility grounds, because they did not see a specific business need for it. Implementation was forced by a Y2K compliance requirement, and so the infrastructure was in place to support a complex integration of two enterprise systems following a ‘surprise’ merger, which could not otherwise have been completed in the required time frame. This may be an example of the potentially negative effect of a high degree of ‘alignment’ between business and IT noted by Tallon & Kraemer (2003).

More generally, senior organizational management staff interviewed all described IT investments required for integration extension as ‘too expensive’. They did not generally qualify this, which is rather suggestive of a view of IT cost as a simple overhead, without consideration of benefit, an often cited cause of IT undervaluation in the past (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Hirschheim et al., 2003). Many of them had experienced budget overruns on integration projects which will have conditioned this response. Presumably, if the investments were initially judged ‘too expensive’ relative to anticipated benefits, they would not have been made.

Despite this ‘anecdotal’ divergence of views expressed in the interview phase, quantitative comparison of the rather more narrowly focused specific aspects of IS functionality, implementation and benefits showed very similar perceptions by IT and general management staff, and the few significant individual differences observed show no particular pattern. There was a general tendency for IT staff to rate IS benefits and system reliability more highly, and a difference in the correlations of IT and general management importance ratings for business functions, with the degree of reported business function usage in our respondent sample.

General management’s importance ratings were weakly correlated with usage and IT’s moderately. If implementation and use is a reflection of ‘true’ importance, or ‘fitness’, as a Darwinian view of business functions would suggest (e.g. Astley & Van De Ven, 1983), then this result is inconsistent with the conventional view of IT staff as relatively lacking in business awareness.

## Research limitations and suggestions

It seems possible that the apparent consensus is conditioned by the high-tech industry sector we chose to study. Broadbent & Weill (1997) suggest a classification of enterprises on the basis of the extent of, and motivation for, their investment in shared IT infrastructure. Enterprises with ‘No’ shared infrastructure investment allow complete IT autonomy to business units, those with a ‘Utility’ view invest for economies of scale, ‘Dependant’ enterprises are motivated by the existing synergistic benefits of data sharing between business units, and ‘Enabling’ enterprises by the promise of current and future strategic flexibility to exploit business opportunities. IT investment is relatively higher at each successive level. Schein (1989) produces a four-way classification of individual attitude of CEOs to IT (‘automate, informate up, informate down, transform’) which, while different in detail and application, similarly encapsulates different degrees of commitment to IT.

While we have not undertaken a formal classification of our respondents, or their enterprises on these scales, it seems likely that most enterprises undertaking ERP, SCM or CRM implementations would be considered ‘Enablers’ or at the least ‘Dependent’ and thus would have a relatively positive attitude to IS and IS investment, corresponding to a ‘vision to transform’.

To an extent the absolute distinction implied in our comparison of IT and general management is artificial, especially for our sample of organizations. Production managers, who form roughly half our general management group, are likely to have a relatively intimate knowledge of the IS, and more generally, as IS become pervasive and the ‘old guard’ retires, IT naivety is likely to become rare. One of the case study organizations had a policy of recruiting IS staff from within the business functions where possible, which would also tend to blur any distinction. Possibly any future studies of this nature will have to apply a more specific classification to its subjects, while avoiding the creation of artificial differences through sampling bias.

It seems possible that the level of user consultation required in the implementation of ERP systems, and its associated business process re-engineering, has promoted stakeholder dialogue and consensus, as noted by Ward & Elvin (1999).

The obvious suggestion is to address the above limitations by including organizations in the survey with a wider range of IT commitment. Inclusion of enterprises with ‘no’ or a ‘utility’ view of shared IT infrastructure investment might be expected to produce more divergence between the IT and general management views, but would require a more generalized questionnaire than that employed in this study, which is specifically oriented to organizations with experience of implementing ERP or optionally SCM and CRM systems. An approach similar to that of Bharadwaj (2000) who conducted a paired comparison (in this case of productivity) of 56 ‘IT leader’ with non-leader ‘control’ companies might be appropriate. The relationship between the level of IT commitment and any IT–general management divergence could be investigated as part of the interpretation of such an extended study.

Disentangling the effect of user consultation from the actual implementation of the technology would require carefully timed horizontal studies comparing user perceptions before and after user consultation, and after system implementation. Such studies would most easily and effectively be incorporated into the systems analysis efforts supporting business process reengineering, and thus would require the support and cooperation of a commercial implementation practitioner.

We find the correlation between the implementation level and importance ratings for individual business functions an intriguing (although unproven) indicator of ‘true’ IT-business awareness, and think it would be potentially interesting to apply it to other data sets covering a wider range of IT commitment, although unfortunately, a rather large sample of organizations is likely to be required.

As noted above, here we take a Darwinian view of implementation (e.g. Astley & Van De Ven, 1983) as a reflection of ‘true’ business importance. An alternative, perhaps even a conventional view, would be that general management perception of business importance is, almost by definition, the ‘true’ one, and that IT perception of importance is more influenced by what has been implemented, implying they have to some extent implemented the wrong things. We find this view less persuasive.

There is quite extensive documentation on senior management perception of IT as lacking in business awareness or relevance (reviewed above). There is also published documentation of allegedly inadequate understanding of IT business relevance by senior management. Hirschheim et al.’s (2003) Texaco case study, in particular, describes a cumulative long-term misuse and abuse which builds into a tragedy. This side of the story is, however, much less prominent. To some extent this is an imbalance in the literature caused by the relative power and prominence of these groups (‘Its never the boss’s fault’) but in another sense it is not an imbalance, because the perceptions of the more powerful group (accurate or not), are, by definition, more important. We think the use of actual implementation frequency as an indicator of actual business importance has potential to contribute measurement to the understanding of relative IT-business awareness.

The present study is limited to an overall comparison of IT and management perceptions of ERP (and optionally SCM and CRM systems). Narrowing the focus somewhat, a similar comparison of perceptions within and between the organizations making up a supply chain might be informative, because there is evidence to suggest that a lack of mutual trust and understanding currently limits the development of collaboration (Murphy & Simon, 2002; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003; Buxmann et al., 2004; Ibbot & O’Keefe, 2004; Bunduchi, 2005).

## Summary

Returning from the realms of speculation, for our restricted group of ERP practitioner organizations, although there was evidence from the interviews of some differences, even hostilities, quantitative differences in IT and general management perceptions were generally insignificant. For this group, our overall conclusion is that there is a broad consensus between general and IT management perspectives on the importance and benefits of ERP, SCM and CRM systems and on the main issues facing implementation of these systems.

Perhaps this consensus is in fact the more important aspect. It is not unknown for groups to reinforce their own identity by a semi-humorous deprecation of other groups with which they nevertheless cooperate effectively. IT in general, and the extension of its integrating capacity beyond the boundaries of the organization, via CRM and SCM systems, in particular, is now generally recognized as an important source of competitive advantage. It appears that this general recognition may transcend, if not entirely displace, any traditional animus between IT and general management. Perhaps this represents a maturity of the IS development process and of the people involved. It remains to be seen whether this is becoming a general phenomena, or is restricted to sophisticated organizations with a high degree of system integration experience.

We chose to study ERP systems because we thought IT management agreement would be especially important there. In retrospect it seems very likely that a consensus has emerged from the ERP implementation process, issues arising having been addressed as they were encountered, so we looked for differences where they were least likely to be found. In order to place it in context, this type of study needs to be extended to, and interpreted in terms of, a broader range of organizational IT sophistication.
