## Abstract

With the ongoing challenge of successfully managing information technology (IT) projects, organizations are recognizing the need for greater project management discipline. For many organizations, this has meant ratcheting up project management skills, processes, and governance structures by implementing a project management office (PMO). While anecdotal evidence suggests that implementing a PMO can be quite difficult, few studies discuss the specific challenges involved, and how organizations can overcome them. To address this gap in existing knowledge, we conducted a Delphi study to (1) identify the challenges of implementing a PMO for managing IT projects, (2) rank these challenges in order of importance, (3) discover ways in which some organizations have overcome the top-ranked challenges, and (4) understand the role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools in the implementation of a PMO. We identified 34 unique challenges to implementing a PMO and refined this list to 13 challenges that our Delphi panelists considered most important. The top-three challenges were (1) rigid corporate culture and failure to manage organizational resistance to change, (2) lack of experienced project managers (PMs) and PMO leadership, and (3) lack of appropriate change management strategy. Through follow-up interviews with selected panelists, we identified a series of actions that can be taken to overcome these challenges including having a strong PMO champion, starting small and demonstrating the value of the PMO, obtaining support from opinion leaders, hiring an experienced program manager who understands the organization, bringing the most talented PMs into the PMO implementation team, adopting a flexible change management strategy, and standardizing processes prior to PMO implementation. The interviews were also used to better understand the role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools. In terms of PMO structure, we found that ‘light’ PMOs were more likely to be implemented successfully. Most organizations eschew formal metrics, instead relying on subjective indicators of PMO success. Lastly, it appears that PMO tools are difficult to implement unless a project management culture has been established.

## Introduction

As information technology (IT)-enabled innovation has taken center stage in organizations, the focus on successful execution of IT projects has increased tremendously. While there is some evidence that IT project management may be improving over time, success remains elusive for a significant proportion of IT projects. In 1994, the Standish Group reported that only 16% of IT projects were successful (i.e., delivered the desired functionality, completed on time, and within budget). In recent years, the success rate of IT projects has been more encouraging, with estimates of successful projects varying from 35% in the 2006 Standish Report (Rubinstein, 2007) to 67% in a survey of over 400 project managers (PMs) (Sauer et al., 2007). Still, this suggests that between 33 and 65% of all IT projects are, in some ways, less successful than we would like them to be, resulting in cost and time overruns, deficiencies in features and functions, or falling short of customer expectations (Keil et al., 2000; Keil & Robey, 2001).

The challenges of managing IT projects have increased as organizational and technological complexity, and the size of projects, have grown (Xia & Lee, 2004; Martin et al., 2007; Sauer et al., 2007). While there are undoubtedly many reasons for the relatively high incidence of IT project failure, research suggests that inadequate project management discipline and knowledge are among the key contributing factors. In a study of information system (IS) project risks, lack of project management skills was ranked among the top-five risks by a panel of experts (Schmidt et al., 2001). In recent years, organizations have begun to recognize the need for greater discipline in managing IT projects. Organizations can take numerous approaches, which are not mutually exclusive, to address this problem. One approach calls for assessing the organization’s current software development capability and embarking on a program to move the organization to a higher level on the Software Engineering Institute’s Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) (Chrissis et al., 2003, p. 3). The underlying assumption here is that higher CMMI levels will yield more successful project outcomes. Another approach is the Organizational Project Management Maturity Model (OPM3), which was released by the Project Management Institute (PMI) in 2003 (Crawford, 2006). Modeled after the CMMI, the OPM3 approach focuses on the maturity of processes involved in project management.

A third approach to improve the management of IT projects is the establishment of an organizational entity called PMO (Rad & Levin, 2002; Richardson & Ives, 2004). In a survey of 500 PMOs, Hobbs & Aubry (2007) found that these entities are identified by several names: Program Management Office, Project Management Oversight (Christiansen & Sherry, 1992), Project Management Center of Excellence (Rad & Levin, 2002), Directorate of Project Management (Rad & Levin, 2002), Strategic Project Office (Crawford, 2001), and Enterprise Project Office (Englund et al., 2003). In this research, we use the term Project Management Office (PMO) as this is the most commonly used label (Hobbs & Aubry, 2007). We focus on the role of the PMO in establishing IT project management discipline within an organization, as this appears to be the most popular approach (Richardson & Ives, 2004).

Previous research has advocated implementing PMOs to improve IT project performance. Frequently cited benefits of PMOs include training and mentoring PMs, developing project management standards, establishing project management discipline and oversight within an organization, facilitating knowledge management, leveraging previous solutions, sharing good practices and delivering project objectives (Dai & Wells, 2004; Hill, 2004; Desouza & Evaristo, 2006; Liu & Yetton, 2007). However, it is unclear how to implement a PMO successfully, for anecdotal evidence suggests that many PMO initiatives are eventually abandoned or fail to produce the improvements that were originally hoped for (Kendall & Rollins, 2003, p. 8). Unfortunately, we know very little about the challenges that represent the most serious threats to the successful implementation of a PMO. As a result, managers lack any guidance in overcoming the challenges they are likely to encounter. Clearly, we need further research to understand these challenges, and to find ways to address them.

We have organized the remainder of the paper into four major sections. First, we review the relevant literature relating to PMOs and introduce the research questions that we seek to address. Next, we discuss the research approach and then present and discuss our results for each research question. We conclude with the implica tions of our findings for both research and practice.

## Background and research questions

We conducted a systematic and rigorous literature review of both project management and IS journals in order to determine the current level of knowledge associated with PMOs. Based on this review, we now briefly discuss the definition, history, role, configuration, and implementation challenges associated with PMOs.

## Definition and history of the PMO

A project office has been defined in two ways: (1) an organizational entity established to manage a specific project, or a related series of projects, usually headed by a project manager, and (2) an organizational entity established to assist PMs and teams throughout the organization in implementing project management principles, practices, methodologies, tools, and techniques (Ward, 2000). The latter entity, referred to as PMO, is the focus of our study. A PMO is a formal, centralized layer of control between senior management and proiect management (Liu & Yetton, 2007; Martin et al., 2007). Some researchers have described the PMO as a source of centralized integration and a repository of knowledge that can be used to inform more effective and efficient IT project management (Desouza & Evaristo, 2006). Other researchers have defined it as the administrative mechanism by which project management activities and procedures are integrated into the enterprise policies and culture (Rad & Levin, 2002). In this research, we use the Project Management Institute’s definition of a PMO: ‘an organizational body or entity assigned various responsibilities related to the centralized and coordinated management of those projects under its domain’ (PMI, 2004, p. 369). Underlying all these definitions is the growing realization that PMOs can play an important role in organizational management (Aubry et al., 2008). Thus, the PMO is an organizational innovation (Hobbs et al., 2008) that can not only improve IT project management practices, but also facilitate organizational transformation (Aubry et al., 2008).

The use of some kind of PMO has a long history dating back to the 1930s (Dai, 2001). PMOs have been reported in the telecom, aerospace, and defense industries for a long time (Desouza & Evaristo, 2006). It has only been in the last decade, however, that widespread interest emerged in establishing a PMO as a means of improving IT project performance and reducing the incidence of project failures (Dai, 2001, p. 5). Many organizations began to establish PMOs in the mid-1990s (Dai & Wells, 2004), primarily to oversee Y2K-related IT projects. Some organizations disbanded their PMOs after the Y2K remediation and transition work was completed. However, in many other organizations, the PMOs evolved and expanded in scope to take on other types of projects (Desouza & Evaristo, 2006). In a survey of 704 North American IT decision-makers in 2003, Forrester Research found that 67% of respondents had established one or more PMOs in their organizations (Pohlmann, 2003).

## Role of PMO and PMO configurations

A PMO helps both the IT project manager and the relevant organization to ‘understand and apply professional practices of project management, as well as to adapt and integrate business interests into the project management efforts’ (Hill, 2004). A PMO may perform one or more of the following four roles in an organization: consulting, knowledge management, standard setting, and hands-on program implementation (Desouza & Evaristo, 2006; Letavec, 2006; Liu & Yetton, 2007). The different roles that a PMO can play have led to a wide spectrum of different PMO configurations. At one end of the spectrum, a PMO can have minimal staff and no direct control over the management of individual projects. In this configuration, commonly referred to as PMO-light, the PMO plays a relatively passive, supporting role to IT PMs by creating standards for project implementation and acting as a project information repository. At the other end of the spectrum, a PMO can have a large complement of full time PMs and direct control over individual projects. In this configuration, commonly referred to as PMO-heavy, the PMO plays a more proactive role and is responsible for the outcome of IT projects. Thus, there are two basic configurations of a PMO: one that acts in a consulting capacity, providing PMs with training, guidance, and best practices; and a centralized version, with PMS on staff who are loaned out to business units to execute their various IT projects (Santosus, 2003).

The roles, responsibility, and functioning of a PMO in an organization will vary depending upon which configuration (PMO-heavy, PMO-light, or a hybrid) is adopted. In addition, the structure and function of the PMO continuously evolves, based on the changing context, needs, and capability maturity of the organization (Aubry et al., 2008). A continuum of PMO competency, with associated capabilities and roles, has been suggested by Hill (2004), and is represented in Figure 1. According to this continuum, as the organization (and PMO) achieves greater maturity, the PMO roles and responsibilities change from basic project oversight to a center of excellence closely aligned to the strategic business goals of the organization. Rad & Levin (2003) have suggested a similar five-level PMO Maturity Model based on organizational competency and role of the PMO.

## PMO implementation challenges

Anecdotal evidence reported in practitioner-oriented publications suggests that the failure rate of PMO implementations is very high. Based on a survey of 750 companies, Stanleigh (2006) asserts that ‘over 75% of organizations that set up a PMO shut it down within three years because it didn’t demonstrate any added value’. Similarly, based on a survey of 500 PMs, Hobbs & Aubry (2007) reported that nearly half of all PMOs were seen as ‘too costly and as contributing little to project and program performance’. In fact, research has showed that in the majority of cases, PMOs are unstable structures with organizations often reconfiguring their PMOs every few years (Hobbs et al., 2008). These findings suggest that the implementation of a PMO is a difficult challenge for most organizations.

There appear to be a number of factors that bear heavily upon the success of a PMO implementation, including a clear mission, an agreed-upon structure and scope of governance, and whether the corporate culture is conducive to a PMO (Santosus, 2003; Wren, 2005). Wren (2005) advises that without a culture of governance, the PMO is likely to fail. Leemann (2002) also suggests that ‘to be successful, project management must be part of a company’s culture’. Kendall & Rollins (2003) suggest seven factors that may lead to failure of a PMO. These include (1) not defining the PMO value proposition, (2) lack of perceived impact of PMO on project delivery abilities, (3) PMO becoming too authoritative, (4) low position of PMO in management reporting structure, (5) no buy-in from senior functional managers, (6) PMO creating unnecessary overhead, and (7) PMO trying to micromanage projects. This research aims to provide a rigorous and comprehensive list of challenges involved in implementing a PMO for IT projects in an organization.

The lack of empirical research on the challenges of implementing a PMO, and the fact that there is no uniform recipe for success (Santosus, 2003), suggests that there is a need for research in this area. Moreover, there is no clear understanding of the actions that organizations can take to overcome these challenges, and of the role of PMO structures, metrics, and tools in these implementations. The first objective of this research was to move beyond anecdotal data, and to conduct a scientifically rigorous investigation that would allow us to identify and rank order the critical challenges associated with implementing a PMO. The second objective of this research was to provide insights into strategies employed to overcome the top-ranked challenges. The third objective was to explore the role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools in implementing a PMO. PMO structure relates to the configuration (PMO-light or PMO-heavy) preferred in PMO implementations. PMO metrics refers to the measures that organizations use during a PMO implementation to assess its success and organizational impact. PMO tools refer to the software applications used for project portfolio management. Accordingly, we sought to address four basic research questions:

RQ1 What are the challenges in implementing a PMO in an IT organization?

RQ2 What is the relative importance of these challenges?

RQ3 How can organizations overcome the top challenges?

RQ4 What is the role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools in a PMO implementation?

In order to address these research questions, we organized a panel of 22 IT project managers with experience of implementing PMOs, and conducted a Delphi study. The study contributes valuable insights for both research and practice as it represents the first systematic attempt to identify and rank PMO implementation challenges, and to explore ways to overcome some of the top challenges.

## Research approach

We selected the Delphi method as our primary research approach as it was particularly well suited for addressing our first-two research questions, RQ1 and RQ2. We used semi-structured interviews with selected Delphi panelists to address research questions RQ3 and RQ4.

The Delphi method was developed by researchers at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s with the objective of defining important issues and reaching consensus among a panel of experts through an iterative process of controlled feedback (Dalkey & Helmer, 1963). In recent years, researchers have extensively used the Delphi method in IS research (e.g.. Brancheau & Wetherbe.

1987; Brancheau et al., 1996; Dekleva & Zupancic, 1996; Schmidt, 1997; Chang & Gable, 2000; Schmidt et al., 2001; Holsapple & Joshi, 2002). The Delphi method is recommended when ‘the problem does not lend itself to precise analytical techniques but can benefit from subjective judgments on a collective basis’ (Linstone & Turoff, 1975). Since our study focuses on understanding the challenges faced by IT project managers in implementing a PMO, the Delphi method was an appropriate choice because it allowed us to gain insights from the collective experience and understanding of our expert panel. Delphi panel size requirements are modest (Paliwoda, 1983; Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004) and panels can vary from small to large (Linstone & Turoff, 1975). Another advantage of the Delphi method is that it allows follow-up interviews with panel members for deeper understanding of the fundamental research questions (Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004).

We followed the Delphi procedure outlined by Schmidt (1997) to brainstorm, select, and rank the challenges to implementing a PMO in IT projects. This Delphi procedure uses a structured, anonymous, multi-pass process to establish group consensus. Each panelist is asked to independently record what s/he thinks is important in dealing with the given problem. The researcher collects and aggregates the individual responses from the panel, and removes the duplicate items. The resulting refined list of items is collectively exhaustive of all panelists’ responses. The researcher presents this list back to the panelists – requesting that they validate the list, and then narrow it down by selecting the items they consider most important. In the next step, the panelists rank the narrowed list of items. The researcher collates the individual item rankings from each panelist, and computes an overall rank for each item. Next, the researcher presents this ranked list to panelists, inviting them to review their earlier ranking (of each item) while considering the aggregated group response. This ranking continues until the panel reaches a consensus (as determined by Kendall’s coefficient of concordance, W). Figure 2 shows the Delphi procedure used in this study. Rowe & Wright (1999) have suggested a four-fold criteria of a Delphi study: anonymity of panelists, iteration, controlled feedback, and statistical aggregation of group response. This study meets all these criteria.

## Panel selection

Our Delphi panel included 22 experienced IT project managers. As the primary aim of our research was to identify and prioritize the challenges of implementing a PMO, experience with at least one PMO implementation (either as an employee or as a consultant) was required in order to serve on the Delphi panel. Using this predetermined criterion to select the panel of domain experts (Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004), the initial set of IT project managers with PMO experience was recruited through the researchers’ personal contacts. After the initial group agreed to participate. their help was sought to recruit additional panelists who also met the criteria (of experience with at least one PMO implementation) using the snowball sampling technique (Lincoln & Guba, 1985, p. 233; Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 28; Trochim, 2001, p. 58). IT project managers with experience of managing PMO implementations were difficult to find. Therefore, snowball sampling proved to be particularly useful in expanding our expert panel by putting us in touch with experienced professionals who might otherwise be inaccessible. For the interview phase, we asked all panelists if they would like to participate. Six of them agreed, and we conducted follow-up interviews with these panelists.

To begin the process, we asked each of the 22 panelists to provide six or more items that s/he considered major challenges to implementing a PMO. Our objective in seeking six items from each panelist in the brainstorming phase was to ensure that we achieved reasonable coverage of the domain without making the process too timeconsuming for the panelists. This was an open-ended solicitation of ideas (Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004), and therefore, the items did not have to be in any particular order of importance. We also asked the panelists to provide a brief description of each item. These descriptions helped us to (1) understand the various challenges, (2) remove duplicates by consolidating items with the same underlying meaning, and (3) categorize the challenges into meta-categories in a subsequent phase of the Delphi process. By the end of Phase I, 20 panel members had provided a list of 88 PMO implementation challenges. Each panelist provided between three and nine PMO implementation challenges. Two panelists did not participate in the brainstorming phase, but participated in the subsequent selection and ranking phases. We believed that their absence in the brainstorming phase would not make a material difference in later stages as we already had a large pool of challenges. Hence, we retained both these panelists in the Delphi panel.

Two researchers worked together to consolidate and refine this list by removing duplicates, grouping similar items together, and sharpening some item descriptions to improve clarity. This process yielded a consolidated list of 34 challenges, grouped into 10 conceptual meta-categories. The third researcher reviewed the consolidated list, independently verified that the panelists’ descriptions of challenges were accurately reflected, and further refined the list. This iterative process of refinement (by eliminating duplicates and grouping similar items into meta-categories) continued until all three researchers arrived at a consensus. Then, following Schmidt (1997), we circulated the consolidated list to all respondents, sought their feedback, and revised our mapping of the challenges accordingly.

In Phase II, we narrowed the consolidated list into a more manageable set for the ranking phase. Following Schmidt’s (1997) suggestion, we presented the panel with a randomized list of the 34 challenges from Phase I, and asked each panelist to select (not rank) his/her top-20 challenges to implementing a PMO (i.e., those challenges that they considered to be the most important). All 22 panelists responded in this selection phase. We then reviewed this selection of top-20 challenges from each respondent and retained only those challenges selected by an overwhelming majority of the panel. We chose a cut-off value of 70%, as this gave us our target range of 12–15 items for the subsequent ranking phase. Using this process, the initial list of 34 challenges became a more manageable, reduced list of 13 items at the end of Phase II.

Phase III involved ranking the 13 top challenges from Phase II. We asked each panelist to review the list and rank the items in order of priority (i.e., rank the challenge that they consider the most important as first, and so on). We also provided controlled feedback in the form of the percentage of panelists that had selected a given challenge to be among their top-20 challenges in Phase II. As part of the ranking exercise, we also asked the panelists to explain the rationale for selecting their topranked challenge. Following Schmidt (1997), we used Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) to measure the degree of consensus among the panelists. The values of W range from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating no consensus, and 1 indicating perfect consensus. When Kendall’s W is greater than 0.70, it signifies strong consensus; when the value is between 0.50 and 0.70, it signifies moderate consensus; and if the value is less than 0.50, it signifies that there is little consensus among the panel members (Schmidt, 1997). Seventeen panelists participated in the first round of ranking in Phase III, yielding a Kendall’s W of 0.28, which suggested a weak level of consensus among the panelists.

Following Schmidt’s (1997) recommendation, we decided to continue the ranking process until either: (1) the coefficient of concordance indicated good consensus, or (2) the level of consensus for the panel leveled-off in two successive rounds. Therefore. we conducted a second round of ranking in Phase III where we once again asked the panelists to review and rank-order the list of challenges. This time, we provided the following information to each panelist as controlled feedback: (1) the average rank of each PMO challenge, (2) the ranking by that panelist for each challenge in the first round of ranking, and (3) the panelist’s deviation from the group’s mean ranking for each challenge. As a second form of feedback, we also provided the panelists with a summarized list of comments collected in the first round, explaining why the panelists considered a particular challenge as their top-ranked challenge. We believed that this additional information would help the panel members to consider their own ranking in light of the group’s ranking and give them an opportunity to adjust their ranking where it made sense to do so. Twenty panelists participated in this second round of ranking, yielding a Kendall’s W of 0.54, which suggested that a moderate level of consensus had been reached and that we could have a reasonable degree of confidence in the rankings.

Following Kasi et al. (2008), we also calculated the standard deviations associated with the mean rankings in Round 2 to determine if there were any items that stood out as being particularly controversial (and therefore weighing down the concordance). All the standard deviations ranged between 1.30 and 4.22. This suggests that the moderate level of consensus was not the result of a few items that exhibited greater variance in terms of their ranking. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we dropped each of the 13 challenges, one at a time, and recomputed Kendall’s W. The consensus levels did not change significantly (W ranged between 0.41 and 0.56).

At this stage, we discussed whether we should conduct another round of ranking to obtain a greater level of panel consensus. In making such decisions, the trade-off between feasibility (the indulgence of the panelists, the researcher’s resources, and the additional time required), and potential gain to be achieved must be considered (Nelms & Porter, 1985; Schmidt, 1997). We felt that some fatigue had set in among our panel members (reflected in the increased number of reminders required to get them to respond). Since Round 2 had met one of the stopping rules suggested by Schmidt (1997), we decided against a third round of ranking.

In order to address the research questions RQ3 and RQ4, we conducted telephone interviews with six of the panel members who agreed to participate. The purpose of these semi-structured interviews was to understand how some of the organizations successfully addressed the challenges to PMO implementation. Specifically we asked our panelists to elaborate on (1) how the top-ranked challenges were overcome, (2) the role of PMO structure (PMO-heavy vs PMO-light), (3) whether any metrics were used to measure the success of the PMO implementations, (4) what role, if any, was played by PMO tools in these implementations. We conducted the interviews between May and June 2007, and each interview lasted between 30 and 45 m. A semi-structured interview protocol (Appendix C) guided the interview process. Two researchers were present for each of these interviews. The researchers took notes, and also recorded and transcribed the interviews for subsequent analysis.

## Results

In this section, we present the results of our study, organized according to each of the four underlying research questions.

RQ1: Identification of PMO implementation challenges Phase I of the study resulted in a list of 34 unique PMO implementation challenges, which we grouped into 10 conceptual meta-categories. These meta-categories (identified by the letters A through J) and their associated PMO implementation challenges (identified with alphanumeric character combinations A1, B1, etc.) are shown in Appendix A. The meta-categories and challenges (Appendix A) do not represent any particular order of importance.

## RQ2: Relative importance of PMO implementation challenges

As described earlier, in Phase II of the study, the panelists narrowed the list of 34 challenges to retain only the most important challenges. This allowed us to narrow the list to a manageable number of 13 challenges that could be rank ordered in Phase III of the study. Table 2 shows the results of these two phases of the study. We used the average rank given to each challenge in Round 2 of Phase III to determine the final ranking of the 13 PMO implementation challenges (shown in the right-most column of Table 2). We discuss the top-three challenges in more detail below.

Rigid corporate culture and failure to manage organizational resistance to change According to our panel rankings, organizational culture and resistance to change is the most important challenge that managers need to consider. As one panelist said, ‘Culture here represents probably about 95% of consideration of what decisions are taken and how we move forward’. Another panelist also discussed rigid culture and resistance to change in their organization, and noted, ‘It is very difficult to change the way people are used to doing things in an organization [and] this places an almost insurmountable road block to establishing an effective PMO’. As these comments suggest, it is critical for organizations to manage resistance to change. Successfully implementing a PMO often requires a change in mindset and a shift towards a more project-centered organization. Interestingly, the resistance to change may not be reflected consistently across the entire organization, and may manifest itself only in certain groups within the organization, as the following comment suggests, ‘A system which one group considers ‘‘golden’’ is considered ‘‘crap’’ by another’.

Lack of experienced PMs and PMO leadership Many of the current PMO initiatives do not have experienced PMs who understand the challenges of a PMO implementation and who are aware of the political terrain of the organization. Comments from our panelists suggest that organizations do not always staff the PMO with the most experienced project managers or with people who have played lead roles in large PMO implementations. The qualities that make for an effective PMO director may be quite different from the qualities that make someone an excellent PM. As one panelist noted, PMs are ‘better at coordinating and influencing the work within the team, but much less effective in managing the client’s requirements and expectations at a strategic level’. Another panelist noted, ‘Many project managers do not understand the scope of work involved in a PMO implementation, and fail to correctly estimate the resources required for the PMO implementation, or to analyze the impact of any scope change on the budget and schedule of the implementation. They fail to understand project risk and, consequently, fail to put in place a risk mitigation plan’. These create problems in effective implementation of the PMO. One panelist highlighted the need for PMO-related experience in very clear terms, ‘The PMO is an office which can be stronger with real experienced staff’.

Lack of appropriate change management strategy Any PMO implementation involves managing all aspects of change – including people, process, and structure issues. One of the most challenging aspects of establishing a PMO is to evolve and implement a clear change management strategy that looks beyond the immediate needs of the PMO project. As one panelist commented, ‘Most [PMO] projects focus strictly on the goal and the means without addressing the related impacts which have long lasting consequences and unseen and untracked additions to the scope and cost of projects. Serious consideration and planning of organizational development impacts, and observation of indirect effects, need to take place in order to account for, and mitigate these as necessary’. This commitment to an appropriate change management strategy needs to occur at all levels in the organization – operational, as well as strategic.

## RQ3: Overcoming the top-ranked challenges to PMO implementation

As indicated earlier, following the Delphi ranking exercise, we conducted telephone interviews with selected panelists to determine ways in which their organizations tried to overcome the top-ranked challenges to implementing a PMO. We focused only on the top-three challenges in order to keep the interview process manageable, and to allow the panelists to elaborate how their organization succeeded in overcoming those challenges.

Overcoming rigid corporate culture and resistance to change The panelists offered several suggestions for overcoming resistance to change. The first was to make sure there is a strong PMO champion. As one panelist put it, ‘The only real way to overcome that resistance is to have a strong champion up there who really evangelizes the value of the PMO’. Another panelist mentioned the need for a ‘high level management mandate’ to overcome organizational resistance to change. Another strategy for dealing with a rigid corporate culture and overcoming resistance to change is to start small and to demonstrate the value of the PMO with some early successes. As one panelist explained, ‘On most successful [PMO] implementations we start small with pilots or pathfinders where you can get people excited about the value and personal benefit from following these processes. And then they become additional champions for your organization to help you with rest of the implementation’. A third strategy is to identify the opinion leaders in an organization and to assess their attitudes towards the PMO concept. Those who are favorably inclined towards PMO could help in promoting the PMO implementation. This strategy also involves isolating the ‘naysayers’, and addressing their concerns on an individual basis. One panelist, a PMO consultant with experience of multiple implementations, explained in detail how this approach has worked:

You y start by mapping the organizational culture. In every organization, there are two kinds of people. One group, of what I call the real leaders of the organization, who are not necessarily the leaders because of their job description. They are the ones [who], when [any] change comes sweeping through, everybody looks up to and says, ‘If they like it, I’ll like it, if they do not like it; I’m not going to like it either.’ What I found is that I can take the people who are being positive into a big conference room, and bring them into the loop early. And [then] they go out and help me y The other guys who are negative most of the time ... You can’t stop them from not liking the change, but you can start working with them to explain why this [PMO implementation] is a good change and ask them to at least give it a chance.

Overcoming lack of experienced project managers and PMO leadership The panelists suggested two approaches to overcome the challenge of lack of experienced PMs and PMO leaders. The first approach is to bring in a credible program manager to oversee the PMO implementation. As one panelist explained, ‘[You need] a program manager who is trusted and accepted by the key stakeholders y [someone who can] understand the culture and power relationships within the client organization, and who can successfully direct the PMO implementation’. Another panelist shared his experience of how their organization found a PMO program manager ‘who was trusted and accepted by the key stakeholders’. This organization chose a program manager who had prior employment with that organization. As this panelist suggested, it was a useful approach, as the prior relationship helped the program manager to understand the culture and power relationships within the client organization, and successfully direct the PMO implementation. Another approach is to bring the most talented and experienced PMs into the PMO implementation process. There is simply no substitute for bringing on board an experienced team of PMs, as one panelist observed:

What works, in my experience, is to structure the PMO team in such a way that people who are most experienced in project management have operational roles and responsibilities that require these skills on a day-to-day basis. In situations requiring a lot of interaction with various stakeholders, one of the most experienced persons – not necessarily well versed in PMO implementations, but who has been around in organization for many years – should take on the role of program management to guide the program in consultation with the stakeholder.

Overcoming lack of appropriate change management strategy Organizations need to tailor their change management strategy according to the situation at hand. PMO implementations often get into trouble when there is a lack of understanding and participation by some stakeholders. Frequently, some of these stakeholders view the PMO as an extra layer of bureaucracy that does not add value, and end up disliking this more disciplined approach to project management. One panelist recommended flexibility: ‘One size didn’t fit all very well, and people were just choosing not to use any of it y people were facing mind-numbing bureaucracy. [You] have to engage the people that are involved [and] get their buy-in through participation’. Some of the other panelists we interviewed felt that it was important to move towards some form of process standardization before implementing the PMO. The following remark was typical:

We first pushed towards CMM in some of the groups where we wanted to rollout the PMO first. This involved designing a repeatable process – like scheduled releases in the case of one of the IT management groups – before implementing the PMO. We looked at their workflow and tried to make incremental changes in definable segments of work. We designed, what we called, an agile process, in which we took small chunks of critical tasks, and used CMM methodologies to improve them. You need a consistent process before you start automating it. Once the processes were somewhat standardized, we brought in the PMO.

## RQ4: Role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools

As part of our interview protocol (refer Appendix C), we also explored the role of PMO structure, metrics, and tools. Regarding PMO structure, we found that PMO-light implementations were significantly easier to manage than PMO-heavy implementations. Considering the fact that rigid corporate culture and organizational resistance to change was our top challenge, it was not surprising that our panelists shared the view that PMO-light implementations face lower resistance as they involve a lesser degree of change in comparison to PMO-heavy implementations. Nevertheless, as one of our panelists remarked, even with PMO-light implementations, ‘you can still run into the problem of managing bureaucracy’. One of our panelists shared his experience thus:

We have had both PMO-light and PMO-heavy structure in different implementation projects. We noticed predominantly greater success in PMO-light projects – in which the assistance to various project teams from the PMO group was ‘voluntary.’ PMO-heavy implementations needed more convincing of all the stakeholders involved, and generally ran into more problems.

Interviews with panelists indicated that there were no formal metrics in place to assess PMOs. Instead, there was considerable reliance on subjective indicators of success. As one panelist put it, ‘Overall, hard metrics were less important, and soft metrics were given more importance’. Another panelist indicated, ‘The only metrics we have is the feedback from managers about their pain points today, as compared to the pain points before the PMO implementation’. One approach to evaluating the impact of a PMO was to canvass different stakeholders – including senior management, PMs, and project teams – in order to gauge the impact of the PMO and their satisfaction with it. PMO managers can seek this information through both formal and informal feedback, using questions like ‘Are the projects implemented better now, than a year ago? Is planning better? Is the communication better now when a project is kicked off?’ Another panelist, a PMO program manager, had a similar approach in assessing the feedback from the perspective of senior management:

You look back upstairs at the people in the executive suite y are they feeling more empowered or less empowered by the kind of reports that they are getting from the PMO? Are they more actively engaged in capturing value, or benefit from the project? Has the level of sponsorship – the level of active engagement in championing activities, team building, participating in actual execution or delivery of projects – gone up? If the executives are becoming more involved and more engaged, then you know that the PMO is effective. If you see them disengaged from the project team, then you know that the PMO has become just another piece of cartilage in between the bones.

Another panelist’s comment illustrates the use of a similar approach in seeking informal feedback from the project teams, ‘We would ask if they [project teams] were satisfied. We would seek feedback from the project teams [during and after the PMO implementation], and this feedback, if positive, told us that the PMO implementation was on track’. These subjective metrics were not only useful in assessing the value of a PMO, but were also helpful in establishing the credibility of the PMO.

Our panelists indicated that PMO tools (such as sophisticated project management software and portfolio management applications), although useful, were difficult to implement unless a project management culture has previously been established within an organization and processes have been defined. As one panelist observed, ‘Basically when they see the requirements for the tool implementation, a lot of organizations panic because they know they are not mature enough in their processes to actually get any value-add out of that implementation. They run away’. Another reason for not rushing to buy PMO tools is that they vary greatly in terms of functionality and, until the structure of the PMO is decided, there is a risk of buying into a toolset that is a poor fit for the type of PMO envisioned. The organizations must address the challenges to PMO implementation independent of the tool, and should be cognizant of this before they shop for PMO tools in the marketplace.

## Discussion

As noted earlier, organizations can pursue several approaches to improve project management practices. Many organizations are using PMOs to manage multiple IT projects, and to align them with the organization’s strategy and goals (Thiry & Deguire, 2007). While PMOs can be beneficial, evidence suggests that many organizations struggle with PMO implementation. Based on the results of this study, we offer the following key findings:

(a) Resistance to change represents the chief obstacle to implementing a PMO. A conducive corporate culture is critical to implementing a PMO (Santosus, 2003; Wren, 2005) as it can reduce the organizational resistance to change (Rad & Levin, 2002). Our panelists reported that to overcome this challenge, it helps to have a strong PMO champion who can identify, and influence, opinion leaders within the organization.

(b) Experienced project management leadership plays a key role in PMO implementation. The program managers often need to work in complex, multistakeholder projects. In such situations, lessons learned and project experience from prior engagements is often critical (Dinsmore, 2005, p. 110). As our panelists suggested, an understanding of the corporate culture and power relationships within the client organization is also helpful.

(c) Organizations need to employ an appropriate change management strategy. Norrie (2008) refers to the process of assessing the extent of change due to PMO implementation, and managing it, as ‘change dynamics’. There cannot be a one-size-fits-all change dynamics strategy: each organization must adopt a flexible strategy that fits the organizational needs. For example, some of our panelists reported that their organizations instituted process standardization prior to PMO implementation. In other cases, however, process standardization followed the PMO implementation.

(d) Some organizations have favored the PMO-light model, while others have favored the PMO-heavy model. The panelists reported lower resistance to change in the case of PMO-light, which is understandable as this configuration has only a supporting role of PMO, with limited direct responsibility for project execution. The downside of this approach, however, is that the PMO-light model almost certainly has less influence on project execution.

(e) One of the more surprising findings emerging from our study was the heavy reliance on ‘soft’ metrics to assess the impact of PMOs. These metrics reveal stakeholder feelings towards a project, and are useful qualitative indicators that should be tracked over time (Reuvid, 2005). However, organizations must complement these soft metrics with ‘hard’ statistics to evaluate the impact of PMOs on project and organizational performance. None of the panelists reported the use of hard metrics.

(f) Finally, organizations need to evaluate the role of PMO tools (such as project management software and portfolio management applications). Although some organizations rushed to acquire the most potent PMO tools available in the market, such an approach may be counterproductive if the organization lacks the project management maturity needed to utilize the tools.

The results of our study also offer two tools for practice that managers may find useful. First, the full list of 34 PMO challenges identified by our expert panel (Appendix A) can serve as a comprehensive checklist of challenges that IT project managers can use to prepare themselves before they undertake a PMO implementation in their organization. This identification of challenges is critical to designing strategies to overcome them. There is, however, a cost associated with relying on a list that contains 34 discrete challenges. Such a list may prove too cumbersome to use in practice. The ranked list of 13 challenges provides a more compact list that contains only the most important challenges (as determined by more than a two-thirds majority of our panelists). Managers can use the rankings to direct their limited resources towards addressing the most critical PMO implementation challenges. The results of the interviews with selected panelists offer the second tool for practice – actionable advice on how to overcome the top-ranked PMO implementation challenges (Table 3).

Finally, our study provides three practical implications regarding PMO structure, metrics, and PMO tools. First, we identify a weak spot in many PMO implementations – the failure to obtain hard metrics on the impact of the investment. Most IT project managers appear to be relying heavily on soft metrics to assess the impact of the PMO. To ensure the continued investment and senior leadership’s support to the PMO, managers should consider collecting baseline data on the status of their project portfolio before the PMO, as well as performance data on all projects carried out after the PMO is implemented. By collecting hard data on cost, schedule, functionality (in terms of meeting customer requirements), and quality of project deliverables, managers will be in a better position to justify the cost associated with maintaining a PMO. Second, this study suggests that IT project managers and senior leadership must carefully consider the desired PMO structure – whether to choose a PMO-heavy or a PMO-light configuration. Such an analysis must take into account the culture of the organization, and its ability to move towards more disciplined project management structures and processes. On a speculative note, we believe that organizations are more likely to succeed if they implement a PMO-light structure to start with, and subsequently move towards a PMO-heavy structure after realizing the initial value and benefits of PMO-light structure. Third, this study suggests that managers should avoid the temptation of taking a ‘tools-first’ approach in driving the PMO implementation forward.

## Limitations and directions for future research

Before turning to directions for future research, it is appropriate to point out the limitations associated with the present study. First, we base our results on a limited number of subjects. Our study had 22 panelists, a number that is consistent with other recent IS-related Delphi studies (refer Table 4). It is important to note that the Delphi methodology does not require the panel to be a representative sample in a statistical sense (Powell, 2003). Nevertheless, given the nature and size of our sample, one must be cautious in generalizing. Having said this, the sample is relatively diverse in terms of IT project management and PMO implementation experience (see demographic data for panelists in Table 1).

A second limitation relates to the moderate level of consensus among the panelists after Round 2 of ranking in Phase III, as suggested by Kendall’s W of 0.54. This level of consensus may reflect the diverse organizational settings and the nature of the PMO implementations that each panelist represented. While another round of ranking might have resulted in a greater level of consensus, we felt that some degree of panel fatigue had set in. During the second ranking round, the researchers had to send increased number of email reminders and make multiple phone calls to coax the panelists to continue their participation. Rather than risk a drop-off in participation (Mursu et al., 2003) we elected to stop at this point, having already reached a moderate level of consensus (Schmidt, 1997). Several researchers have halted Delphi studies when their panel reached a similar level of consensus (Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987; Couger, 1988; Doke & Swanson, 1995; Keil et al., 2002; Kasi et al., 2008). The Kendall’s W value that we obtained after two rounds of ranking is comparable to the values reported after three rounds of ranking in other Delphi studies, as shown in Table 4.

Future research should focus on the key PMO implementation challenges identified in Table 2. While we have gathered some input on how organizations can overcome the top-three challenges, future research should investigate and identify strategies and tactics for overcoming the other ten challenges. Future research should also investigate both successful and unsuccessful PMO implementations through in-depth case studies in order to understand, from a process perspective, how and why these efforts succeed in some instances and fail in others. Further, researchers could examine PMO implementations in various industries to understand domainspecific differences in terms of the challenges, and the various approaches employed by organizations to overcome them. For instance, a newspaper organization may respond differently to PMO implementation challenges than a financial services firm or an IT services provider. We also suggest further research to gather ‘hard’ statistics on the impact of PMOs on project and organizational performance. This would complement the ‘soft’ metrics that most organizations use to evaluate the PMO. Another area of future research could include investigating the relative costs and benefits of PMO-heavy vs PMOlight configurations.

This study also offers guidance to future IS researchers on conducting a Delphi study. We want to emphasize choosing a topic that will resonate with expert panelists, as it is critical to capture and maintain their interest throughout the Delphi process. We suggest sending a summary or an extended abstract of the study to the panel members before they sign up for the study – this allows them an opportunity to assess their interest in the subject. Sending frequent updates to the panelists about the progress of the study, and using multiple means of communication (mail, email, phone, and personal contact, if possible) also help to keep the panelists engaged with the Delphi process. Future researchers also need to manage the duration of the Delphi process by keeping it short, as it becomes increasingly difficult to sustain interest among the panelists when the process stretches too long.

## Conclusions

The growing popularity of PMOs is a relatively recent phenomenon that represents a significant step in the evolution of IT project management (Rad & Raghavan, 2000; Aubry et al., 2007; Letavec, 2006; Aubry et al., 2008; Hobbs et al., 2008). However, there has been little or no research to guide researchers and practitioners in identifying and overcoming the challenges of implementing a PMO. The potential of the PMO remains unfulfilled, in large part, because these initiatives have proven to be more challenging to implement than many believed they would be. This study represents the first rigorous attempt to identify PMO implementation challenges. It identifies a comprehensive list of 34 challenges that organizations face during PMO implementation, ranks a list of 13 challenges that our panelists considered most important, and presents some of the actions that organizations can take to overcome the top-ranked challenges. Thus, the study contributes to the broader body of literature on IT project governance by highlighting the challenges involved in optimizing project management and establishing repeatable processes and procedures (Thiry & Deguire, 2007).

The top-three challenges identified were (1) rigid corporate culture and failure to manage organizational resistance to change, (2) lack of experienced PMs and PMO leadership, and (3) lack of appropriate change management strategy. In order to overcome these challenges, organizations need to have a strong PMO champion, start small and demonstrate the value of the PMO, obtain support from opinion leaders, hire an experienced program manager who understands the organization, bring he most talented PMs into the PMO implementation team, adopt a flexible change management strategy, and standardize PM processes prior to PMO implementation. Managers should carefully consider the tradeoffs between ‘light’ vs ‘heavy’ PMO structures, recognizing that while ‘light’ PMOs may not have the same impact on the organization, they have a greater likelihood of being implemented successfully. Managers should consider obtaining baseline data on project performance so that they can put objective measures in place to assess the impact of the PMO. Finally, managers should avoid the temptation to embrace PMO tools until a project management culture has been established.

## Appendix C

Semi-structured interview protocol

## (1) Overcoming the challenges to PMO

\- How did your organization respond to the top-three challenges to PMO implementation? What worked, or did not work?

## (2) PMO structure

\- What was the nature of PMO structure in your organization (PMO-light/PMO-heavy/any other)?

\- Do you think it was an appropriate governance structure? In hindsight, would you recommend any changes to this structure? Is there a governance structure that is more likely to be successful, and where would that be on the PMO-light, PMO-heavy scale?

## (3) Metrics for PMO evaluation

\- Has your organization successfully implemented a PMO? Did the organization meet the original objectives of establishing the PMO?

\- Did the implementation of PMO have measurable impacts in terms of project success? How did you measure the impact? What metrics did you use?

## (4) PMO tools

\- What project management software or portfolio management applications did you use for PMO implementation?

How satisfied are you with those applications in terms of ease of use, functionality, and customizability?

\- Do you consider the PMO tools to be useful devices in pushing change related to PMO implementation?

\- If an organization wants to maximize its chances of success in a PMO implementation, is it better to standardize its processes first before buying one of these tool sets, or after procuring them?
