# An application of the learning curve and the nonconstant-growth dividend model: IT investment valuations at IntelR Corporation B

Shana Dardan<sup>a,b,\*</sup>, Doug Busch<sup>c</sup>, David Sward<sup>a,d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Intel Corporation, United States

<sup>b</sup>California State University at Sacramento, United States

<sup>c</sup>VP of IT and CIO, Intel Corporation, United States

<sup>d</sup>Arizona State University East, United States

Available online 8 December 2004

## Abstract

Technology benefits last years longer than the standard ROI valuation analysis but are rarely enumerated. In this paper, we utilize a nonconstant dividend growth model to <sup>b</sup>capture<sup>Q</sup> lasting marginal productivity gained through the <sup>b</sup>reinvestment<sup>Q</sup> of labor capital rather than the standard the one-time gain of reducing the labor force to realize labor productivity gains. This innovative methodology for capturing the productivity value of maintained employees enables the valuation of continuing marginal productivity gains and the management of workload for the affected employees at Intel. This methodology is applied to the valuation of a standard operating system and hardware upgrade. © 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Information technology; Valuation; Finance; Productivity

## 1. Introduction

As IT investment increases and other areas compete for investments, measuring payoff from IT investments is no longer just an exercise. It is demanded by stakeholders and expected by senior managers. . .— Rajiv Kohli and Susan A. Sherer, [17].

Technology valuation is an increasingly important corporate question. Intel spends hundreds of millions of dollars on technology investments annually. In a retracted economy, these expenditures are not to be taken lightly.

The purpose of this work is to capture the financial value of productivity gains when the employee group is not going to be reduced. Many companies have gone through recent labor reductions and are operating with a core group of personnel. The question becomes, if a company is unwilling to reduce the work force to realize the productivity gains achieved by an IT investment, does that mean that the investment has a lessened or no value? We must assume that the value is more intrinsic than this, and that the resulting productivity gain is realized through the capability of the employees to yield higher levels of output. Thus, we seek to enumerate that value in this research.

In this paper, we will describe Intel’s work to provide and apply a methodology for enumerating the technology’s lifetime marginal productivity benefit within a corporate environment to specifically address the question of valuing productivity gains in an environment where the labor force will remain constant. Specifically, we apply the nonconstantgrowth dividend model to capture the marginal productivity benefits both during the learning of the technology and, once the technology has been adopted, to an operating system and hardware upgrade at Intel. We embed the learning curve function within the nonconstant dividend growth model to adjust the productivity rate for both learning effects.

The technology investment that is being studied is a standard operating system and hardware upgrade. Intel uses an approximately 3- to 4-year PC refresh cycle, so upgrades are a fairly regular occurrence. The pitfalls of cost and overexuberance of technology investment, while a controversial topic in nature [8,5,23], do warrant consideration by the prudent manager. Thus, even such an obvious investment can and should warrant a standard financial analysis.

We start the analysis by conducting a time and motion experiment to study the marginal change in short-term labor productivity [9]. This methodology, although infrequently used in IS literature, is foundational in the operations field and was created by the <sup>b</sup>father of the scientific management,<sup>Q</sup> F.W. Taylor (1856–1917) [9]. As is standard in both finance and corporate practice, we use a multiple of productivity gains and the average after-tax cash burden rate per employee to determine the cash flow value gain in each time period [11,13,3]. We will then suggest a methodology for determining the learning impacts to the productivity rate following Refs. [12,22] and [25]. Due to the estimated cost of running a longitudinal study to determine the actual learning rate, we did not study Intel learning rate impacts. We assumed for the purposes of this valuation application that the learning rate was zero. Literature suggests that even a small technology change will yield a knowledge increase, so we expect that this limitation will bias our results downward.

This marginal productivity impact is then captured in the nonconstant-growth dividend model, enabling us to enumerate both the short-term high-growth productivity impacts and the long-term constant productivity value.

## 2. Background on productivity and learning

The impacts of labor productivity become increasingly important during economic downturns, as organizations focus their attentions on cost reduction or avoidance. While the actual productivity impacts of technology are still debated [6], recent work done by several senior economists at the Federal Reserve Board suggests that labor productivity is increasing over time, and the lack of productivity growth in the past is due to the learning aspect of adopting a technology [19,24]. Economists suggest that while productivity is not initially affected positively by new technologies due to the learning aspect, the learning itself helps to drive future productivity growth [2,15]. This is because learning and knowledge gain enhance information capital, which ultimately improves productivity [15,22].

In addition, research conducted by noted economists has shown that the effects of learning by doing have a larger impact than simple cost reductions. This learning aspect can yield a series of incremental innovations at the process level and which Rosenburg (1982) [21] suggests will yield both long-term productivity and influence change [10].

According to Hitt et al. [14], <sup>b</sup>The one true source of lasting competitive advantage is knowledge.<sup>Q</sup> Edgington and Chen [12] have outlined learning curve methodologies to estimate the economic value derived from the addition of new knowledge, and new research on strategy suggests that their organizations can realize significant gains when they increase the knowledge base of the employee group. By offering technology that has learning value or otherwise enables an environment of knowledge creation, an employee can increase his or her competence.

## 3. Methodology

3.1. Learning curve and nonconstant-growth dividend models

The introduction of a new technology or even an incremental technological change requires that workers learn how to use the new system(s). To enumerate the impacts of learning on the productivity enhancement of a new technology, we implement a <sup>b</sup>learning by doing<sup>Q</sup> curve [12,22]. While learning and the learning curve are only one aspect of productivity, Malbera [18] states that learning has been shown to increase productivity growth on both the macro and the industry level.

We chose the time constant learning curve because of its robustness and wide applicability [22]. As with similar models, it follows an exponential learning path [12,25].

The model is defined as

$$
Y _ {m} = Y _ {c} + Y _ {f} \left(1 - e ^ {- t / \tau}\right)\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { \mathrm { m } }$ is the predicted output rate, $Y _ { \mathrm { f } }$ is the gain in performance due to learning, s is a model time constant, and t is time. $Y _ { \mathrm { c } }$ is defined as the initial productivity rate and is designated by Towill [22] as occurring when the consecutive numbers of tasks can be performed without committing errors or <sup>b</sup>fumbling.<sup>Q</sup>

The time constant learning curve first displays a period of nonlinear growth, then approach a constant level of productivity enhancing performance [22]. This behavior is shown in Fig. 1, where $Y _ { \mathrm { c } } { + } Y _ { \mathrm { f } }$ is the level of asymptotic performance that is achieved once the learning has taken place.

Since there are periods of both high nonlinear growth due to learning and a following ongoing period of constant productivity gain, we apply a nonconstant dividend growth model and methodology to the enumeration of discounted total marginal productivity benefits [20].

The nonconstant- and constant-growth dividend models are widely used financial tools, easily found in financial literature, such as the work by John Campbell, Andrew $\mathrm { L o } ,$ and A. Craig MacKinley [7]. Peter Boer, a professor of management and engineering at Yale University, makes a strong case for the constant dividend growth model, which he refers to as the Growth in Perpetuity model, for ongoing value in technology valuations. His argument follows the rationale that if an investment in a competitive environment is not yielding a growing benefit over time, then it will be liquidated.<sup>1</sup> Thai is, that any investment that does not yield continuing value would not be supported in a competitive environment.

This argument is convincing because no rational company would continue to invest in a technology (such as an e-mail system) if the benefits did not outweigh the costs. Furthermore, the argument is intuitively strengthened by the understanding that productivity gains continue to accrue with the use of the technology.

We apply the nonconstant-growth dividend model first by estimating the after-tax cash flow (and discounting them) during the high-growth period individually, then applying the constant dividend growth model once the constant-growth period, known as the horizon time, has been reached.<sup>2</sup> Thus, by definition, after the horizon point in time, the benefits per year will either steadily increase, decrease, or remain the same—they will not fluctuate. The summation of the cash flows from the highgrowth period and the constant-growth period by definition yield the total discounted benefits associated with the investment.<sup>3</sup>

The nonconstant dividend growth model summation of expected high-growth and constant-growth after-tax cash flow (expected total discounted benefits) is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {DB_ {t}} = E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {t + 1}}{1 + k} \right] + E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {t + 2}}{(1 + k) ^ {2}} \right] + \dots \\ \quad + E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {\mathrm{HT-1}}}{(1 + k) ^ {\mathrm{HT-1}}} \right] + \sum_ {\tau = \mathrm{HT}} ^ {\infty} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {\tau}}{(1 + k) ^ {\tau}} \right] \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Let $\mathrm { C F } _ { t }$ be the after-tax cash flow at time t, HT is the horizon point in time, k is the discount rate, and $E _ { t }$ is the expectation function at time t.

We rewrite the summation of the ongoing constant returns for ease of use,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {DB_ {t}} = E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {t + 1}}{1 + k} \right] + E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {t + 2}}{(1 + k) ^ {2}} \right] + \dots \\ \quad + E _ {t} \left[ \frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {\mathrm{HT-1}}}{(1 + k) ^ {\mathrm{HT-1}}} \right] + \left(\frac {\mathrm{CF} _ {\mathrm{HT}}}{(1 + k) ^ {\mathrm{HT}}}\right) * \left(\frac {x}{1 - x}\right) \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where

$$
x = \frac {(1 + g)}{(1 + k)}\tag{4}
$$

and $g$ is the constant percent rate of growth, while k is the discount rate or the cost of money. Following Boer [3], Brigham, Gapenski, and Ehrhardt [4], and standard financial use of this model, we apply the theoretical limit that $g { < } k$ . If this constraint was not enforced, the infinite summation above could not be algebraically resolved, as the resulting flow would grow without bounds as t increased. It should be noted at this point, that $k ,$ as a discount rate, reflects the inherent risk associated with the investment and will vary by company and by information technology within that company. The choice of k for this study was the standard used by Intel of 15%.

## 3.2. Data

Our study established user performance benchmarks by measuring performance on a defined set of tasks for five Intel user segments. Users performed these tasks on two Intel IT-standard desktop configurations. These were the upgrade from an Intel PentiumR III processor-based system with Microsoft Windows\* 2000 and Microsoft Office\* 2000 to an Intel PentiumR 4 processor-based system with Microsoft Windows\* XP and Microsoft Office\* XP for office employees. Data were gathered through a time and motion study to test the employee productivity impacts of the upgrade before learning took place.

The stratified random data sample consisted of 45 employees. It should be noted that while it is not expected that the population of Intel employees differs from the population of all employees, this study was stratified (per Intel Human Resource department specifications) to represent Intel’s population and thus may not be generalizable to other IT organizations.

Participants were chosen from the groups of project and/or programming and people management, technical support, administrative assistants, design engineering and programming, and general office (e.g., human resources, business analysts, and finance). Participants were not tested on their existing Microsoft Office\* skills. However, a prestudy survey of the participants yielded the finding that 75% of the participants considered themselves to be <sup>b</sup>experienced<sup>Q</sup> PC users, and 25% considered themselves to be <sup>b</sup>self-sufficient.<sup>Q</sup> Due to scope limitations, this study only focused on the time saved for 10 tasks, as listed in Table 1.

We made no attempt to separate the impact of the hardware upgrade from the impact of the software upgrade. Instead, we examined the productivity gains produced by the entire system bundle. Since we believe that most organizations tend to upgrade hardware and software together, we judged this method to be more indicative of actual IT practices. Furthermore, productivity impacts were studied only for those tasks supported by both <sup>b</sup>bundles.<sup>Q</sup> Any capability found on the new system that was not inherent to the old system bundle was not studied.

## 3.2.1. Task list

This study defined <sup>b</sup>user tasks<sup>Q</sup> as the broad set of users’ PC activities. Because more tasks can be performed than could be reasonably tested, we selected a sample of <sup>b</sup>typical<sup>Q</sup> tasks.

We sought to collect performance data and usage frequency estimates. Our decision to include a particular task was based on its estimated value in establishing user performance, the estimated frequency with which the task is performed at Intel, and the task’s ability to fill out the cross-section of task types we needed without duplicating activities. We also considered the capabilities of the two test bundles in task selection; the task had to be capable of being performed on each system bundle and should be executable in the usability laboratory.

## 3.2.2. Testing

We did not randomize the order of the presentation of each task to the users due to the progressive nature of the test scenarios. However, to control <sup>b</sup>learning effects<sup>Q</sup> within the experimental time, we varied the order of the test platforms; just as many participants started with the Pentium 4 processor-based system bundle as started with the Pentium III processor-based system bundle.

The test followed standard experimental protocol (e.g., see Refs. [1,9,16]). Before starting the test, we gave each participant an overview of the study and a participant packet. We allowed each participant to ask questions and complete the prestudy survey.

At the start of each task, the participants read the task’s scenario aloud to the test administrator and were allowed to ask clarifying questions. When ready, the participant started a timer program, which automatically minimized. At the completion of the task, the user stopped the timer and informed the test administrator that he or she had finished. Automating the timing of tasks addresses the inherent human bias in timing, which is a critique of the time and motion study [9]. The test administrator recorded the time and whether the task was completed successfully.

The participant then completed three survey questions to ascertain his or her perception of the daily frequency of use of the task and the bundle’s ease of use and responsiveness for that task. The ease-of-use and responsiveness surveys used a seven-point Likert scale (1=unsatisfied. . . 7=satisfied). Additionally, at three points during the test, familiarization tasks ensured that the participant was familiar with the features to be tested in an upcoming task.

At the end of each platform bundle test, the participants completed a System Usability Scale\* (<sup>n</sup> Digital Equipment, 1986) survey or <sup>b</sup>SUS.<sup>Q</sup> This survey instrument is a general purpose survey tool developed and tested by Digital Equipment in 1986 and was designed for use with any information system. This instrument yielded a subjective score of the bundle’s overall effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction. SUS scores range from 0 to 100, with a score of 100 indicating a highly usable platform.

## 4.1.1. Automated tests

Test results depended on two factors: a human performance component and a hardware/software performance component (user input time versus platform bundle performance). To help us distinguish between the two factors, we scripted the tasks performed by participants in the Human Factors Engineering laboratories. We ran the scripts on test machines using test automation software; each test was run 30 times per system bundle, and task times were collected by the test software. The scripting tool used was IBM’s Rational Visual Test\* 6.5 and Rational Visual Test Runtime Engine. The CPU load generated by the Rational Visual Test Runtime Engine is relatively the same on each machine, and the results were not adjusted for this load. This load is also relative to the CPU loads of any other agent service, such as ISS Apex Agent\*, WAAT\*, McAfee VirusScan\*, etc. We did not adjust for these types of services either, as they also should not skew the results.

## 4.3. Lifetime value: nonconstant-growth dividend

We complete our analysis by first evaluating the productivity value during the growth period, which are months 1–12, and during the constant-growth period, which are the following months and years. This was done by evaluating the financial value of increased worker productivity attributable to an upgrade to a Pentium 4 processor system bundle from the cost of each employee’s time. This is a straightforward calculation, time saved multiplied by employee cost.

We found that for the 10 tasks evaluated, the Pentium 4 processor system bundle would save each employee approximately 3% of a typical 40-h work week before learning takes place, assuming that their work consistently utilizes these tasks, and the number of expected task iterations per week remains constant as generated by a preliminary study. If the employee did not work solely on the computer or varies how many times per week he/she completes these tasks, we would apply a scalar multiple to the productivity value. We assume, for the purposes of the valuation, that the benefit of upgrading workers to the faster configuration starts yields a starting gain of 3% marginal productivity increase and follows the time constant learning curve with the dynamic 10% gain. We also assume that the company has 25,000 employees, and the per-employee total cost/burden is \$100,000 per year. In addition, as stated earlier, the discount rate is set at the Intel standard for investment valuations: 15%.

We first find the gain during months 1–12, which are the <sup>b</sup>growth<sup>Q</sup> months. This can be done period by period, and the discounted benefit of \$75,854,831 is derived. This will be added onto the constant aspect of the growth dividend model, which itself yields a discounted benefit of \$516,666,666. The total lifetime discounted benefit from a marginal productivity increase is then \$592,521,497.

## 5. Discussion

This work offers a valuation technique to enumerate the lifetime value of a technology investment while addressing the impacts of learning. For the purposes of the upgrade in hardware and software to the Microsoft Windows XP and Pentium 4 processor system bundle, we have assumed that the learning curve for users moving from Microsoft Windows 2000 to Microsoft Windows XP is minimal. This analysis has yielded some impressive numbers.

Intel did decide to move forward with the OS and hardware upgrade and expects to deploy the upgrades to 35,000 clients. Costs were not included in the analysis; while the benefits of an upgrade are generalizable to other companies, the costs may not be.

Yet, these costs may be incorporated into the valuation using the same nonconstant-growth dividend model. Instead of using discounted benefits, one may substitute discounted net value, which is benefits–costs at any given time period. The lifetime discounted benefits and costs generated by this model can then be used within the ROI model to determine the lifetime return on investment for any given technology initiative.

The limitation of the constant- and nonconstantgrowth dividend models is that they use an expectation of a future return. This implies that a risk assessment or probability of attaining those future benefits is inherent to the validity of the resulting valuation. For the purposes of this work, we assume that our expectation of future benefits is equal to the actual future benefits. The probability of expected returns equaling actual returns is 100%. However, expectation of actual return will vary by organization, technology, labor skill set, etc. and will rarely be 100%.

We note here that project risk can be incorporated through the discount rate, per traditional valuation, and organizational and outside risk factors can be addressed through probabilities associated with the expectation. A more elegant way to incorporate risk and potential lifetime benefits may be reached by combining options and the nonconstant-growth dividend model. We leave this as future research.
