# The coevolution of IT innovation and copyright institutions: The development of the mobile music business in Japan and Korea

## a b s t r a c t

In order to understand how IT impacts at the industry level, this paper adopts a theory of the coevolution of technological innovations and copyright institutions and applies it to examine how the mobile music business in Japan and Korea has developed. In Japan, mobile music business is controlled by incumbent recording companies and is complementary to offline CD sales. In Korea, however, the online music business (including mobile and fixed-internet) is dominated by mobile carriers and has replaced offline businesses, which has caused disruptive changes in the music industry structure. This paper suggests that diverging national copyright institutions give rise to the contrasting industrial changes, which in turn emphasizes how political processes drive the interactions between technologies and institutions.

## 1. Introduction

The traditional focus of information systems (IS) on studying IT use at the firm-level has caused concerns that researchers may overlook critical factors to facilitate and constrain IT related effects at the industry-level (Crowston and Myers, 2004; Chiasson and Davidson, 2005). The study of the interrelationship between IT development and industry transformation is of significance for the field of IS because an industry-level phenomenon is not reducible to an organizational one. Furthermore, the development of a particular technology effects all organizations in an industry, and industry-level factors may shape the development and use of IT innovations (Crowston and Myers, 2004). The wide diffusion of IT in a variety of industries could offer IS research important opportunities to develop new knowledge and broaden its relevance beyond existing research boundaries (Chiasson and Davidson, 2005).

Addressing these concerns, this paper examines the interrelations between the development of IT and the mobile music business in Japan and Korea based on the theory of the coevolution of technology and social institutions. The coevolution theory has gained much attention in the innovation literature (Nelson, 1994, 2008; Rosenkopf and Tushman, 1994; Van de Ven and Garud, 1994; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001; Lynskey, 2006), and there is an increasing awareness of its value in the IS literature (Orlikowski and Barley, 2001; Davidson and Chismar, 2007). A coevolution approach has a bi-directional rather than a uni-directional sense of causality, which incorporates both the impact of technology innovation and institutional effects. The theory of coevolution draws on interrelationships involving how IT innovations cause music industry transformation on the one hand, and how different copyright institutions may facilitate and constrain the technological effects on the other hand. This interplay generates both positive and negative feedback effects, which jointly lead to complex changes in industry structure.

Both Japan and Korea have been at the forefront of global mobile internet diffusion and technological development. This advanced mobile telecommunications platform has provided the mobile music business with a sound technological and market base. However, the development paths of the mobile music business in these countries, as well as their impact upon the music industry, has shown striking differences. The mobile music business accounted for 33% of the total Japanese music market in 2009 (Fig. 2). In this market the recording companies have both controlled the truetone ringtone and full-track music download business, and keep them complementary to existing offline business or CD sales. In contrast, the online music business in Korea integrating both mobile- and fixed-internet music services grew to account for a startling 87% of the total music market in 2009 (Fig. 3). In this market, the online music business has replaced offline CD sales, and the mobile carriers, rather than the incumbent recording companies, have emerged as the dominant player of the music industry.

To analyze these marked industrial changes, this paper examines the coevolution of IT innovation and music copyright institutions. This paper highlights the critical effects of the diverging characteristics of national copyright institutions in the development of IT and industrial changes and thereby overcomes the narrow economic and strategic perspectives of existing IS literature (Crowston and Myers, 2004; Chiasson and Davidson, 2005). This paper differs from existing coevolution studies that highlight the supporting role of institutions in industrial and technological development (Nelson, 1994, 2008; Rosenkopf and Tushman, 1994; Van de Ven and Garud, 1994; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001; Lynskey, 2006), by casting a new light on the problems of disharmony between radical technological changes and rigid copyright institutions. This paper applies the theory of coevolution as a lens to focus on the political processes where incumbent firms and new entrants adopt contrasting strategies. Finally, to understand the effects of social institutions, this paper takes a balanced approach to both the formal and informal aspects of copyright institutions governing technological effects and industrial changes.

The remainder of the paper is structured in the following manner. Section 2 presents the analytical framework of the coevolution of technological innovations and music copyright institutions. Section 3 discusses the issues of data collection at industry level. Section 4 briefly overviews the changes in the music industry in Japan and Korea and then examines the development of mobile music business and their impacts on the music industry as a whole. Section 5 discusses the theoretical and practical implications of this research and draws conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical perspective

Technological innovations never happen without changing the existing structure of an industry (Abernathy and Clark, 1985; Anderson and Tushman, 1990; Henderson and Clark 1990; Christensen, 1997), whereas social institutions resist changes with strong durability and stability, constraining variations and the diversity of economic behaviors in the support of existing orders and relationships (Clemens and Cook, 1999; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001; Dacin et al., 2002; Garud et al., 2007). Innovations in IT and the digitalization of music profoundly alter the patterns of music production, distribution, and consumption and significantly change how value is created and how revenue is generated in the industry (Clemons and Lang, 2003) (Fig. 1). Significant innovations for music are low-cost digital recording, efficient file formats for music production, advances of fixed and mobile internet for distribution, and cheap digital music players. Using the internet as a new distri bution channel for digital music may create a variety of new business models with superior cost benefit and consumer con venience compared with incumbent models (Clemons and Lang, 2003). The decoupling of value creation and revenue generation in business process allow new entrants to offer better services to meet new customer demands. The fundamental changes in market structure enabled by technological innovations allow entrants to compete against existing players, and organizational constraints may often prevent the incumbent firms from effectively responding to new entrants (Abernathy and Clark, 1985; Anderson and Tushman, 1990; Henderson and Clark, 1990; Christensen, 1997; Clemons and Lang, 2003).

Music copyright institutions provide an important base upon which the music business forms and develops, and they shape patterns of competition brought about by technological innovations (Fig. 1). Music copyright not only provides diverging incentives and improves music-related assets but also legitimates and regulates applications of technological innovations and the development of new products and new entrants (Maskus, 2000). Without enforcement music copyrights cannot be meaningfully emploved to monitor commercial activities throughout the supply and distribution channels. The monitoring is enforced not only by formal institutions such as copyright laws, copyright enforcement organizations, and government policies but also by informal ones including customs, habitual practices, and cultural norms in industrial and social contexts (Pejovich, 1999; Maskus, 2000; Merges, 2000; Nelson, 2008). The patterns of music copyright define the technology strategies of firms, the ease of market entry, the level of market competition, and competitive advantages amongst firms (Maskus, 2000).

The more radical the changes that technological innovations promise to bring, the stronger the social and political tensions that build up between incumbents and new entrants (Fig. 1). The established copyright institutions are inherently designed to support the operation of existing technologies and incumbent firms while conflicting against, or oppressing, the business activities of new entrants’ applying innovative technologies (Aldrich and Fiol, 1994; Chesbrough, 1999; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001). Incumbent firms try to constrain the rise of new ventures by relying on existing copyright rules, norms, and regulations (Hargadon and Douglas, 2001). Political tensions may rise as new entrants countervail the incumbents’ efforts by adopting a variety of strategies that range from building a coalition, lobbying government, and launching social campaigns to directly appealing to the public (Aldrich and Fiol, 1994). The industrial changes initiated from technological innovations are a result of the complex interactions of technological potentials to disrupt industrial orders and existing copy right institutions to regulate innovative activities.

## 3. Data collection

Data collection activities of this research included conducting interviews, participating in industry seminars, and collecting archival data. A total of fourteen interviews were carried out. Six interviews were conducted with content providers (CPs), five were made with mobile carriers, and three were with public/private copyright enforcement organizations in Japan and Korea. Interviews usually lasted 60–90 min and were transcribed for later analysis. The major questions for corporate informants related to the characteristics of new business models, which included the different means of value creation, the target market, and changed patterns of production and distribution of music. There were also specific questions for copyright enforcement organizations which concerned copyright issues of new services. These covered the general structure of existing copyright institutions, their conflicts with new music services, changes of copyright laws in accordance with IT innovations, and new roles of the newly established copyright enforcement organizations (refer to Appendix A).

Another important source of data, concerning the Japanese market, consisted of six SSK Seminars. These industry-specific seminars were made up of various mixes of leaders of major CPs, carriers, and copyright organizations. They provided a private, fee-based forum to share information on IT technologies, newly emerging music businesses, and industry-level concerns and issues with invited speakers, such as CEOs of leading firms, industry experts, and technicians. These SSK Seminars provided not only the latest information regarding new business developments and industry-common copyright issues but also chances to make personal contacts with guest speakers for further information and ideas (refer to Appendix B)

A variety of archival data provided industry information, and they are selectively cited in this paper. Reliable archival data were generated from industry-specific associations such as Mobile Content Forum (MCF) and the Recording Industry Association of Japan (RIAJ); private research companies such as Seedplanning Inc. and NetAsia; and magazines such as IT Media, Impress Watch, ASCII, and Nethanbai. The government agency Japan Fair Trade Commission (JFTC) also produced critical data on copyright strategies and market power of major recording companies

In Korea, K-mobile, an internet portal site specializing in mobile telecommunications provided an online data repository covering a broad range of issues, including new mobile music businesses, copyright legal litigations, changes in government policies, and industrial and consumer trends. Participation in a K-mobile offline seminar also provided insights on industrial trends. Industry-specific magazines, such as ZDNet Korea and Digital Times, were used to provide information on the trends of IT and changes in the music industry. Furthermore, and backed by a strong cultural content promotion policy, numerous government agencies such as Korea Creative Content Agency, National IT Industry Promotion Agency, and Korea Information Society Development Institute produced various data, such as whitepapers on the music industry, industry survey researches, and policy and legal change reports. In particular, the Korea Fair Trade Commission generated reliable research on the close monitoring of anticompetitive actions of CPs and copyright management practices.

## 4. Developments of the mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea

## 4.1. Overview of the mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea

Based on a highly advanced mobile telecommunication platform, mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea have become the most popular form of service and a powerful driver of the further development of mobile telecommunication systems. As of January 2002, mobile communication subscribers amounted to 66% of the total population in Japan and 76% in Korea. Among the subscribers in 2004, those owning handsets with a mobile internet function amounted to 86% in Japan and 92% in Korea. Mobile internet services, embodied in the 2.5G (Generation) mobile communication system, were first launched in February 1999 in Japan and September 1999 in Korea. Both Japan and Korea adopted a 3G mobile communications in October 2001 and June 2002, respectively. A variety of mobile music business, based on the highly advanced mobile telecommunications platform, was introduced in the two countries. and these are shown in Table 1.

Although there is a large commonality in the structure of mobile music businesses in Japan and Korea, the developmental patterns of these businesses have shown significant differences. Turning our attention to the speed of introducing new services, it can be seen that the first polyphonic ringtones were started in Japan in December 1999, as opposed to their later introduction in Korea in 2000. However, the launch of more advanced services such as truetone ringtones and full-track music were made in November 2002 in Korea, 1 month earlier than in Japan (Table 1). Differences in two national markets are also reflected in their perspective business models. For example, the strong market segregation between the mobile- and fixed-internet in Japan means that music purchased through the mobile internet cannot be replayed on other portable music players. However in Korea, the introduction of Ubiquitous-Music (U-Music) by SKT in November 2004 broke down the boundary between mobile- and fixed-internet and allowed consumers to play digital music on mobile handsets, PCs, and other portable devices. The mobile music business in Japan accounted for 33% of the total music market in 2009 (Fig. 2), where recording companies continue to dominate the market. In contrast, the online music business (integrating both mobile and fixed-internet) in Korea grew to 87% of the total music market in 2009 (Fig. 3), where the mobile carriers control the music industry structure.

## 4.2. Polyphonic ringtones business in Japan and Korea

Early types of mobile music business were constrained by 2.5G mobile communication technologies, which were designed for voice telecommunication rather than for data. The data memory chips and music-replying technologies of mobile handsets could facilitate only a simple melody rather than complex vocal music. Under these technological restrictions, the earliest form of mobile music business was polyphonic ringtones, which used fragments of melodies from popular music. Because the service used only the melody of original music without vocal sounds, a copyright fee was paid only to the composers (Takeishi and Lee, 2005, 2006).

In Japan, the majority of music copyrights are managed by JASRAC (the Japanese Society for Rights of Authors, Composers and Publishers) on behalf of the copyright holders. For the polyphonic ringtones service, JASRAC mandated that content providers (CPs) of ringtones should pay a copyright fee of US\$0.05 (or 7.7% of the information usage price) to JASRAC. This rule was established through negotiation between JASRAC and the Network Music Rights Conference, which represents nine institutions, including the Association of Musical Electronics Industry, Recording Industry Association of Japan, and Internet Association Japan to promote music business on the internet.

This official copyright rule contributed to the smooth launch and subsequent growth of the polyphonic ringtones business in two ways. First, because anyone can provide a polyphonic ringtones service by simply paying a copyright fee to JASRAC, a large number of CPs entered into a highly competitive market. The intense competition that resulted led to better and cheaper services. Without any significant entry barriers, it was the former Karaoke companies, such as GIGA and XING that first introduced polyphonic ringtones service as they had not only a large stock of digitalized music files that were easily convertible to the service, but also know-how for copyright coordination. Second, because polyphonic ringtones were gaining wide popularity, a large number of free unofficial services that neglected to pay copyright fees to JASRAC sprung into existence. JASRAC vigorously monitored and charged the copyright violators in order to counter this undesirable trend, and, in doing so, established a sound market basis for legal services.

Because of this effective and strict copyright system, the growth of polyphonic ringtone services in Japan in the early days of mobile music development established the world’s largest internet music market at that time. Whereas the world internet music download market (excluding polyphonic ringtones in Japan) was estimated to be US\$341 million in 2004, the Japanese polyphonic ringtones market had already reached US\$736 million in 2001 (Takeishi and Lee, 2005).

In Korea, CPs of polyphonic ringtones paid 8% of their revenue to the Korea Music Copyright Association as the copyright fee. New entrants of the polyphonic ringtone services included IT ventures such as YAHO, and other CPs which had friendly relationships with mobile carriers. Although the basic structure of the service in Korea looked similar to that of Japan, there were some seemingly subtle but critical differences that cannot be overlooked (Takeishi and Lee, 2005, 2006). First, a copyright fee was charged based on a certain percentage of the CP’s total monthly revenue, not on a per melody download. CPs could thus exert larger control on setting their service prices. Because Korean CPs often provided a variety of content services, such as screen savers and games, they used free polyphonic ringtones as a promotional tool to attract other contents subscribers. Second, as a result of Korean mobile carriers and CPs not making available detailed data on melody downloads, copyright management remained inaccurate and opaque.

Copyright holders complained about ineffective copyright management, but no significant improvement was made in a timely manner. Indeed, Korea’s fundamental copyright problem resided in the fact that the country’s general recognition and norms of copyright protection had been comparatively loose not only among business users and consumers but also among copyright claimers, such as recording companies, composers, and lyricists, who were in the position to uphold their rights strongly (Pek, 2004). Under such a loose copyright norm, a customary practice among CPs was ‘‘service first, copyright permission later’’ in designing their business, casually ignoring copyright negotiations and permission processes.

Such loose copyright norms and monitoring stemmed from the old structure and history of the Korean music industry. According to the established practice, small record retailers usually neglected to report correct sales revenue so as to avoid taxation. Also, recording companies usually gave ‘‘advances’’ to artists and producers, and the copyright fee was paid only when record sales reached a pre-negotiated target volume, which rarely occurred. As a result of such practices, even the recording companies had not been eager to build up strict copyright institutions. An international comparison on copyright revenue reveals how the Korean copyright system was loosely structured (Table 2). Compared to Japan and the United States, copyright revenues from copy and distribution were significantly smaller in Korea, indicating the fact that illegal copies and distribution were prevalent in the loose Korean copyright system.

## 4.3. Mobile music downloads businesses in Japan

As Japan moved towards a 3G mobile communication system in October 2001, most of the technological constraints for full-track music download services such as networks’ data transmission capacity, handset music processing capabilities, and data memory were quickly solved. Although polyphonic ringtones and new advanced services such as truetone ringtones and full-track music appeared similar, there was a critical difference in terms of copyright management. To provide ‘‘vocal music’’ downloads services, not simply melodies, the service providers both in Japan and Korea use an original ‘‘song’’ and pay a full set of copyright fees not only for the music copyright held by the composers and lyricists but also for the ‘‘neighboring music copyright,’’ which includes ‘‘copyright in sound recordings’’ held by various contributors.

In Japan, KDDI, a mobile carrier, launched the truetone ringtones (‘‘Chaku-Uta’’) service in December 2002, positioning it as an advanced version of polyphonic ringtones already in the market, rather than as a music download service. In gaining popularity, the market size of these new services grew rapidly from US\$28 million in 2004 to US\$568 million in 2007 and began significantly replacing the polyphonic ringtones service in 2004. In November 2004, KDDI also launched a full-track music download service (“Chaku-Uta-Furu") with six CP sites having 10.000 songs available. The price was generally set at US\$3 20 per song which was equivalent to the offline CD prices. The market size had dramatically increased from US\$99 million in 2005 to US\$506 million in 2007. Both the truetone and full-track music download services have enjoyed high popularity and largely leveraged KDDI’s competitive advantage in attracting mobile subscribers.

Although the music copyrights were collectively managed by JASRAC and other registered copyright organizations based on the Copyright Management Business Law, the neighboring music copyrights in Japan were ‘‘specifically’’ defined and separately owned by a variety of contributors such as singers, musicians, recording companies, and artist management offices. Under this complex copyright structure, the CPs faced several problems related to managing the neighboring music copyright. First, the copyright holders were so widely scattered that it was highly costly and time-consuming for CPs to accurately identify claims. In Japan, and in contrast to the US and European music industries, there were many cases in which artist management offices or subsidiaries of television network companies rather than recording companies held neighboring music copyrights (Takeishi and Lee, 2005; Mino, 2009). Without specialized copyright organizations to centrally manage these copyrights, the CPs of truetone and full-track music services were compelled to negotiate with the copyright claimers one-by-one in advance.

Second, and more critically, Japan has such strong norms to protect music copyright that the copyright owners have strongly resisted third-parties’ using neighboring music copyrights. Because the copyright claimers, such as large recording companies, exercised copyrights exclusively, there was no guarantee for the CPs to receive permission. Rather than allowing third parties to use neighboring music copyrights, the existing recording companies have provided music services directly and collectively through their own fixed- and mobile internet sites.

At the time, there was anger among recording companies who felt that they were not making a ‘‘fair’’ return from the polyphonic ringtones market, in spite of their immense investment to make hit music and star artists. Learning from their bitter experience, five leading recording companies (Avex, Sony Music Entertainment, Toshiba EMI, Universal Music, and Victor Entertainment) established a joint venture in 2001. The joint venture was called ‘‘Recochoku’’ (formally Label Mobile), which stands for ‘‘recording companies directly providing’’ in Japanese, and it was formed in order to exclusively use neigh boring music copyrights, as well as to provide music download services. The joint venture enabled the recording companies to block entries of third-party CPs, oppress free competition, and control market prices to prevent mobile music business from ‘‘cannibalizing’’ CD sales. By collectively controlling the business structure, the recording companies positioned true tone and full-track music download services as promotional tools complementary to offline CD sales. In addition to the founding companies, 22 recording companies also joined as participatory members of the joint venture, and another 100 recording companies provided music (JFTC, 2008). In 2004 the founding recording companies claimed 47% of the top-100 Japanese-pop music titles and also accounted for 46% of truetone ringtone downloads on the Recochoku site (JFTC, 2008).

Complex copyright institutions and the recording companies’ practices of exclusively using copyrights caused several interrelated problems to disturb the growth of music download services. First, some popular songs were not available in a timely manner, because copyright holders such as artist management offices and recording companies did not give permission to CPs. Because the truetone service for such music was offered either exclusively by a specific CP or not at all, it was inconvenient and time consuming for customers to know the availability and providers of their favorite music.

Second, limited copyright institutions became a significant factor in promoting the spread of illegal sites after mobile carriers adopted fixed pricing for unlimited data transmission in the summer of 2005. According to a survey by the RIAJ, about 60% of teenagers used illegal downloads in 2006. The lack of sufficient songs available through legal channels and the difficulties in searching for favorite songs led to the surge of illegal download sites. Furthermore, the high prices of services controlled by recording companies also exacerbated the use of illegal sites. A survey by the RIAJ in September 2008 revealed that 51.1% of consumers would stop using illegal sites if prices of truetone ringtones were lowered.

Third, and in contrast to the growth of the polyphonic ringtones business, the high entry barrier generated by the importance of copyrights retarded the market growth of the music download business. The growth of the full-track music service has been comparatively slow as recording companies have set high service prices and exclusively used neighboring music copyright without giving permission to third-parties, which has oppressed market competition.

Noticing the problems caused by the use of copyright, the JFTC initially ‘‘recommended’’ Recochoku founders to allow third-party copyright use in March 2005. However, because the recording companies, with the exception of Toshiba EMI, resisted the recommendation, the JFTC initiated a formal anti-trust investigation to find the violation of the antimonopoly law in July 2008 on the basis that antimonopoly laws had been violated. The agency finally confirmed its decision in February 2011 by dismissing the appeal of the recording companies.

Although no public data are available, in mid-2009 an industry specialist estimated that only about 20% of the officially downloadable music in both fixed- and mobile–internet had an ‘‘open copyright policy’’ to allow third-party CPs to provide music services (Mino, 2009). The mobile music download market has a highly concentrated structure such that Recochoku accounted for about 50–60% of the mobile music download market with 18 million active users in 2009, and the combination of Dwango and Mobilephone Telecommunications International accounted for 20% of the market (Nethanbai, 2009; Mino, 2009). There was no public organization has been established to collectively manage the neighboring music copyrights, and several private copyright licensing firms, such as E-license, have meditated the copyright licensing between the owners and CPs.

Apple’s iTunes Music Store (iTMS), the world’s leading internet music provider, made a debut in the fixed-internet music services in Japan as early as August 2005, competing against Mora and Yahoo Japan. However, the performance has been unimpressive. Because of the market segregation between fixed and mobile internet, the market share of iTMS in the fixed internet music service is estimated to be equivalent to the share of iPod in the portable music device market (Seedplanning Inc., 2008). The fact that iPod had 57% of Japan’s market at the end of 2008 led to the estimation that Apple’s iTMS claimed about a half of the fixed-internet music market. Given that the size of fixed internet music market was only 5% of the mobile internet market in 2009 (Fig. 2), a reasonable estimation is that iTMS accounted for about 3% of total online music market combining both fixed- and mobile–internet in Japan.

## 4.4. Mobile music downloads businesses in Korea

The first 3G mobile telecommunication system was introduced in June 2002 in Korea, and SKT, the largest mobile carrier, introduced truetone ringtones and full-track download services simultaneously in November 2002. A notable fact is that although Japan and Korea had very similar music copyright laws specifically defining the neighboring music copyright holders, Korea’s introduction of the full-track download service was 2 years ahead of Japan. Upon introducing the new services, the mobile carrier took strong leadership by directly designing the business structures. For the music download services, SKT launched its own site where CPs worked behind the scenes to arrange copyright issues and develop digital files so consumers could not recognize individual CPs. Another difference is that in Korea, right after truetone ringtones and full-track music download services started, new public and private copyright enforcement organizations were established in 2003 to collectively manage and mediate the ‘‘neighboring music copyrights’’.

The first neighboring music copyright enforcement organization, the Korean Association of Phonogram Producers (KAP-Ps), was established in March 2003 through a government initiative. However, the major recording companies, which opposed the idea of entrusting copyrights to a public third party, jointly founded Manine Media, a private copyright publisher. These copyright enforcement organizations simplified the cumbersome process of copyright negotiations and systemized copyright management. In this open copyright system, any CP could provide most of popular Korean music by paying standard copyright fees to these organizations.

New copyright institutions were quickly established in Korea for a number of reasons. First, the Korean music industry had bitter experiences of confusion, conflicts, and crises in fixed-internet caused by illegal music downloads. Since 2000, Korea had developed one of world’s most advanced fixed internet networks. However, the music copyrights, particularly neighboring music copyrights for digitalized files, were not modified quickly enough to deal with the rise of illegal internet music download services. During this institutional vacancy, free music services such as Bug’s Music (free music streaming service) and Soribada (free peer-to-peer file download service) had emerged to gain wide popularity among internet users. During their peak time Bug’s Music and Soribada attracted 8 million and 20 million users, respectively, causing devastating effects on the music industry. In order to force these companies to respect copyrights, the recording business and copyright holders had to engage in many rounds of legal and political haggling.

Second, the Korean government has actively engaged in reforming the music copyright laws and rules in accordance with the development of internet technologies. After the historical financial crisis in 1997, the Korean government began strategically promoting the cultural content industry as a new dynamo for economic development. The government recognized the importance of ‘‘open’’ music copyrights in the internet environment, and has pursued a ‘‘one-source multi-use’’ policy that allows consumers to enjoy the legally downloaded digital music files with PCs, mobile handsets, and other devices.

Together, these two factors – bitter industry experiences and influential government policies – prompted the establishment of new institutions and the development of free music market competition in both fixed and mobile internet.

Another radical turning point in the development of mobile music business came in March 2004 when mobile handset makers devised a plan to introduce the ‘‘MP3-Phone’’, a mobile handset to play MP3 music files, which could enable mobile users to break down the technological barrier between fixed- and mobile-internet and replay illegal files downloaded through fixed-internet. A critical industrial concern was that the illegal free services dominating the unregulated fixed-internet could threaten the viability of the mobile music market, where the mobile carriers had constructed a secure business platform by preventing illegal downloading. Before the market launch of the MP3-Phone, the key copyright issue was whether it should be compatible with existing illegal MP3 files saved on users’ PCs. Large handset manufacturers wanted to produce fully compatible handsets to stimulate a rapid diffusion of the new handsets. Recording companies, however, understandably opposed such a plan, demanding full incompatibility with the existing illegal files. Mobile carriers stayed closer to recording companies, calculating the potential growth of the music download market.

Following several rounds of torturous government mediations, a tentative agreement to limit the replaying time of illega files to 72 h was about to be reached. However, at the last minute, LG Telecom, a mobile operator, and LG Electronics, a handset manufacturer, withdrew from the negotiations and began selling ‘‘fully compatible’’ MP3-Phones to increase market share. Following the success of this move, the other mobile carriers also began to provide fully compatible mobile handsets. As MP3-Phones blurred the boundaries between fixed- and mobile–internet, SKT introduced the U-Music service in November 2004 with about 900,000 songs. The competing mobile carriers, LGT and KTF, lost no time in introducing their own versions of the U-Music services.

For a monthly subscription fee, the U-Music service provided a combination of full-track music in MP3 format and music streaming service over fixed-internet. Upon launching the U-Music services, mobile carriers internalized the service without relying on third-party CPs. In an attempt to strengthen its bargaining power over other recording companies, SKT, the largest mobile carrier, acquired YBM Seoul Records in 2005, which was one of the largest recording companies in Korea. By 2009 consumers of the U-Music service could not only download 150 full-track music files that were playable on almost any device but also enjoy unlimited music streaming through PCs for only US\$11 a month.

By 2007, the total size of music market began to outgrow pre-internet era and by 2009 the online music business expanded to become seven times larger than the offline market. This implies that the basic structure of the new music copyright institutions has evolved to cope with internet platforms, which in turn governed a variety of internet music businesses (Fig. 3). Recording companies and copyright holders succeeded in transforming free illegal services, such as Bug’s Music and Soribada, into legal services in 2005. Consequently, in this highly concentrated market structure, telecommunication carriers rather than recording companies dominated online music industry. In 2009 SKT’s MelOn claimed a dominant 39.1% of the online music market, KTF’s Dosirack and Bug’s Music accounted for 11%, respectively, and Mnet had 9.7% of the online music market. Apple’s iTMS, the world’s leading music provider, has no service in Korea.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

This paper presents a comparative analysis which shows how the complex coevolution of technological innovations in the mobile–internet platform and national music copyright institutions has differently shaped the development pattern of the mobile music business in Japan and Korea. This analysis takes into account the speed at which innovative businesses are introduced, the winners and outcomes of competition, and the subsequent change in industry structure.

In Japan, during the early days of simple mobile services such as polyphonic ringtones, the ‘‘strict’’ music copyright institutions effectively supported business growth by ensuring free market entry and by effectively prohibiting illegal services. However, in the case of more advanced mobile music businesses such as truetone ringtones and full-track music, the overly strict copyright institutions caused problems of rigidity and disharmony. Incumbent recording companies and copyright holders exclusively used neighboring music copyrights and successfully organized a coalition to build entry barriers against third-party CPs, keeping service prices within their control. Consequently, the strict and rigid copyright institutions led the incumbent recording companies to slow down the speed of introducing new services, restricting further the growth of mobile businesses, and constraining potentially disruptive technological innovations so that the existing music industry structure was sustained in their favor.

In Korea, even before the introduction of mobile music businesses, legal enforcements and general music copyright norms had been so ‘‘loose’’ that they often failed to govern the distribution of illegal copies. These weak institutions failed to prevent the surge of illegal music download services via the fixed-internet, and no adequate protection was provided for the mobile businesses at the beginning. However, ironically, the weak institutions effectively facilitated flexible copyright adjustments and coordination and quickened the introduction of new innovative services, such as U-Music, in accordance with technological integration of mobile- and fixed-internet platforms. The government’s open copyright policy prevented recording companies from exclusively using copyrights and ensured an open market for third-party service providers. With these open and flexible copyright institutions, mobile carriers, as new entrants to the music industry, accelerated the growth of online music services to surpass offline CD sales, causing a disruptive change in the music industry as a whole.

Economic or strategic perspectives (Crowston and Myers, 2004; Chiasson and Davidson, 2005) have formed the dominant approach in IS research. These were useful in projecting the economic effects of IT or potential consequences on competition of adopting technological innovations. However, the coevolution theory of this paper suggests that changes in the music industry were shaped not by the intrinsic characteristics of information technologies but by their complex interactions with copyright institutions and political processes.

As an emergent theory (Markus and Robey, 1988), coevolution theory defies any simple deterministic approach, either through technological or institutional imperatives. It provides rich explanations of how and why different patterns of industry change occur by examining interactive coevolution processes. To understand industrial change the existing coevolution studies emphasize the supporting roles of social institutions (Nelson, 1994, 2008; Rosenkopf and Tushman, 1994; Van de Ven and Garud, 1994; Har gadon and Douglas, 2001; Lynskey, 2006). However, this paper brings a new focus to the problems of disharmony between new technologies and institutions in shaping industrial developments. Though technological innovations may occasion dynamics of industry change, their outcomes are driven by political interaction processes in which new entrants rush to adopt technological innovations whilst incumbents rely on social institutions to defend them. The changes in industry structure result from different patterns of political alignments between the emerging potentials of IT innovation and copyright institutions.

The emergent nature of coevolution theory is less clear in terms of normative posture than with technological or institutional perspectives (Markus and Robey, 1988). Instead, it advocates adopting diverging policy measures in accordance with the different processes and patterns of change. Whilst there may be no strategic panacea, this paper stresses timely policy interventions in accordance with foreseeable patterns of outcomes. The Korean case suggests that the government could take a flexible approach, especially during the period of ferment in industrial change initiated by technological innovation. Government could also take timely and frequent policy measures to align the copyright institutions with the emerging potential of IT, for instance, by introducing open and fair copyright use rules, setting the principles of ‘‘one-source multi-use,’’ and establishing new copyright enforcement organizations.

This paper presented contrasting patterns of industrial change in Japan and Korea, and we should continue research efforts in different national contexts, particularly in the United States and European, which together claim a dominant share in the world’s music market. Further research may provide us more wisdom regarding how to solve problems of disharmony and effectively align copyright institutions with technological potential. To better understand institutional impacts upon industry change, this paper also suggests the significance of the informal aspect of social institutions, such as habitual transaction practices, general copyright recognition, and their subsequent effects.

Finally. this paper examines industrial change from the perspective of the coevolution of technology and “social" institutions. However, in this respect, the online music industry seems to have some limitations with regard generalizing the research results. The music industry relies so heavily on copyright institutions that the research findings may be less applicable to other industries that are less dependent on such complex legal structures. Though this narrow scope may appeal to a limited set of IS researchers, copyright institutions and other intellectual property rights are also an important so cial institution for the development of IT and other technologies in a broad number of industries.
