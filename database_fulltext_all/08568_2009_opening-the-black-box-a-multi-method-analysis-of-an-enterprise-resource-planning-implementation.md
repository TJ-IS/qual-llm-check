## Abstract

This paper presents a multi-method analysis of the implementation of an enterprise resource planning system. It argues the majority of research treats these systems as having essential technical properties that enable them to bring about positive organisational effects. In contrast, we adopt a post-essentialist perspective that understands discourse as constructing ERP systems, rather than reflecting their essential properties. Discourse analysis of interviews and focus groups was used to compare justifications for the implementation produced by members of the ERP project team with end users’ reactions to the system. In contrast to previous studies, this opens the ERP black box by illustrating how the capabilities of these systems, and their organisational effects, are constructed through language. Moreover, it demonstrates the important function these constructions perform in legitimising or undermining ERP implementation. Statistical analysis of post-implementation questionnaires identified the predictors of established measures of users’ reactions to IT systems. By comparing these data with end users’ discursive reactions to the project, we identify several constructs for inclusion in future studies of users’ reactions to ERP systems. The paper concludes by summarising the contributions and limitations of the study and its implications for future research and practice.

## Introduction

he past 15 years has witnessed the widespread adoption of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. Indeed, 70% of Fortune 1000 companies have implemented them in some form (Yen et al., 2002). However, despite the pervasive of these technologies, it has been reported that projects are, on average, 178% over budget, take two and a half times longer than intended, and deliver only about 30% of their promised benefits (Krumbholz et al., 2000). Moreover, high profile implementation failures include Aerogroup, Allied Waste Industries, Boeing, Dell, Foxmeyer, Mobil, Panasonic and Siemens (Davenport, 1998; Ragowsky and Somers, 2002). Nevertheless, a report by AMR Research estimated the market for ERP software will grow from \$28.8 billion in 2006 to \$47.7 billion by 2011, fuelled by the adoption of these systems by small and medium enterprises (Jacobsen et al., 2007). A likely contributory factor to the sustained confidence in these systems is the technological determinist rhetoric of their advocates. ERP implementations are typically framed as an opportunity to replace differentiated legacy systems with a single system comprising a central database and software modules designed to manage all of an enterprise’s data and business processes. For example, vendors including SAP and Oracle offer modules which it is claimed enable companies to manage processes as diverse as sales, distribution, inventory management, procurement, billing, financial accounting and human resource (HR) management (see Davenport, 1998; Ragowsky and Somers, 2002).

This paper highlights a number of problems within the literature on enterprise resource planning systems. First, it argues that technological determinism is also prevalent within the academic studies. In response to the challenges practitioners have faced in implementing ERP systems, the majority of research has focused on identifying critical success factors or developing process models to enhance implementation outcomes (Adam and O’Doherty, 2000; Parr and Shanks, 2000). These studies treat ERP systems as black boxes with immutable technical capabilities that enable them to bring about positive organisational effects. This is problematic as it is well established within the enterprise resource planning and wider IT literature that successful implementation outcomes are elusive (Krumbholz et al., 2000; Butler, 2004). Second, few studies have explored how these deterministic constructions are produced or the function they perform in legitimising technology-led change. This is surprising as contemporary perspectives on technological change have stressed the importance of analysing how information technologies are constructed through language for rhetorical effect (e.g., Symon, 2005). Finally, despite their ubiquity there is a dearth of research into users’ reactions to these systems (Bagchi et al., 2003). Existing research is predominately quantitative and is rarely complemented by qualitative analysis. In view of the limited research into users’ reactions to these systems, claims for their benefits should be treated with caution.

In response to these limitations, this paper presents a multi-method analysis of an ERP implementation. It makes a number of contributions to the literature on enterprise resource planning systems. Discourse analysis of interviews and focus groups was used to compare justifications for the implementation produced by members of the ERP project team with end users’ reactions to the system. This opens the ERP black box by demonstrating how the capabilities of these systems, and their organisational effects, are interpretively flexible. Second, it illustrates the important rhetorical function constructions of these systems perform in legitimising or undermining ERP implementation. Finally, the discourse analysis of end user’s reactions was compared with statistical analysis of questionnaires designed to explore the predictors of established measures of users’ reactions to information technology. This enabled us to validate our findings against previous research and identify constructs that could be incorporated in future studies of users’ reactions to ERP systems. In the following section, we offer a critical review of the ERP literature before moving on to describe the organisational context in which the study was undertaken.

## Enterprise resource planning: A review of the literature

There have been a number of excellent reviews of the ERP literature (e.g., Robey et al., 2002; Dery et al., 2006a). Building on these, this section delineates approaches that have sought to understand the process of ERP implementation and users’ reactions to these systems according to their ontological perspective; essentialist, anti-essentialist or post-essentialist (see Grint and Woolgar, 1995).

## Essentialist perspectives

Essentialist perspectives assume ERP systems have essential material properties that enable them to bring about definite, and often positive, organisational effects.

## - Critical success factor and process innovation models

These approaches attribute problems faced by companies attempting to implement ERP systems to deficiencies in the management of the change process. By developing taxonomies of critical success factors and/or process models for implementation, they aim to deliver ‘better planning and, hence, more successful implementation’ (Parr and Shanks, 2000: 200). They have provided useful recommendations that corroborate findings within the change management literature, including adequate training of end users, project champions and effective communication (Sumner, 2000). Nevertheless, they share some limitations. For example, they understand ERP systems as having ‘beneficial effects which remove the need for often disparate and unreliable end user applications, standardise operating procedures and reporting, and optimise some of the key processes of the firm’ (Adam and O’Doherty, 2000: 306). This treats ERP systems as a black box, ignoring how deterministic constructions are produced and how they are performative in legitimising change. Second, these researchers often focus on managerial imperatives, including getting the system in on time and on budget (Parr and Shanks, 2000). While this represents a problem for practitioners, it has resulted in the recommendation for ‘vanilla’ ERP implementations, where organisations re-engineer their business processes to fit the package (Sumner, 2000: 325). In the short term this minimises programming enhancements, thereby reducing implementation costs, timescales, and potential difficulties during system upgrades (Scott and Kaindl, 2000). However, adopting the processes embedded within ERP software can lead to gaps in the required functionality and does not necessarily deliver organisational benefits (Gattiker and Goodhue, 2002). Finally, ERP systems typically affect a large proportion of organisational stakeholders, who interpret ERP success differently (Markus et al., 2000). Consequently, claims that vanilla implementations represent a ‘best practice ERP implementation process model’ (Parr and Shanks, 2000: 302) should be treated with caution where end users’ views are inadequately represented.

## - Studies of user acceptance of ERP systems

Despite the ubiquity of ERP systems there remains a dearth of research into end users reactions to them (Bagchi et al., 2003). In recent times, expectancy approaches (Lim et al., 2005), the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (e.g., Bagchi et al., 2003) and the technology acceptance model (TAM) (e.g., Amoako-Gyampah and Salam, 2004) have tested the antecedents of ERP usage. The TAM and TRA are popular as they are parsimonious and account for approximately 40% of variance in usage. Nevertheless, they have some limitations. First, applications of the TAM assume that usage reflects ‘the acceptance of technology by users’ (Amoako-Gyampah and Salam, 2004: 732). However, these studies do not include subjective norms and voluntariness from the revised model, TAM2 (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). Consequently, it is difficult to confirm whether usage is a reflection of user acceptance or behavioural compliance. For example, when Bagchi et al. (2003) applied the TRA they found a relationship between subjective norms and usage. They attributed this to managerial pressure and employees volunteering to use the system in order to improve their marketability. Second, while research has focused on other measures of users’ reactions such as attitudes (Abdinnour-Helm et al., 2003) few studies have focused other established measures of user acceptance from the wider IT literature (e.g., user satisfaction, commitment to the system, competence using the system). Moreover, the selection of predictor variables in these studies is often partial. This makes it difficult to assess whether any relationships are real, or spurious, resulting from covariation with other factors. Finally, statistical analyses are rarely compared with qualitative analysis of users’ reactions to these systems. In summary, there is an opportunity for multi-method studies of users’ reactions to ERP implementation.

## - Processual perspectives

Processual perspectives (e.g., Hirt and Swanson, 1999; Ross, 1999; Avital and Vandenbosch, 2000) argue that to understand implementation outcomes, it is important to ‘introduce the reader to the type of decision making related to an ERP adoption and implementation and provide some interesting examples of factors which may influence actual decisions and outcomes’ (Hirt and Swanson, 1999: 243). For example, they elaborate how the decision to adopt SAP R/3 was influenced by an array of environmental factors. Furthermore, they show how the outcomes of the implementation can be traced to decisions made at critical junctures, including the choice of consultants, hardware and even the implementation approach. An advantage of these accounts over the perspectives discussed so far is they reject a unitary understanding of power, politics, and conflict. Instead, they adopt a pluralist perspective treating these as inevitable facets of ERP implementation (see Fox, 1974 for discussion of unitary, radical, and pluralist perspectives). Also, they emphasise the importance of agency, treating social structures as ‘reference points for the choices, decisions and negotiating activity of key actors and groups within organizations’ (McLoughlin, 1999: 69). Nevertheless, the idea that external factors (including the technology) act as a constraint on managerial decision making has been described as soft determinism (Majchrzak and Borys, 1998). Moreover, in common with the approaches discussed so far, they treat technology as a black box, assuming a definitive line can be drawn between the technical and social.

## Anti-essentialist perspectives

Anti-essentialist perspectives open the ERP black box by showing how, over the course of their development, these systems are shaped by exogenous social structures (e.g., industrial capitalism) or are socially constructed, reflecting the interests and values of social groups involved in their development. These embodied, or inscribed characteristics, are presumed to constrain interpretative flexibility (how

ERP systems are interpreted) and as a consequence how they are used in practice.

## - Social shaping and critical perspectives

As McLoughlin (1999) observes, social shaping perspectives ‘work inwards’, exploring how the properties of technologies are shaped by broader socio-economic structures. For example, it has been argued that ERP systems are shaped by national/organisational culture (e.g., Krumbholz et al., 2000) and organisational memory (Van Stijn and Wensley, 2001). Krumbholz et al. (2000) hypothesise that the supplier’s culture, which is implicit within the ERP package, clashes with the customer’s corporate culture’, thwarting successful implementation. Critical perspectives (e.g., Koch, 2001; Oliver and Romm, 2002) reject the technological determinism within the literature. They highlight how rational justification for ERP adoption, such as the need for integration, may be motivated by desire for firmer control at the apex of the organisation. Hall (2002) argues that ERP implementation is synonymous with downsising and delayering while Koch contests that these systems and their supporting communities ‘can act as a steamroller for management politics’ (2001: 76). The implication is that ERP systems come to embody the ideology of industrial capitalism during their development. A key problem for critical perspectives is that their radical view of organisational power. They assume that managers and workers hold homogeneous and diametrically opposed motives that inevitably embroil them in conflict. A further difficulty for both of these perspectives is their residual essentialism (Grint and Woolgar, 1997). Specifically, the idea that social or political structures become embodied in the technology implies the technology was neutral at some point. Also, these socio-economic structures are equivocal and open to interpretation. This risks substituting technological determinism with social and political determinants.

## - Structuration and actor network theory

The perspectives introduced so far understand the process of ERP implementation, or users’ reactions to these systems, with recourse to structural explanations (critical success factors/process innovation accounts and user acceptance of ERP systems) or the dialectic between structure and agency (processual accounts, social shaping and critical perspectives). In contrast, structuration perspectives reframe structure and agency as a duality. For example, Dery et al. (2006b) applied the ‘technologies in practice’ lens to explore how branch managers understood their interaction with an ERP system. Similarly, actor network perspectives reject the social–technical dichotomy. Elbanna (2006) applied this framework to explore improvisation during the implementation of an ERP system. However, implying that structures are inscribed or embedded in artefacts ‘fixes one half of the duality in which action and structure are seen to be inseparably linked’ (Jones, 1999: 109). Furthermore, although these perspectives presuppose social construction, they have little to say about how ERP systems are constructed through language.

## Post-essentialist perspectives

These perspectives reject the idea that the properties of technologies can be known objectively, on the grounds that they are accessible only through our interpretations and descriptions of them. In short, the capabilities and effects of these systems are taken to be interpretively flexible and constructed through language.

## - Discursive perspectives

Unfortunately, there is a paucity of discursive studies that have explored how language is used to construct ERP systems. The advantage of these perspectives (e.g., Grant et al., 2006; Shepherd, 2006) is that they illustrate how talk is organised to legitimise or undermine ERP implementation. Moreover, they counter the determinist rhetoric surrounding ERP implementation. However, within the wider IT literature, post-essentialist perspectives have been criticised for privileging agency explanations and inadequately attending to wider social and economic structures (Kling, 1992). However, a counter argument offered by post-essentialists is that global factors, such as class, ethnicity and gender are themselves produced through discourse (Grint and Woolgar, 1997).

## Summary of perspectives towards understanding ERP implementation

In this review we argued that technological determinism is common within the ERP literature. Critical success factor and process innovation models, and studies of user acceptance of ERP treat ERP systems as a black box with the technical capabilities to bring about beneficial organisational effects. While anti-essentialist accounts (social shaping, critical, structuration and actor-network perspectives) attempt to open the ERP black box, most treat these systems as coming to embody, or having inscribed into them, social conditions over the course of their development. This makes it unclear which properties of ERP systems are obdurate and which are interpretively flexible. Third, although anti-essentialist perspectives often assume social construction, they typically fail to explore how the properties of ERP systems are constructed by their users. Indeed, there is little research into how ERP systems are discursively constructed. Fourth, there are few studies of end users’ reactions to ERP systems. Moreover, the majority of research is quantitative and is rarely compared with qualitative data on users’ interpretations. Table 1 summarises the findings of this review. Each theoretical perspective is differentiated according to: (1) Its ontological perspective (essentialist, anti-essentialist, and post-essentialist); (2) The form of explanation (e.g., structure vs agency); and, (3) Their treatment of power, politics, and conflict (unitary, radical, or pluralist). Key assumptions, benefits, limitations and examples of each perspective are also described. In the following section, we introduce the organisational context before elaborating on the research methodology.

## Organisational context

The study focused on the implementation of the Human Resources module of a leading ERP system. This was introduced across the UK sites of a multinational company, employing in excess of 37,000 people. The project affected 12 company sites, 21 areas of the business and just under 10,000 users. Its objectives were similar to those identified in other studies (Ross, 1999; Adam and O’Doherty, 2000). They included: cost savings in the region of d17 million per annum, systems integration, harmonisation and streamlining of business processes, replacement of paper systems, centralisation of payroll processing, greater visibility and integrity of data, improved reporting, and reduced administration in the HR department. The project affected typical HR activities, including: selection and recruitment, payroll and pensions, reward and remuneration, travel and expenses, personal development, and sickness and absence management. A major business process change was the decision to devolve several tasks from HR and administrative staff. Specifically, all employees in the United Kingdom were expected to use the system to update their own personal data (address, bank account details, telephone numbers, and so on), book their own UK travel and accommodation and enter their holidays, sickness, overtime, and expense claims.

## Methodology

Philosophically, we adopt a post-essentialist understanding of technological change (see Grint and Woolgar, 1995). We argue for epistemological constructivism on pragmatic grounds; it is impossible to step outside of language and arrive at an objective understanding of the social world. Consequently, discourse is understood here as constructing ERP systems, rather than reflecting their essential properties. Consequently, our approach would be located in row 8 of Table 1.

We opted for a multi-method design, incorporating semi-structured interviews, focus groups, and questionnaires. Discourse analysis of the interviews and focus groups was used to compare justifications for the implementation produced by members of the ERP project team with end users’ reactions to the system. This enabled us to illustrate the discursive techniques used to formulate constructions of the ERP system and how these constructions were performative in legitimising or undermining the implementation. Statistical analysis of the questionnaire data was used to establish the key predictors influencing users’ reactions to the ERP system. However, in contrast to previous research, we were able to determine whether these findings were corroborated by, or conflicted with, the in-depth qualitative analysis. The remainder of this section describes the methods of data collection and analysis in more detail.

## Semi-structured interviews and focus groups

Thirty-nine interviews and eight focus groups were conducted between January and November 2002. The participants were selected using snowball sampling as the company was restructuring its workforce and care had to be taken to ensure that employees were not invited to participate if they had been given notice of redundancy. There were 63 participants, from 5 company sites in the UK, 11 areas of the business, and a wide variety of roles. They included seven members of the ERP implementation team, secretaries, engineers, payroll administrators, line managers, HR professionals, purchasers, financial accountants, software designers, corporate lawyers, and external consultants. The interviews and focus groups were conducted at the participants’ place of work and varied in duration between 35 and 155 min. All were tape recorded and fully transcribed. The focus groups were homogeneous (e.g., composed of just HR employees) to explore whether interpretations of the technology were shared across similar roles.

## Discourse analysis

The interview and focus group data were analysed using a form of discourse analysis known as systematic constructionism (Potter, 1996). Analytically, this is broader than conversation analysis but more focused on the processes of fact construction than ‘the Foucauldian notion of discourse’ (Potter, 1996: 105). It involved scouring the transcripts for interpretative repertoires, or ‘systematically related sets of terms, often with stylistic and grammatical coherence’ (Potter, 1996: 116). It is important to be reflexive about how the data produced were shaped by the organisation of the interviews and focus groups. Both explored argumentative contexts designed to explore participants’ interpretations of: the objectives of the implementation; whether it had been managed effectively; satisfaction with the ERP system and new business processes; and the impact of the implementation on participants’ roles. Two key interpretative repertoires that emerged from the discourse analysis are presented here: (1) ERP as improving efficiency and (2) Administration as everyone’s responsibility. We illustrate the interpretative flexibility of the ERP implementation by showing how these repertoires were countered by end users ‘reciprocally positioned’ within them (see Dick and Cassell, 2002). Unsurprisingly, the first repertoire emerged from the first argumentative context, which explored what participants thought the objectives of the implementation were. Similarly, the second repertoire emerged from discussions surrounding the third and fourth augmentative contexts, which investigated participants’ satisfaction with the ERP system and new business processes and the impact of the implementation on their roles. This illustrates the importance of reflecting on how the organisation of the data collection influences the data produced. The extracts presented in this paper were chosen to best illustrate the variety of rhetorical strategies used by speakers to construct and undermine these repertoires. Using systematic constructionism to focus on participants’ talk enables one to explore how the capabilities of ERP systems, and their organisational effects, are discursively constructed.

## Development of the questionnaires

The majority of measures included in the questionnaire had been validated in earlier research into users’ reactions to new technologies. As discussed earlier, the selection of predictors in previous studies of users’ reactions to ERP has been partial. Consequently, a diverse range of predictors was included in this study. In addition, four outcome variables were included. The variables were delineated into six groups.

## A. Biographical and situational variables

These included age, gender, ethnicity, company site the participant is located at, length of service in the company, job tenure and whether participants were line managers. These variables were not purely included to control for potential confounding effects. For example, age and job grade have been shown to be significant predictors of users’ reactions to new technology when other variables were controlled for (e.g., Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1991; Clegg et al., 1997).

## B. Earlier experiences

Earlier experiences have been linked to user acceptance of IT systems (e.g., Dolan and Tizner, 1988). General attitudes towards computers were adapted from Clegg et al. (1997). Trustworthiness of employees was a bespoke scale included to measure perceptions of whether employees could be trusted to enter their own overtime, expenses, holidays and sickness into the system unsupervised. Participants were also asked to indicate whether they had previously used this ERP system.

## C. Job impacts

The impact of a new system on job content has been linked to users’ reactions to IT (e.g., Majchrzak, 1990). Impact on efficiency in your area, impact on skill demands in your area, and impact on access to information in your area were bespoke scales. Measures were also included to capture the different roles users were expected to perform in the ERP system as a part of their jobs (see variables 10–19 in Table 2). For example, the HR’s role (variable 15) comprised several tasks including recruitment activities and supporting employees’ personal development.

## D. Satisfaction with the ERP system

Measures of satisfaction with the ERP system were included in the study as they have been found to predict users reactions to IT. Perceived ease of use, system flexibility, system performance and perceived usefulness were adapted from Horton (2001). Effort demands, error demands, consequences of errors, and anxiety about making errors were adapted from Clegg et al. (1997).

## E. Satisfaction with change management

Evidence suggests that effective management of change leads to more successful IT implementation outcomes (Holman et al., 2000). User involvement, satisfaction with training, and satisfaction with communication were adapted from Clegg et al. (1997).

## F. Users’ reactions to ERP implementation

Four established measures of users’ reactions to IT systems were included: reported usage, satisfaction with the implementation, commitment to the system and competence using the system (see Clegg et al., 1997).

As discussed earlier in the paper, reported usage has been the focus of previous studies of users’ reaction to ERP systems (e.g., Bagchi et al., 2003). Therefore, it was appropriate to include this for comparison. However, as far as we are aware, the other three variables have not been the focus of earlier research into users’ reactions to ERP systems.

As Podsakoff et al. (2003) observes, it is important to mitigate for the effects of common method biases during questionnaire design. In this study, socially desirable responding was addressed by guaranteeing participants anonymity. Item characteristic effects were reduced by avoiding double barrelled, ambiguous or socially desirable items and by using different response scales. Finally, intermixing of items from different scales was avoided to lessen item context effects.

## Pilot study

The questionnaire was piloted in November 2002 with 11 employees from four company sites. Participants were asked to fill in the questionnaire and provide feedback on the item wording, layout, and clarity of the instructions. All of the pilot questionnaires were returned and minor changes were made to the instructions and the wording of a small number of items.

## Questionnaire participants

A sampling frame of all active users in the United Kingdom (9346 employees) was downloaded from the ERP system. Random stratified sampling was used to select a proportionate number of users from each site and area of the business, because although some of the company sites had over 4000 employees, others had less than 20. The sample comprised 1356 employees, distributed across all 12 company sites and 21 areas of the business. Paper questionnaires were distributed to participants from the company’s internal mail centre in December 2002. Four hundred forty were returned, a response rate of 32.4%. The participants were 82.5% male and their ages ranged from 22 to 62 years (M ¼ 40.78, SD ¼ 9.2). Job tenure varied from less than 1 to 42 years (M ¼ 4.75, SD ¼ 5.47) while their length of service in the company ranged from less than 1 to 47 years (M ¼ 17.53, SD ¼ 10.26). The participants were distributed across eight company sites and 96.5% of them described themselves as White-British. A t-test revealed no significant difference between the mean ages of respondents versus non-respondents. While the cross tabulation for ethnicity was not significant, $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was significant for gender (po0.05) and company site the participant was located at (po0.01). A significantly greater proportion of females (40.3%) responded compared with males (30.9%). Similarly, the percentage of respondents ranged widely from 7.3% to 45.3% across sites. This constitutes a potential source of bias, limiting the strength of the inferences that can be made about the population.

## Analysis of the questionnaire data

Exploratory factor analysis was used to determine the grouping of items into measurement scales (Fabrigar et al., 1999). The common factor extraction model used was maximum likelihood with oblique (direct oblimin) rotation. As recommended in recent reviews of factor analysis (Fabrigar et al., 1999; Conway and Huffcutt, 2003) the number of factors extracted was determined by using a combination of techniques. Multiple solutions were evaluated by comparing extractions using Kaiser’s criterion (eigenvalue41), solutions with different numbers of factors and Cattell’s screen test. As Fabrigar et al. (1999) observe, a variable to factor ratio of greater than 4:1 is desirable for a reliable factor structure. This criterion was satisfied in this study. Harman’s single factor test was also used to test for common method variance. For each set of item groupings that emerged from the factor analysis Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to assess the reliabilities of the scales.

Next, a series of univariate ANOVAs (Bonferroni adjustment) were performed to test whether the company site the participant was located at was significantly associated with the dependent variables. Before computing the zero-order correlation coefficients, categorical variables with more than two categories, such as the roles employees used the system for were dummy coded (variables 10–19 in Table 2). Continuous variables with severely non-normal distributions were subjected to appropriate transformations (see Table 2 for details).

Standard multiple regression analysis was used to test whether variables from each group of predictors (A, B, C, D, and E) were significantly associated with the outcome variables in group F. This was appropriate in this context for a number of reasons. Previous research has shown that variables from all of the groups of predictors in this study have significantly predicted users’ reactions to IT when other variables were controlled for (Clegg et al., 1997). Moreover, as far as we are aware there are no studies in an ERP context that have explored the antecedents of satisfaction with the system, commitment to the system or competence using the system. Finally, the main purpose of the analysis was to identify key predictors of each outcome variable to see whether the findings were corroborated by, or conflicted with, the discourse analysis of users’ reactions to the system. This enabled us to identify constructs that could be incorporated in future studies of users’ reactions to ERP systems. Consequently, we favoured an exploratory approach to the statistical analysis over techniques such as structural equation modelling which adopt a hypothetico-deductive approach, demanding the specification of a priori structural and measurement models. Regressions were firstly used to test whether variables from each group of predictors were associated with the outcome variables in group F at the po0.01 significance level. For example, all variables in group A were fed into regression equations predicting each outcome variable in group F. Equivalent regression procedures were used for groups B, C, D, and E. However, this ignores possible covariance between variables from different groups of predictors. Spurious effects were controlled for by simultaneously entering variables from groups A, B, C, D, and E to identify those which remained significantly associated with each dependent variable. Multicollinearity was tested for by computing the tolerance and variance inflation factor.

## Findings

The findings of the study are delineated into two main sections. First, we present the statistical analysis of end users’ reactions to the ERP system, identifying key predictors of the outcome variables. Second, we discuss the findings of the discourse analysis of the interviews and focus groups. We begin this section by explaining how two interpretative repertoires, ‘ERP as improving efficiency’ and ‘Administration is everyone’s responsibility’ were constructed by members of the implementation team as justification for the decision to implement the ERP system and devolve tasks from the HR department and administrative staff to all employees. Next, we illustrate the interpretative flexibility of the ERP implementation by showing how these repertoires were countered by end users ‘reciprocally positioned’ within them (see Dick and Cassell, 2002). Finally, we reflect on the findings of the statistical analysis, considering whether they are corroborated by, or conflict with, users’ discursive reactions to the implementation.

## Statistical analysis of users’ reactions to the ERP system

## - EFA, reliability coefficients and univariate ANOVA

The EFA revealed satisfactory discriminant and convergent validity of the measures after items which had cross-loaded or had weak loadings on all factors were removed. System flexibility (Group D) was removed after it was found that it cross-loaded with perceived ease of use (Group D). Crossloadings were also found for items from scales designed to measure perceived usefulness (Group D), commitment to the system (Group F) and satisfaction with the implementation (Group F). Items from these scales were combined into a single measure of satisfaction with the implementation. Appendix A illustrates the 15 factor solution extracted during the analysis, the corresponding questionnaire items and Likert response scales for each item. Harman’s single factor test revealed that that no one factor accounted for the majority of covariance among the measures. The reliability of the measures retained was within acceptable limits, ranging from a ¼ 0.77 to 0.93 (see Table 2, for the reliability coefficients). A univariate ANOVA revealed that the site the participants were located at had no significant effect on any of the dependent variables. However, the strongest statement that can be made about this finding is that there is no evidence to suggest there is a statistically significant main effect in the dependent variables. The observed power in these analyses ranged from 0.05 to 0.43, whereas statistical power of X0.8 is desirable at the po0.05 significance level to avoid type II errors.

## - Correlations

The zero-order correlation matrix is presented in Table 2. This reveals that at least one variable from each group of predictors A, B, C, D, and E were significantly correlated with one or more of the dependent variables in group F. However, the strongest correlations were found between variables in groups C, D, and E with the dependent variables. In group C, there was a moderate correlation between HR’s role and reported usage of the system. Impact on efficiency in your area was also correlated with satisfaction with the implementation and competence using the system. Of the measures of satisfaction with the ERP system (group D), the strongest correlation was between perceived ease of use and competence using the system. Finally, in group E, user involvement was correlated with reported usage and satisfaction with the implementation. Similarly, satisfaction with training and support and satisfaction with communication were correlated with satisfaction with the implementation and competence using the system. The correlations revealed no evidence of multicollinearity. ## - Regressions

Table 3 presents the results of the regressions for each of the three dependent variables. Rows 1–5 give the results of regressions for each dependent variable where only variables from each group of predictors were included (Groups A, B, C, D, and E, respectively). For example, row 1 presents the results of regressions for each dependent variable including only biographical and situational variables (group A). Row 6 shows the results of regressions that controlled for spurious effects by simultaneously entering the variables in rows 1–5. There was no evidence of multicollinearity as the diagnostic tests revealed the lowest tolerance was 0.38 (variance inflation factor of 2.61).

The results in rows 1–5 show that at least one variable from each group of predictors significantly predicted each dependent variable, with the exception of satisfaction with the implementation (column 2, rows 1 and 2). However, as the results in row 6 demonstrate, many of these relationships disappeared when spurious effects were controlled for. There is no evidence that biographical and situational variables (group A) predict the dependent variables. However, there is support for a link between earlier experiences (group B) and users’ reactions to ERP implementation. Previous use of the ERP system was found to be an important predictor of reported usage (column 1, row 6). Moreover, general attitudes towards computers remained a significant predictor of competence using the system when the effects of other variables were controlled for (column 3, row 6). This confirms findings from the wider literature (e.g., Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1991; Clegg et al., 1997).

There is also evidence that job impacts (group C) predict users’ reactions to ERP. For example, HR’s role remained significant predictors of reported usage (column 1, row 6). This is understandable when one considers that ERP implementations are typically combined with business process engineering designed to harmonise organisational processes. Once these processes have been defined, employees are mapped to roles, which they are expected to perform in the system. In the context of this implementation of the HR module of an ERP system, it is unsurprising that this variable predicts reported usage. This role would be undertaken by HR professionals who are likely to be heavy users of the system. This finding has important implications for research into the antecedents of ERP usage. Specifically, future applications of the technology acceptance model and theory of reasoned action should consider incorporating measures designed to capture the role that employees are expected to perform on the system. Of the other variables in group C, impact on skill demands in your area significantly predicted satisfaction with the implementation (row 6, column 2). Impact on efficiency in your area predicted satisfaction with the implementation and competence using the system (row 6, columns 2 and 3).

Measures of satisfaction with the ERP system (group E) were strong predictors of all the dependent variables. For example, perceived ease of use was the strongest predictor of reported usage and competence using the system (row 6, columns 1 and 3, respectively). The finding that perceived ease of use predicts reported usage corroborates findings from the wider IT literature (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). System performance predicted competence using the system (row 6, column 3) while effort demands predicted all three dependent variables (row 6, columns 1, 2, and 3). This finding confirms previous studies that have found that cognitive demands influence user’s reactions to IT systems (e.g., Clegg et al., 1997).

Finally, of the measures of satisfaction with change management (Group E) user involvement was found to be an important predictor of both reported usage and satisfaction with the system (row 6, columns 1 and 2, respectively). The finding that user involvement predicts ERP usage corroborates previous studies (Bagchi et al., 2003) and findings in the wider literature on technological change (e.g., Butler, 2004).

In the following section, we present the findings of the discourse analysis. We begin by discussing the rhetorical devices used to justify the implementation of the system and key business process changes that affected all users.

## Discourse analysis of interviews and focus groups

A key business process change which accompanied the implementation was the requirement that all employees should use it to update their own personal data (address, bank account details, telephone numbers, so on), book their own UK travel and accommodation and enter their holidays, sickness, overtime, and expense claims. We begin this section by illustrating how the interpretative repertoires ‘ERP as improving efficiency’ and ‘Administration is all employees’ responsibility’ were constructed by members of the implementation team as justification for the decision to implement the ERP system and devolve these tasks from the HR department and administrative staff to all employees.

## - Justifying the implementation of the ERP system

The extract below is from an interview with the implementation manager (IM), who was responsible for keeping track of the progress of the project. In the context of interviews and focus groups, discourse analysts understand the researcher as an active participant, who plays a significant role in shaping the interaction and influencing the data produced. Therefore, it is important to be reflexive about how the constructions produced may have been influenced by the researcher’s positioning as an ex-ERP consultant and Ph.D. student interested in the social and psychological aspects of change management. It is possible IM positioned the researcher as an investigator, searching for evidence of a disproportionate focus on technology over the social aspects of change. This may explain why IM constructs the decision to implement the system as motivated by a desire for a leaner, more efficient organisation that empowers and trusts its employees:

IM: this is all about making ((company name)) leaner and more efficient, transferring a lot of the responsibilities of work from the manager down to the individual and trusting them

(Implementation manager)

A second interpretative repertoire, ‘Administration is all employees’ responsibility’ was used to justify the decision to devolve administrative tasks to all employees. For example, in the extract below, the implementation manager, IM argues this process change simply returns responsibility for maintenance of the data to where it should be, with the individual employee. Arguing they should be doing this ‘as a manager or an individual anyway’ constructs ‘managers and ‘individuals’ (other employees) as shirking responsibility for ‘their own data’. This suggests these tasks should have always been a part employees’ work. By constructing line managers as not taking responsibility ‘for their own people’, IM also implies they were not doing their job properly:

I: What do you think this project is trying to transform? Now it sounds quite abstract.

IM: It’s down to having people responsible for their own data and their own, having people responsible for their own line areas as it were, line managers responsible for their own people. And having the responsibility and the authority to do things that they should be doing as a manager or an individual anyway. So, I think it’s transferring some of the responsibility from HR and payroll, and things like that, and putting it where it should be, with the individual.

(Implementation manager)

In summary, members of the implementation team normalised the decision to implement the ERP system (see Potter, 1996: 177). They achieved this by arguing the project was motivated by the rational pursuit of a leaner and more efficient organisation. Similarly, the decision to transfer key tasks from HR and administrative staff to all employees was constructed as trusting and empowering individuals to manage their own data. In the next section, we illustrate the interpretative flexibility of the implementation by showing how it was constructed quite differently by end users. Where appropriate, we consider whether the findings reinforce, or conflict with, the statistical analysis of end users’ reactions to the system.

In the extract above, taken from an interview with an engineering team leader (TL), the ERP system is constructed as providing few benefits for users. By drawing a boundary between work and non-work, and formulating the system as an inefficient use of resources which takes ‘hours away from doing the job’, TL counters the repertoires ‘Administration is all employees’ responsibility’ and ‘ERP as improving efficiency’. This provides some support for the finding that impact on efficiency in your area (Group C) was a key predictor of satisfaction with the implementation (Table 3, column 2, row 6).

TL also alludes to the fact that the system placed significant demands on employees. Indeed, the process of claiming expenses was identified as particularly onerous by participants. Before the introduction of the ERP system, employees filled out paper expense claim forms and passed them to their line manager, who checked the claim and signed it if they were satisfied. Thereafter, the form would either be passed to a more senior manager for countersignature, or forwarded directly to the finance department for payment. However, post-ERP implementation, all employees were expected enter their claims directly into the system. In the extract below, a line manager (LM) is reciprocally positioned within the repertoire ‘ERP as improving efficiency’. LM constructs the procedure for claiming expenses as placing heavy demands on users:

LM: The difficultly we have, as users, is fucking expenses, which is a nightmare for us all

I: Tell me a little bit about that, why is it causing you problems?

LM: I am getting bombarded with complaints about the difficulty that they are having inputting their own expenses, poor training and communication, if they make a mistake it seems to be sealed in, they can’t correct it [y] ((colleagues’ name)) had a really difficult time in the paperwork system and now we have got him on the electronic system he really struggles. Everybody I have met have said that it takes three or four hours for them to do the completion of their first claim

(Line manager)

In the extract above, LM uses various rhetorical techniques to reinforce the argument that the system is demanding. First, rather than simply receiving complaints about the system, LM uses extremitisation (Pomerantz, 1986) to create an image of being ‘bombarded with complaints’ about the difficulty of inputting expenses. Then, LM builds his entitlement to criticise the excessive demands placed on users by describing how he has personally witnessed a colleague really struggling with the new process. He then uses what Potter (1996) describes as a non-specific plural ‘everybody I have met’. This enables him to corroborate his assertion that the poor usability of the system and consequent demands it places on users directly result in colleagues taking ‘three or four hours’ to complete their first expense claim. LM also highlights the demands when inputting data, describing mistakes as getting ‘sealed in’. Specifically, if employees entered their expenses incorrectly they no longer had the safety net of their manager’s signature to fall back on. If they claimed too little or too much money, and didn’t notice their error, the claim would be paid anyway. In the extract below from a focus group, a purchaser, PC, illustrates this problem using active voicing (Wooffitt, 1992) to corroborate this argument; ‘oh, did I say that or did I do something different?’. This builds the authenticity of the account by creating an image of PC questioning whether they made an error and checking the paper records to confirm this:

PC: ‘Prior to it ((the ERP system)) there was always a check, so you did something on paper, you could look at it, you could maybe turn over a couple pages and say oh, did I say that or did I do something different? There was always a second check in that it went through your boss, before it moved on, if there were any discrepancies they were sorted there and then

(Purchaser)

These findings provide a possible explanation for why effort demands predicted all three outcome variables and perceived ease of use predicted both competence and reported usage (Table 3, row 6, columns 1, 2, and 3, respectively).

The line manager, LM also alludes to ‘poor training and communication’. End users described the training as generic, focused on button pushing and generally not customised to meet the local needs of different areas of the business. Similarly, communication was described as inadequate with one participant constructing it as ‘spasmodic and haphazard since go-live’. However, there was limited support for this finding from the statistical analysis, which revealed that the effects of satisfaction with communication and satisfaction with training on the dependent variables (Table 3, row 5, columns 2 and 3) disappeared when spurious effects were controlled for (Table 3, row 6, columns 2 and 3). Nevertheless, the importance of effective change management is reflected by the finding that user involvement was found to predict both satisfaction with the implementation and reported usage of the system (Table 3, row 6, columns 1 and 2).

In summary, the discourse analysis of semi-structured interviews and focus groups illustrates the interpretive flexibility of ERP implementation. It showed how two interpretative repertoires, ‘ERP as improving efficiency’ and ‘Administration is all employees’ responsibility’ were constructed by members of the project team to legitimise the decisions to implement the system and devolve administrative tasks to all employees. Moreover, it demonstrated how these repertoires were countered by end users reciprocally positioned within them (Dick and Cassell, 2002). They formulated the system as difficult to use, offering them few benefits, impairing their efficiency by placing excessive demands on them, and distracting them from their core work. A variety of micro-discursive techniques were instrumental in building and countering these repertoires, including; normalisation (the rational aim of the project is making the organisation leaner and more efficient); negotiating boundaries (work/non-work); extremitisation (‘bombarded with complaints’); non-specific plurals (‘everybody I have met’); and active voicing (‘say oh, did I say that or did I do something different?’). The multi-method approach enabled us to compare the statistical analysis of users’ reactions to the ERP system with the discourse analysis of interview and focus groups with end users. The findings showed how a variety of variables designed to measure earlier experiences (group B), job impacts (group C), satisfaction with the system (group D), and satisfaction with change management (group E) remained significant predictors of users’ reactions to ERP implementation when spurious effects were controlled for. In the following section, we discuss the contributions and limitations of this study and its implications for future research and practice.

## Discussion and implications

This paper opened with a review of perspectives that have sought to understand the process of ERP implementation and users’ reactions to these systems. This revealed several limitations within the literature. First, technological determinism is endemic; ERP systems are often treated as a black box with the capability to deliver positive organisational outcomes (e.g., Adam and O’Doherty, 2000; Parr and Shanks, 2000). Second, while recent constructionist perspectives have countered this determinism (e.g., Dery et al., 2006b; Elbanna, 2006) they have little to say about how ERP systems are actually produced through discourse. Few studies have attempted to open the ERP black box and explore the rhetorical function constructions of these systems perform in legitimising or undermining the implementation process. Finally, despite the pervasive of these systems there are surprisingly few studies of end users’ reactions to them (Bagchi et al., 2003).

In response to these limitations, this study presented the findings of a multi-method analysis of the implementation of the HR’s module of an ERP system. It adopted a postessentialist theoretical perspective. In contrast to the majority of previous research, this treats language as constructing ERP systems, rather than reflecting their essential technical properties. A form of discourse analysis, systematic constructionism (Potter, 1996) was used to analyse interviews and focus groups with both members of the ERP implementation team and end users of the system.

This opened the ERP black box by demonstrating how the capabilities of these systems, and their organisational effects, are interpretively flexible. Moreover, it illustrated the important rhetorical function constructions of these systems perform in justifying or undermining ERP implementation. The findings of the discourse analysis of end users’ reactions were compared with statistical analysis of questionnaires designed to explore the predictors of established measures of users’ reactions to information technology. This helped to identify constructs that could be incorporated in future studies of users’ reactions to ERP systems.

Before discussing the implications of the study, it is important to acknowledge some of its limitations. The cross-tabulations revealed significant differences in expected response rates for gender and the company site participants were located at. As the observed power in these analyses was o0.8, this presents the risk of type II errors. While these limitations must be acknowledged, our multimethod design enabled us to compare the quantitative and qualitative analysis. This gives us added confidence in our findings. Finally, one could argue that statistical analysis is incommensurable with our ontological and epistemological perspective. However, as Deetz (1996) observes, although quantitative methods are synonymous with the search for universal truth, dropping this pretence may prove useful.

We believe the paper carries several implications for future research and practice. First, it illustrates the advantage of adopting a post-essentialist perspective towards understanding the implementation of enterprise resource planning systems. By demonstrating the interpretative flexibility of an ERP implementation, it problematises essentialist and anti-essentialist perspectives that treat these systems as having definite capabilities that enable them to bring about specific effects. In short, it illustrates the need for further discursive analyses to unpack the rhetoric surrounding these systems. Second, the research has implications for future applications of theoretical models designed to test the antecedents of ERP usage (e.g., technology acceptance model, theory of reasoned action). The results confirmed the importance of perceived ease of use as an important predictor of system usage. However, future studies should consider incorporating measures of effort demands, user involvement and previous experience using the ERP system into their analysis. Moreover, the findings also suggest that the role that users are expected to perform in the system is likely to be an important determinant of system usage. Finally, the analysis shows the value of combining qualitative and quantitative data in future studies of users’ reactions to these systems. The discourse analysis both corroborated and conflicted with the findings of the statistical analysis. For example, effort demands predicted all three of the dependent variables, reported usage of the system, satisfaction with the implementation and competence using the system. This was supported by the finding that end users constructed the system as placing excessive demands on them, especially when claiming expenses. However, the discourse analysis also revealed considerable dissatisfaction with communication and training. These were not found to be significant predictors of any of the outcome variables in the study.

An important practical corollary for organisations considering adopting ERP systems is to reflect on how these systems are constructed by their promogulators and be wary of the hype that accompanies them. Despite the pervasive of these systems, and projected growth of the ERP market, the difficulties organisations experience in implementing them successfully are well documented. Moreover, a recent report on ‘The Challenges of Complex IT Projects’ by the Royal Academy of Engineering and the British Computer Society suggested that ‘a mere 16% of IT projects can be considered successful’ (Butler, 2004).

Consequently, a major consideration is whether or not organisations really need the new IT system on offer (Clegg and Shepherd, 2007). They argue that challenging questions of the following nature should be asked – Do we really need this system? Is this investment the best use of our resources?

The results of this study highlight several issues that should be considered by managers interested in enhancing the process of ERP implementation. Perceived ease of use, effort demands, user involvement, impact on efficiency in your area, impact on skills demands in your area, general attitudes towards computers, system performance, and the role employees were expected to perform on the system were all found to be significant predictors of end users reactions to the implementation. The importance of perceived ease of use is well established within the IT community. However, customising ERP systems to enhance their usability is not popular with practitioners as it is expensive and can have implications during system upgrades (Scott and Kaindl, 2000). Consequently, this magnifies the importance of adopting a systemic approach to change. In this project, limited consideration was given to the impact of the implementation on job designs and the work organisation of users. This influenced end users’ reactions to the system who reported the new system and tasks they were expected to perform on it, offered them few benefits, were excessively demanding and negatively affected their efficiency. Finally, the finding that user involvement predicted reported usage and satisfaction with the implementation reinforces the need for managers to actively encourage users to participate in the design process (Clegg and Shepherd, 2007). As they observe, they should be encouraged to ‘pull’ these systems into the business, rather than managers ‘pushing’ them onto the user community.
