# The dark side of computer-mediated control

João Vieira da Cunha,\*<sup>,†</sup> Andrea Carugati<sup>†,‡</sup> & Aurelie Leclercq-Vandelannoitte<sup>‡</sup>

\*Universidade Europeia, Estrada da Correia, 53, Lisbon, Portugal,email: joao.cunha @europeia.pt, <sup>†</sup>Aarhus School of Business, Fuglesangs Allé, 4, Aarhus, Denmark, and <sup>‡</sup>IESEG School of Management (LEM, CNRS UMR 8179), 3, Rue de la Digue, Lille, France

Abstract. Research on the dark side of computer-mediated control has explained the consequences of computer-mediated control when work is tightly coupled with its electronic representation because information systems record work automatically. Our study complements prior research by addressing the dark side of computer mediated control when work and its electronic representation are loosely coupled, because it is employees who report their work in IT systems. Data from a 15-month ethnographic study of the appropriation of a customer relationship management system in the sales department of a large organization reveal that loosely versus tightly coupled computer-mediated control produce key differences that re-specify the dark side of computer-mediated control.

Keywords: information technology, organizational control, dark side, loose coupling, tight coupling, representation of work

## INTRODUCTION

This article deals with the dark side of computer-mediated control (CMC) in organizations. The dark side refers to negative, unintended, dormant characteristics possessed by individuals or groups that can make consequences abnormal and deviant from their planned and explicit purpose (Pritchard, 2004). The dark side of CMC are the negative, unexpected and unintended effects of managers’ attempts at using information technology (IT) to monitor employees. Research on the dark side of CMC follows research on emergent consequences of IT in calling attention to the unintended effects of technology on organizations that appear and endure as employees and managers apply IT to address their everyday challenges at work (eg. Zuboff, 1988; Sewell, 1998). However, research on the dark side of CMC departs from research on the emergent consequences of IT on organizations, because it focuses on the consequences of IT that are harmful for both managers and employees (Tarafdar et al., 2007; Linstead et al., 2014) and on the behaviours that are exploitative and deviant (eg. Ball & Wilson, 2000).

Prior literature notes the oppressing and alienating aspects of ‘disciplinary technologies’ for employees (Braverman, 1974; Beniger, 1986; Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Kallinikos, 2005;

Weckert, 2005). In studies that explore the dark side of CMC, a common assumption holds that technology automatically records the work activities of employees (Zuboff, 1988; Poster, 1990; Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Lyon, 1994). Such a tight coupling between work and its representation is common, but the assumption ignores the question of whether IT that establishes a looser coupling between work and its representation can generate different dark sides. Research has surfaced changes in the principles and the goals of IT implementation in organizations, including processes to implement and improve computer-mediated control, towards more decentralization, more interactivity and increased social focus (Nan, 2011; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). These changes can lead to this looser coupling and eventually to a different dark side of CMC.

Research on the consumerization of IT in organizations has shown that companies increasingly tolerate employees who prefer to use their own IT to work rather than use the IT that their company provides, such as employees who use their own smartphones instead of their company’s cellphones (Harris et al., 2012).

Research has also shown that companies are adopting technologies that employees can adapt to their own needs, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems that allow em ployees to create their own reporting practices (eg. Soh & Sia, 2004).

Finally, research has also emphasized the increased use of social media for knowledge sharing in organizations, which often complements but may occasionally replace ERP, sales force automation and incident tracking systems as a means to share knowledge among employees Zhao & Rosson, 2009).

Increasingly decentralized, interactive and social technologies loosen the coupling between work and its electronic representation, because they disperse data across information systems to which their company has limited access, and because they diversify the ways in which that information is encoded (eg. Jensen & Aanestad, 2007). In these conditions, managers can only exercise CMC if employees compile all of this dispersed information about their work and enter it using standardized reporting procedures in the information system that managers use to monitor their work (cf. Doolin, 2004). It is the need for this reporting work that creates a loose coupling between work and its electronic representation, which can have enough of an effect on CMC to change the negative, unintended and unexpected effects, which constitute its dark side as organizations increasingly adopt decentralized, interactive and social technologies. In the research reported next, we specify the dark side of CMC under these technologies, by drawing on a 15-month ethnographic study of the implementation of Siebel (a customer relationship man agement system) to address the following research question:

How does the dark side of computer-mediated control manifest when employees have to report their own work in their company’s information system?

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

In this section, we explain how to extend the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dark side of CMC by distinguishing two relationships between work and its electronic representation (cf. Berente and Yoo, 2012; Weick, 1976): tight coupling and loose coupling.

Work is tightly coupled with its representation when information systems record every work practice that employees do (eg. information systems reported in Bain & Taylor, 2000; Orlikowski, 1991; Sia et al., 2002). Work is loosely coupled with its representation when information systems only record those work practices that employees report (eg. information systems reported in Cunha, 2013a; Doolin, 2004; Orlikowski, 1996). Under both speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, CMC is an instance of organizational control, which Tannenbaum de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows (1962, p. 237):

Control processes help circumscribe idiosyncratic behaviors and keep them conformant with the rational plan of the organization. Organizations require a certain amount of conformity as well as the integration of diverse activities. It is the function of control to bring about conformance to organizational requirements and achievement of the ultimate goals of the organization.

Computer-mediated control is the exercise of control through electronic representations of employees’ work. The dark side of such organizational processes involves their dysfunctional, antiso cial outcomes, such as ‘situations in which people hurt other people, injustices are perpetuated and magni<sup>fi</sup>ed, and the pursuits of wealth, power and revenge lead people to behaviours that others can only see as unethical, illegal, despicable or reprehensible’ (Grif<sup>fi</sup>n & O’Leary-Kelly, 2004 p. xv).

## The dark side of CMC under tight coupling between work and its representation

Research has focused on the aspects of the dark side of CMC that are caused by a tight coupling between work and its electronic representation, in which exhaustive and detailed data about employees’ work practices and their achievements are produced automatically by technology without any human intervention. Research has studied the negative and unintended consequences of IT that helps employers control employees (Zuboff, 1988; Poster, 1990; Sewell, 1998), by recording their work. This exempts employees from the burden of creating such inputs while improving the manager’s ability to specify their employees’ work practices. Zuboff (1988, p. 9) describes the ‘informating property’ of IT by explaining:

The devices that automate by translating information into action also register data about those automated activities, thus generating new streams of information. […] The same systems that make it possible to automate of<sup>fi</sup>ce transactions also create a vast overview of an organization’s operations, with many levels of data coordinated and accessible for a variety of analytical efforts.

In these situations, companies trade off the potential of IT to loosen the constraints of prescribed procedures on employees’ work against automatically produced information that helps managers determine company strategy, improve organizational processes and avoid inef<sup>fi</sup>cient or inappropriate behaviours (Cunha, 2013a: Doolin, 2004: Orlikowski, 1991: Winiecki, 2004)

Facing the increased potential for centralized surveillance through such IT uses in organizations, labour process theory and critical studies denounce an IT-based superstructure of surveillance that not only allows managers to scrutinize employees’ activities and analyze individual work tasks but also implicitly forces employees to monitor and enforce their own compliance (Poster, 1990). This structure has been described through the metaphor of the panopticon (Foucault, 1977): a prison design, where guards can observe all prisoners without the prisoners being aware that they are being watched (Bentham, 1995). The panopticon is characterized by a sentiment of invisible omniscience, which encourages self-discipline (Foucault, 1977). Because inmates never know whether they are being observed, they remain on their best behaviour. This Foucauldian view of the panopticon, which is an archetype of social control (Brivot & Gendron, 2011), has inspired researchers to analyze the relationships between modern IT and the mechanisms for monitoring and normalizing modern organizations (Poster, 1990; Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Lyon, 1994; Webster, 1995; Sia et al., 2002). It suggests that the exposure imposed by IT puts employees on a stage, which they can exit only at the end of thei workday. They cope with this threat through self-discipline (Elmes et al., 2005) or anticipatory confor mity (Zuboff, 1988), which increases compliance and changes the nature of surveillance. It strengthens control by increasing the information that supervisors have about employees’ work (and that employees have about one another’s work; Sewell, 1998) but also by increasing the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of resistance and reducing the extent to which employees can conceive of resistance, improvisation or deviance as alternative means to cope with their everyday work challenges.

The parallel between CMC and a Foucauldian panopticon suggests the dark side of CMC in terms of domination, discipline, subordination, exploitation and alienation of employees (Sewel & Wilkinson, 1992; Webster & Robins, 1993; Sewell, 1998). Studies denounce the potential in vasion of employee privacy, loss of autonomy and breach of dignity, as well as impacts of CMC on workers' stress. health and iob satisfaction, which result from its contradictions with indepen dence and self-development (Garson, 1988; Hodson et al., 1999; Miller & Weckert, 2000; Ottensmeyer & Heroux, 1991). In this view, the dark side of CMC emerges in the negative personal experience that it imposes on employees, who are passive victims, ‘trapped in totalizing institutions combined with new, oppressive forms of regulation and surveillance’ (Warhurst & Thompson, 1998, p. 1962). These working conditions have some bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for organizations but also some potential negative impacts. They may ensure that employees achieve quantita tive targets, but they cannot guarantee that they achieve qualitative targets, because their demotivation, stress and standardized requirements may render them incapable of sensitive, responsive interactions with customers (Taylor & Bain, 1999) and reluctant and perhaps unable to engage in the small but necessary improvisations necessary to adjust prescribed procedures to the everyday challenges that employees face at work.

## The dark side of CMC under loose coupling between work and its representation

The tight coupling between work and its representation is but one possible con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the use of IT to monitor employees’ work. The chief alternative is an implementation of IT that requires employees to input records of their actions and their achievements themselves. In this view, employees replace technology as the authors of the electronic representations of their work, which managers use to enforce goals and procedures and to collect information for the formulation and evaluation of strategy. In this case, employees do not need information systems to do their work, and therefore, they can only prove compliance with prescribed procedure and prescribed achievements if they add to the everyday work the representation of work necessary to provide evidence that they comply with procedure and prescribed goals (Doolin, 2004; Cunha & Carugati, 2013). Orlikowski (1996, p.76) described the relationship between these two types of work by explaining that at Zeta, a company where she conducted an ethnographic study:

[…M]anagers began to evaluate the process documentation itself, not merely using it as an indicator of actions and strategies. In this way, they reinforced the new de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the customer support job as comprising both problem-solving and documentation. Indeed, keeping process documentations up to date was presented as just as critical, or even more important than problem solving.

Although some information about employees’ practice is automatically produced by IT, this information does not refer to their work practices, but instead to the practices for producing of electronic records of their actions and achievements in the information systems that their company uses to monitor work. This displacement of supervision, from employees’ work to their representation of their work, transforms information systems from a barrier into a means for supporting deviance, improvisation and manipulation (Cunha & Carugati, 2013).

Doolin (2004) shows that doctors used the data that they reported in their hospital’s informa tion system to demand more resources from their emploving hospital while refusing to repor enough about their work to support the relational processes of control that the hospital sought to encode in its information system. Orlikowski (1996) showed that employees can go a step further and actually use IT for ‘impression management,’ reversing the logic of surveillance from something that managers do to employees into a logic of self-presentation that employees do for their managers.

Thus, when work is loosely coupled with its representation, IT allows room for agency by em ployees and transforms control into a dynamic, situated, emergent process. The consequences of CMC then may be different and more subtle, insidious and unexpected than when work is tightly coupled with its representations. The study that we report next seeks to specify the dark side of CMC under such conditions, when it is employees rather than technologies who produce electronic representations of work.

## METHODS AND RESEARCH SETTING

The research we report was part of an ethnographic study of the implementation of a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system in a large European telecommunications company.

## Ethnography as a method to study computer-mediated control

Prior studies of the implementation of new technologies and their organizational conseguences con<sup>fi</sup>rm the appropriateness of using ethnography (e.g. Orlikowski, 1996; Carlile, 2004; Levina & Vaast, 2005). In particular, ethnographic studies of people’s use of IT to address their situated challenges can accommodate different, even contradictory effects of IT on organizations (Orlikowski, 1991). Ethnographic studies are also popular for research on control, because they document how the actors, whose work is being monitored and evaluated, participate in the process (Zuboff, 1988; Orlikowski, 1991), which entails a dialectic of control:

Power within social systems which enjoy some continuity over time and space presumes regularized relations of autonomy and dependence between actors or collectivities in contexts of social interaction. But all forms of dependence offer some resources whereby those who are subordinate can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the activities of their superiors. This is what I call the dialectic o control in social systems (Giddens, 1986, p. 16).

Ethnographic studies of organizations point towards a pragmatic approach that enjoins researchers to build theory from observations of everyday practices, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the arrays of action that apply a set of rules and resources to address situated conditions for action (Giddens, 1986; Barnes, 2001). Ethnographic methods thus help researchers collect data about the practices people develop to adopt an information system in their work, as well as data about the structure and the situated challenges that shape those.

This study is based on a 15-month ethnography in a desk sales unit in the period (3 months) just preceding the implementation of CMC technology and the subsequent 12 months of usage of the technology.

## Research setting

DeskSales, the unit where the research that grounds this study was conducted, was an outcome of the Multiple Channels to Market Program, a large planned change process unfolding at Europe Telecom (or E-Tel, for short), the unit’s parent company. DeskSales was created to take on the low and medium complexity sales and sales-related work away from E-Tel’s <sup>fi</sup>eld sales force. Figure 1 shows the structure of E-Tel’s sales operations.

## Europe Telecom, the Multiple Channels to Market Program and Desksales

Europe Telecom is a large, European company in the information and telecommunications sector. In the 2013/2014 <sup>fi</sup>nancial year (which spanned from April 2013 to April 2014), its revenue was close to \$30 billion, generating around \$9 billion in pro<sup>fi</sup>t. It employed approximately 100 000 people, 92% of whom worked in the company’s domestic market. The Corporate Customers Division was the arm of E-Tel Retail (the part of the company that dealt with <sup>fi</sup>nal customers) for large corporate clients, which included many Fortune 500 companies. Most of the Corporate Customers Division’s 7000 employees worked in service call centres and <sup>fi</sup>eld service units; 800 were <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, and 80 worked as desk salespeople at DeskSales. Both the desk and <sup>fi</sup>eld sales units were divided by customers’ industry, with teams devoted to the retail, <sup>fi</sup>nance and technology and media sectors. In practice, each customer was allocated to an account team, headed by a <sup>fi</sup>eld sales manager who supervised two to <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople. Each account also was allocated a desk salesperson, who might be allocated to up to four accounts. Desk salespeople were grouped in teams headed by a DeskSales manager. These managers reported to the unit’s general manager.

In early 2002, E-Tel Corporate Customers Division launched the Multiple Channels to Marke Program, launched with the mandate of providing alternative channels to <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, who at the time were the single point of contact between E-Tel Corporate Customers and its clients. The overall goal was to reduce costs and improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. One of those channels was a desk sales unit which was implemented to reduce transaction costs and increase sales for the simple and cheaper products that E-Tel sold to its corporate customers.

Most of the desk salespeople were in their early twenties, and their previous work experience was mostly in customer service call centres. Desk salespeople had two prescribed set of tasks: to sell and to engage in sales-related work. Selling consisted of persuading customers to buy the products and services offered by E-Tel. Sales-related work involved any ancillary activities, such as creating sales plans, gathering information and contacting customers regularly to identify sales opportunities.

## Siebel: the software system implemented for performance and control

E-Tel implemented Siebel to assist desk salespeople in their sales and sales-related work, as well as to assess their performance. Siebel became operational 3 months after the start of the ethnography and was used throughout the period of observation. This CRM system is designed to help salespeople throughout the sales process by giving them a means to store and access information about their customer contacts, keep track of their present and past sales-related work and store and access information about open sales. In addition, Siebel is a management tool, through which managers can scrutinize and supervise salespeople’s sales and sales-related work.

As a self-reporting system, Siebel requires employees to report data about their sales opportunities, closed sales and sales calls. At E-Tel, salespeople were required to report sales calls, sales opportunities and closed sales. Desk salespeople only were required to report calls to customers and link them to a sales opportunity record. In this sense, the electronic records of salespeople’s calls to customers were only loosely coupled with their electronic representation, because employees could report calls to customers without placing them or make calls without recording them in Siebel.

The records of sales opportunities (and sales, which were records of sales opportunities for which the salespeople changed the ‘probability of sale’ <sup>fi</sup>eld to 100%) were also loosely coupled with desk salespeople’s sales and sales-related work. However, Siebel checked each closed sale against E-Tel’s invoicing system, used to bill customers for their purchases. If a sale opportunity was marked as closed, Siebel checked that the customer named in the opportunity was billed for the amount of the sale. Therefore, desk salespeople could not report sales of products that their customers had not actually purchased. Yet, they retained a lot of leeway to report enough sold revenue to reach their sales targets, without actually doing any selling. They could report as their own sales orders that customers placed on their own initiative, without any effort by field or desk salespeople. They also achieve their sales target by reporting sales that resulted from work performed solely by <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, for which desk salespeople took partial credit.

One of the authors undertook an ethnographic study during the <sup>fi</sup>rst 15 months of DeskSales existence (which coincided with the 3 months of implementation of Siebel and 12 months of Siebel’s use in the company). The data collection procedures consisted mostly of observing DeskSales’ open space of<sup>fi</sup>ce (Table 1). We supplemented these observations with formal inter views with desk salespeople, sales managers, the unit’s general manager, <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople and some of E-Tel’s senior managers. We also collected more than 3000 pages of documents, including <sup>fi</sup>nal and draft versions of all sales performance and customer call reports produced during this time. We took pictures of team whiteboards (often used to report daily performance) every time the contents changed, and we listened in to and took verbatim notes about 51 phone-based weekly team meetings. Finally, we sat in on all of the meetings of the senior man agement team responsible for setting up, implementing and evaluating DeskSales.

The second stage of the analysis consisted of organizing these categories into a thematic structure. Following previous qualitative studies of IT in organizations, we used Giddens’ (1986) model of agency as a basis for this process. In Giddens’s model, people’s actions are improvisations in response to a structure of interpretive schemes, rules and resources to deal with situated challenges. This model can specify the dialectic of control, in which the process of control is a joint accomplishment by managers and employees, because ‘all forms of dependence offer some resources whereby those who are subordinate can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the activities of their superiors’ (Giddens, 1986, p. 16). We used this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation rather than Foucault’s model of control, which is more pop ular in research on CMC, for two reasons. First, research on ITand organizations mostly relies on the metaphor of the panopticon rather than the model that Foucault developed from that metaphor, so we lack a strong analytical framework on which to draw to build a theory of the dark side of CMC. Second, several authors emphasize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Giddens’s framework for studying the effects of IT on organizations (e.g. Jones & Karsten, 2008), because it incorporates the effects of structure and action into a single, mutually constituted process, where IT (including CMC) shapes people’s practices but also is shaped by those practices (Orlikowski, 1991). We grouped the code sets that we developed from our open coding into the categories in Appendix 2, following the agency model from Giddens’s structuration theory as applied to IT and organizations (Jones & Karsten, 2008). The third stage of analysis consists of inducing links between the codes in each of these categories. We coded a link between two or more categories from different branches of our thematic structure when they co-occurred in the same coded segment. We integrated these links into a model of the dark side of CMC that includes its antecedents and its outcomes. Appendix 3 maps the links that we induced in this stage of analysis by using the practices of managers and the practices of desk salespeople to link antecedents of their use of Siebel to its outcomes.

## COMPUTER-MEDIATED CONTROL UNDER LOOSE COUPLING AT DESKSALES

The practices and outcomes of CMC for managers and employees at DeskSales contrasted with the accounts of practices and outcomes of CMC in settings where work is tightly coupled with its electronic representation.

Desk sales managers attributed their use of Siebel for CMC to two sets of conditions.

## The structuring of desk sales managers’ CMC

One type of conditions that desk sales managers used to explain their practices to monitor and enforce desk salespeople’s reported sales and sales work in Siebel was the set of prescribed goals that E-Tel imposed upon their salespeople and the prescribed procedures for using Siebe to report progress towards those goals.

Desk sales managers attributed the structuring effect of the goals set by E-Tel’s management for desk salespeople to the need to prove the value of DeskSales as a new unit in E-Tel’s business-to-business sales force. Robert explained:

They feed it [sales data] back to [the company] so that they can say that ‘we are getting value for money'. If vou look at this desk salesperson, he's bringing X amount of dollars. Yeah. you’re paying 26 000 dollars for the year but he’s helped this year 4 million dollars, on his own from the desk he’s brought further half a million dollars worth… You know, [the company] could turn around and say, ‘right, I could get that money in with or without him anyway’, they never know that. But I think the reports show in some way the value we add into the accounts.

Ted explained the requirement to report sales calls in addition to reporting sales as a matte of legitimation:

If the business doesn’t work, if higher ups say why don’t you have a \$4 million revenue, you can turn and say ‘my [salespeople…] made 2 million calls last year and they’ve contacted 2 million people, they’ve sent 10 million e-mails, and unfortunately they don’t want to buy, the customer doesn’t want to buy, because of climate.’ How can you justify in that case, if you don’t have the revenue, how can you justify that your salesforce has been working? Because they can’t come around here and check everyday that you are with your headset and that you’re taking calls, but if you log all these data it’s a backing for them.

Desk sales managers also emphasized the structuring effect of the prescribed procedures that their company speci<sup>fi</sup>ed for Siebel. Sales manager Roger told his desk salespeople that:

“KPIs [key performance indicators extracted from Siebel] measure the way we do business and how successful we are” and that “ Siebel is completely transparent”, and that's one of its major advantages. He explained that there is a conference call about Siebel. He con cludes that “everything we do is highly visible”, “it’s fully on the radar”.

Samantha emphasized that following procedure also helped the unit prove its value:

She told desk salespeople that “we need your support on Siebel”. She said that “Siebel is where DeskSales can be ambassadors and leaders”. She added that “as you know, Siebel is part of the E-Tel transformation and DeskSales is on top in accuracy”. She adds that “we need to keep being the most accurate”. She says that Siebel “is part of the way that we add value to [the company…] if we can deliver we have to do it.”

However, there was a cost to using Siebel to prove the value of DeskSales. When asked to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the negative elements of her job as a Desk Sales Manager, Linda complained:

One of the major problems we have had at DeskSales is that we need to keep proving ourselves and one of the main problems is that we have to keep producing statistics and spread sheets and things like that and for me personally as a Desk Sales Manager, having to produce these things to feed them up so that [our General Manager] can justify what we’re doing and when we’re doing it, so that [top managers] and especially [senior managers] are happy. So it takes a lot of time and it’s unproductive work as well because it doesn’t help you make more sales, it’s just looking back, so that justi<sup>fi</sup>cation process is quite frustrating.

## The situated challenges of desk sales managers

The other type of condition that desk sales managers used to explain their use of Siebel for CMC was related to desk salespeople’s performance. During the <sup>fi</sup>rst few months of the unit’s operation, desk salespeople lagged behind their targets for reported sales and reported customer calls. Some sales managers, such as Samantha attributed this bad performance to the unskilled people that DeskSales was hiring:

I don’t see too many proactive salesmen in DeskSales.[…] A lot of the people that [we]’ve employed, because [we] had to employ 70 people in a short space of time came in on agency contracts. They didn’t have a job, so you’re taking on unemployed, show them how to use the systems and all they’re doing is managing their email, […] they do take a lot of orders, they do a lot of work that they’re not supposed to do and I don’t know how much time is wasted, […] My initial feeling is that there is a lot of time wasted, but I don’t know how to measure it.

Other sales managers such as John argued that even those employees who were willing and capable to sell would fail their targets because they were unwilling to report sales and sales calls in Siebel:

I believe that that must be what they need to do because the more you ask people to do the administration the less they are inclined to… they don’t want to do that, human beings don’t want to do that side of administration really, particularly the kind of people that you have in here that want to earn money, that want to spend their time making money. They don’t want to spend their time on Siebel, do they? […] They want the information and they want to get on with the sale.

## The practice of CMC by desk sales managers

The practice of CMC by desk sales managers attempted to address the situated challenges im posed by some salespeople’s inability to sell and other salespeople’s unwillingness to repor their work in Siebel, under the structure of prescribed goals and prescribed use of Siebel that their company imposed.

## Monitoring and exposing sales and sales-related work

Sales managers improvised three types of information systems to monitor and expose the customer calls and sales that desk salespeople reported in Siebel. First, some managers used a set of tables, written on whiteboards or printed from an electronic spreadsheet, which listed the revenue and customer calls reported by each of their desk salespeople. Some sales managers, such as Peter, used these tables to rank their salespeople; others, such as Nathan, simply listed how many sales calls and how much sales revenue each desk salesperson had reported in Siebel, without ranking them. Nathan explained that he used a whiteboard because ‘it gives us a very good visibility of how every individual is doing and how we’re doing as a team.

Second, there were sales managers who e-mailed to everybody on his team the sales and customer call data reported by each salesperson on the team each week. Before one of his weekly team meetings, Jon received an e-mail from his sales manager Jean with a bar graph showing the sales <sup>fi</sup>gures for each person on the team and a message that read, ‘just to know where we are.’ Jean had been recently promoted to sales manager and explicitly contrasted the whiteboards placed in the of<sup>fi</sup>ce for everybody to see with an electronic spreadsheet that was distributed only among team members:

[With a whiteboard, you are] exposing people that are not doing so well, exposing people that are new…. I don’t particularly want to be exposed. Exposed the people that made the least deals.… [The spreadsheet is] not something people can walk by everyday and look at. That is a management report that gets sent to the team, so you can look at it if you want to, but quite honestly, unless you’re trying to beat them, people are not interested in what you’re doing anyway. I’m not particularly worried.… [The team] all voted that they didn’t want a whiteboard, ‘we don’t want to have a whiteboard,’ so we won’t have a whiteboard!

Third, some sales managers encouraged salespeople to monitor themselves and contribute to a report that would be shared among team members. Robert introduced this report to his team by stating:

“It’s really important for you guys to take ownership of your own numbers and to compare them with each other.” He said that because, when they are preparing their own report, “you need to be consistent about how you get your numbers.” He said, “next week, we’ll go into a meeting room and I’ll walk you step by step on what you need to do to get the right queries on Siebel. […] it’s the way we’re going to help you monitor yourselves, so that you know where you are at any moment in time.” He went on by saying that with senior managers being around the <sup>fl</sup>oor, in addition to all of the top management’s visits, “if somebody comes and ask you, you want to know [your targets].”

These three methods for monitoring the sales and customer calls that salespeople reported in Siebel re<sup>fl</sup>ected that desk salespeople kept whatever information needed to do their work in alternative information systems, even if doing so cost them some bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Therefore, managers sought to complement their monitoring practices with practices that induced desk salespeople to report their sales and customer calls in Siebel.

Managers used team meetings to expose desk salespeople performance. They reported the team’s performance as a whole, such as Jeremy did in a team meetings:

“If you are anything like me, you are really focused on your bonus, and to hit your bonus, it’s important to know where you are against target.” Jeremy added that they, as a team, were 55% against target, “that’s good.” He added that “this week you need to be on 60% against target, so if you could pull some good numbers this week, that would be great.” He said that he was willing to pay for a night out for them if they did “really good numbers in these next six weeks.”

Managers also told desk salespeople about their individual performance. Some, such as George, even used these meetings to compare salespeople against one another. George had created a ‘baseball league,’ in which each salesperson was represented by a team that scored points whenever they reported a sale. He used his ‘baseball league’ thus:

George started his meeting by saying that he wanted to talk “about your numbers,” he said that “the girls are on top this week,” that the team’s baseball cup was “dominated by the girls.” Jeffrey wondered, “Have you been updating them? Am I not in the top anymore?” George said: “Haven’t you looked at them lately?” He got up and went through the <sup>fi</sup>rst six rows in his league table and told Jeffrey, “you’re in sixth.” Mark was in second; “I am the one who has to put things right then,” he said, referring to the abundance of women in the top positions. George then talked about the top wins for last week worth about 2 million dollars each. He then said that they had logged in 19 opportunities last week and congratulated them on that.

## Improving records of sales and customer calls in Siebel

Managers used weekly meetings with desk salespeople to improve the reports of sales and cus tomer calls in Siebel. During these meetings, they enforced targets for reported sales and targets for reported sales calls. They did so by specifying salespeople reporting work. Sometimes they only told salespeople the individual goals that they had to achieve. In one of his team meetings:

George asked salespeople on his team if they knew what their targets were, “Do you know what’s the target for desk salespeople-only wins on a weekly basis?” A few people mumbled “20K a week.” George emphasized, “This is what we need to bring every week to be on target.” He added that “next week we need to be on 63% target.” He then told each person what their percentage was against their target and told the lower scorers, Peter, John and Ann, “you’re on 30% but with a couple of big wins, you’ll be able to pull back.”

At other times, they enforced speci<sup>fi</sup>c reporting practices:

Victor reported that his sales managers had admonished him about not having anything on his pipeline. Victor explained that he didn’t have anything in the pipeline because his ac counts are subcontracting, so he is dependent on his customers having a pipeline and sharing it with him. He said that his manager told him that he had to have a pipeline because “it looked bad on Siebel” if he didn’t have one.

Finally, managers used their weekly meetings to help their desk salespeople <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to report enough revenue to reach their targets, such as telling them how to share credit for some of the <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople’s sales. In one team meeting, Nina, a DeskSales manager in one of the retail teams, told the story of how Robert, a desk salesperson on her team, got credit for an \$8.6 million opportunity that involved putting E-Tel broadband access CDs in a retailer’s stores:

Nina explained that though Robert’s involvement was limited to calling the stores to make sure they got the discs, ‘even making a quote [giving the price of a product] is involvement. She concluded: ‘What I would do is ask the account team [i.e., <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople] if there’s something I can do to help.’ She added, ‘basically, the more you look, the more you’ll <sup>fi</sup>nd, and it's a way to get revenue without having to sell anything. Of course, you will eventually have to sell something, but it’s a good way to hit numbers.

As another means for helping desk salespeople reach their revenue targets, managers explained how to get orders from customers and report them as sales. They recommended that salespeople become the ‘central point of contact’ for customers. DeskSales manager John advised desk salespeople: ‘Tell them that you can respond quickly to any needs if they have orders, queries, issues, all of that.’ He explained, ‘we should get more orders that way, we will also get the odd billing and service request but we can forward those on if needed.

## The structuring of desk salespeople’s participation in CMC

Managers’ attempts to induce desk salespeople to produce records of sales and sales work in Siebel structured desk salespeople’s everyday work. Desk salespeople reported three structur ing effects of Siebel on their everyday work.

Some interpreted Siebel as an additional burden on their everyday work, a set of rules tha they had to follow to comply with prescribed procedure. Paul complained:

I don’t really use Siebel, I hate it … because I’ve got so many systems that I need to use, it’s just another system. Something that I have to use, something that I have to put stuff on there, otherwise I get penalized, so that’s the reason I hate it.

John also insisted:

I believe that my sales managers are stats driven. They have to produce spreadsheets day in and day out, and they can only talk about <sup>fi</sup>gures. Figures isn’t the real world. Figures is not a sales world. As a salesman I know that <sup>fi</sup>gures are important because without <sup>fi</sup>gures no one knows what’s the revenue you’re bringing in. But we have databases that do that for you. So I think that most salespeople are driven to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the role of Siebel, and if you ask who my internal manager is, it’s Siebel.

Other desk salespeople were more accepting of Siebel. Martha described Siebel thus:

I supposed [it’s there] because of transparency, so that the account team [desk and <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople] can see what sales have been made, see what deals are currently underway, get an idea of the customer is leaning towards, what products they’re buying.

When asked what she reported in Siebel, Martha answered, ‘pretty much anything at the moment […,] for me it’s just a sort of reporting tool – I put down opportunities, leads, […] every thing’. Jen was more ambivalent:

[Siebel] probably makes my job easier to a certain respect but in other respects no. I just want to get on with it and not have to do all this stuff. But it’s quite a helpful tool, I haven’t had time to use it properly as well. […] I can see what the other members of my account team are do ing, what opportunities they have put on […and] view what they have done on those opportunities.

The practices that managers used to monitor and enforce the representation of sales and sales calls in Siebel affected desk salespeople’s interpretation of the structuring effect of Siebel. For some salespeople, the practices that managers used to monitor and enforce reporting work made it more palatable. Salesman Simon argued:

That’s quite helpful. I mean [sales manager] Jeremy said this morning [in the team meeting] that they’re going to introduce something that will have red-amber-green, which will tell you how far away from your target for the month that you are. For me that is useful because you’ve got your normal sort of job to do. So I need to know how far have I done. I mean, I would look on Siebel and see how much I’ve closed. And that’s how far as it is. But if somebody can put that on a board somewhere so I’ve got a good visible view, that would kind of help me to see where I am. And I also think it’s kind of good that you get friendly competitiveness with the rest of the team. So you can see how you’re doing and if it’s not really good you can really really go for it and I don’t know how but I’m sure that if you pushed and pushed and pushed you’d get somewhere, so I think it’s quite useful.

For others, these practices only compounded their dissatisfaction. Ann complained:

I am absolutely 100% percent against it. [Our manager] asked us if we wanted one and we al said no. I hate that. I think it’s really like a call center, it’s patronizing.… I hate [the whiteboards]. I don’t think they’re useful at all: they don’t help, they don’t increase morale, they don’t make you want to work hard, they make you feel like kids, you know. You’ve got a list of people in the team and it show who’s doing well and who’s doing bad, I think that’s really bad.

For both sets of desk salespeople, reporting sales and sales calls in Siebel was work that was separate from and that they had to perform in addition to their everyday tasks, even if they were content to do so. Some desk salespeople, such as Mark, attributed to money their willingness to do this representation work. He confessed that:

“My goal is to make money.” He did not have any career aspirations within E-Tel, “this means that my goals are aligned with the company because what the company wants is to make money.” He told me that this was the reason he was at DeskSales: “it’s a quick way to save \$10000,” which is the money Mark needed to start the career of his choice: storyboarding. He complained that “I feel trapped in there [at DeskSales].” As soon as he makes the money he wants, he’ll leave “if I got a \$9000 bonus tomorrow, I’d hand in my resignation.”

Other desk salespeople, such as Hannah and Tim, did this work to avoid the sanctions of failure to report compliance with prescribed procedure and prescribed targets. Hannah complained about having to report sales and sales calls in Siebel by arguing that, ‘I have to do it otherwise it would be too much hassle, I’d get too much grief.’ Tim complained:

“I’m getting pressure from [my sales managers] to make up <sup>fi</sup>ctitious sales calls to hit targets”. He said that “I’m not really comfortable with that, granted I haven’t done much but at least [the number of calls reported in Siebel] is an honest representation of what I’ve done. What if my [<sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople] look at Siebel and see them [the fake sales calls] and ask me ‘what is this?’”

These two sets of motivations are further explored below in the description of the different reactions of desk salespeople to their managers’ attempts at CMC.

## The situated challenges that shaped desk salespeople’s participation in CMC

Salespeople complained about the amount of reporting required to comply with the targets for sales and sales calls reported in Siebel. Their chief objection was the extent to which reporting their work con<sup>fl</sup>icted with doing the service work that they performed for thei customers and <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, which they interpreted as their core contribution to the company and which they traded for revenue, which they could report as their own sales in Siebel. Tom explained:

A lot of our job is actually service…. You know, we’re order takers. And I think … myself, I found opportunities and I found them in accounts where they wouldn’t be found. The key to it all is the actual relationship with the customer because the customer will come to me.… One of the good things about [DeskSales] is that there’s always somebody there for the customer to speak to, outside the service arena. And I think that as far as I’m concerned, I’ve built trust with my customer and that helps. And I think maybe we see more business because I’ve been there. More business comes to E-Tel purely from the fact that I’ve been there. As far as me seeking out opportunities, yes I’ve sought out some opportunities, but a lot of them, the customer has come to me rather than me going to the customer.

For desk salespeople such as Marion producing records of opportunities, sales and sales calls in Siebel hindered this service work:

After <sup>fi</sup>nishing entering three new opportunities in Siebel, Marion complained aloud, “not enough time for customers in a day, there’s so much internal stuff to do”. Jeremy, another desk salesman on Marion’s team warned: “focus on your customers, girl, otherwise you won’t earn any bonus.” Marion replied: “it’s all fun and games!” Jeremy agreed, “it’s complete a waste of time […,] it takes forever”. Marion concluded, “this is what I hate about this job, having to do all this admin crap.”

## Desk salespeople’s participation in CMC

Desk used Siebel to present themselves as successful salespeople while trading service work for revenue which they could report as their own sales. Desk salespeople added representation work to their everyday service work and used deception rather than compliance to report enough sales and sales calls in Siebel to reach their targets. Their practices for reporting sales were different from their practices to report customer calls. Some desk salespeople such as Geoff attempted to report as many sales as they could, even if they did not make any sales. Geoff gave an example:

He said that Mitch had a \$15 million opportunity under his name on Siebel with 95% probability. “Do you want to know what he did for it?” He made a zero with his fingers, grinning. He said that Mitch was able to persuade a <sup>fi</sup>eld salesperson to let him share credit for this sale, “Mitch is trying to see what he can put against it [what he did for the opportunity to close so that he can get bonus for it].” He added that he had reported around \$5.8 million under his name (his sales target was \$300,000) and that he had probably closed, at the most \$60,000.

## Geoff further explained:

Siebel is a reporting tool and a reporting tool only, and this is why I’m amazed we’re paid off a reporting tool … because you can put a <sup>fi</sup>gure in and that <sup>fi</sup>gure can be anything and you’re paid on that.… [Field salespeople] are paid on [the revenue tracking system], which is money that goes into E-Tel’s bank. We’re paid on the money that we think is going to go into the bank, but nobody ever checks that it’s going into the bank. The reason is that it’s so dif<sup>fi</sup>cult with all the opportunities on there to track them through to [the revenue tracking system] that I am not sure it will ever happen.

Other salespeople such as Mark reported only enough sales and customer calls to reach their targets. As Mark confessed, ‘I know the bonus is uncapped, but I don’t want to go silly; oth erwise they will raise my target for next year.’ He further explained, ‘I also don’t want to be in the spotlight, I don’t want to attract attention to the way I got my stuff on Siebel.’ Finally, he noted, ‘it’s all a game isn’t it, and we all have to play it.

Marianne explained, ‘our job, really, is to keep customers happy, especially at the top level so that when they want to do large purchases they come to E-Tel and so that the [<sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople] can go in and deal with the big opportunities’. She did not attempt to claim credit for big cus tomer orders because ‘we’re not here to dig big opportunities, that’s the [<sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople’s] role, I don’t even try because I’d just look silly.

There were also desk salespeople such as Tom and Mary who had ethical qualms about reporting revenue as the outcome of their own work or reporting customer calls they had not placed. Tom argued that, ‘putting your name on [<sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople’s] sales is wrong because [desk salespeople] are not supposed to close that type of deals.’ As far as he was concerned, ‘you shouldn’t put your name on a deal to which you added no value whatsoever.’ Mary also warned:

I think those people are going to get caught out, whether it’s today, tomorrow, next week or next year…. I’ve done it a couple of times but it’s not the right way to get about it. These desk salespeople lagged behind their targets as they struggled with the delicate balance between complying with their managers’ attempts to enforce compliance with targets for sales and customer calls but avoiding behaviour they interpreted as unethical.

We found less variation in how salespeople used Siebel to report compliance with their targets for sales calls. Salespeople such as Peter and Sarah used two practices to achieve their target for sales calls in Siebel. They reported their service calls as sales calls and they reported calls that had not happened.

In a conversation over breakfast, Peter and Sarah discussed their practices for reporting sales calls. Peter explained that when he calls a customer. 'I never prepare for the call.' He says that he very rarely replies to a customer’s query or question on a call, ‘I always tell them that I can call them back’. He says that the reasons are ‘to make sure that I have the right answer’, and that this also allows his ‘to log a couple more calls, one to <sup>fi</sup>nd the answer [from the appropriate service unit] and one to tell it to them [ie. the customer].’ Sarah confessed, ‘every thing I put on Siebel is bullshit,’ adding ‘if I only make three calls in one day, I’ll just add some bullshit calls on Siebel to meet the daily target.’ He told me, ‘as you know contacts is a numbers game to keep the man upstairs happy that he paid for his Siebel.’ Desk salespeople’s use of Siebel to provide an image of successful salespeople points to the dark side of CMC when work is loosely coupled with its representation: managers and employees respond to the burden of representing work by providing limited, inaccurate and sometimes purposefully false data about their actions and achievements.

## CONTRIBUTIONS TO THEORY

Research blames the dark side of CMC on the tight coupling between work and its electronic representation, which makes employees’ work transparent and therefore induces oppression and prompts anticipatory conformity. Our research shows that the dark side of CMC remains, even when work is loosely coupled with its representation. This means that there must be a techno-organizational process that explains the dark side of CMC that includes but extends beyond the computer-mediated production of records of employees work. Next, we discuss the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dark side of CMC under loose coupling that we induced from our research at DeskSales, and then use it to articulate the techno-organizational process that generates the dark side of CMC, of which the anticipatory conformity induced by the informating property of technology is but a single instance.

## Nature of the dark side of CMC

The dark side of CMC under tight coupling between work and its electronic representation consists the unintended oppression that IT imposes on employees by making completely transparent to the rest of their organization the extent to which employees comply with prescribed procedure and goals, which may be at odds with the situated practices that employees need to enact to cope with their everyday challenges at work (Warhurst & Thompson, 1998; Taylo & Bain, 1999). The most insidious cases of the oppression induced by CMC under tight coupling involve anticipatory conformity, which unfolds under a cloak of autonomy, such that employees believe their job offers ample latitude and agency, while in reality they are bound to prescribed procedures and rei<sup>fi</sup>ed worldviews embedded in the IT they use for thei everyday work.

The dark side of CMC under loose coupling between work and its electronic representation that we induced from our research at desk sales consists of the deception that managers, will ingly or unwittingly, impose on employees by having employees reporting compliance with prescribed procedure and goals, which may be at odds with the situated practices that employees use to address their everyday challenges at work. When IT requires employees to report their own work and accomplishments, they can report completing tasks they did not carry out and report achieving goals that they did not reach, even if this violates their own behavioural norms, to be able to cope with their everyday challenges at work (in the case of desk salespeople, their service work for customers and <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople). Some salespeople at DeskSales relished the opportunity for deception offered by the loose coupling between work and its representa tion. However, many others complained about the deception that their managers encouraged or that they felt they had to adopt to comply with prescribed targets for sales and sales work, even as they voluntarily performed what they interpreted to be valuable service work for customers. In this sense, deception is as much of a dark side as is oppression when managers en force representations of compliance with prescribed procedures that employees cannot follow or prescribed targets that employees are unable to achieve.

## Causes of the dark side of CMC

The dark side of CMC under tight coupling comes from the tension between having to comply with prescribed procedure while facing everyday challenges at work that cannot be addressed by doing so. The conformity imposed by CMC when work is tightly coupled with its representa tion introduces a burden of compliance upon employees’ everyday experience at work. The tight coupling between work and its representation forces employees to comply with prescribed procedure because employees expect any deviation to be immediately visible to and punished by the organization (Orlikowski, 1991; Sewell, 1998; Taylor & Bain, 1999). Thus, computermediated surveillance imposes a burden of compliance on employees (Zuboff, 1988), inducing them to follow procedures that do not add to or even may hinder their everyday work.

Our research at DeskSales suggests that the dark side of CMC under loose coupling comes from the tension between the need to allocate time to work and the need to allocate time to pro duce electronic representations of work.

The representation work imposed by CMC when work is loosely coupled with its representation introduces a burden of reporting that requires employees to take time away from addressing their everyday challenges at work, to produce enough records of compliance with prescribed procedure to appease their managers. Our research at DeskSales shows how much effort this representation work may demand. Desk salespeople complained about spending much of their day producing records of sales and sales-related work in Siebel. In addition, many of the interactions of sales managers with desk salespeople entailed efforts to monitor and enforce this representation work, rather than monitoring and enforcing sales and sales-related work. Speci<sup>fi</sup>ed thus, the burden of CMC consists of an increase in the effort that employees must undertake to represent their work, after they have completed it, and the effort that man agers expend to ensure employees do so.

The time that employees and managers spend on this representation work competes with the time employees have available for their everyday tasks, and managers need to ensure that employees carry out their everyday tasks. Our research at DeskSales speci<sup>fi</sup>es the dark side of CMC as the deception that emplovees do when they produce records of their work which are at odds with their everyday practices. They do this to avoid the sanctions of having records of their practices and achievements that report failure to comply with prescribed procedure and prescribed goals. They do so, in some cases at least, to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the rewards of having electronic records that report compliance with both.

## Moderators of the dark side of CMC

Research on CMC under tight coupling between work and its representation shows that the extent of oppression can vary with the size of the gap between the procedures that companies prescribe (and monitor) and the practices that employees interpret as necessary for their every day work (Callaghan & Thompson, 2001; Fernie and Metcalf, 1998; Orlikowski, 1991; Taylor & Bain, 1999; Zuboff, 1988).

Our research at DeskSales shows that when work is loosely coupled with its representation, the extent to which CMC induces deceptive practices among employees depends on the gap between the data that employees need to do their work and the data the organization requires them to report. Desk salespeople performed service work for customers and <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, for which they did not need the data that their company needed to monitor sales and salesrelated work. Salespeople had to produce these data only to appease their managers and, in the case of salespeople who took advantage of that gap, earn higher bonuses. The extent of the pressure to engage in deception also depended on how much data managers monitored, bevond that which emplovees needed to do their everyday work. Reporting sales calls – which managers only checked to determine if desk salespeople had reported enough calls, without monitoring the contents of each call record – involved far less deception than producing records of sales, because E-Tel extracted all its sales data from these latter reports.

Our research at DeskSales suggests that this potential for deception is improved by the extent to which the data that employees report can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed. At DeskSales, desk salespeople could easily report calls to customers that they never made, but they could only report sales when customers actually purchased products and services. Siebel’s connection with E-Tel’s in voicing system prevented desk salespeople from reporting sales of products that customers did not purchase. Still, desk salespeople found ways to take advantage of the opportunity to report sales calls that they did not make and report customer orders as their own, even if the sales came from customers’ orders or <sup>fi</sup>eld salespeople, to maximize their bonus.

## Outcomes of the dark side of CMC

Research suggests that the dark side of CMC offers few bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for employees. Instead, it im poses many costs upon them, such as feelings of anomie, job alienation, role strain and even shame when IT records and broadcasts employees’ failure to comply with prescribed targets (Garson, 1988; Ottensmeyer & Heroux, 1991; Warhurst & Thompson, 1998). Organizations bene<sup>fi</sup>t because CMC improves compliance, but they have to pay for that compliance with de creased <sup>fl</sup>exibility and unhappier employees (Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Webster & Robins, 1993; Sewell, 1998).

Our research at DeskSales shows that when work is loosely coupled with representation, employees may actually bene<sup>fi</sup>t from CMC, because they can produce a representation of compliance without following prescribed procedure and without complying with prescribed goals and earn the rewards for pretending to do so. Even when they are reluctantly pushed into deception to report compliance by their managers and companies, employees bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a backstage where they can at least <sup>fi</sup>nd shelter from surveillance.

Our research at DeskSales shows that the dark side of IT when work is loosely coupled with its electronic representation brings at least two signi<sup>fi</sup>cant costs for organizations. One is the cost of doing and managing representation work. At DeskSales, salespeople spent a lot of time performing representation work to produce records of sales and sales work, which competed for their time available to interact with customers. Therefore, the company had direct compen sation costs, because it paid employees to produce records of their actions and achievements and indirect opportunity costs, because representation work reduced the time employees had available to do their jobs.

The other cost is the poor quality of the information that feeds companies’ strategy process. Some of the information that companies use to decide upon their strategy comes from external sources, such as market research. However, a lot of the information that companies need to de cide how to best respond to market dynamics and a lot of the information that companies need to assess the effectiveness of such responses comes from internal information about organiza tional processes and their results. When work is loosely coupled with its representation, it is people who provide this information, and those people may be as motivated to distort some of this information in their favour as some desk salespeople at DeskSales or feel as reluctant to put the effort necessary to report all of the data that their company requires as did desk salespeople. This means that the data that companies use to decide strategy and to monitor its implementation and its outcomes are, at best, an incomplete and, at worse, a deceitful repre sentation of companies’ internal processes and their outcomes.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, when work is loosely coupled with its representation, the dark side of CMC can have the following consequences: for companies, it replaces detailed information and enforced compliance with biased information and invisible deviance; for employees, it replaces oppressive control for corruptive autonomy.

## A techno-organizational theory of the dark side of CMC

Comparing and contrasting the dark sides of CMC that emerge under loose coupling and under tight coupling between work and its electronic representation suggests a speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dark side of CMC that frames the informating property of technology, which has been portrayed as the technological driving engine of the dark side of CMC, within a tension between prescribed procedure and improvised practices, which is the organizational driving engine of the dark side of CMC.

Our research at DeskSales emphasizes the role of the gap between prescribed procedure and everyday challenges at work on the dark side of CMC. It suggests that the dark side of CMC is generated and sustained by this gap. If desk salespeople’s prescribed tasks consisted of service work, the task of producing a record of their service work and its outcomes would be part of their everyday job, because desk salespeople needed to keep some records of this work to have the information necessary to carry the service requests placed by their customers. Employees would still have to produce this information manually, but this representation work would not be a burden added to their everyday work – it would be part of their everyday work.

Research on the dark side of CMC under tight coupling can be examined from this vantage point to build a broader speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dark side of CMC that includes but goes beyond the effect of the informating property of IT. Our research at DeskSales suggests that the informating property of IT may not generate an oppressive dark side if people’s actual work practices match those which are prescribed by their company and which technology monitors. It is only when people need to improvise work practices that are at odds with those that their company monitors that people experience the oppression imposed by the dark side of CMC under tight coupling between work and its representation.

The explanation of the dark side of CMC under loose coupling that we induced from our research at DeskSales and the vantage point which it offers to explain the dark side of IT under tight coupling, points to a general speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dark side of CMC. It speci<sup>fi</sup>es this dark side as the unintended and negative consequences of the gap between people’s prescribed practices and the practices that they need to carry out to address their everyday challenges at work when their work is monitored through IT. The level of coupling between work and its representation speci<sup>fi</sup>es the prescribed procedures that generate the tension between prescribed and actual work practices. It also speci<sup>fi</sup>es the coping mechanism that employees use to address that tension. When work is tightly coupled with its representation. it is the set of prescribed procedures for work which speci<sup>fi</sup>es the tension that induces feelings of oppression which prompt people to engage in anticipatory conformity. When work is loosely coupled with its representation, it is the set of prescribed procedures for representing work that induces feelings of encumbrance which prompt people to engage in deceptive representation of work.

## CONCLUSION

The dark side of CMC is an instance of the processes featured in the call for papers for this special issue: ‘complex social and individual situations where the very bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that use of IT brings contain the seeds of potentially transformative changes in ways of working, collaborating and living – changes that can lead to non-bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, if unintended consequences.

Research on the dark side of CMC has emphasized how the use of the informating property of ITwithin the relationship of authority, which promises less direct supervision and more autonomy for employees, leads to insidious negative outcome, which is a sense of oppression that erodes the ties between employees and the organization that employs them, and that hinders the <sup>fl</sup>ex ibility that organizations need to adapt to situated challenges. Our research at DeskSales shows that CMC still has unintended negative consequences for organizations and their employees, even if organizations implement IT that does not have this informating property – technologies such as Siebel where people have to record their own work. Our research at DeskSales suggests that in this case, IT has the potential to help people improve their work practices by providing them with an information system that can improve their results by helping them to process the information that they need to do their work and organize it in a way that facilitates the decisions that they have to make to address their everyday challenges at work. However, it imposes a bur den of representation that induces deception so that people have enough time to do their work.

Our research suggests that managers who want to avoid the dark side of CMC in their orga nizations need to anchor the design and implementation of information systems on the work practices that employees actually use in their everyday work rather than anchoring them on the practices that employees should use in the course of their everyday work. Our research at DeskSales suggests that if managers fail to do so when implementing an information system where work is loosely coupled with its representation, then reporting work in that information system will become a burden that will tempt employees into creating a representation of work that is a caricature of their actual practice. Future research should explore other effects of the tension between people’s everyday practices and the procedures that their company prescribes on the dark side of CMC when work is loosely coupled with its electronic representation. It should also explore the effects of this tension on the dark side of CMC when there is enough of a tight coupling between work and its electronic representation for the informating property of IT to affect the dark side of CMC.
