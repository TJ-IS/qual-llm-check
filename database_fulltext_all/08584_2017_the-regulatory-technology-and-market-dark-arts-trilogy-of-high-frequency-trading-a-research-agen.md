# The regulatory, technology and market ‘dark arts trilogy’ of high frequency trading: a research agenda

## Abstract

Computerization has transformed financial markets with high frequency trading displacing human activity with proprietary algorithms to lower latency, reduce intermediary costs, enhance liquidity and increase transaction speed. Following the ‘‘Flash Crash’’ of 2010 which saw the Dow Jones Industrial Average plunge 1000 points within minutes, high frequency trading has come under the radar of multi-jurisdictional regulators. Combining a review of the extant literature on high frequency trading with empirical data from interviews with financial traders, computer experts and regulators, we develop concepts of regulatory adaptation, technology asymmetry and market ambiguity to illustrate the ‘dark art’ of high frequency trading. Findings show high frequency trading is a multi-faceted, complex and secretive practice. It is implicated in market events, but correlation does not imply causation, as isolating causal mechanisms from interconnected automated financial trading is highly challenging for regulators who seek to monitor algorithmic trading across multiple jurisdictions. This article provides information systems researchers with a set of conceptual tools for analysing high frequency trading.

## Introduction

T <sup>owards</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>end</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>twentieth</sup> <sup>century,</sup> <sup>financial</sup>markets underwent significant regulatory and technological change. Electronic trading began in 1971 when NASDAQ opened the world’s first electronic stock market. Since then, financial markets have become increasingly de-regulated and fragmented (Funk and Hirschmann, 2014). New securities have been created and trading volumes have increased exponentially. In 1985, for example, the London Stock Exchange (LSE, and the 3rd largest exchange in the world) matched over 20,000 trades each day. By 2015, this figure was over thirty times larger. In this position paper on the intersection between financial markets and information technology, we examine the phenomenon of high-frequency trading (HFT) which uses proprietary algorithms to trade in financial markets. Emerging in a two-tiered market with traditional brokers and dealers (humans) or low-frequency traders (LFT), HFTs benefit from capturing minute price differences unavailable to their technologically inferior rivals (Blocher et al., 2016). The transition from human traders to algorithmic trading (MacKenzie, 2015) positions HFT as a controversial form of financial trading, attracting the attention of multi-jurisdictional financial regulators, industry participants, the media and academics (Angel, 2014).

To date, most HFT research is published by the finance discipline, with some studies addressing information systems issues on how automation facilitates HFT strategies (Gomber et al., 2011). Finance researchers show how HFT and algorithmic trading impact foreign exchange markets (Chaboud et al., 2014) improve liquidity (Hendershott et al., 2011) and reduce latency (Menkveld and Zoican, 2014). HFT embodies the use of powerful computers to manipulate complex trading algorithms, often co-located near the electronic matching system at an exchange (Aitken et al., 2014). Close proximity to exchanges enable HFTs to ‘compete on speed’ by sending orders in milliseconds, giving them competitive advantage over industry participants with slower connections (e.g. LFTs) (Pagnotta and Philippon, 2012; Menkveld and Zoican, 2014). Technological superiority acquired at significant cost by HFTs creates barriers for smaller firms without the technological infrastructure to compete (Biasis et al., 2011).

A growing body of theoretical and empirical work on HFT is emerging from the finance discipline, mostly using quantitative models to simulate HFT activity. Few qualitative studies, however, exist on the societal, organizational and technological impact of HFT, despite ongoing concerns from regulators, industry participants and media about the shortcomings of algorithmic trading (e.g. in relation to market stability and integrity) (WFE, 2016). Prior work in information systems about the disruptive effects of technological innovation on industries, organizations and working practices (Straub and Wetherbe, 1989) including the pervasive and radical effects of internet computing (Carlo et al., 2011) resonates with HFT as a transformational technology with far-reaching implications for the finance industry.

To understand HFT in its wider societal, industry and organizational context, information systems researchers need to go beyond linear, unidirectional and atomistic explanations of technological innovation to understand the fundamental discontinuities arising from internet-based technology (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003). So far, the information systems community has remained relatively silent on the transformational effects of information technology on financial markets, with leading journals calling for more work in this area to reach wider audiences, including policy-makers (Lucas et al., 2013).

Insights on the ‘sociology of algorithms’ suggest a topological shift has taken place with network links between technologists, HFT firms and trading venues displacing the traditional role of human traders (MacKenzie, 2015). To understand this shift, and the potentially disruptive and unforeseen effects of HFT, more inter-disciplinary theoretical and empirical work is needed in areas of HFT regulation, market structure, algorithmic trading practices and investment outcomes (Gomber et al., 2011; Seddon and Currie, 2016). To address this challenge, we review the current literature on HFT from academic, government, industry and media sources. Our aim is to develop a research agenda for information systems which goes beyond positioning HFT as a contemporary form of financial trading where speed technologies displace human traders, to one which embraces a wider agenda to include the impact of HFT on financial markets and regulatory regimes (Pagnotta and Philippon, 2012; Foresight, 2012).

This paper adopts an inductive, theory-building research method (Eisenhardt, 1989) to build concepts that ‘‘capture the qualities that describe the phenomenon of theoretical interest’’ (Gioia et al., 2012, p. 2). HFT is a complex phenomenon which spans many disciplinary fields including finance, information systems, business ethics, law, economics and sociology. To understand the transformational impact of HFT and to provide more ‘impactful’ research for policymakers (such as financial regulators) (Lucas et al., 2013), we build a multi-level, inter-disciplinary conceptual framework.

This position paper on HFT benefits from field work carried out in the financial districts of Chicago and New York (USA) and London (UK) with semi-structured interviews with HFT market participants, including, HFT firms, banks and an independent software vendor (ISV). HFT strategies are heterogeneous (Biais and Foucault, 2014) despite media reports suggesting HFT is a one-dimensional activity (Ahmed, 2015). Equally, multi-jurisdictional regulatory regimes need to be understood in their own right as directives (from the European Union) and laws differ between countries (Europe) and across government states (USA) (Bell, 2013).

Field work combined with our extensive reading of secondary source data (government reports) reveal how HFT is shaped by the darks arts trilogy of regulatory mandates, technology artefacts and market structure. Three concepts are generated from literature and empirical data: (1) regulatory adaptation, (2) technological asymmetry and (3) market ambiguity. By identifying HFT-related concepts and describing their effects, this paper advocates three areas for further research on the intersection between finance and technology for the information systems community. First, our inductive, theory-building approach generates insights on the HFT landscape from a data-driven, rather than theory-led methodology. While the substantive HFT literature in finance builds theoretical models to measure, for example, latency, speed and liquidity, information systems research needs to go beyond the mechanics of HFT to provide detailed analysis of how HFT firms adapt their market and technological strategies to meet changing regulatory mandates.

Second, we show how the technological arms race with HFTs using proprietary algorithms to attain automated superiority over rivals creates data/information asymmetries among financial regulators and HFTs. Regulation lags technological change (Shorter and Miller, 2014), with regulators responding to disruptive market events (such as Flash Crashes) by seeking to moderate the effects of rogue algorithms by using circuit breakers to slow down trading (SEC, 2014). While regulators adopt a technological solution to oversee of HFT activity, they lack the proprietary knowledge of how algorithmic strategies are developed and executed. Important questions for information systems research need to address how risks can be mitigated from future unpredictable events and outcomes which go beyond the technical dimension (Williams, 2012).

Third, HFT is a contentious practice with HFTs pursuing heterogeneous strategies perceived to have either a positive or negative effect on financial markets. The views of stakeholders, including regulators, HFTs, LFTs, investors and the media, about beneficial and adverse market effects and outcomes from HFT are not helped by limited empirical evidence (Biais and Foucault, 2014). Confusion about HFT strategies presents an ongoing challenge to regulators, who continue to develop regulation from conflicting empirical evidence. Market ambiguity emerges from our study with opportunities for information systems researchers to examine how financial trading strategies differ between HFTs, with their sophisticated technological infrastructure, and LFTs, relying on business processes developed in the more traditional trading era of previous decades.

## Literature review

In this section, we categorize the academic literature on HFT under three themes: regulation, algorithmic trading and financial markets. The discussion combines the observable, material features of HFT including performance measures and outcomes with the hidden mechanisms of algorithmic trading, which we refer to as the dark arts of HFT.

## HFT regulation

Since the financial crisis of 2008, HFT has attracted more public interest, increasingly coming under the radar of international financial regulatory bodies (Kumar et al., 2011). The challenge for regulators is to decide whether HFT is an unfair market practice or simply the next step in the evolution of computer-based business models in financial markets. Regulators are concerned to preserve the stability and integrity of the financial system from risks associated with asymmetric information, threats of volatility, out-of-control rogue algorithms and flash crashes (Bell, 2013, p. 1). Much of this attention is prompted by fears that a core incentive for developing computerized trading systems for executing HFT strategies is for front-running the market (McGowan, 2011). Front-running is illegal, and occurs when a trader with a fiduciary responsibility uses private customer orders to create profit for their own account. Knowing that a large order is about to be sent creates opportunities to profit from any price movements. HFT, however, has no fiduciary obligation, so the term front-running inappropriately describes how HFT benefits from delays in the propagation of orders.

Regulators are also concerned about dark pools, which are private (hidden) exchanges for trading securities not accessible to public investors (Zhu, 2013; Eng et al., 2013). Critics of HFT believe dark pools infringe investors’ rights as many are operated by banks that use the platforms to fulfil clients orders without sending them to public markets such as the New York Stock (NYSE) Exchange or NASDAQ Stock Market, where there is greater transparency. Order types also feature as a controversial issue in HFT regulation as they are instructions on how to handle trades, with some order types allowing traders to post orders that remain hidden, giving them a competitive advantage over other investors (Bodek, 2013). The SEC is now developing rules to ensure that dark pools comply with similar rules facing public exchanges, such as disclosing the types of order available on platforms and sources of pricing data (Michaels and Baker, 2015).

Financial regulation is a critical element for understanding HFT, with different jurisdictions and even exchanges within the same jurisdiction adopting different policies with little coordinated effort for global convergence of HFT regulatory oversight (Bell, 2013). In the US, Regulation National Market System (Reg. NMS) was released in 2005 to promote a new market structure. Since then, HFT has come under the radar of international financial regulators (SEC, 2010a; Prewitt, 2012) with U.S and EU policy-makers passing new laws to tackle HFT loopholes (SEC, 2010b; European Commission, 2015). There are significant differences, however, between the U.S. and EU regulation. The U.S uses a rules-based approach, with the EU adopting a principles-based system. This is reflected in two features of the U.S and EU market systems.

First, Reg. NMS codified the national best bid best offer (NBBO). In order to calculate the NBBO, marketplaces are required to distribute their best bid and best offer for securities they are listing to a securities information processor (SIP). The processor aggregates the quotes from marketplaces and calculates the nationwide best bid and offer in a given security as the NBBO. This system enables market participants to trade on the best available prices in the U.S. Second, a trade-through rule (611 Reg. NMS) is implemented to guarantee trades are executed at the best available price. This prohibits marketplaces trading at prices less beneficial for their customers than the NBBO (SEC, 2005). If a marketplace is unable to match an incoming order at the NBBO, the order is routed to the trading venue offering the best price. This requires inter-linking all the markets. Executing incoming orders at the NBBO and the focus on better prices significantly changes the U.S. market system. As the price is the most crucial factor determining where a trade is executed, new marketplaces displaying aggressive quotes can receive orders from other exchanges.

In the EU, the MiFID principles-based best execution system requires firms to ‘‘…take all reasonable steps to obtain, when executing orders, the best possible result for their clients taking into account price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size, nature or any other consideration relevant to the execution of the order’’ (CESR, 2007, p. 5). The best execution is an obligation relating to investment firms in Europe while outsourced to market venues in the U.S. The actual implementation of best execution, however, is not codified in Europe. There are two ways to implement this obligation. The first is to implement best execution as a process, i.e. to establish an order execution system that routes orders to execution venues proven to provide best execution results on a consistent basis. The second is to determine the best execution venue on an order-by-order basis. This goes beyond the minimum regulatory requirements of MifID and relies on access to real-time market data to determine where to route and execute an incoming order to provide customers with the best possible execution on a trade by trade basis. The regulatory regimes of MifID and Reg. NMS facilitate how new marketplaces compete with established exchanges by improv ing fee and service competition among the trading venues (FCA, 2016).

Both U.S and EU regulators continue to develop policies to tackle market abuse and manipulation from HFT. The U.S regulatory approach is two-pronged. The SEC’s Quantitative Analytics Unit (QAU) works with the FBI to investigate how firms use HFT strategies. The role of the FBI is to assist the regulator in tackling the threat of market manipulation from algorithmic trading by developing new surveillance and monitoring techniques which go beyond traditional policing. The Commodities Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has established a subcommittee of its Technology Advisory Committee to look at the risks posed by HFT.

In the EU, wide variation exists in how Member States apply HFT directives and laws. In Germany, for example, the HFT Act became effective on 15 May 2013. The Act makes HFT a licensed activity, even where it is not offered as a service to others. A high-frequency trader who trades in German securities must apply for a Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin) licence. The Act requires market participants to configure and maintain trading systems with an adequate ratio of orders to executed trades. Penalties and fees are imposed on those making ‘‘excessive use’’ of HFT. This enhanced supervision and intervention enables BaFin to scrutinize algorithmic trading parameters and prevent some HFT strategies. In Italy and France, HFT has been targeted through taxation. Under the transaction tax (FTT), which came into force in 2013, HFT transactions are subject to a 0.02% tax on equity transactions purchased. This type of regulation is controversial and reflects different priorities across region as a whole.

While most of the HFT literature examines the effects on market quality, empirically based studies from finance largely conclude that ‘‘…no academic study could be found that provides evidence that HFT is applying unfair or illegal strategies’’ (Gomber et al., 2011, p. 17). These authors found the debate about co-location concentrates mainly on fair access rather than the assumed negative effects of faster trading, where it is the ‘‘…task of regulators, venues and traders to agree on rules in order to ensure a level playing field providing non-discriminatory access to co-location and proximity services’’ (Gomber et al., 2011, p. 37). Other authors caution that ‘‘…as markets change, appropriate safeguards must be implemented to keep pace with trading practices enabled by advances in technology’’ (Kirilenko et al., 2016, p. 37). One reason is that HFT has come under the media and regulatory spotlight as an unfair practice, where some market participants are unable to benefit from speed of access by co-locating their technological infrastructure next to the exchanges.

## HFT and algorithmic trading

Computerization has impacted financial regulation with improved electronic audit trails, increased order and trade transparency, enhanced ability of market participants to develop and apply automated risk controls, and greater monitoring and use of those controls (IOSCO, 2011; Bamberger, 2009). Since the mid-1980s, the growth of computerization in financial markets has been unprecedented (Henrikson, 2011; Weber, 2006). Technological advances are changing the economics of finance and banking (Davies and Richardson, 2010). The extant literature shows how trading processes have become increasingly automated from order entry to trading venues and back office systems (Kirilenko and Lo, 2013). Computerized trading enables more efficient risk sharing and allows firms to reduce the cost of capital (Hendershott et al., 2011). It also impacts asset markets through transforming the trading process from asset selection to the clearing and processing of trades (Linton and O’Hara, 2011).

The growth in HFT and algorithmic trading mirrors these changes. Often seen as the same activity (SEC 2010a, p. 3606), HFT is a sub-set of algorithmic trading which has two forms. First, execution which typically involves a large order implemented via a computerized algorithm using a program is designed to secure the best price. The order may be split into smaller pieces (tranche) and executed at different times. Second, algorithms search for small trading opportunities in the market rather than executing a set order (NASDAQ, 2015). All HFT activity involves algorithmic trading, although not all algorithmic trading is HFT. The distinction is important in the wider context of HFT regulation and more specifically for establishing causal links between HFT and market outcomes (Biais and Foucault, 2014).

Studies from the sociology of finance focus on algorithms in the context of how human beings interact with other human beings. Both the market and the human actor are theorized as algorithms (MacKenzie, 2014). This observation suggests HFT is a hidden and opaque practice. Disruptive market events coupled with intensifying regulatory scrutiny and monitoring of HFT strategies further suggest that disentangling human activity from the technology is a difficult challenge. For example, the flash crash of 2010 revealed an inherent structural problem in the U.S equity market where circuit breakers that automatically pause trading in the event of violent price swings only became engaged once the entire market had reached a certain threshold (CFTC-SEC, 2011). Since this time, improvements have been made where single-stock circuit breakers can identify any stock that moves up or down by 10% or more in a 5-min period. Measures of this kind are designed to tackle rogue algorithms when the velocity of price movement exceeds pre-set levels (MacKenzie, 2015). While the focus here is oversight of the mechanical aspects of executing financial trading, regulators are also keen to understand the market strategies of HFTs.

## HFT market

The growth of computerized HFT is linked to fragmentation and unpredictability of global financial markets (Menkveld, 2014). Characterized by high speed/low latency and enhanced liquidity, HFT strategies are implicated in disruptive market events, but unpacking the complexity of HFT is difficult as quantitative performance measurement data are scarce (Kirilenko et al., 2016). As a relatively new research topic, HFT differs from traditional forms of trading by large investment firms who hire fund managers to handle investment portfolios on behalf of their clients. With HFT, the need for human involvement is replaced by algorithms which automatically select and then execute each order (MacKenzie, 2014). De-regulated, fragmented and inter-connected global financial markets allow HFTs to use dark pools to reduce transparency of trade execution whilst simultaneously using computerized systems conveniently co-located for accessing the exchanges at rapid speed (Aitken et al., 2014). HFT allows buy and sell orders to be quickly placed for strategic purposes and then cancelled (CFTC-SEC, 2011). Some enable HFTs to detect or anticipate changes in the depth and direction of order flow from institutional and retail investors.

In the early 2000s, the HFT market accounted for less than 10% of equity orders (Kumar et al., 2011). From 2005 to 2009, trading volume (including a proportion for HFT) grew by around 164% (Duhigg, 2009). Following the 2007–2008 financial crisis, the first quarter of 2009 saw total assets under management for hedge funds with HFT strategies around \$141 billion, down about 21% from their peak (Rogow, 2009). By 2009, HFT was estimated to account for 60%–73% of all U.S equity trading volume, decreasing to approximately 50% in 2015 (Iati, 2009, NASDAQ, 2015). In the US in 2014, HFT represented almost 49% of equity and 60% of futures volumes. In 2015, two-thirds of the 7 billion shares traded each day were estimated to be generated by computer programs (Mayer-Scho¨nberger and Cukier, 2013).

## Method

This study was motivated by a general interest in how computerization has transformed financial trading, and a more specific focus on the emergence of high-frequency and algorithmic trading. We received funding from our university to carry out field research in HFT firms based in the U.S and UK. HFT as a form of financial technology, or FinTech, is a fertile research area for information systems researchers. The 2008 financial crash raised serious issues about the use of speed technologies to disrupt traditional business models in financial trading (Pagnotta, and Philippon, 2012) and how technology is used to create and execute financial innovation in the form of new products and services (Funk and Hirschmann, 2014), whilst the extensive media attention following the ‘Flash Crash’ of 2010 suggested that computerization of the financial markets had got out of control. Our review found no studies on the practices of HFT and algorithmic trading in the leading information systems journals. Outside information systems, HFT was viewed as a contributory (rather than causal) factor in the Flash Crash, leading to calls for academic research to evaluate how computerization impacts financial market fluctuations, including stock market events (Kirilenko et al., 2016).

## Empirical context

The intersection between finance and information technology is characterized by immense political, economic and technological change creating opportunities for researchers to examine the wider impact of computerization on financial markets, and more focused studies on financial trading strategies in firms (Muniesa et al., 2011; Mackenzie, 2014). HFT is part of a technological arms race played out among rival firms who deploy technology assets to gain competitive advantages in financial markets (Jones, 2013). This study used qualitative techniques to collect primary data from informants engaged in HFT activities, either as quantitative analysts/traders, compliance officers, business and product development managers and financial regulators. To provide a rich context for discussion, we strengthen our primary data with extensive secondary source material, including academic and media coverage of high profile financial market events. We began our study by compiling a list of the interdisciplinary academic and practitioner literature on HFT. Our aim was to situate HFT as a sub-set of automated or algorithmic trading within financial markets, and to consider how financial regulators develop new rules and laws to monitor HFT market strategies.

## Data collection and analysis

There are many studies in finance journals on HFT influence on market quality and efficiency using concepts of latency, liquidity, speed and risk. With the exception of some quantitative papers on financial trading from management science (Graham et al., 2009; Kozhan and Tham, 2012; Yan, 2010), we identified no contributions from the information systems literature linking HFT with financial regulation and market structure, and more specifically, on how computerization plays a mediating role in these activities. With such a scarcity of academic scholarship on these topics, we began our study with two broad research questions: 1. How does computerization facilitate high-frequency and algorithmic trading? 2. What is the role of financial regulators in providing oversight of HFT?

To answer these questions, we arranged telephone interviews and meetings with traders, regulators, software vendors and compliance officers based in Chicago, New York and

Boston (USA) and in London, UK to include 4 banks, 4 hedge funds, 1 independent software vendor (ISV) and 2 regulators (see Table 1). All these firms (including the ISV) were engaged in HFT activities. The selection of different HFT market participants supports prior work which recommends a functional rather than institutional approach to studying HFT (Gomber et al., 2011) to ring-fence the many different forms of HFT activity rather than just the activities of larger players. For example, the ISV selected is an unlikely market participant in HFT. This firm initially set up a dark pool to enter the HFT market, but found it was unable to gain sufficient market participants to sustain this activity. In selecting several firms for data collection, our interest was not to compare and contrast HFT firms’ strategies, but to obtain comments, views and insights from knowledgeable informants about HFT regulation, technology and market structure.

Our study benefited from the opportunity to contact informants to clarify their answers to our initial questions, and to raise topics for further empirical enquiry. We used a semi-structured questionnaire to ask informants about their knowledge and perceptions of HFT theory and practice. Example questions included the following: (1) How do you define HFT? (2) What are the benefits of HFT to market operations? (3) What are the risks of HFT to market operations? (4) How does automation shape trading strategies? (5) What are the different types of HFT strategy? (6) What do you think were the causes of the 2010 Flash Crash? (7) Has financial regulation changed the markets to favour HFTs over LFTs?

The above exploratory research questions (Silverman, 2011) are designed to gain a broad understanding of regulatory, technology and market dimensions of HFT. We use an inductive (theory-building) approach (Locke, 2007) rather than a hypothetico-deductive model (theory-testing) (Guba and Lincoln, 1994) as this is more appropriate for a research enquiry that potentially spans many social science (management) disciplines, with opportunities for empirical work on a wide range of topics. The recent discussion in the information systems field on the merits of adopting a theorylight (Avison and Malaurent, 2014) avoids the pitfalls of applying a theoretical lens to analyse a complex topic that is relatively new and under-investigated (Gioia et al., 2012). Rather, it is more fruitful to build theory from extensive case studies (Eisenhardt, 1989) and from a multi-disciplinary approach using value-adding contributions which help widen rather than narrow the research agenda.

To understand HFT more deeply, we draw from finance, management and information systems disciplines. Reviewing the extant literature on HFT and algorithmic trading, we observe that computerization is a key factor in executing trading strategies, yet often treated as a black box. Unpacking financial technology, or FinTech, is crucial for understanding the shift from human to algorithmic trading (Mackenzie, 2014) yet more work is needed to understand how this shift alters the traditional relationship between investors and their market access intermediaries, such as HFTs and non-HFT firms (Cooper et al., 2016), and between HFTs and regulators. Questions also arise about the impact of computerized HFT on market movements, and whether HFT has positive market effects (by generating competition among market participants, reducing trading costs and providing liquidity)

## Findings

Our empirical study reveals HFT to be a complex and controversial topic among academics, regulators, industry participants, media commentators, politicians and investors. To organize our primary and secondary data collection, we adopt the ‘Gioia Methodology’ designed to enhance ‘qualitative rigour’ by building first-order, second-order and aggregate concepts (Gioia et al., 2012) to organize and present the findings from our qualitative (inductive) research. The framework presented below (Figure 1) offers a systemic approach for identifying concepts of regulatory adaptation, technology asymmetry and market ambiguity, and for providing potential new areas for information systems research.

## Regulatory adaptation

The global financial system is more complicated now than in previous decades. The U.S has over sixty trading execution outlets with electronic networks, operating platforms and trading systems leading to further fragmentation of the financial market structure. In Europe, the Commission aims to strengthen financial markets through integration, enhanced efficiency and greater competitiveness (ESMA, 2014a, p. 17) with lack of transparency and accountability tackled through regulation. We develop the concept of regulatory adaptation to describe two significant features of financial regulation of HFT: market oversight and systemic risk avoidance.

## Market oversight

Reg. NMS was introduced to improve the structure and operation of the markets following the growth of electronic trading. One trader commented [#B2], ‘‘The market was fragmented and this meant you could not be sure you were getting the best price. Reg. NMS was released to change this. It made sure that you knew where the best price was, and if your order was sent to one exchange but a better price was found at another it would be sent there’’. This regulation was presented as a necessary change so that ‘‘…markets can move forward with certainty concerning their future regulatory environment and appropriately respond to fundamental economic and competitive forces’’ (SEC, 2005, p. 7). A decade later such requirements are still a cause for concern. Every exchange competes to win business. A Head Trader [#B1] commented, ‘‘Brokers, in my opinion, don’t always work for us. They say they will execute our trade at the best prices but then split it, and send blocks to venues that will serve their economic interests and not ours’’. All exchanges are self-regulatory organization but operate under conflicting interests. Following de-regulation, a stock exchange will list itself on its own exchange, attracting investors by generating profits from the fee it charges for services such as co-location or matching orders. However, each exchange is in direct competition with every other trade venue and must attract business, for example, by paying a fee on each order which provides liquidity. In April 2015, the SEC imposed fines on several exchange operators because they had not enforced their own conduct rules.

The 2010 flash crash highlighted the SEC’s lack of market data to quickly identify problems. In 2012, the SEC passed Rule 613 which required that every order, cancellation, modification and execution for exchange listed equities and options (in the U.S) were reported to the Consolidated Audit Trail (CAT). A regulator said [#R1], ‘‘We decided to develop a system which allowed us to retrospectively build trading sequences. Real time data capture did not make sense because we don’t have the capability to act at this speed if we detect market issues’’. The estimated cost to build a tool capable of capturing 58 billion records a day was \$53 m with a further annual running and maintenance cost of \$50 m (DeFrancesco, 2014). While it is possible to capture every trading instruction, it is not possible to determine the causality of trading decisions from every market participant. One Head Trader [#B3] at a leading investment bank commented, ‘‘Automated trading has changed the global financial market structure and impacted every part of the value chain. But with algorithmic trading, it is probably easier to track all trades, so technology may improve transparency, measurement and monitoring. I don’t think technology is responsible for the lack of transparency, but I also think that HFT regulation seriously lags behind technological change’’. By mid-2016, the development of the CAT had still not begun.

In Europe, new oversight rules for HFT apply to investment firms, market operators (trading on stock or financial markets) and services providing post-trade transparency information in the European Union. MifID II requires European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) to develop technical standards for implementing HFT rules (ESMA, 2014a). HFTs must be authorized and subject to regulatory supervision and oversight. To capture as many trades as possible, the rules require firms to trade on organized venues (regulated markets such as stock exchanges). In 2014, the European Parliament and the Council agreed the legislative texts for MifID II and MiFIR. These wide-ranging regulatory initiatives aim to increase the transparency of algorithmic trading.

Since 2002, Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) rules expressly ban creating fictitious trades or attempts to engage in the manipulation of prices. However, it was not until the

15 September 2014 that the CME provided regulatory guidance with Rule 575 on the various types of disruptive behaviour that it had said were encompassed by this earlier rule. Techniques such as layering and spoofing are now identified by name and defined by process (Cadwalder, 2015a, b). Just how HFT is defined differs not only in the media and in the financial industry but also regulatory institutions. For example, the CFTC Working Group, ‘‘emphasizes a mechanical description of HFT that is deliberately neutral regarding types of trading strategies and how they interact with the marketplace’’ (CFTC, 2012, p. 4). Discussing this issue with HFT firms, one compliance officer [#H1] commented, ‘‘Different regulators adapt their definitions of HFT to include either broad or more narrow lists of activities. For example, the CFTC does not think all forms of proprietary trading are HFT, unlike the SEC and EU who use a broader definition. The regulatory landscape is constantly adapting to meet new challenges’’.

Regulators and enforcement agencies including FINRA, the SEC and the Justice Department, and members of Congress have raised concerns about ‘‘unfair HFT practices’’, with some instances of illegal activity. SEC Chair, Mary Jo White, suggests the growth of ‘‘dark trading’’ is harmful to markets, with a pledge to tackle the issue, initially by setting up a panel to look at dark pools, HFT and conflicts of interest in the routing and execution of stock orders. A US-based trader [#B3] commented, ‘‘Preventing disruptive trading practices by intensifying the monitoring of algorithmic trading is a priority of the regulators. More and more rules, laws and directives are introduced, yet each one resolves an issue by seemingly creating a new one. While the public think that more regulation of the markets is a good thing, they don’t understand the problems of regulation and some of the negative effects on how it is transforming the capital markets’’.

## Systemic risk avoidance

The events of the 2010 Flash Crash (which saw the Dow Jones Index plummet over 700 points within minutes, dropping almost 1000 points at its lowest point that day before returning to normal levels) led regulators to seek improvements in systemic risk avoidance identifying the causes of disruptive market events and through policies to prevent future financial market volatility. It took the regulators over four months to give an explanation of whether HFT was implicated in the Flash Crash, concluding that correlation is not proof of causation. The complexity and opaqueness of HFT suggest the exact cause is difficult to determine. One trader commented [#H3], ‘‘It wasn’t a complex HFT strategy that started the crash but an algorithm that was run by a mutual fund company. Their code was written to trade on volume not time or price – surely a bug?’’ One compliance officer [#B4] thought that, ‘‘The crash is only of interest to academics and HFT traders because the prices came back. There was no market news to explain the fall’’. This does not consider the fact that 2bn shares were traded in just 20 min - the automated market had lost control.

In 2013, another episodic volatility spike occurred as the Dow Jones plunged 140 points, returning to normality 3 min later. This event is explained by trading algorithms monitoring and reacting to electronic news feeds triggered by algorithms reacting to word combinations such as ‘‘White House’’ and ‘‘explosion’’. Although these two flash events have different causes, they had similar effects as regulators look for reasons why markets become exacerbated, resulting in extreme price volatility and a rapid erosion of liquidity. Interviews with traders and computer experts reveal a common theme to the regulatory response to prevent systemic market events. One senior IT developer said [#I1], ‘‘Regulators want to create a level playing field, but working out who creates a fair competitive advantage using better technology and those who are gaming the market is really difficult. The regulators and exchanges simply can’t keep up with the pace of technological change’’. This view was echoed by an HFT trader [#H4], ‘‘Regulators are not experts in new technology or algorithmic programming. Complex trading networks conceal trading activities, especially in dark pools where high frequency traders can send and then cancel orders immediately without any penalty’’.

While dark pools share similar features to lit exchanges (e.g. order types, pricing rules and prioritization), their liquidity is not revealed since they do not send the trades to any public data feed. Even where it is legally required, there are significant delays in presenting these data to reduce the market impact of any trade. One Head Trader [#B2] commented, ‘‘It is difficult to regulate HFT where dark pool trading activity is opaque. Private brokers use dark pools because they are subject to fewer regulatory and public disclosure requirements. Trading rates are lower and this reduces overall cost’’. A report from the financial supervisors in 10 countries (including the US, UK and the European

Central Bank) highlighted the risks associated with algorithmic trading and whether this activity has outpaced existing control improvements, and if the existing risk management tools are still effective (SSG, 2015).

Regulation Automated Trading (Reg. AT) released by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) in 2015 proposes rules on risk control and transparency measures to enhance automated trading and reduce the propensity for rogue algorithms to disrupt market events. One requirement would be to give regulators access to code used in their strategies. The compliance officer at an HFT said [#H1], ‘‘There have been comments about letting some official body look at our source code to make sure that problems don’t happen. I have no idea who could be good enough to read our code and then decide if it’s good enough to run in production. I have no confidence in people looking at our code and keeping it secret, and even less confidence that their systems won’t get hacked and our code taken’’.

Regulator interest to reduce adverse market events extends to developing algorithmic trading parameters to prohibit undesirable trading behaviour. The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) proposes that firms engaging in HFT maintain records of data and information on all placed orders, including quotations to enable the regulator (or competent authority) to meet its supervisory duties under MiFID II, the Market Abuse Directive and the Market Abuse Regulation. While this step aims to improve HFT oversight and reduce systemic risk, ESMA watered down calls for HFT firms to store details of each algorithm parameter and market data messages due to industry pressure that it would create a disproportionate burden on HFT firms relative to the benefits gained.

## Technology asymmetry

We define technology asymmetry in HFT to mean one party has superior technology compared to another, whether it is used in ‘lit’ markets or in dark pools. HFT combines the use of fast, sophisticated computers and computer algorithms to submit and cancel orders rapidly (and frequently) and to trade securities quickly, typically resulting in very short holding periods (Bell, 2013). As a sub-set of algorithmic trading, HFT is described as ‘‘computerized trading controlled by algorithms’’ (Prix et al., 2007). Computers used in algorithmic trading displace human intervention as they directly interface with trading platforms, to place (and cancel) orders (Chaboud et al., 2014). We develop the concept of technology asymmetry to describe two significant features of the infrastructure supporting HFT: technological superiority and speed, and automated trading.

## Technological superiority and speed

The release of Reg. NMS did not consider the impact of technological change to market operations. Comments made by a trader #B2 were similar to those given by others, ‘‘The problem was that the new rules designed to make the market more competitive benefited those with the fastest networks, and created the two- tiered trading market that we have today [LFT and HFT]’’. Having a fast network is just one part of being able to process information quickly. Co-location is a service offered by an exchange to those companies who, for a fee, place their trading equipment next to the exchange’s order matching engines. One trader commented [#H1], ‘‘We had to co-locate because the other [HFT] companies we compete against had already done this. If we didn’t then we would never get our orders out as quickly as they did. The longer you wait for an order to execute the less profitable it is going to be. And if it takes too long, then you are going to lose money’’. Reducing latency along every segment of the order chain is vital for any trading strategy. An HFT product manager commented [#H2], ‘‘All of our data is stored in memory on solid state boxes, so our bottleneck was getting the data from the feed [cable]. CPU technology hasn’t changed for 70 years so we developed parallel processing to get the data in as fast as possible. We use FPGA and this really decreased the load time’’. It is not just hardware that has changed significantly; so too has software. A trader commented [#H3], ‘‘We don’t use ordinary database querying tools because they just aren’t quick enough for the size of data sets we have. We scan trillions of rows and have specialized tools that can do this’’. When asked what these were, the trader politely said they preferred not to discuss technology strategy. At HFT conferences, presentations are given with languages such as C, C++, Perl, FinQL (a financial version of SQL) and machine learning packages (artificial intelligence). However, which tools are used and how they have been developed is rarely discussed in an open forum.

The technological arms race pursued by HFT firms by investing in superior technology is for gaining competitive advantage (Goldstein et al., 2014; Pagnotta and Philippon, 2012). In 2000, HFT trades had an execution time of several seconds. By 2010, this had decreased to milliseconds (SEC, 2010b, p. 26), with the fastest traders now measuring their latencies in microseconds (Budish et al., 2015, p. 1549). Proprietary trading strategies are adopted where traders move in and out of positions in seconds or fractions of a second. These trades can be executed in about 300 microseconds (a microsecond is one-millionth of a second) which means that 1000 trades can be made ‘in the blink of an eye (Moshinsky, 2015).

Research on the externalities of HFT show that exogenous technology shocks that increase the speed of trading from microseconds to nanoseconds dramatically increase the order cancellation/execution ratio from 26:1 to 32:1 without having any detectable impact on liquidity, price efficiency or trading volume. The speed at which orders can be sent to the market and then cancelled before they are executed has resulted in many fines levied against those found guilty of fraudulent behaviour. The term ‘‘quote stuffing’’ is used to describe the situation where an exchange receives tens of thousands of quotes which are then quickly cancelled. Some believe this is done to slow competitor systems down as they have to process all of this information. It is only the market makers and other large players who are able to utilize this tactic because a direct link to the exchange is needed. Egginton et al. (2014) found that quote stuffing is commonplace. Of several hundred events occurring each trading day, 74% of U.S listed equities were impacted by this activity at the time of the research. One HFT trader [#H3] said, ‘‘We were accused of quote stuffing and simply paid the fine. This is not what we were doing. It was part of an HFT strategy that we don’t want to explain. We negotiated with the regulator to pay the fine and then made a few adjustments to our code’’.

## Automated trading

Electronic trading is used by all investors, not just those labelled HFT. A fund manager will create many orders during the course of the day and it is up to the trader to place these orders in the most efficient and economical way possible. One head trader commented [#B4], ‘‘We get sent two types of orders. If it is big and worth a lot of money, then I will spend more time on how it is executed to get the best price. If it is a small, liquid order, then I just link it to an algo and push it out of the system’’. This suggests computers control every aspect of when, what quantity and what price an order is traded. Different algorithmic strategies are used by HFTs for buying and selling, but the human decisions on what stock to select is removed. An HFT trader said [#H1], ‘‘An HFT algorithm does not wait for signs that a price might be about to move before it changes direction. It has been coded to capture a defined value [such as 1 bp] and when it has got this, it immediately looks to unwind. Some people are driven by the chance of making a little bit more, or chasing a loss, but the algo removes all emotion from decisions’’. How an HFT will capture profit is essentially the same for an LFTs (Cooper and Van Vliet, 2015); however, the key difference is the high fixed costs incurred by LFTs (staff, buildings) compared with the high technology costs essential to carry out HFT. Technology asymmetry differentiated the HFT from the LFT with the HFT able to compete at a much lower profit margin per trade.

In 2012, both the SEC and the Financial Industry Regulation (FINRA) successfully fined Hold Brothers Online Investment Services LLC (HBOLIS) \$6 m for automated trading violations (Reuters, 2012). Over a two-year period, their trading platform had sent deceptive trades into the market. Originating overseas, order patterns were generated to trick other algorithms that HFT events, for example an arbitrage opportunity, were occurring (Chaboud et al., 2014). HBOLIS had no supervisory procedures to monitor any potential fraudulent trading activities, nor were there sufficient written procedures to achieve compliance. The techniques used did not employ trading algorithms so were not regarded as HFT. However, the aggressive techniques did employ computer-assisted tools that enabled a high rate of order entry and cancellations. These activities and how the company operated could be mistaken for HFT activity.

Whenever an order is sent to an exchange, it must include an order type which identifies how it is processed. In January 2015, the SEC fined Direct Edge \$14 m and UBS \$14.4 for order types which were not, as required by the SEC, documented in the public domain. A computer expert at a leading software firm [#I1] commented, ‘‘Most of the media and trade press mention unfair trading practices in terms of who has the best technology. Another thing to consider is how special order types may be used to exploit trading practices that lead to unfair advantages for HFTs’’. This view is supported by one of the interviewed HFT traders [#H4], ‘‘What bankrupt my company was quite simply that I didn’t know about the special sauce other HFT companies used when they sent their orders to the exchange. These orders types weren’t documented. They had been written specifically for one or two customers’’. A different view was presented by a trader at another HFT [#H2] who said, ‘‘Yes I am aware of order types that can be used to treat an order in a certain way but we don’t use any of them. Why spend millions reducing latency to then have the exchange slow it all down for you?’ The above comments amplify the notion of technology asymmetry, not only as a material feature of computerized trading, but also by the various opinions of regulators and market participants about the attributes and significance of proprietary algorithms in HFT.

Regulator concerns extend to whether software has been adequately tested prior to automation (CFTC-SEC, 2011). One senior IT developer said [#I1], ‘‘Circuit breakers are the default answer to slow down the markets or to halt trading if price volatility occurs. All code should have checks in pace to capture random behavior and I am amazed that stuff is ever released without it’’. Findings suggest that, irrespective of how much testing is done, it is impossible to test for the unknown unknowns. A Trader [#B2] said, ‘‘Complex technology in fragmented, de-regulated and high-speed financial markets makes it difficult to identify and mitigate the risks from rogue algorithms. Everything is now too complicated’’. This finding supports prior research which suggests, in highly automated systems operating in complex markets, ‘‘…there is the risk that the permutations of what can occur are beyond what you’re able to model. Sometimes the only way to find that problem is to stumble over it’’ (Sheppard and Spicer, 2011).

## Market ambiguity

Whilst competing within the same markets as other traders, HFT should be seen as a new paradigm which, for highly capitalized stocks, has replaced the traditional market makers. A study has shown that 25% of the largest HFT firms trading on the NASDAQ in 2008-9 earned an average gross trading revenue of \$2351 per stock per day (Hendershott and Riordan, 2011). These trading revenues are only a fraction earned by market makers and other specialists in the early 1990s, which suggests that compensation to trading intermediaries has decreased over two decades (Jones, 2013). We develop the concept of market ambiguity to describe two significant features of the operational environment of HFT: competitive differentiation and strategic heterogeneity.

## Competitive differentiation

One of the core differences between HFT and LFT is discussed by the compliance officer at a bank [#B3] who said, ‘‘Unlike other finance companies, a HFTs use their own money and are not accountable to any investor or shareholder for their strategies. There is no need to discuss anything that they do and it is only now, in the wake of bad publicity, that they are prepared to talk to anyone’’. Whilst consolidated data and information on HFT firms are scarce, a wide range of market participants leverage technologies to implement HFT strategies. Its impact on financial markets is not one-size-fits-all and different strategies are used within large and small organizations. These include banks, brokerdealer operated proprietary firms, specialized HFT boutiques and hedge funds (Easthope and Chermaine, 2009). Some strategies target structural weaknesses where high speed and fast delivery of market data identifies trades occurring at a stale price. Others look for arbitrage opportunities, capturing price differences due to the inefficiency between exchanges (Menkveld and Zoican, 2014). Some banks operate a dark pool and within this run their own HFT strategies. Researchers suggest a functional rather than an institutional approach for evaluating the HFT market is more appropriate as market participants may apply HFT strategies as either their core business or as a peripheral activity (Gomber et al., 2011).

Discussions with HFT firms underline how algorithmic ‘‘speed’’ trading goes beyond automated buying and selling of shares, towards a new business model in financial trading. One HFT trader said [#H2], ‘‘HFT is a completely new paradigm. Job adverts ask for mathematical and statistical skills rather than financial qualifications. Trading is based on statistical opportunity and not the fundamentals of the market or the stock being traded. At the end of the day we close flat. Throughout the day we will buy and sell discrete volumes so we don’t end up with the risk in holding a position overnight’’. Liquidity, perhaps the most important ingredient in the trading mix, is the by-product of most HFT strategies.

Profits may accrue from only 51% of trades, but an analyst [#H1] explains: ‘‘The law of large numbers states that if you are able to do things enough times you will ultimately capture more than you lose. We back test our models and only ever use them if they have a higher statistical probability of success’’. HFT firms can offset low margins with high volumes of trades numbering in the many millions, and in the US, this approach nets over \$1.3 billion each year (Tabb, 2014). These firms typically compete against other HFT firms rather than long-term investors. The strategy for many HFT firms is therefore not to consume large amounts of capital, accumulate positions or retain their portfolios overnight (Kumar et al., 2011) as the volatility in the markets and global trading activity of 24-h cycles involves high levels of risk which may substantially decrease profits.

HFTs do not target all stocks, only those frequently traded in high volumes, as they trade large numbers of stocks to profit from fractional price differences (Aldridge, 2013). Some of their strategies try to detect as much information as they can about an order from as many data points as possible (size, direction and date), and then try to capture this opportunity. Interviews with HFT firms about the finer points of their strategies and technologies remained a closely guarded secret. One HFT trader said [#H3], ‘‘HFT is a fiercely competitive business. Firms don’t discuss their technology with rival firms, not even the programming language they use. I have a condition in my contract that says I can’t work for another HFT for two years’’.

## Strategic heterogeneity

State-of-the-art technology infrastructure and its application are critical to HFT, not only as an enabler, but also as major part of HFT strategy to compete with rival market players. In 2010 a fibre optics company spent \$300 m laying a new cable between Chicago and New York. One HFT trader discussed why they had bought this service [#H2], ‘‘We all knew who was using Spread Networks because they had an office where the data hub was located. Even if you are just a millisecond slower you are still going to be behind them when you get to the exchange and being first on the order book is not up for debate’’. This network saved 3 ms when compared to other networks. Within a few years, companies had turned their attention to microwave towers because transmission speed through air is quicker than through glass. In 2011, the chip iX-eCute was reported to process trades in 740 ns (one nanosecond is a billionth of a second) (Millar, 2011). When you have two securities that track the same index, their prices will appear identical, but when viewed at the millisecond interval, prices can be different by a penny or more. This allows the HFT company to buy when the price is low and then to sell when it has moved up, sometimes a few milliseconds later. Academic research on HFT trading profits from S&P 500 futures found that this activity was concentrated among a small number of HFT firms (Baron et al., 2014).

Although two HFT companies may share the same network, exchange supplied data and co-location services, their strategies are coded differently. One product manager [#I1] commented, ‘‘We sell the same system to hundreds of companies. Workflows are customised to fit how they want to create an order, and then send it to the trade desk. They select one of many different broker supplied ‘algos’ when they send it to the market. These ‘algos’ trade the order in different ways, but exactly the same code is sent to all brokers’ customers’’. This differs from how HFTs continuously alter their proprietary code. An HFT trader[#H1] said, ‘‘The market is changing all the time. If we don’t spot and change our code we’ll go out of business’’. HFT and fund managers look for different short- and long-term profit opportunities from the same security. Fund managers compete with other fund managers on stock selection and market growth. HFT firms are all looking at fleeting opportunities and must continuously adjust their code and enhance their infrastructure if they are to be the most successful.

One significant outcome following Reg. NMS has been the change to the market maker, the original source of liquidity. These traditional specialists have been replaced by HFT companies who leverage their low-latency systems to get their limit orders first onto the exchange’s book, where they are later matched. Unlike the traditional market maker who is contractually obliged to always offer liquidity, no such obligation exists for HFT. A rule in Reg. NMS allowed exchanges to pay liquidity providers for either supplying or taking liquidity (maker/taker fee). Without any obligation, concerns about liquidity disappearing when prices adversely move have been raised. An HFT trader said [#H3], ‘‘For years it was the brokers who provided market liquidity. They charged a high commission and made money from a wide spread. Automation has changed all of that and they can’t compete any more. It’s an urban myth to think that they always provided liquidity – when prices fell they just didn’t pick up the phone’’. Research has shown that in general HFT aids market liquidity, improved prices and reduced cost (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2012; Brogaard et al., 2014; Brogaard et al., 2015), but there is still concern that the markets are not operating in the interest of the long-term investor. Regulators are looking at the market structure more closely as higher volatility in asset prices and larger fluctuations in liquidity may be a direct consequence of the reduced role of traditional (regulated) market makers and increased high frequency and algorithmic trading (Chung and Chuwonganant, 2012).

## A research agenda

This section summarizes the above discussion and outlines a research agenda for information systems. HFT at the intersection of finance and technology is discussed in broader policy, strategy and technology debates within the financial industry and capitalism (Picketty, 2014) and in studies on how financial innovation alters market structures and operations (Funk and Hirschmann, 2014). Theoretical and empirical understanding of HFT is strengthened by multidisciplinary and multi-jurisdictional (i.e. comparative country) research as globalization, de-regulation and fragmentation of markets increase complexity in financial trading.

As a qualitative, inductive study on a relatively new practice, this position paper generates concepts and ideas, rather than revisits and refines existing theoretical paradigms as a way of discovering new knowledge (Gioia and Pitre, 1990; Gioia et al., 2012). In particular, it supports an interdisciplinary approach as terminology, such as liquidity and latency, used in the finance literature, are critical for understanding HFT in an information systems context. Prior work from information systems on thematic areas including disruptive technology (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Carlo et al., 2011), data analytics and business model transformation (Loebbecke and Picot, 2015; Seddon and Currie, 2016), business value from technological investments (Dehning et al., 2003) and industry-wide technology standardization in financial services (Markus et al., 2006) provides a theoretical and empirical context for further work on computerization in financial markets.

Regulation, technology and markets theorized in thematic silos provide the low-hanging fruit for HFT research. To gain a deeper understanding of HFT, however, researchers need to consider the relationships between these silos, such as how regulation impacts HFT, why technical innovations offer strategic advantages to HFT and what advanced database systems (such as those using artificial intelligence) may assist regulators in monitoring disruptive market events. HFT emerges as an activity shrouded in secrecy, with HFT firms reluctant to discuss their strategies with regulators, market participants, investors and academic researchers. This paper suggests HFT embodies the dark arts trilogy comprising regulatory regimes designed to monitor and control HFT firms, technological innovation for transforming financial trading practice and market structure with HFTs competing with LFTs disadvantaged by their operational paradigm. We present these themes below as potential research topics for information systems, recognizing that some areas will be more challenging than others.

## Financial regulation and policy

HFT research on regulatory oversight, market inefficiency and systemic risk linked to disruptive market events, such as flash crashes (Kirilenko et al., 2016), provides fertile areas for further research by information systems scholars. Presented as single (isolated) case studies, HFT emerges not as the cause of these events, but as a contributory factor (SEC, 2010c). The concept of regulatory adaptation is used to describe how regulators develop and refine their policies to provide oversight of HFT to avoid systemic risk in financial markets. Regulatory adaptation produces intended and unintended consequences as regulators impose new rules and mandates on dynamic and changing financial environments, with HFT firms obscuring their trading strategies using proprietary algorithms which are difficult to isolate, track and monitor.

Regulators experience difficulty in keeping pace with technological change (Lucas et al., 2013), with opportunities for future research on whether more regulation is needed, and how additional regulation will improve market oversight without adversely affecting financial innovation (Cooper et al., 2016). For example, a ‘rogue bank trader’ who pursues reckless investments for personal gain does not bring down the entire banking system, but triggers a regulatory change. This is also the case for fraudulent HFT activity which results in fines and penalties for exchanges and companies. More longitudinal research on financial regulation is needed to disentangle institutional and individual trading patterns that signal dishonest behavior, recognizing that algorithmic (robot) trading strategies displace ‘humans’ from direct accountability. Currently, most research on financial regulation adopts a narrow problematic of regulatory effectiveness (or lack of) which frames enforcement in terms of inputs or outputs (Williams, 2012). Information systems research can go beyond this limited agenda by exploring HFT regulation, not as a decontextualized and mechanical activity problematized in terms of non-compliance versus compliance, but as multi-faceted enterprise which involves the interaction of numerous stakeholders with competing agendas.

## Data and technology asymmetries

The regulatory-lag is further complicated by technology asymmetry between regulators and HFTs. Technology outpaces regulators’ ability to develop policy to moderate speed technologies to provide a level playing field for all market participants. At one level, the concept of technology asymmetry describes how HFTs benefit from state-of-the-art infrastructure by utilizing the skills of quants analysts and programmers to build proprietary algorithms executed at all venues, with regulators struggling to keep pace with technological change. A more detailed approach, however, goes beyond technology ‘haves’ (HFTs) and ‘have nots’ (regulators) to include a wider range of issues such as big data analytics.

HFT uses volumes of (big) data, with regulators thwarted in their attempts to capture all market event data to inform financial policy development (Seddon and Currie, 2016). Big data is fundamental to HFT, but real time surveillance has proven unrealistic and impractical for regulators. The lack of technical capability to capture and interpret data signals, the difficulty of navigating vast volumes of trading data, and the increasingly marginal role of fund managers and traders in automated trading make it difficult for regulators to develop viable HFT monitoring tools. More academic research is needed which uses actual market data to identify trading patterns of HFTs and exchanges. This is crucial for gaining insights into the operations of financial markets and for understanding the mechanics of fleeting market events such as flash crashes.

HFT firms benefit from technology asymmetry in their relationship with regulators, but HFT and algorithmic trading is not a one-size-fits-all. Significant competitive differentiation exists across the HFT market, and between HFTs and LFTs (Blocher et al., 2016). HFT is an institutional and functional activity with pure-play HFTs, large banks and even software vendors setting up HFT activities. Despite the problematic that HFTs disadvantage LFTs due to their superior use of technology, more research is needed to understand the heterogeneous strategies pursued by the various market participants. Strategies may include technological resources, but also how some HFTs benefit from more effective networks of relationships which include regulatory bodies.

## Financial markets and technology

Competitive differentiation and strategic heterogeneity create HFT market ambiguity. The ‘ambiguity effect’ is a cognitive bias where decision making is affected by a lack of information or ambiguity (Ellsberg, 1961). Market ambiguity characterizes HFT as regulators face difficulty in identifying market participants and algorithmic trading practices (ESMA, 2014b; Kirilenko et al., 2016). Literature from regulatory agencies shows variation in the definitions and terms used to describe HFT activity, with some regulators preferring a narrow definition and others extending it to include a wider group (WFE, 2016). Research is needed to provide a deeper understanding of HFT market participants to remove the current confusion where regulators produce different definitions of certain forms of algorithmic trading.

Our study recommends a much closer collaboration between the academic disciplines of finance and information systems, particularly as practitioners in finance have deep industry knowledge about financial markets, yet less expertise about the technological features and functionalities of algorithmic trading. Conversely, information systems and computer science disciplines focus on the technological artefacts, but need to gain a wider appreciation of how financial markets work, and more specifically, how HFT uses technology to facilitate the dark side of trading, which may include illegal activities, including anti-competitive practices.

With the financial market so fragmented and complex, the regulatory challenge is to co-ordinate responses across different, and often, incompatible regulatory regimes which govern competing national trading venues (Bell, 2013). Narrowly channelled regulatory responses in the form of technological quick-fixes and one-off fiscal constraints on HFTs, however, may only produce piecemeal change with limited oversight potential. In summary, this research positions HFT as a dark art with regulators seeking to design, implement and enforce robust policies and standards on HFTs, while at the same time challenged by an automated trading environment which closely guards its secrets.

## Conclusion

This work develops three aggregated concepts from the extant literature on HFT and from interview data from HFT market participants and regulatory bodies. Theoretical and empirical work is enriched by inter-disciplinary quantitative and qualitative studies on HFT regulation, technology and markets. Regulatory adaptation, technology asymmetry and market ambiguity reflect the dark arts trilogy of HFT. Future research can use these combined concepts to build on current HFT and FinTech work.
