# Do too many goals impede a program? A case study of enterprise system implementation with multiple interdependent projects

## A B S T R A C T

Complex or large information technology (IT) implementations are typically managed as single programs composed of multiple projects. Program managers must be able to manage the interfaces of multiple interdependent projects to realize the goals of an IT implementation. However, the common problems of conflict and poor coordination often hinder the achievement of a program’s goals. Part of this difficulty may lie in the structuring of the program goals themselves. Guided by Strategic Consensus Theory (SCT) and Realistic Group Conflict Theory (RGCT), we examine three enterprise system programs to study the relationship between the shared understanding of multiple or single program goals and conflict resolution and the coordination of programs in an attempt to explain a program’s successful implementation. We find that a shared goal understanding as suggested by SCT is not sufficient to facilitate essential coordination. However, a superordinate goal as suggested by RGCT appears to resolve any discrepancies. The results imply that goals should be structured as superordinate requirements.

## 1. Introduction

The deployment of complex Information Technology (IT) such as an enterprise system within an organization is becoming increasingly complex, represents a large commitment of resources, and spans functional areas and even organizations [9,60]. Current implementations include a greater dependence on resource allocation to move an organization toward a wide-ranging system through a number of closely controlled and related projects that are managed as a comprehensive program [21,65]. This management structure is beneficial when spanning functional areas, involving a variety of internal and external stakeholders at multiple organizational levels, pursuing different goals, and adhering to resource constraints [8]. However, these same conditions lead to inter-team conflicts across the multiple projects that lead to management difficulties [19,29].

Two major characteristics of an IT program are the interdependence of projects and a focus on organizational goals [20,51]. Interdependence is the extent to which stakeholders and team members on each project realize that the goals of the project are intertwined, tasks share overlapping but not identical characteristics, and limited resources are allocated to accomplish the individual project tasks to achieve every goal at the project and program levels. The goals of a program are higher-order strategic goals that are undeliverable by any single project with its own set of achievement goals [51,61]. Specific program goals should be clearly established and shared among all key program members to provide the expectations for each individual project and the coordination necessary among projects [50]. In short, to be a success, an IT program must achieve the goals common to its multiple projects in spite of differences in localized goals, tasks, and resources [34]. Coordination and cooperation among interdependent projects are crucial during the implementation process to achieve the program’s goals; unfortunately, effective cooperation and coordination among projects is not commonly observed [24,56]. How to structure program goals to best foster the essential cooperation and coordination among the multiple projects is not clear.

The management and psychology literatures contain an extensive body of work on goal studies and theories [6,18]. Goals can play a significant role in the achievement of success at the individual, group, and organizational levels [13,16]. However, the literature does not provide guidance on how to influence behaviors to best achieve IT program success. Among the many goal theories in the literature, Strategic Consensus Theory (SCT) indicates that to unify multiple projects and achieve the necessary coordination and cooperation, one must pursue a shared understanding of all program goals. In contrast, Realistic Group Conflict Theory (RGCT) argues for structuring a single overreaching goal to achieve the same ends [14,57]. This contrast between a multiple goal structure and single goal structure leads to the general research question in this study: What type of goal structure is best for fostering effective coordination and cooperation that are important in attaining IT program implementation success?

SCT emphasizes that goal agreement among key stakeholders is a critical factor of organizational performance [14]. This suggestion is also echoed in the IT project literature [32]. SCT implies that a shared goal understanding among key members of a management team at the strategic formulation and implementation levels will lead to greater success [31]. However, it is widely observed in practice that conflict among project managers within an IT program often hinders goal achievement, cooperation, and coordination, even when the IT program goals are well established [23]. This difficulty suggests a need to examine the multiple goal structure of typical IT programs. In contrast, RGCT suggests that a superordinate goal (one that is compelling to members of different groups and attained only when groups work together) should lead to satisfactory conflict resolution, the coordination of effort, and necessary cooperation [57]. Based upon RGCT, a single compelling program goal must be perceived by the different stakeholders of an IT program. To help resolve the issue of attaining a better goal structure, we address whether goal understanding alone will lead to IT program success. Instead, should a superordinate goal be formed based on RGCT? It may be that a combination of SCT and RGCT provides a sufficient explanation of how goals can be shared and structured to achieve IT program success.

In this study, we draw on SCT and RGCT to examine the relationship between goal consensus among key program members and satisfactory conflict resolution during the implementation of an enterprise system. More specifically, we examine cases of conflict in implementation programs for Enterprise Systems to test how goal consensus can drive successful programs in IT. We consider whether the concept of shared goal understanding applies to multiple levels within the organization or whether a superordinate goal provides a better foundation for understanding how to develop cooperation within IT programs. Understanding the nature of structuring goals will assist IT program managers in establishing essential drivers for success.

## 2. Theoretical background

Enterprise systems are highly complex information systems. Numerous studies have identified a number of critical factors to the success of an enterprise system. Frequently identified critical success factors include a clear understanding of strategic goals, the commitment by top management, effective project management, and reliable interdepartmental communication [1,3,62]. In this body of literature, the establishment of clear goals and objectives is often regarded as being among the most important in enterprise system implementations [2,54]. For example, when top management sets and communicates goals, it show its support and nurtures involvement. Thus, as with any IT program, the implementation of an enterprise system should begin with clear goals to move all parties toward success. These findings provide insight on the importance of having an established goal for an IT program, including the implementation of an enterprise system. However, goals can be established in many forms, and the best way to structure goals in an IT program has not been clarified in the literature.

## 2.1. Goal consensus as shared understanding

Consensus is defined as the general agreement among all participants and is viewed as an important outcome of group decision making [15]. The logic behind this notion is that consensus enhances organizational performance by improving coordination and cooperation within the organization. Consequently, the key constructs of strategic consensus theory are consensus, organizational performance, and coordination/cooperation. The boundaries of this work maintain a relatively narrow set of participants and domains: that of top managers agreeing on strategic goals and the means of attaining those goals [4]. The central proposition of SCT is that a higher degree of bounded consensus is positively associated with team process effectiveness and organizational performance [30,31].

Due to the potential to drive success, researchers have examined the nature of the consensus construct. Consensus on strategic goals can be segmented into four dimensions: locus, scope, content, and degree [42]. The ‘‘locus’’ of consensus refers to the members of an organization who should participate in achieving goal consensus. Early studies focused exclusively on top management teams with regard to this dimension. Kellermanns et al. [30], however, indicate that the positive impact of goal consensus would be diminished unless the locus is extended to include broader groups (e.g., middle managers). Because effective implementation demands compliance and active cooperation from middle managers, it makes sense that the locus should be expanded to achieve the implementation of goals as well as the setting of goals.

The ‘‘scope’’ of consensus considers ‘‘how many’’ members in the target group should participate, ranging from a small selection of team members to the entirety of the team [22]. Because SCT relies on coordination and cooperation as a mechanism of achieving desirable outcomes, common knowledge among team members is a crucial facilitator. A high degree of shared understanding of common goals can enable the coordination and cooperation among management at all levels [30,31]. Each individual must have a collective awareness of the team tasks and goals to facilitate information exchange and coordination [45]. In an enterprise system implementation team, to promote coordination and cooperation effectiveness, we argue that shared goal understanding should be as pervasive as possible.

Researchers frame the ‘‘content’’ of consensus to include specific goals, the priorities of goals, and even the means of achieving them [14,27,52]. Managers at lower levels are not as likely to be as aware of specific strategic ends and means as the top managers involved in the decision-making process [24]; however, their shared understanding of the assigned strategic goals is critical to the efficient implementation of any plan [59,66]. A shared understanding of goals is the foundation of planning and communicating the related actions, knowledge and objectives of interdependent members. Without a shared understanding of common goals, harmonization and synchronization could not be achieved effectively. With everything else being equal (e.g., team competence), people tend to work harder when they know the precise goals and expected outcomes [40].

Finally, the ‘‘degree’’ of consensus refers to how strongly the members involved actually agree on the content [42]. Empirical studies argue a linear relationship between the degree of consensus and firm performance [14]. The degree of consensus leads one to believe that an overpowering goal might be effective in directing the organizational effort by providing a bearing. A higher degree of consensus adds to the shared understanding of the goals. However, agreement does not mean inclusiveness. The goals of an IT program may all be worthy, but still lack breadth to represent all possible participants. All four dimensions of consensus are included in determining a level of shared understanding.

## 2.2. Cooperation and coordination in IT program success

Cooperation in a team means working collectively toward the stated objectives. Effective cooperation brings harmonization and synchronization to the individual contributions [36]. Coordination in teams involves integrating and aligning the actions, knowledge, and objectives of interdependent members to achieve the desired ends [5]. Coordination ensures that a team functions as a unified whole [10] – that is, when a team attains a high level of coordination, all members contribute to the results. Positive organizational performance is an outcome of coordination and cooperation among the managers of different units within organizations, as influenced by goal consensus [31,33,67].

In the IT program context, success refers to the completion of the full scope of the program and typically of the enabled business functions. Specifically, a successful enterprise system program involves performance goals and a high level of coordination and cooperation among stakeholders [34]. Sharing goals, having similar priorities, and achieving collaborative support among stakeholders are critical in the chartering phase of implementation [60]. However, the implementation of a typical enterprise system does not exhibit much in the way of collaborative efforts to integrate the various projects [55]. Given that each project team within an implementation program for Enterprise Systems has its own set of tasks, the interactions and coordination among project teams are critical to achieve the program’s goals. This requires consensus be achieved through sharing content with executives, program managers, and project managers. The success of the implementation of an enterprise system is achieved only when all projects are successful. With the abovementioned factors taken into account, SCT suggests that the shared understanding of an agreed-upon set of program goals among all key program stakeholders and management levels will lead to the desired outcomes [22]. The concepts from this and the preceding section are illustrated in the following propositions.

P1a. A greater extent of shared understanding of the goals of an IT program among all key program team members will facilitate levels of cooperation and coordination during the implementation process.

P1b. A greater extent of cooperation and coordination among all key IT program members during the implementation process will enhance program success.

## 2.3. Goal consensus as a superordinate goal

RGCT considers the significance of intergroup behaviors and interactions across groups. Open lines of communication and contact are a necessary but insufficient factor in the reduction of group tensions. Indeed, it is maintained that contact in and of itself may cause an escalation of intergroup hostilities when goals are set under such conditions. The basic premise of RGCT is that intergroup conflicts result from localized goals leading to a competition for resources but can be lessened by the presence of a superordinate goal achievable only through intergroup collaboration. Unlike SCT’s focus on organizational performance as a result of the shared understanding of goals, the objective of RGCT is to reduce intergroup conflicts by having a superordinate goal. By definition, superordinate goals are compelling to members of all groups and only attainable when groups unite to work toward their overarching goal [57].

Intra-group identity, values, customs and practices are affected by associations with other groups, and the overall tenor of intergroup exchanges is determined by joint interests [57]. The overall favorability of intergroup interactions is determined by the reciprocal interests and goals of the groups involved [58]. Hostility toward out-groups may arise if the identity, interests, resources, or status of one’s group is threatened. For example, in an enterprise system, one department may perceive other departments to be recipients of greater benefits. Only under the existence of a superordinate goal for all groups is contact and communication utilized to reduce conflicts and enhance cooperation. In other words, RGCT argues that only a superordinate goal can reduce the intergroup conflicts that stand in the way of coordination and cooperation.

Thus, different goal structures should lead to differences in the cooperation and collaboration that are essential to achieving the desired outcomes. When broadening the locus and scope required for strategic consensus, the degree of shared understanding and content must be broadened to have importance and relevance. To accomplish this, RGCT emphasizes the creation of a single compelling goal that can only be achieved by the efforts and resources of all participating groups. The RGCT view leads to the following propositions.

P2a. When an IT program goal is perceived as a superordinate goal by all key program members, satisfactory conflict resolution will be more likely achieved.

P2b. Satisfactory conflict resolution among all key program members will result in greater cooperation and coordination.

The above propositions are depicted in Fig. 1. A consensus about program goals is crucial in any respect but may not be sufficient to ensure success. The shared understanding of goals is crucial in attaining the cooperation and coordination necessary for the success of the implementation of an IT program. However, the concept of a superordinate goal includes the relationships highlighted by the dashed lines in Fig. 1, which basically address the aspects of mitigating conflict to improve coordination, cooperation and the attainment of desired outcomes.

## 3. Research methodology

One objective of this study is to test the propositions between goal consensus among key program members and satisfactory conflict resolution, coordination, cooperation, and the success of the implementation of a complex IT program. Another objective is to redefine the theoretical constructs and propositions for subsequent empirical studies. To achieve these objectives, we conduct a multiple-case study as our research strategy [68]. Case study researchers and methodologists have articulated both positivist and interpretivist approaches [37,63]. We follow a positivist process while allowing sufficient evidence to surface that may suggest additional relationships [17,48,54]. The process and expectations of validity and rigor for our study are derived from current practice, as outlined in Table 1.

## 3.1. Case study design

A central issue in case study design is how many cases to include in the research project. A single case can be sufficient to disconfirm an existing theory if its predictions do not hold [43]. However, this study focuses on how and why the outcomes might occur and looks for a literal replication of these expectations from case to case. The causal relationships that are the basis for generalized knowledge should predict patterns of behavior across situations. Therefore, a multiple-case design for this study is selected [49]. To confirm, disconfirm, or refine the propositions of the study, a theory-based sampling approach was adopted. Confirming and disconfirming cases are sampling strategies in which data are sought that will support or challenge the propositions.

We selected our sample in two steps. First, the cases were firms that have implemented an enterprise systems program and have gone live. The criteria used in the case selection process were based on a totality of implemented projects within an enterprise systems program and the presence of conflict during the implementation process. The participating firms came from different industries and were based in Taiwan. Second, we selected participants from each firm as key informants. To gain a deep understanding of conflict resolution, we chose key members of the IT program team among executives, program managers, project managers, and IT managers. Program managers are in charge of the implementation of the entire program and are in a good position to respond to interview questions regarding conflict resolution. Project managers are in charge of their own project implementation and represent the interests of internal, functional users. IT managers are in charge of system solutions. From each firm, five participants were interviewed. These criteria ensured that the data gathered focused on the proposed relationships with prior knowledge about outcomes in similar contexts. After contacting a number of potential firms, we selected three as representative cases. Table 2 summarizes the demographic data of the firms and interviewees.

Case Alpha was established as an organization in 1976 and is one of the largest connector manufacturers in the world focusing on the development and manufacturing of a variety of connectors. Alpha’s business strategy is to provide high quality, the best leadtime and competitive prices to their customers. Alpha has offices and factories in Taipei City (Taiwan), Suzhou (China), and Chung Shan (China). Five key program members were identified: one program manager, three project managers, and an IT manager named Cheng, Liang, Lee, Chang and Hsiao, respectively.

Case Beta, established as a company in 1996, is one of the leading firms in the computer peripheral industry. Beta is a global manufacturer of card readers and external enclosures. Beta has its offices and factories primarily in Taipei City (Taiwan). Beta is an OEM/ODM manufacturer and presents its consumer products backed by well-retained production lines, an innovative R&D team, and strict quality control. For its program implementation, five key program members were identified: one Chief Information Officer (CIO), one program manager, two project managers, and one IT manager named Ho, Lee, Liang, Yu and Lin, respectively.

Case Gamma, established as a firm in 1999, devotes itself to LED wafer and chip manufacturing. Gamma has been a fast-growing enterprise since its establishment. Currently, Gamma is the only LED company with the capability of offering a full spectrum of products, including high-power InGaN Blue, Green, and near-UV LEDs with customer bases in Korea, Taiwan, the US, HK and China. Five key program members were identified: one program manager (also the CIO), three project managers, and one IT manager named Lin, Tsai, Wang, Chen and Hung, respectively.

## 3.2. Conduct case study – data collection

A case study protocol including the procedures and general rules that should be followed was created prior to data collection. The aim of the case study protocol is to ensure the consistency of the interviews for each case and to increase the reliability of the study. The protocol for the study as shown in Table 3 consists of guidelines suggested by Yin [68] and Pare´ [49]. These guidelines are applicable to the process of collecting primary data from semistructured interviews [47]. A semi-structured interview approach is recommended when the researcher identifies many of the interview questions but cannot predict the answers [49]. It allows open questions and follow-up conversations between the researchers and interviewees to provide full responses and the freedom to respond in detail with illustrated concepts.

Each interview team consisted of two researchers. The interviews were taped, with agreement from the interviewees. The taped interviews were transcribed verbatim into text files. The interview questions included five parts: (1) IT program background, (2) consensus among key program members, (3) interdivision relationships and conflict resolution, (4) cooperation and coordination among project teams, and (5) program outcomes. Case Beta: An ERP implementation was initiated by the CEO to reduce redundant human resources and enhance productivity. To achieve these objectives, the organization adopted an ERP system, implemented BI practices, and revised its business processes. The timeframe extended from August 2005 through August 2006 even though the original plan was for six months. After the program’s implementation, 30 employees from material purchases, production, and inventory control were released or assigned to other departments. The accounting department was among the most supportive of the implementation because the proposed system would enhance the department’s control. After implementation, approximately 50% of the workforce was removed from those departments. Furthermore, conflicting views existed among the departments involved in data and document management. Prior to the new system, data and document inputs were handled by the accounting department; however, with the new system, document entry was distributed. The purchasing and manufacturing departments argued that the new system benefited accounting and sales at their expense. Many workers that were transferred to other departments had difficulty adjusting to their new job roles and resigned from the organization.

## 4.1. Goal consensus as shared goal understanding

Alpha provides an example of the impact that goals can have in company advancement. However, a superordinate goal was not established even though a shared understanding had been achieved. The storyline starts with the set of goals shared among program participants through the following comments (italics added to indicate the major point in the context of the entire comment). According to the program manager and a project manager:

‘‘. . .We would have a program plan including the general direction, goals, program implementation methodology, and overall program schedule. . .the purpose of this program plan is to make sure that key members within this implemented program agreed on the overall directions. . .’’ (Alpha, Cheng)

‘‘. . .We had a meeting agenda for our weekly meetings. . .the agenda included: (a) discussion issues and (b) proposed alternatives. For those issues not resolved in the meetings, they were retained in the minutes on an issue list. Furthermore, we also set expected dates for the action items in the meeting minutes. Therefore, the meetings were a way to achieve consensus among our key users. . .’’ (Alpha, Lee)

The above examples show a clear channel of communication and the establishment of an understanding among key program members about the directions and expectations of the program.

Beta adds strong evidence to this consideration under conditions of greater conflict. Again, the quotes lead us through the evidence gathered. The program goals were shared and appeared to be understood by those involved, but they did not seem to be overarching and were decided at the top management level. In the words of two project managers:

‘‘. . .All the stakeholders involved in the implementation understood that the objective of this program was to enhance data sharing within the organization. It was perceived to benefit top management, including the CEO, for analyzing historical data . . .’’ (Beta, Liang)

‘‘. . .It seems that there was a consensus on the implementation because our top management told us that our old ERP system needed to be changed. We must follow our top management’s command. . .’’ (Beta, Yu)

In short, in both the Alpha and Beta cases, the program objectives and directions were communicated and understood among the key program team members; however, in the Beta case, the team members perceived the program to have the purpose of enhancing the top management’s control while maintaining the passive role of other stakeholders. The team members in case Beta were accepting of the program but not enthusiastic about it.

## 4.2. Goal consensus as a superordinate goal

Gamma shows a different scenario. The evidence collected points to the establishment of a superordinate goal by the management that drove greater cooperation. The statements collected as part of the evidence illustrate the relationship among the constructs in the model. To begin, the superordinate goal is identified:

‘‘. . .The key program members understood that the overall goal of this ERP implementation was to enhance the business processes of the organization for all departments. . .’’ (Gamma, Chen)

‘‘. . .Our sales, product management, manufacturing and human resource departments were all convinced that the system could integrate their business functions more smoothly. It improved each department’s operational efficiency and effectiveness . . .’ (Gamma, Tsai)

In summary, the managers in case Gamma were very supportive of the proposed program implementation. They clearly identified what the system could do to enhance their functional productivity. The identified program objective was well received by the key stakeholders.

## 4.3. Satisfactory conflict resolution

In Alpha, where there was a shared understanding of the program goals that were not well received by the key program members, the CEO communicated with department managers about the benefits of implementation. Furthermore, the CEO discussed the needs and requirements of the proposed system with key members of each department and decided which modules would be included in the proposed enterprise system. Therefore, conflicts arose during the implementation process and were typically addressed at lower levels in terms of resolution. According to an IT manager:

‘‘. . .Our IT department often needed to resolve conflicts among business units when conflicts couldn’t be resolved by the business managers. . .usually conflicts among business units would not be reported to the board of directors unless really serious problems arose. The IT department played a critical role in the integration of those business unit demands to resolve conflicts in our organization. . .’’ (Alpha, Hsiao)

Shared goals, such as schedules, were often used to push coordination to overcome conflict. In the words of a project manager:

‘‘. . .The module leader might talk with our program manager when conflict occurred among module leaders. However, in most cases, they would resolve the conflict with each other because our program manager monitored the program schedule carefully. For example, the purchasing module and manufacturing module often conflicted during the implementation process. Our program manager insisted that each module must understand the goal of adhering to the schedule so they would resolve their conflict within an acceptable period of time . . .’’ (Alpha, Liang)

However, as a result of not having one driving goal, it was often difficult to reach an agreement on how to proceed. In the words of an IT manager:

‘‘. . .The implementation of the systems would fail if we couldn’t reach agreement on how to conduct an intensive user training program effectively and efficiently. However, it was really difficult for us to resolve this conflict among different users’ demands and needs. . .’’ (Alpha, Hsiao)

When conflicts occurred at Alpha, the IT manager had to attempt to integrate the demands and needs for those units because not all of the key program members perceived that the new system would benefit their individual units’ interests. In fact, program managers pushed conflict resolution to the units by stressing the schedule rather than a functional goal.

At Beta, the system implementation was initiated by the CEO to reduce redundant human resources and enhance productivity. However, other organizational members did not identify with the benefits. This caused an extensive level of conflict among the departments involved, especially in terms of data and document management. The goal was shared with lower management but did not resonate with lower management or with those tasked to use the new system. The following remarks are from key participants of the program (CIO, program manager):

‘‘. . .Several departments showed a low level of support for the ERP II because these departments were adjusted to the habits built by the old ERP process – they perceived the old one as simple without much interdependence among departments. Now, they had to change their habits to adopt a complicated process system, although it was understood that the new system would benefit the organization’s overall control. . .’’ (Beta, Ho)

‘‘. . .It benefited top management; however, many operational managers thought the new ERP II system would add to their workloads and generate no benefit to their business operations. For example, manufacturing believed that the ERP II system would require them to key in much more data than before; with the new ERP II system, each step of the purchase order change process had been stuck in the system and was very inconvenient, the Change Notice of the R&D Department must be refiled each time instead of simply revising the original. . .’’ (Beta, Lee)

The proposed system was negatively perceived by these key program members of case Beta. Negative comments and complaints emerged during the system implementation process. The system could only be completed by the force of the top management authority.

In Gamma, all key members not only understood but also agreed on the established goals; therefore, conflicts were present but not extensive:

‘‘. . .There were extreme difficulties for the production management department in making their production plan changes due to the sales department’s changes in delivery dates and sales forecasts. . .therefore, these two departments always had conflicts. . .’’ (Gamma, Wang)

There was no intensive conflict mentioned by the entire program team. When asked to clarify any conflicts that arose, the team perceived those conflicts to be normal and unavoidable. They maintained a positive attitude toward conflict resolution during the implementation process.

## 4.4. Coordination and cooperation

Within Alpha, different units showed a certain level of coordination and cooperation when conflicts occurred. Because not all of the units involved perceived the benefits of the proposed system, an approach emphasizing compromise to resolve conflict was adopted on many occasions. According to two project managers:

‘‘. . .We set up our program goals and methodology for our ERP program. For example, key performance indicators, inventory turnover . . . when those program goals were apparent to the business units involved, they knew what tasks needed to be accomplished. . .’’ (Alpha, Lee)

‘‘. . .It was really hard not to affect the operations for every department involved. . .so, we needed to compromise on certain technical issues. After that, we had a clear direction for achieving our program goals. . .’’ (Alpha, Chang)

On the other hand, despite the fact that the goals were understood by those at the level implementing the system, Beta exhibited resistance and a lack of desirable coordination and cooperation. In the words of a project manager and the program manager:

‘‘. . .Actually, we took a very passive role in the decision. We didn’t say anything . . . Not surprisingly, it caused a lack of effort and coordination during the system implementation process. . .’ (Beta, Liang)

‘‘. . .Without the support of top management, this new ERP II would be abandoned, even with goals of substantial savings in manpower and easily and quickly collecting history data for analysis. . .’’ (Beta, Lee)

In Beta, cooperation and coordination among different units were low. They participated with only a passive attitude and, in fact, did not want the proposed system to be implemented successfully.

In Gamma, the benefits were well communicated to and received by the participating departments. The coordination and cooperation among different groups in the program were evident. Two project managers and the program manager indicate this in a number of instances:

‘‘. . .Different departments were willing to take care of other departments’ problems instead of only considering their own. For example, the sales department never considered the complexity and difficulty of the return product process from other perspectives (such as the financial and production management departments). During this new implementation, the sales department actively participated in the entire process design and was willing to take on extra effort. . .’’ (Gamma, Tsai)

‘‘. . .Communication was very important and was well done by the program manager and CEO. For example, the program manager showed the sales department the benefits of having the new ERP and figured out the current task difficulties so it could provide better access to inventory data and resolve data inconsistency problems of undelivered orders. . .’’ (Gamma, Chen)

‘‘. . .In general, there were small conflicts during the system implementation process; however, these conflicts had been resolved successfully through the meetings and communications among the key members. For example, in addition to the cooperation between sales and production departments, the logistics and financial departments also had good cooperation and coordination during the system implementation process . . .’’ (Gamma, Lin)

That is, in Gamma, coordination and cooperation existed among the units involved and an integrative conflict resolution approach was taken to resolve any conflicts.

Overall, the above results from Alpha and Beta show that having a shared goal understanding among the projects does not necessarily promote essential coordination and cooperation. Coordination and cooperation were observed in Alpha and Gamma but not in Beta. We consider the precepts of RGCT, an element of common benefit to all, to be missing. With that in mind, Propositions 1a and 1b are not supported, as should be the case if SCT translates down management levels.

## 4.5. IT program success

Participants in all three cases expressed levels of achieving success. Those in Alpha indicated that system performance was acceptable but not to the point of realizing the expected business goals. The implemented system met their current business functions and schedule set by top management. Nevertheless, the system improved the integration and synergy among different units. According to two project managers in Alpha:

‘‘. . .Our plan was not achieved because of expansion. The coordination among departments was not very smooth. However, we didn’t expect to achieve 100% of our program goals. . . we only wanted to improve our current business functioning and meet user expectations. . .’’ (Alpha, Lee)

‘‘. . .Different departments had different demands; we tried to have a compromise point. So the outcome of the implemented process was on-time delivery. . .’’ (Alpha, Chang)

Key members in Beta reported that participants in different departments only cared about their own interests, not those expected by top management. For example, one project manager and one IT manager indicated:

‘‘. . .The main problem of the program implementation process was the communication among team members. It caused incorrect data, rework and extra cost. . .’’ (Beta, Liang)

‘‘. . .Our purchasing department rejected using the electronic input form because they needed ways to double-check loadings. The system caused conflict and resistance. The program schedule was delayed because of changes of scope and user requirements. . .’ (Beta, Lin)

In Beta, the system was completed as requested by top management; however, the completed system did not finish on schedule or within quality expectations.

For case Gamma, on the other hand, the implementation was expected to improve organizational efficiency and effectiveness. Two project managers indicated:

‘‘. . . Our implementation was smooth, the coordination and interaction among team members went well. Therefore, the golive was more productive than expected. For example, operational efficiency and effectiveness were improved after implementation . . . sales increased, trust among partners increased.’’ (Gamma, Wang)

‘‘. . .There were some conflicts during the implementation process, but most of them could be solved through communication among departments. . .the expected goals were achieved, to improve integration and synergy among departments. . .’’(Gamma, Tsai)

The Gamma team members were satisfied with not only the program implementation process but also the outcomes. It was believed that the proposed system improved organizational performance significantly. In summary, the cases indicate that when the goal can be made relevant to the entire organization as a superordinate goal, a good deal of cooperation and coordination arises along with clear conflict resolution. Propositions 2a and 2b are supported by the cases. This supposition certainly deserves greater attention in future studies as programs become a more standard way of implementing complex systems.

## 5. Discussion

This study begins to address an important consideration – should an understanding of goals be spread throughout an IT program, and how should these goals be structured? Based on this case study, a shared goal understanding, as suggested by the strategic consensus literature, is not sufficient to facilitate the cooperation and coordination essential to the success of an enterprise system program. However, the superordinate goal in RGCT, a goal that is compelling and highly appealing to the different stakeholders, explains the higher levels of coordination and cooperation necessary for system implementation. Interestingly, when the program team members perceived the program goals as legitimate for the entire organization, conflicts were satisfactorily resolved and led to more cooperation and coordination. Nevertheless, we argue that neither theory may fully explain IT program success.

## 5.1. Additional propositions

A foundation of SCT focuses on the degree of agreement and understanding of the strategic decisions manifested as goals within the group of participating managers [31]. The central proposition is that a higher degree of strategic consensus is positively associated with team process effectiveness and organizational performance [30,31]. However, the literature also suggests that when middle-level managers are substantive actors, the focus would be expressed as a shared understanding of the program goals [27,52]. The cases indicate that although achieving a shared goal understanding of the IT program’s goals is likely to be important for program success, it does not sufficiently facilitate the cooperation and coordination that is necessary among all of the key program members. Under certain circumstances, the program members perceived the program goals as having no value to the organization and even conflicting with individual interests.

The data reveal what may be missing. The program members perception of goals as overarching became a key to determining commitment. What types of goals would influence the levels of goal commitment by the program members? From case Gamma, the program goals of the Enterprise Systems were perceived as compelling and/or highly appealing to the members of different groups. Two key program members in Gamma indicated:

‘‘. . .We liked to solve problems via meetings and communications when conflicts occurred during the implementation process. . .Our members believed that the program goals were reachable expectations. . .’’ (Gamma, Chen)

‘‘. . . We found many conflicts among functional areas, especially in that their modules were interdependent. . . In this case, their commitment to the schedule and objectives helped those business units resolve conflicts successfully. . .’’ (Gamma, Wang)

These quotes indicate that the existence of superordinate goals facilitates conflict management among project teams instead of generating interfering behaviors and negative emotions. Thus, a superordinate goal is a condition in which to promote conflict resolution. The interviews collected indicate this is due to an ability to commit to a more universal goal as benefit that is evident to all in the organization. Members of the Gamma program further indicated:

‘‘. . .Most of our team members believed that the established goals were worthy pursuits because they perceived that the outcomes of the system implementation would reduce their own work. . .’’ (Gamma, Lin)

‘‘. . .Our top manager explained difficulties in an early meeting, so when conflicts occurred, we could solve the problems. . .also, our team members were considerate of each other’s problems and difficulties, so we pursued overall benefits for our organization as a major concern. . .’’ (Gamma, Hung)

This additional finding may be explained by commitment theory. Commitment is a force that binds an individual to a course of action and can be accompanied by a mindset that conflicts with the required action [50]. Commitment is different from related constructs such as motives and attitudes and can even overcome conflicting motives or attitudes [11]. The commitment to a course of action is experienced as a mindset composed of desires, costs, and obligations. In general, commitment to a team goal is generally understood in an expectancy-value framework [64]. Specifically, commitment is a function of the expectancy that goal attainment is possible and the attractiveness or value placed on reaching the goal. Thus, commitment would be higher when the value of superordinate goal attainment can be explicitly expressed.

Individuals who are highly committed to a goal direct their cognitive and behavioral resources toward attaining that goal. On the other hand, individuals with low goal commitment may be distracted from the assigned goals and may put their efforts into unrelated activities because they have not internalized the goal [53]. These are expressed in coordination and cooperation, which are teamwork behaviors that are critical to IT program success. Thus, there is both existing theory and evidence in the data to add:

P3. An IT program goal perceived as a superordinate goal will facilitate a high level of goal commitment.

P4. A greater goal commitment will facilitate cooperation and coordination during the IT program process.

Because conflicts among major stakeholders of IT development projects are almost unavoidable, cooperation and coordination among IT project stakeholders are not guaranteed. Therefore, satisfactory conflict resolution among project teams represents one of the key issues in the successful management and implementation of programs [12]. Only RGCT includes a direct link from goals to conflict resolution. Nevertheless, a shared understanding of goals with commitment in case Alpha was also associated with a degree of conflict resolution. This more modest amount of conflict resolution can be discerned from prior studies and commitment theory.

Barki and Hartwick [7] identified four components of conflict that characterize the IT environment: interdependence, disagreement, interference and negative emotion. Interdependence exists when distinct parties share common resources or when outcomes are affected by the actions of the other parties. In the absence of interdependence, the actions of one group have no consequence on the outcomes of another group. Disagreement is present when groups think a dissimilarity of objectives is prevalent. Individual project teams within a program will have singular objectives. Interference exists when one or more groups oppose or interfere with the attainment of another group’s objectives, outcomes or goals. Interference represents a key behavioral element of any conflict between groups, even across project teams that interfere with a smooth allocation of resources. Negative emotions emerge in conflicts when there are major disagreements or when parties interfere with the attainment of each other’s goals. Therefore, negative emotions such as anger and anxiety are an affective element of a conflict situation.

However, when commitment is present, it is reasonable to expect that individuals will resolve conflicts to achieve the overall goals [46]. An absence of perceived conflict between project goals and a global program goal (e.g., superordinate goal) can promote commitment among project teams. Therefore, when team goal commitment is high, the individual team’s values and goals are likely to be congruent with those of the program. This leads to one further proposition:

P5. An IT program team with a high level of goal commitment is more likely to resolve conflict satisfactorily than one with a low level of goal commitment.

All propositions are shown in Fig. 2. The confirmed original propositions are shown with solid lines. The new propositions are shown with dashed lines. The dashed lines also represent the expected but unconfirmed relationship from shared goal understanding to cooperation and coordination. Though not supported directly through our data, such a condition still seems likely to be a necessary condition in the development of the mediator variable. If nothing else, the superordinate goal must be fully shared and understood. The data collected in the study are not sufficiently granular to accept or dismiss the importance of this relationship.

## 5.2. Competing theories

During the course of study, alternative frameworks to SCT and RGCT were considered and dismissed. The main issue centered on the motivation of the program component teams to work toward the shared goal. Two other theories that showed promise include Social Identity Theory (SIT) and group Goal-Setting Theory (GST). SIT explains potential motivation in this context through the need to enhance group-based self-esteem by pursuing a sense of relatedness, respect, peer recognition, and the attainment of group goals [25]. Needs associated with specific group membership are internalized and serve as a behavioral guide. This could explain why goal understanding is not sufficient for a program team to work cooperatively. For example, in case Beta, different divisions or projects may have their own social identity and thus their own goals. On the other hand, in the Alpha and Gamma cases, the entire program team shared the same desirable social identity in pursuit of the overall program goals to lessen the explanatory power of SIT.

Based upon GST [38,39], the pursuit of assigned goals is mediated by self-set goals that people develop in response to their assignment as well as by self-efficacy [38,41]. Self-set goals and self-efficacy are immediate, conscious motivators of action. As such, they mediate the effects of external incentives. In our study, the Beta case showed an assigned goal of enhancing productivity by reducing its labor force, which might evidently conflict with team members’ self-set goals of preserving their positions. On the other hand, in both the Alpha and Gamma cases, the divisions involved were able to determine self-set goals that do not conflict with the program goals. Thus, complexities must be considered to explain the behaviors exhibited under GST. The proposed superordinate goal of RGCT is more parsimonious in explaining the actions taken.

## 5.3. Implications for researchers

The results of this study provide new insights for our understanding of how goals should be structured in the context of IT programs. First, IT program goals must be well established and understood by key program members to facilitate cooperation and coordination with commitment as a likely antecedent. A perceived superordinate goal among all of the key program members is able to meet this condition. That is, this study goes beyond the message of published studies on enterprise system critical success factors by indicating that top management should not only establish clear goals but also set a single goal that could be perceived as a superordinate goal. Second, this study suggests that SCT might be applicable to the IT program context; however, the context of the goal consensus should consider the goal commitment dimension. In short, we suggest that in addition to locus, degree, content, and scope, the goal commitment dimension should be considered when it includes the middle mangers in charge of implementation.

The potential antecedents of goal commitment were not examined in this study. This opens a new research avenue on examining the nature of goal characteristics and its impact on goal commitment. Furthermore, the results of this study enhance our understanding of IT conflict management, indicating that in addition to conflict management practices, goal commitment by stakeholders can be added to the foundation for satisfactory conflict resolution. That is, in addition to the traditional focus on the particular conflict management mechanisms, goal commitment among parties should play an important role in determining satisfactory conflict resolution. It is possible that effective conflict resolution will not be dependent on which conflict resolution mechanism was adopted in the presence of a superordinate goal. Finally, RGCT focuses on how superordinate goals enhance conflict resolution and reduce hostile behaviors across groups. Our proposed model suggests that a superordinate goal would not only facilitate conflict resolution but also lead to goal commitment among groups, potentially extending the theory of RGCT.

## 5.4. Implications for practice

There are several steps that an organization can take to create an environment in which program managers and project managers are forced to raise the type of questions that can avoid conflicts. The prescriptions that follow are meant to be suggestive of the types of actions that an organization can take to create a shared understanding of the program goals as well as goal commitment among key program members during the implementation of enterprise systems.

## 5.4.1. Assess benefits early during the implementation process

As in the case of IT system implementation, IT programs typically progress through a number of defined stages. At the earliest point possible, program managers (and top management teams) need to identify and establish the benefits of the program implementation and identify one key goal that is inclusive of all participating functions. The formulation stage should then focus on several activities: identifying the key stakeholders during the program selection process and gaining an understanding of their expectations; defining the program objectives and their alignment with the organization’s strategic objectives; communicating the strategic value and benefits to every stakeholder; and promoting the superordinate goal as a unifying theme. During these activities, top management and program managers should involve middle mangers (project/division leaders) to share and enhance understanding, which goes against the more common practice of making strategic decisions solely within top management teams.

## 5.4.2. Gain key managerial commitment to the program goals

Program managers must ensure that program goals are well received by all of the key stakeholders, including the project managers. IT programs such as enterprise system programs are designed to implement changes in an organization. The superordinate goal of these changes should be established and fully shared with all the key stakeholders. Top management should consider not only the changes to the organizations but also the divisions involved. In the case of Beta, one of the major problems was that the program goals were not perceived as superordinate. Program managers should organize events and meetings to communicate the program’s objectives and role expectations under the umbrella of the subordinate goal. Feedback should be solicited at these meetings and seriously considered as potential for changes or conflicts. Be certain to involve multiple levels of management to resolve conflicts on task-related issues. Establish procedures for conflict resolution at the start of the program that are agreeable to all key team members. These management activities will facilitate the sharing of program objectives among the program team members, help reach an agreement on the means of delivering objectives, and clarify role responsibilities. Early feedback on the concerns held by the key stakeholders will also serve to identify the risks and major challenges of the implementation of the program.

## 5.4.3. Conduct regular meetings and enable frequent communication

To avoid competition among projects within an IT program, the involvement of project managers during the planning of the implementation of the program and during the implementation process itself should be an effective means of securing the desired behaviors. Critical issues should be openly discussed among business managers and project leaders to reach satisfactory resolutions of conflicts in regular meetings and communicated via status reports. Unless those conflicts are resolved satisfactorily, the cooperation and coordination among project managers will be deficient. To promote the resolution of conflicts, a superordinate goal should be emphasized to drive the entire program. These regular meetings and performance reports will help maintain the commitment of stakeholders and facilitate the needed coordination and cooperation. A program communication plan should be built that is similar to those suggested for projects, and the superordinate goal should always be stressed in meeting agendas and when designing program communications.

## 5.5. Limitations

The study holds some limitations. First, this study did not consider how superordinate goals are formulated or what characteristics define such goals. We believe that these are important topics that deserve exploration. Future studies should consider why a goal is perceived as superordinate by different stakeholders and focus on cases with a superordinate goal. Such studies would be able to address the question of what characteristics define a superordinate goal from the perspectives of multiple stakeholders. Second, the study did not consider whether different degrees or types of conflict might be resolved by a program’s superordinate goals. Different types of IT programs and organizational environments may contribute unavoidable conflicts. For example, studies may focus on cases with specific types of conflict (e.g., relationship conflict, goal conflict, resource conflict, and/or task conflict) to examine the relationship between superordinate goals and conflict resolution. Third, the difference between the time since the cases went live and the time of the study is large. This could have introduced bias by allowing for dissatisfaction to be decreased through continued improvements. However, we attempted to remove that bias through a focus on the implementation process. Fourth, conflict is a behavior that is manifested but not openly discussed. Response bias cannot be avoided by strictly using an interview approach for data gathering. Participant observation should be conducted on an active implementation. Fifth, the study uses a case study approach without corroborative quantitative support of the conclusions. Future work should employ quantitative methodologies to more fully cement the observations as true patterns.

As a final concern, the participation of users in system development and its role in IS success have been core topics of IS research for many decades [26]. Nevertheless, traditional IS participation research focuses on participation in terms of a monolithic concept of users. For example, Markus and Mao [44] argue that ‘‘users as participants are typically assumed to be employees of the organization engaged in solution development. Furthermore, they are generally viewed as hands-on users or operational personnel, although some research has examined the involvement of managerial personnel’’ [28, p. 527]. In an enterprise system (multi-project) implementation context, ‘‘representative users’’ are often carefully selected and appointed as project managers to represent the various functional areas, as in the cases of this study. However, the traditional users of hands-on personnel are not directly questioned. Future studies are encouraged to examine the effects of program goals on the ‘‘interaction’’ within each project down to the individual team members to include this class of users.

## 6. Conclusion

The results of this study provide new insights into our understanding of SCT and RGCT in IT programs. The sufficiency of achieving a shared understanding of the goals for program management is not confirmed, as a shared understanding among key members is not sufficient to lead to effective coordination and cooperation, although it may still be a necessary condition. The relationship appears to be strengthened when accompanied by goal commitment. It is more essential to have a compelling benefit common to key participants to facilitate necessary behaviors, expressed as a superordinate goal. This finding suggests that top management should involve all key IT program members during the goal formulation stage to arrive at a goal that serves to move the program forward to the perceived benefit of all. However, this is but one aspect of consensus theory that is important to the achievement of goals in an organization where managers compete over limited resources. Researchers need to further break open the relationships suggested by consensus theories to continue the identification of the goal attributes required to attain commitment and secure coordination and then achieve the highest benefit for all parties in differing contexts and structures.
