Janet K. Ply

Jo Ellen Moore and Clay K. Williams

Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, Campus Box 1106,

Taking a control theory view of software process innovation, we tested prevalent beliefs regarding software process maturity and Information Systems employee attitudes and perceptions by surveying 736 IS professionals in 10 organizations at varying levels of the CMM (capability maturity model). Although anecdotal reports and the scant empirical studies to date suggest job attitudes and perceptions are more positive for employees in organizations at higher levels of software process maturity, we found evidence of a more complex picture. While our data supported expectations that role conflict and perceived work overload were lower for IS professionals in organizations at a level of maturity where software process behavioral controls are implemented, other results were not fully in line with prevalent beliefs. Most notably, IS workers reported significantly lower professional efficacy and lower job satisfaction in organizations at CMM Level 3, where behavioral controls are the dominant form of formal control, than in organizations at Level 1, which is relatively free of formal controls. Some anticipated positive attitudes and perceptions surfaced in organizations at the highest rungs of software process maturity (CMM Levels 4/5), where the established behavioral controls are supplemented by substantial outcome controls, as IS professionals reported lower role ambiguity and higher job satisfaction than did their counterparts in organizations at CMM Level 3.

Keywords: Software process improvement, CMM, job satisfaction, role conflict, role ambiguity, work overload, cynicism, professional efficacy, control theory, IS professionals

## Introduction

In this study, we surveyed Information Systems professionals to investigate job attitudes and perceptions as organizations practice higher levels of software process maturity. The largely anecdotal literature to date implies that job attitudes and perceptions are more positive for employees in organizations at higher levels of software process maturity. The present study represents the first systematic effort to test these assumptions and anecdotal reports. To operationalize software process maturity, we utilized the Software Engineering Institute's capability maturity model (CMM). In doing so, the present study further contributes by providing in-depth analysis of the popular CMM framework as software process innovation in light of control theory.

We begin by bringing forth relevant theory and research regarding software process innovation (SPI) and CMM to formulate our research questions. Next, we introduce control theory as an informative lens by which to understand changes to software processes, and then apply control theory and the literature in SPI and CMM to develop hypotheses regarding job attitudes and perceptions of IS professionals relative to software process maturity. The remainder of the paper reports our research methods and findings and discusses implications for practice and research.

## Software Process Innovation and the CMM

Implementing software with high quality, rapid time to market, high user satisfaction, and low costs continues to be a major challenge. For decades, organizations have pursued what Fichman and Kemerer (1997) termed software process innovations (SPIs) by applying various tools, technologies, and methodologies to alter processes used to develop software applications. SPI efforts seek to simultaneously improve quality and productivity while reducing cycle times and cost (Harter et al. 2000). SPI goes beyond attempts to improve project level performance to seek organizational enhancements through the institutionalizing of new software development work practices (Slaughter and Kirsch 2006).

Research on SPI explores changes to the software development process through different types of innovations. This includes investigating the use of specific software development tools and techniques such as CASE tools (e.g., Orlikowski 1993). Researchers have also focused on exploring the adoption and impacts of various software development methodologies, such as structured development (e.g., Leonard-Barton 1987), waterfall and prototyping (e.g., Khalifa and Verner 2000), custom developed or proprietary vendor methodologies (e.g., Riemenschneider et al. 2002; Sawyer and Guinan 1999), object oriented methods (e.g., Fichman and Kemerer 1997), and, more recently, the agile development approaches (e.g., Harris, Collins, and Hevner 2009; Maruping et al. 2009). The third major area of SPI research focuses on the effects of reference models such as CMM and SPICE on development outcomes (e.g., Agrawal and Chari 2007; Harter et al. 2000). In general, the SPI research seeks to understand the adoption of the various innovations or assess the outcomes achieved through adoption in terms of software quality, or project and organizational performance.

The research on the adoption and performance outcomes associated with different SPIs has frequently demonstrated inconsistent or conflicting results. For example, mixed results have been reported for CASE tools (e.g., Orlikowski 1993), SPI technology tools (e.g., Fichman and Kemerer 1999), waterfall methodologies (e.g., Khalifa and Verner 2000), general system development methodologies (Fitzgerald 1998), object-oriented methods (Fichman and Kemerer 1997), and agile methodologies (e.g., Chan and Thong 2009). The empirical research on reference model SPIs, while reporting some unsuccessful initiatives (e.g., Aaen et al. 2007; Mathiassen et al. 2005), has generally shown consistent, positive results in terms of adoption and performance outcomes (e.g., Galin and Avrahami 2006; Subramanian et al. 2007).

Within the landscape of software process innovations, our focus is on exploring individual-level impacts of software process reference models. Understanding the impacts associated with efforts to enhance software process capabilities by advancing CMM levels is an appropriate context for three reasons. First, CMM is widely considered the most prevalent SPI reference model in use globally, and recent reports indicate increasing adoption based on both initial and repeat organizational appraisals (SEI 2010). Second, the CMM does not dictate the use of a particular software development methodology or the adoption of specific development tools and technologies. In fact, organizations adopt a variety of software development methods and tools in pursuing compliance at higher maturity levels. Recent research suggests that even the newer agile methods are compatible with the CMM (Fitzgerald et al. 2006). $^{2}$ And third, the organizational level processes embodied in the reference model approaches to SPI, such as the CMM, may explain some of the mixed results in studies of specific software development methodologies and tools. That is, the way a methodology or tool is incorporated into a full spectrum of software development practices may influence outcomes associated with its use. Thus, investigating individual job attitudes and perceptions within the context of CMM provides the potential to leverage results of the broader SPI literature to gain deeper insights into the influences these initiatives have within organizations and on IS professionals.

Appendix A provides an overview of the capability maturity model, or CMM. Each of the five maturity levels represents a distinct stage of software process capability: 1 – Initial, 2 – Repeatable, 3 – Defined, 4 – Managed, 5 – Optimizing. With the exception of Level 1, each maturity level carries with it a set of key process areas (KPAs), which in turn contain the key practices for that level of software process maturity. For example, one KPA at Level 3 is “Organization Process Focus” and key practices within this Level 3 KPA include

\- The software process is assessed periodically, and action plans are developed to address the assessment findings

\- A group that is responsible for the organization's software process activities exists (Paulk, Curtis et al. 1993)

Given this understanding of the CMM framework, including the concept of key practices undertaken by employees to achieve levels of the CMM, we next consider the academic literature regarding individual-level outcomes associated with organizational initiatives to reach higher levels of the CMM.

Corporate initiatives to move up in the levels of the CMM are common, as companies pursue software process innovations to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of development efforts. Frequently cited benefits associated with CMM initiatives are defect reduction and identifying defects earlier in the development process where they are less costly (Diaz and Sligo 1997; Dion 1993; Haley 1996; Pitterman 2000; Wohlend and Rosenbaum 1994); productivity improvements, such as number of lines of code per staff month (Diaz and Sligo 1997; Dion 1993; Haley 1996; Wohlend and Rosenbaum 1994); cycle reduction time or reducing the time to market (Diaz and Sligo 1997; Wohlend and Rosenbaum 1994); cost and schedule predictability (Brodman and Johnson 1995; Dion 1993; Haley 1996; Pitterman 2000; Wohlend and Rosenbaum 1994); and customer satisfaction (Pitterman 2000; Wohlend and Rosenbaum 1994).

While these business measures are important when considering the benefits of the CMM, a critical area that has been underemphasized in the literature is the impact of the CMM reference model form of SPI on the individual worker. Organizational initiatives such as SPI change individuals' attitudes and behaviors (Wynekoop 1992) and clearly impact individual performance (Slaughter and Kirsch 2006). Several publications mention higher job satisfaction, improved employee morale, less overtime, and reduced turnover as intangible or secondary benefits to be gained through CMM initiatives, but in nearly all cases the remarks are anecdotal (Brodman and Johnson 1995; Dion 1993; Humphrey et al. 1991; Paulk, Curtis et al. 1993). Our research identified only three empirical studies examining job attitudes of IS workers in conjunction with software process maturity, and we discuss these in turn.

Yamamura and Wigle (1997) of Boeing Defense and Space Group examined employee satisfaction at three intervals: before beginning SPI activities, midway through the activities, and after the activities were concluded. Employee satisfaction was assessed by a single-item measure using a scale of 1 to 10, with 1 being “highly dissatisfied” and 10 being “extremely satisfied.” They found that, prior to the SPI activities, the average employee satisfaction score was 5.7; midway through the activities, the average employee satisfaction score had increased to 7.5; after achieving CMM Level 5, employee satisfaction was 8.3. However, the organization did not undergo a formal CMM assessment until the one that determined they were Level 5, so the level of process maturity when the “before” and “midway” measures were taken is unclear.

Hyde and Wilson (2004) surveyed software professionals in an organization recently assessed at CMM Level 2, asking them to indicate their extent of agreement with statements such as “Fewer overtime hours” and “Increased levels of confidence.” Interpretation of results is awkward because means and standard deviations are not reported; instead, a bar chart of responses is presented for each item. Hyde and Wilson interpreted the charts as indicating mostly agreement to “Fewer overtime hours” and “Increased levels of confidence.” The single-firm nature of this study limits the generalizability of findings to other firms that adopted CMM.

Finally, Herbsleb et al. (1997) utilized an SEI database of CMM appraisal results to identify organizations to survey regarding a number of performance dimensions, including “Staff morale.” The percentage of respondents in this sample reporting that their organization had “excellent” or “good” staff morale at Level 1 was slightly more than 20 percent. At Level 3 and above, the percentage rose to 60 percent. This suggests an increase in morale as organizations climb the CMM ladder, but the number of organizations and assessments represented in the final results as well as the number of respondents from each organization or assessment are not made clear. The authors also acknowledge that the grouping of excellent and good into a percentage to be compared across CMM levels may be problematic.

Taken together, the three empirical studies of employee attitudes and perceptions at varying levels of the CMM reveal methodological issues in construct definition and measurement and in sampling and comparison of results by CMM level. Moreover, the absence of theoretical framing to hypothesize and interpret results considerably reduces the ability of the prior studies to inform in ways that advance our understanding of the IS worker experience related to software process maturity.

Given our awareness of key practices being implemented as organizations progress in software process maturity, we believe the experience of the IS professional may be more intricate than the literature to-date implies. When considering challenges associated with managing software process innovation, Qin (2007, p. 429) recognized that

the process users—are busier than ever before. For example, software project teams are always under pressure of balancing the schedule, budget, and quality of a software delivery....In addition, the benefits of process change almost inevitably reside with the business owners—employees still work the same hours everyday and remain as busy as they were prior to the improved process, though with the extra burden of retraining and infringements upon their comfort zones.

Most organizations, while emphasizing the need for improvement in the software development process, almost completely ignore the human factors (Baddoo and Hall 2002; Moitra 1998). Laporte and Trudel (1998, p. 195) concluded that the success or failure of an improvement program “has more to do with managing the human aspect than managing the technical aspect.” And more recently, Maruping et al. (2009), having investigated the impact of agile methodologies on project quality, encouraged more focus on the impact of different SPI methodologies at the level of individual practices.

We know from the management literature that employee perceptions and job attitudes demand attention in modern organizations. A recent meta-analysis determined that employee attitudes, such as job satisfaction, predict important work behaviors related to performance and job engagement (Harrison et al. 2006), leading employees to “contribute rather than withhold desirable inputs from their work roles” (p. 320). Given an understanding of key practices that IS workers are expected to support and implement in SPI initiatives such as CMM, along with the paucity of rigorous research on employee attitudes associated with such initiatives, our study investigates the following research questions: Do job attitudes and perceptions of IS professionals working at different levels of software process maturity (as represented by CMM level) vary? If so, in what ways?

## Theoretical Framing: Control Theory ■

In this section, we introduce control theory as a lens to explore the impact of SPI on individuals. In the software development context, control is defined behaviorally as management's “attempts to ensure that individuals working on organizational projects act according to an agreed-upon strategy to achieve desired objectives” (Kirsch 1996, p. 1).

Control theory, as conceptualized by Ouchi (1979), has been applied to examine a variety of software development phenomena. At the level of individual projects, researchers have employed control theory to understand mechanisms of control used on specific projects (e.g., Henderson and Lee 1992; Kirsch 1996), control portfolios and how they change (Kirsch 1997, 2004), and the context of outsourced IS projects (e.g., Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). Beyond individual projects, control theory has been used to evaluate the impact of control matrices on firm performance (e.g., Nidumolu and Subramani 2003-2004), and to explore the role of controls in flexible software development environments (e.g., Harris, Collins, and Hevner 2009; Maruping et al. 2009).

Within the context of organizational efforts to improve software processes, control theory can be viewed as the “study of the mechanisms that can be used to achieve organizational objectives” (Harris, Collins, and Hevner 2009, p. 403). Controls represent the means by which a controller seeks to influence the behaviors of a controller. While frequently related to individual dyads, the controller and controller may be groups of individuals representing various organizational units (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). This higher level view is most relevant to the CMM software process innovation approach and is adopted here. In this context, the controller represents the group that commits an organization to the software process innovation, for example, achieving an appraised level of process maturity (i.e., CMM Level 2, Level 3, etc.). The controllers are the individuals or groups of IS professionals involved in the software process.

Controls are broadly categorized into formal and informal modes. Formal controls are management-initiated mechanisms that utilize formal documentation associated with performance evaluation and rewards to influence the probability that personnel will behave in ways that support objectives (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003; Jaworski et al. 1993; Kirsch 1996). Formal controls are identified as either behavioral or outcome.

With behavioral control, also called process control, management seeks to influence the means of achieving the organizational objectives (Jaworski et al. 1993). Behavioral controls rely on the ability to define rules and procedures that are likely to lead to desired outcomes, and the ability to observe the associated behaviors. An example of a behavioral control emphasized at Level 3 of the CMM is Activity 3 of KPA Organization Process Definition, which identifies methodologies the software developers are permitted to use: Descriptions of software life cycles that are approved for use by the projects are documented and maintained (e.g., waterfall, spiral, serial build) (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993).

Outcome controls involve the specification of desired outputs and rewards for their achievement (Kirsch 1996, 1997). Initiatives and controllees are evaluated and rewarded based on whether the desired output is produced, independent of behaviors or the process used (Kirsch 1996). In the domain of SPI, formal output controls may include a variety of performance standards (e.g., error or defect rates, specified deliverables, targeted dates, or other initiative goals) that are not dependent on the particular approach adopted for software delivery (Kirsch 1997; Maruping et al. 2009). An example of an outcome control implemented at Level 4 of the CMM is Activity 3 of KPA Software Quality Management, which requires the specification of desired quality goals: The project's quantitative quality goals for the software products are defined, monitored, and revised throughout the software life cycle (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993).

Informal controls are social or people strategies that are usually undocumented yet focus on influencing employee behaviors (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Jaworski et al. 1993). The two types of informal controls are clan control and self control. A clan is any group sharing common goals (Ouchi 1979) and clan control is largely a group socialization process that influences the behavior of individuals, encouraging common values, beliefs, and problem solving approaches (Kirsch 1996, 1997). Self control addresses how and when individuals in organizations control their own actions, that is, set goals, monitor work, and reward or sanction another (Kirsch 1997). Whereas formal controls are typically initiated by management, informal controls are typically initiated by employees (Jaworski et al. 1993). Given the undocumented and worker-initiated nature of informal controls, they do not lend themselves to statement in SPI initiatives and associated reference models like the CMM.

Organizations pursuing CMM compliance as the basis for SPI commit to a multi-year effort that adheres to a rigorous, systematic approach to process change. Level 1 Initial represents software development focused at a project level characterized by makeshift project management practices and the absence or inconsistent application of a defined development methodology. To the extent this is an environment wherein required behaviors are not defined and desired outcomes are not measured (Kirsch 1996), formal controls are unlikely.

The organization deciding to move beyond Level 1 Initial to higher levels of process maturity is in effect committing to the implementation of formal controls, both behavioral and outcome, as the basis for software process innovation. In general, an organization pursuing CMM appraisal seeks to increase controller (e.g., management) knowledge of software processes to increase visibility of controller (e.g., IS professional) behaviors and to measure software process outcomes—all attributes related to the application of formal controls (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Tiwana and Keil 2009-2010). Our control theory analysis of CMM culminates in Figure 1, $^{3}$ which presents conceptually this continuum of formal controls in relation to the CMM maturity levels.

Through the implementation of predominantly behavioral controls, Level 2 Repeatable seeks to establish basic project management processes for individual projects that are repeatable across projects. For example, the Level 2 KPA Requirements Management includes the need to ensure system requirements allocated to software are documented and controlled, KPA Software Project Planning specifies the need to create the project plan, and KPA Software Project Tracking and Oversight requires tracking of accomplishments against the documented project plan. These examples typify the Level 2 focus on behavioral controls.

By introducing additional, more comprehensive behavioral controls, Level 3 Defined builds upon the Level 2 individual project management orientation to establish a documented, standardized and integrated software process that spans the organization. For example, Level 3 KPA Organization Process Focus specifies and coordinates software process development and improvement activities across the organization, establishes plans for organization-level development and improvement activities, and institutes the organizational group responsible for software process activities. KPA Organization Process Definition details the organization's software process and related assets such as project life cycles, guidelines for customizing the software process, and process documentation. KPA Intergroup Coordination defines the processes by which the software engineering group interacts and interfaces with other units involved in the organizational software process.

Although all KPAs at all CMM levels include a common feature of measurement and analysis or M&A (see Appendix A), when we examine M&A key practices at Levels 2 and 3, we see that the CMM Practices Guide provides only modest recommendations—typically a single M&A key practice for each KPA—and those focus on measurements to determine if the defined activities (e.g., the behavioral controls) are being followed. For that reason, we judge the primary focus of control at Levels 2 and 3 to be behavioral.

Figure 1. Conceptual View of Formal Controls in CMM Levels

As organizations progress to Level 4 Managed and Level 5 Optimizing, the established and confirmed behavioral controls implemented at CMM levels 2 and 3 are maintained, and they are supplemented with the implementation of a variety of formal outcome controls. These new controls focus on quantitatively assessing process outcomes and continuous improvement of the organizational software process. While behavioral controls are still in place, at the higher maturity levels the preponderance of emphasis is on establishing and leveraging outcome controls to enable software process innovation. Our view that the higher levels of CMM combine outcome and behavioral controls to achieve process maturity is consistent with research findings regarding the relationship of task characteristics and controls: behavioral controls are adopted when tasks are known and observable, outcome controls are used when specific performance targets can be set (Kirsch 1997), and organizations adopt portfolios of controls and change the types of controls used as an initiative evolves (Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003; Harris, Hevner, and Collins 2009; Jaworski et al. 2003; Kirsch, 1997).

In Level 4, the KPAs address the establishment of explicit quantitative measures for software process and quality management. This involves establishing a performance outcome baseline through quantitative assessment of the software development processes, and using quantitative measures of projects relative to this baseline as the means to maintain control of projects. For example, Activity 4 in the Level 4 Quantitative Process Management KPA identifies example metrics to be used, including quality measures as defined in the software quality plan; coverage and efficiency of peer reviews; effectiveness of training; test coverage and efficiency; software reliability measures; number and rate of closure on action items (Paulk, Weber et. al 1993). Similarly, the Software Quality Management KPA identifies the following example software product quality characteristics to be measured in Activity 3 (Paulk, Weber et. al 1993): functionality, reliability, maintainability, and usability. These clearly represent outcome controls whereby the desired outcome state is specified (e.g., as the quantitatively defined baseline for the process under control) and is used to assess and manage software process performance.

At Level 5 Optimizing, continuous improvement is achieved by applying quantitative methods to identify and eliminate defects, to introduce new technologies effectively, and to improve the software process. The stated objectives at CMM Level 5 are to identify root causes of defects and reduce defect rates through planned corrective actions (Defect Prevention KPA), and to (quantitatively) improve software quality, increase software productivity, and decrease product development cycle times (Technology and Process Change Management KPAs) (Paulk, Weber et. al 1993). The quantitative methods of Level 5 present a clear focus on outcomes (e.g., quality and productivity goals) as well as processes (e.g., root cause analysis) and seek to identify ways to improve both. All three Level 5 KPAs include recommendations for a wide range of metrics under the measurement and analysis common feature.

To summarize, CMM Levels 2 and 3 seek to establish the activities required to control the software development process first for individual projects, and then across projects and all organization participants (e.g., having a software development plan and accepted methodology, tracking status and costs, and taking corrective actions). At Levels 4 and 5, the organization seeks to establish performance outcomes desired for processes under control and continuous improvement targets for enhanced outcomes.

Our control theory analysis of CMM (summarized in Figure 1) provides the framework to guide our examination of job attitudes and perceptions of IS professionals at varying levels of software process maturity. While Levels 2 through 5 of the CMM include both behavioral and outcome formal controls, the movement from Level 1 to Level 3 represents a strong influx of behavioral controls. Similarly, the movement from Level 3 to Levels 4 and 5 entails a hefty supplement of outcome controls. Therefore, we examine job attitudes and perceptions of IS professionals in Level 1 organizations (little if any formal control), Level 3 organizations (primarily behavioral controls), and Levels 4 and 5 (outcome controls supplementing established behavioral controls). This segmentation (Level 1, Level 3, Levels 4/5) provides a reasonable delineation to assess the impact of controls inherent in SPI, particularly reference model initiatives. We employed this segmentation to frame our hypotheses.

## Hypotheses

We examine job attitudes and perceptions that are particularly salient to IS professionals, as well as those specifically mentioned in the CMM literature. An examination of IS research reveals three job perceptions germane to IS workers that we view as intuitively pertinent to CMM efforts: role ambiguity, role conflict, and work overload. Role conflict is the perception of inconsistent or incompatible job expectations, whereas role ambiguity represents confusion regarding the definition and expectations of the job (Nelson and Quick 2003). Both role conflict and ambiguity have been shown to be relevant to understanding IS professionals' work lives over the years (e.g., Goldstein and Rockhart 1984; Li and Shani 1991) and, based on a meta-analysis conducted by Joseph et al. (2007), they are the two most frequently examined job-related factors in studies of IS turnover. Perceived work overload is another recurring theme in studies of IS professionals (e.g., Bartol and Martin 1982; Li and Shani 1991; Moore 2000). Perceived work overload is the extent to which one is faced with too much to do in the time available (Kirmeyer and Dougherty 1988). This construct is of interest due to the trend of anecdotal reports of putting in less over-time in higher levels of the CMM (e.g., Dion 1993; Hyde and Wilson 2004; Humphrey et al. 1991).

Role ambiguity is expected to be higher for IS professionals in the improvised, sometimes frenzied CMM Level 1 organization than for IS workers in organizations at CMM Level 3 and higher. Stelzer and Mellis (1998) maintain that organizations tend to underestimate the change management aspect of process improvement initiatives, noting that software teams frequently experience uncertainty and disruption when beginning the efforts (McGuire 1996). Information on the activities, skills, and abilities required to satisfactorily perform a job represents behavioral control and helps to reduce role ambiguity (Challagalla and Shervani 1996). When organizations reach CMM Level 3, they have implemented behavioral controls (in the form of key practices) that specify required tasks and activities and these should alleviate confusion regarding job expectations and how to meet those expectations. Examples of such key practices are (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- Members of the software engineering group and other software-related groups receive orientation on the organization's software process activities and their roles in those activities. [Level 3 Organization Process Focus Ability 4]

\- A library of software process-related documentation is established and maintained. [Level 3 Organization Process Definition Activity 6]

Given such practices required of organizations at maturity Level 3 of the CMM, we extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1A: Role ambiguity reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

As an organization progresses to CMM Levels 4 and 5, the behavioral controls are augmented with outcome controls designed to quantitatively assess performance and identify specific changes in the software process needed to improve overall performance. Prior research has demonstrated that information on outcome goals reduces role ambiguity (Challagalla and Shervani 1996). As outlined in Level 4 Quantitative Process Management Activity 4 (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993), the software process of each project is evaluated based on a number of quantitative measures (e.g., planned versus actual of software size and schedule, productivity, software reliability, software defects, and action item closure rate), and information regarding performance on such outcome goals is expected to contribute to reduced role ambiguity. Hence, the introduction of substantial outcome controls at maturity Levels 4 and 5 of the CMM leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1B: Role ambiguity reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

Role conflict is expected to be higher for IS professionals at CMM Level 1—characterized as ad hoc and disordered—than for IS professionals in organizations at CMM Level 3. Role conflict was apparent in the case study of a CMM Level 2 organization where many software professionals became disenchanted “when caught between managers driving process adherence and customers questioning the value and perceived overhead” (Hyde and Wilson 2004, p. 226). In a related vein, Herbsleb et al. (1997) found lower perceived customer satisfaction at CMM Levels 1 and 2 compared to Level 3, and intimated this may be due to customer pushback on the discipline of requirements management instituted as organizations begin to adopt CMM. Such customer pushback in Level 1 and 2 organizations could manifest as role conflict for the IS professional. When an organization reaches CMM Level 3, however, it is expected that customers have settled in to a disciplined approach to requirements, easing this source of role conflict for IS workers.

Key practices associated with the software process maturity at CMM Level 3 reflect organization-level order and coordination, as well as periodic review of software process improvement activities. These practices include (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- The organization's and projects' activities for developing and improving their software processes are coordinated at the organization level. [Level 3 Organization Process Focus Activity 3]

\- The activities for software process development and improvement are reviewed with senior management on a periodic basis. [Level 3 Organization Process Focus Verification 1]

\- Representatives of the project's software engineering group work with representatives of the other engineering groups to monitor and coordinate technical activities and resolve technical issues. [Level 3 Intergroup Coordination Activity 2]

These practices specify organizational plans and processes (e.g., behavioral controls) to align activities within and across organizational units. Such practices should act to reduce role conflict, leading to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2A: Role conflict reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

The outcome controls implemented at maturity Levels 4 and 5 of the CMM provide the basis for quantitatively assessing the software process and making specific corrections needed to fix errors and improve overall process efficacy. The following key practices are examples of those implemented at upper maturity levels that signal attention to outcomes and can be expected to reduce role conflict (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- The project's software quality plan is developed and maintained according to a documented procedure. [Level 4 Software Quality Management Activity 1]

\- Where appropriate, the software process improvements are installed on a pilot basis to determine their benefits and effectiveness before they are introduced into normal practice. [Level 5 Process Change Management Activity 7]

The introduction of outcome controls such as these support on-going efforts to define clearly the elements of and organizational responsibilities for all aspects of the software process, which should further reduce role conflict at higher levels of software process maturity. We therefore extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2B: Role conflict reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

Work overload is expected to be higher for IS professionals at CMM Level 1 than for IS professionals in organizations at

CMM Level 3. In their case study of a single organization, Hyde and Wilson (2004) found that 107 survey respondents mostly agreed that they experienced fewer overtime hours as their organization progressed to CMM Level 2. In a study of 30 projects conducted by a single software development firm, Harter et al. (2000) found that total effort on projects decreased as CMM software process maturity level increased. Humphrey et al. (1991) found that overtime hours were reduced as the Software Engineering Division at Hughes Aircraft moved from CMM Level 2 to Level 3 and, similarly, Dion (1993) reported that software engineers in Raytheon's Equipment Division were spending fewer late nights and weekends on the job once Level 3 process maturity was achieved.

Key practices—representing behavioral controls associated with effective structuring, standardizing, and resourcing of the work to be done by various units—expected to contribute to lower perceptions of work overload at CMM Level 3 include (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- Software testing is performed according to the project's defined software process. [Level 3 Software Product Engineering Activity 5]

\- Adequate resources and funding are provided for performing the software engineering tasks. [Level 3 Software Product Engineering Ability 1]

Given the empirical findings and anecdotal reports of reduced overtime as organizations move from CMM Level 1 to Level 3, and behavioral controls implemented through key practices associated with Level 3 that reflect improved resource management, we extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3A: Work overload reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

As outcome controls are implemented at CMM Levels 4 and 5 to augment the behavioral controls in place, further reductions in perceived work overload are expected. The adoption of quantitative assessment provides the opportunity to pilot and implement new processes to improve effectiveness, identify and eliminate quality defects requiring extra work, and introduce quality enhancing and labor reducing technologies in the software process. Key practices implemented at CMM Levels 4 and 5 lead to outcome controls that are expected to further perceptions of lower work overload, for example (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- Support exists for collecting, recording, and analyzing data for selected process and product measurements. [Level 4 Quantitative Process Management Ability 3]

\- Adequate resources and funding are provided for defect prevention activities at the project and organizational levels. [Level 5 Defect Prevention Ability 3]

\- Adequate resources and funding are provided to establish and staff a group responsible for the organization's technology change management. [Level 5 Technology Change Management Ability 2]

Given the introduction of these quantitative outcome controls at CMM Levels 4 and 5, we extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3B: Work overload reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

Next, we consider job perceptions and attitudes that specifically emerge from the CMM literature, namely: professional efficacy, job satisfaction, and cynicism. In their case study of an organization at CMM Level 2, Hyde and Wilson included the item “Increased levels of confidence” in their survey of software professionals and the bar chart report of results reflected mostly agreement. The construct of professional efficacy captures an individual’s perceived effectiveness and accomplishment at work (Schaufeli et al. 1995), and we utilize this construct to further investigate the initial finding by Hyde and Wilson.

CMM Level 1 is characterized as makeshift and at times chaotic, with success frequently dependent on the heroic efforts of IS professionals. This type of unstructured, undependable environment can lead to rework and struggles to correct and recover. The basic logic regarding the impact of behavioral controls for Level 3 organizations applies, as project and organization-wide software processes become defined and standardized. These behavioral controls are demonstrated by Level 3 practices ensuring consistency is maintained across software work products, including plans, process descriptions, allocated requirements, software requirements, software design, code, test plans, and test procedures [Software Product Engineering Activity 10] (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993). The introduction of structure through behavioral controls should provide a smoother, more direct path to effective completion of tasks and projects and, hence, higher perceptions of professional efficacy. This is reflected in the next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4A: Professional efficacy reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly lower than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

Moreover, the outcome controls (i.e., measurement requirements) embedded in higher levels of CMM maturity are likely to further enhance perceptions of effectiveness and accomplishment in one's work. For example, the following key practices reflect assessment and reporting of performance and quality (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- The project's defined software process is analyzed and brought under quantitative control according to a documented procedure. [Level 4 Quantitative Process Management Activity 5]

\- Defect prevention data are documented and tracked across the teams coordinating defect prevention activities. [Level 5 Defect Prevention Activity 5]

Individual perceptions of effectiveness and accomplishment are likely to rise as evidence of improved process effectiveness and reduction of defects are observed. We therefore extend the following additional hypothesis concerning professional efficacy:

Hypothesis 4B: Professional efficacy reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly lower than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

Job satisfaction refers to whether employees find their employment sufficiently satisfactory to continue in it, either permanently or until they have prepared for greater responsibilities (Hoppcock 1935). In regard to the introduction of new software development practices in general, prior research offers conflicting findings on how new processes impact the job satisfaction of IS professionals. Orlikowski (1993) found in one organization that highly technical IS professionals expressed lower job satisfaction after the introduction of CASE tools due mainly to the perceived loss of demand for, and direct involvement with, technical skills. However, in another organization, Orlikowski found the opposite:

technical IS professionals reported higher job satisfaction based on the perceived enhancement of skills. Finlay and Mitchell (1994) reported a similar finding, that technical IS professionals generally reported higher job satisfaction after the implementation of CASE tools due to perceptions of requirements for increased technical skills. Salo and Abrahamsson (2007) studied agile software development using qualitative and quantitative data from five case studies and found that developers' attitudes were more positive and satisfaction higher when they had “the opportunity to actually influence daily work practices in an organized manner” (p. 97) with fast implementation of recommendations and immediate feedback on results.

Specific to our examination of the CMM SPI reference model, higher job satisfaction is often anecdotally linked to progression in software process maturity. Our control theory view of the CMM reference model reveals empirical support for this linkage: Oliver and Anderson (1994) found a small but positive effect of behavioral control environments on job satisfaction; Miao et al. (2007) found that behavioral controls focused on enhancing job-related skills and abilities positively influence task enjoyment, a construct similar to job satisfaction. And, although the aforementioned methodological concerns temper interpretation of the findings, Yamamura and Wigle (1997) provided initial empirical support for higher job satisfaction in organizations at higher levels of the CMM. Key practices in the form of behavioral controls implemented at CMM Level 3 provide clarity for and support successful completion of job requirements, which may create an environment perceived positively by IS professionals that contributes to job satisfaction. Examples include (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- The software requirements are...analyzed to ensure they are feasible and appropriate to implement in software, clearly stated, consistent with each other, testable, and complete....The software requirements document undergoes peer review before it is considered complete.... The software requirements document is placed under configuration management. [Level 3 Software Product Engineering Activity 2]

\- Members of the software engineering technical staff receive required training to perform their technical assignments. [Level 3 Software Product Engineering Ability 2]

Based on the empirical research findings and our understanding of key practices associated with maturity Level 3 of the CMM, we expect job satisfaction to be higher for IS professionals in organizations at CMM Level 3 than for IS professionals at Level 1. Accordingly, we extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5A: Job satisfaction reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly lower than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

As outcome controls are introduced at maturity Levels 4 and 5 through the application of quantitative assessment, job satisfaction is likely to increase further. This is supported by Rungtusanatham's (2001) study of statistical process control (SPC) in manufacturing environments where the use of SPC was related to increased job satisfaction. SPC is an integral component of the outcome-based controls employed at CMM Levels 4 and 5, leading us to anticipate increased job satisfaction at these upper levels. Furthermore, a key element of the CMM outcome controls involves expanding the work activities of IS professionals to identify, evaluate, and then implement new technologies and methods; this can contribute to employee empowerment and to perceptions of skill enhancement, which have been linked to increased job satisfaction (Finlay and Mitchell 1994; Orlikowski 1993). SPC and employee empowerment are reflected in CMM Level 4 and 5 key practices such as (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993)

\- Support exists for collecting, recording, and analyzing data for selected process and product measurements. [Level 4 Quantitative Process Management Ability 3]

\- The group responsible for the organization's technology change management systematically analyzes the organization's standard software process to identify areas that need or could benefit from new technology. [Level 5 Technology Change Management Activity 4].

\- A software process improvement program is established which empowers the members of the organization to improve the processes of the organization. [Level 5 Process Change Management Activity 1]

And finally, the combination of behavioral and outcome controls found at CMM Levels 4 and 5 tends toward a high control environment, which has been related to higher job satisfaction (Jaworski et al. 1993). We therefore hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 5B: Job satisfaction reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly lower than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

An interesting theme emerging from the anecdotal literature on SPI initiatives that has not been empirically investigated to date is cynicism, or doubt, on the part of developers. Rainer et al. (2003) acknowledged the need to persuade developers to buy into process innovation efforts, concluding that most developers are skeptical about process improvement and need to see evidence of the benefits of process change. In their report of a longitudinal study of SPI change initiatives at a Danish company, Ngwenyama and Norbjerg (2010) described an initial meeting where developers asked for substantiation that the new processes would be better than the old. Goldenson and Herbsleb (1995) identified cynicism stemming from prior experience as an obstacle that can thwart process improvement efforts. Similarly, Moitra (1998) determined that doubt about the effectiveness of new processes was a major difficulty associated with improvement initiatives, and further observed that software engineers often perceived the process change to only be for the benefit of management. Along this line, Stelzer and Mellis (1998, p. 239) maintained that “mere conformance to a standard, attaining compliance, or reaching a CMM level usually is not a relevant goal for staff members.” Accounts of cynicism in CMM efforts are supported by change management research that suggests workers report cynicism about organizational change initiatives (Armenakis and Bedeian 1999).

Cynicism reflects doubt and distrust. In organizational behavior research, the concept of cynicism has been operationalized as a mental distancing from one's work (Schaufeli et al. 1995). We expect cynicism to follow the pattern in the anecdotal CMM literature whereby negative attitudes decline as organizations progress to higher levels of process maturity (e.g., Brodman and Johnson 1995; Dion 1993; Humphrey et al. 1991; Paulk, Curtis et al. 1993). As we have argued previously, the behavioral controls implemented at Level 3 have direct positive impacts on the individual employee (e.g., reduced role ambiguity, role conflict, and work overload) that should serve to counter cynicism. Furthermore, for an organization to be compliant at CMM Level 3, behavioral controls (in the form of key practices) that specify employee activities and tasks associated with the software process at the project and organizational level will have been implemented and verified, such as:

Adequate resources and funding are provided for performing the software engineering tasks....Skilled individuals are available to perform the different software engineering tasks....Tools to support the software engineering tasks are made available. [Level 3 Software Product Engineering Ability 1] (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993).

Such practices require substantive and ongoing management support (i.e., a fundamental commitment to the SPI initiative). We therefore extend the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6A: Cynicism reported by IS professionals in ad hoc or chaotic software process environments (CMM Level 1) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments with clearly established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3).

Outcome controls implemented in maturity Levels 4 and 5 of the CMM can move the organization toward a dynamic portfolio of controls (Kirsch 1997, 2004) that magnify the impact of the SPI initiative throughout the organization. An example of such outcome controls is found in this key practice (Paulk, Weber et al. 1993):

\- Adequate resources and funding are provided for software process improvement activities. Resources are allocated to: lead, guide, and support the process improvement activities; maintain the process improvement records; develop, control, and disseminate process changes; and, establish and operate the administrative and human resources functions to conduct the communications, motivation, and recognition activities needed to maintain a high level of employee participation. [Level 5 Process Change Management Ability 1]

Key practices at advanced levels of software process maturity require the active participation of all involved employees to identify the source of errors and make changes to eliminate them, and to seek out changes in all related processes and technologies that lead to quantitatively demonstrable improvements in the organizational software process. Similarly, management is required to provide sufficient resources to do this and to engage in assessing process performance. Accordingly, we expect cynicism to subside even further at advanced levels of software process maturity, and this leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6B: Cynicism reported by IS professionals in a software process environment with established behavioral controls (CMM Level 3) is significantly higher than that reported by IS professionals in software process environments that combine outcome controls with behavioral controls (CMM Levels 4/5).

## Method

In this section, we explain our measures, recount the process used to select companies and participants, and describe our method of data collection. We used measures validated in prior research to assess role ambiguity, role conflict, work overload, professional efficacy, job satisfaction, and cynicism. The source and number of scale items for each measure are provided in Table 1 and specific measures for the nonproprietary scales are found in Appendix B. We also collected age, organizational tenure, and company to enter as control variables in our analysis, as these are often-studied correlates of workers' relationships with employing organizations (Meyer et al. 2002; Treadway et al. 2005; Witt et al. 2004).

We employed a theoretical sampling approach to identify organizations to participate in our study of CMM and IS worker attitudes. First, we identified companies that had implemented CMM through the following sources: industry contacts, SEI lead assessors, companies that provide software process innovation services, software process improvement networks (SPIN), and Project Management Institute (PMI) chapters. Next, we were interested in collecting data from companies across multiple industries that implemented CMM because they wanted to reap the benefits (e.g., fewer defects, increased productivity, improved customer satisfaction), as opposed to firms implementing CMM due to external forces. For example, although several of our research sites were consulting firms, we did not survey business units in those firms that contracted with the federal government, which required CMM compliance. In this way, we minimized variance from readily identified organizational factors that might influence the CMM implementation or role of CMM in the firm, such as compliance with government demands or type of client.

Finally, to ensure as much stability and consistency across organizations as possible, three criteria had to be met in order to be invited to participate in the study. First, the company had to be in business for at least two years and have a minimum of 50 IS professionals. This was done to eliminate startup companies where there could be factors associated with being new (e.g., lack of corporate infrastructure, potential funding challenges), and to ensure that companies had a sufficient number of IS professionals to survey. Second, companies could not have had a layoff within the past six months or have one planned in the near future. Finally, the maturity level of an organization had to be determined by a formal

## Results

We patterned our analysis after prior research examining work attitudes and group differences in organizations (Lim and Cortina 2005). First, we conducted preliminary analyses, which confirmed that multivariate normality, homogeneity of covariance matrices, and factor structure assumptions were met. Table 4 reports descriptive statistics, correlations, and reliabilities.

Next, we conducted a MANCOVA (multivariate analysis of covariance) with CMM level as the grouping factor and age, tenure, and company (entered as a series of dichotomous variables) as covariates in SPSS 15.0 (2006). An extension of analysis of variance, MANCOVA allows us to examine whether both type and frequency of job attitudes and perceptions are significantly associated with mean differences across CMM levels in linear combinations of outcomes. By estimating a series of related outcomes simultaneously, this analysis maximizes parsimony while reducing alpha inflation.

We simultaneously entered all of our dependent variables, namely, role ambiguity, role conflict, work overload, professional efficacy, job satisfaction, and cynicism. For CMM, we entered three discrete groups representing CMM Level 1, CMM Level 3, and CMM Levels 4 and 5. We combined CMM Levels 4 and 5 into a single group for two reasons. Our first reason is theoretical. As noted in our theoretical review, the forms of control introduced in CMM Level 4 and 5 are consistent. Our second reason is practical. A single organization was represented in Level 4 and a (different) single organization was represented in Level 5. To offer the richest data set for analysis, we felt it was important to include both CMM levels in our analysis. To ensure that this was appropriate, we employed K-S tests for differences and found no differences in the pattern of relationships across Level 4 and Level 5 respondents. Hence, we proceeded with our MANCOVA, results of which suggest that CMM level significantly relates to our workplace outcomes, Wilks's lambda .96, $F(12, 1452) = 6.10$ , p < .05.

Firm was correlated to some outcome variables; however, in the interest of parsimony, we do not include this additional analysis in the paper. Collectively, our analysis suggests that it was necessary to enter all three (age, tenure, firm) as covariates in order to isolate the variance specific to the grouping variable (CMM level).

Given the significant MANCOVA results, follow-up ANCOVAs were conducted to understand which job attitudes and perceptions differ by CMM level. After adjusting for alpha levels, we made linearly independent pairwise comparisons among the estimated marginal means. See Tables 5 and 6 for a summary of results.

Comparing job attitudes at CMM Level 1 to those at CMM Level 3 (i.e., the “A” hypotheses), inspection of mean differences and parameter estimates indicates that role conflict and work overload are lower at CMM Level 3 (where behavioral controls have been implemented to provide a more mature software process) than at CMM Level 1. Hence, hypotheses H2A and H3A are supported. Hypotheses regarding role ambiguity and cynicism at CMM Level 3 compared to Level 1, H1A and H6A, were not supported.

Results pertaining to H4A and H5A were significant, but counter to our hypotheses. Rather than observing higher job satisfaction and professional efficacy for IS professionals in organizations utilizing the more mature software processes associated with CMM Level 3, the Level 3 individuals reported lower job satisfaction and lower professional efficacy than those in Level 1 organizations. Consequently, hypotheses H4A and H5A were not supported.

Comparing job attitudes at CMM Level 3 maturity to those at the even more mature CMM Levels 4/5 (i.e., the “B” hypotheses), role ambiguity was lower and job satisfaction was higher at Levels 4/5 as predicted. Hence, hypotheses H1B and H5B are supported. The remaining hypotheses (H2B, H3B, H4B, and H6B) were not supported. Table 7 summarizes the results of our hypothesis testing.

## Discussion

As the first systematic effort to test the largely anecdotal reports that job attitudes and perceptions are more positive for IS professionals in organizations at higher levels of software process maturity, we employed control theory and a calculated and extensive sampling approach, as well as established measures of pertinent constructs. Our data tended to support expectations that role conflict and work overload were lower for IS professionals in software process environments implementing behavioral controls (maturity Level 3 of the CMM). Role ambiguity was also expected to be lower at Level 3 but was not, although role ambiguity did show the projected reduction at Levels 4/5 where outcome controls join the behavioral controls.

While we expected to observe higher professional efficacy and higher job satisfaction in IS workers at more mature software process organizations, we found the opposite: IS professionals in Level 3 organizations reported significantly lower professional efficacy and job satisfaction than their counterparts in Level 1 organizations. In our sample, job satisfaction recovers in organizations at CMM Levels 4/5, where IS professionals reported significantly higher job satisfaction than IS workers in Level 1 and Level 3 organizations. This type of rebound was not observed for professional efficacy.

The lack of findings regarding cynicism is worthy of mention, given its recurrence in anecdotal reports of SPI initiatives. Although our hypotheses regarding cynicism were not supported, we observed a (statistically significant) higher mean for cynicism in the Level 4/5 group than in the Level 1 group. Anecdotal reports of cynicism tend to link it to software process innovation initiatives in general, rather than to a specific transition such as advancing to CMM Level 3, and Goldenson and Herbsleb (1995, p. 24) have noted that “discouragement and cynicism from previous experience” occurs in some SPI efforts. Combined with the persistent anecdotal reports, the significant difference uncovered in this first empirical exploration of cynicism and CMM feeds speculation that something is going on in regard to cynicism and SPI.

Before discussing implications for research and practice, we consider limitations of the study. The data collected was at a snapshot in time for 10 different companies; no longitudinal data were collected. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding how job attitudes and perceptions “shift” for IS workers as their organization advances in software process maturity. Also, data from the 10 companies did not include organizations at CMM Level 2.

To keep the survey at a reasonable length for participants, there had to be a limit to the constructs we examined. In this opening study of job attitudes and perceptions at varying levels of software process maturity, we elected to investigate those that have been shown to be especially salient to IS professionals along with those that specifically emerged from the CMM literature. But surely other attitudes and perceptions are of interest, and we propose several in directions for future research.

## Directions for Future Research

Related to our stated limitations, we encourage research that examines software process maturity Level 2 of the CMM, as well as further examination of the more scarce organizations at the highest maturity levels (CMM Levels 4 and 5). Of particular interest may be the extent to which job attitudes and perceptions at Level 2 resemble that of IS workers at Level 3. It could be that the move from Level 1 to Level 2 has as much or more impact than the move from Level 2 to Level 3, since Level 2 is the point where processes and management practices are first introduced. Future research can determine this.

Although we controlled for company (along with age and organizational tenure), researchers are encouraged to identify specific firm-level factors (such as size and industry) that might conceivably affect the organization's level of software process maturity or might affect worker job attitudes. For example, larger firms may put forth a more convincing business case for achieving higher levels of process maturity, but may also have more bureaucratic managers, resulting in less personal treatment of employees and lower employee job satisfaction. The amount of time the organization worked to achieve the present level of software process might influence job attitudes and perceptions, as well as the length of time that the organization has stayed (been “settled in”) at the present level. Such firm-level factors should be controlled or investigated in future studies.

We encourage researchers to examine the relationship of software process maturity to further job attitudes and perceptions. Perceived autonomy may be particularly salient to IS professionals, as many CMM key practices implicitly call for segregation of duties and standardization of processes formerly left to IS worker discretion. Our study used a global construct and measure of cynicism. Given the continued speculation that something is going on in regard to cynicism and SPI, we encourage researchers to incorporate a facet-specific form of cynicism: cynicism about organizational change (Wanous et al. 2000).

Researchers also are encouraged to consider the potential interaction of different types of controls. While the present study focused on the impact of formal controls embodied in CMM, informal controls could play a role in SPI outcomes. Informal controls can manifest through recruitment and selection practices and training initiatives, serving to identify, hire, and develop IS professionals likely to support and enhance software process innovation initiatives. As software process maturity advances, expectations for commitment to improved processes may become engrained in the organization and spawn clan controls that help to identify and reinforce organizational norms, values, and culture (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Ouchi 1979).

Clan control in teams has shown the potential to improve individual satisfaction (Piccoli et al. 2004) but also to increase stress and decrease commitment (Barker 1993), so we encourage research to determine if the nature of clan controls may in part help to explain our unexpected findings related to efficacy and job satisfaction. Combining informal controls with the formal controls of CMM can create a “high control” environment that would be expected to positively affect job satisfaction, and this corresponds to our Level 4/5 results (Jaworski et al. 1993).

Longitudinal designs are encouraged, both qualitative and quantitative. Researchers should consider undertaking qualitative work that focuses on understanding how controls put into place by software process initiatives relate to professionals' attitudes prior to, during, and after implementation within diverse types of organizations. Data and observations can be collected as specific controls are implemented, to develop a more refined view of the effects of each control on work attitudes and perceptions. Börjesson and Mathiassen (2005) emphasize that SPI initiatives are often interrupted or side-tracked due to organizational events, and a case study within a single organization could capture such events and provide additional insight into the experience of IS professionals. Discourse analysis (Heracleous and Barrett 2001) may prove useful in making sense of multiple perspectives that may exist in an organization regarding SPI initiatives, and Sonenshein's (2010) narrative model of strategic change implementation could provide an intriguing framework for understanding IS worker responses to SPI. On the quantitative side, longitudinal survey-based studies can be useful in elucidating the role that past experiences play. Researchers are urged to collect data regarding the individual's past experiences with major change initiatives and examine the effect negative experiences, in particular, may have on an individual's attitudes and perceptions regarding SPI initiatives.

Additional studies might group IS workers by function and job role to determine if attitudes and perceptions vary by job group in organizations at the same level of software process maturity. $^{4}$ We encourage research to understand how the work itself, and the nature of the individual’s specific involvement with the SPI initiative, shape perceptions of the job and the employing organization for IS professionals at varying levels of software process maturity. For example, reports of lower professional efficacy as formal behavioral controls are established (e.g., CMM Level 3) could hint at a perceived “deskilling” of older workers. In other words, the role of the person may change as the process becomes more mature.

Finally, this research was focused on maturity of IS organizations as defined by SEI's CMM process improvement framework. Future studies with companies using the newer version of CMM (CMMI) and using other improvement frameworks (e.g., ISO, Six Sigma initiatives, lean manufacturing, total quality management, Malcolm Baldrige) would provide insight into effects that SPI and process improvement initiatives, in general, may have on a company's workforce in terms of employee attitudes and perceptions.

## Implications for Practice

This research has important implications for managers of IS staff. For one thing, findings suggest that SPI may not be a purely positive experience for IS workers. While behavioral controls implemented through SPI may be expected to reduce role conflict and work overload for IS staff, and perhaps role ambiguity as well, managers need to be aware that such initiatives may weaken the IS professional's efficacy and job satisfaction. For example, the lower professional efficacy observed at CMM Level 3 and beyond suggests that some IS workers feel higher levels of achievement and contribution in the ad hoc environment where heroic efforts are the norm (CMM Level 1) than they do in more mature software organizations.

Our study positions managers to take proactive steps to mitigate these potential negative effects associated with SPI initiatives like CMM. Managers should give close attention to workplace elements that affect the professional efficacy and satisfaction of IS workers, such as: Do the IS professionals believe they have the ability and resources to perform the new processes? Do they understand their role—and contribution—in the organization’s new way of doing things? And as fellow researchers have proffered: Do the IS workers experience a freedom of choice in aspects of their daily work? Do they feel empowered to participate in continuous improvement efforts (Müller et al. 2010; Qin 2007)? Managers need to have their finger on the pulse of worker sentiments so they can take timely action to improve these elements when called for.

The lower job satisfaction observed at CMM Level 3, where behavioral controls have been established, draws particular attention to the importance of management communication in SPI initiatives: “if proposed changes are associated with a ‘healthier’ future for the organization… employees are much more likely to accept them” (Connell and Waring 2002, pp. 353-354). In their analysis of SPI research publications, Müller et al. (2010, p. 2138) identified “communicating clear SPI goals and managing expectations amongst employees” as a critical success factor in managing SPI. We identified three sources of insight and recommendations to improve managerial communication in ways that can preserve the satisfaction and efficacy of IS workers involved in SPI efforts.

Reichers et al. (1997) emphasize the importance of timing in managerial communication regarding major change initiatives such as SPI. If employee questions are not answered thoroughly at the time a change initiative is announced, “people begin to fill in their own answers, either independently or through interaction with coworkers, most of them equally uninformed” (Reichers et al. 1997, p. 53). Allport and Postman’s (1947) basic law of rumor comes into play: rumor activity is directly proportional to the ambiguity of an issue multiplied by its importance. Impending changes to work processes are undoubtedly important to IS staff, making it imperative that managers resolve ambiguities head-on and in a timely manner.

To do so, managers must maximize the effectiveness of their communication. Elving (2005) argues that communication is more than the dissemination of information, and emphasizes communication as interaction. According to Elving, when people perceive an interaction as meeting a felt need, their energy increases; but when people interpret a communication interaction to be operating against a need they have (e.g., a need for autonomy, or a need for no additions to workload), energy is lowered (Quinn and Dutton 2005). This reasserts the importance of knowing what IS workers are experiencing and needing; the manager should take these felt needs into consideration in conveying objectives and goals to IS staff and in making decisions that affect the workers' environment.

Ultimately, managers are seeking buy-in from IS staff. Armenakis et al. (2007) identified five important precursors to buy-in on organizational change efforts such as SPI: discrepancy (employee belief that a need for change exists), appropriateness (employee belief that the change addresses the need), efficacy (employee belief in own capability to execute new behaviors required by the change initiative), principal support (employee belief that support for the change exists throughout the organization), and valence (employee belief that the expected outcome of the change is desirable). Armenakis et al. developed an instrument to assess the five beliefs at various stages of a change process, in order to gauge progress and determine actions to take if buy-in needs to be improved. We note that one of the buy-in elements directly relates to professional efficacy, and encourage IS managers to make use of this instrument in managing SPI initiatives.

Finally, given the behavioral and outcome controls inherent in mature software organizations, hiring managers should consider carefully screening job applicants based on their “fit” to ensure that future hires understand that they will be entering a structured software development environment. As noted by Adler et al. (2005, p. 244), organizations that aspire to higher levels of software maturity may not be a good fit for “free spirits who don’t believe in process.” If a hired candidate fits with the organization, the employee is likely to report higher job satisfaction and perform at higher levels (Kristof-Brown et al. 2005).

A number of factors that impact employee fit, including recruitment and selection practices, orientation training and ways of promoting core organizational values, can stem from informal controls (clan control and self control). Although SPI reference models like CMM are based on formal controls, managers can take steps to create an environment that promotes the evolution of informal clan controls as well (e.g., incentive schemes that align with SPI goals; Mathiassen et al. 2005). Informal controls can supplement the formal controls to provide a portfolio of control fostering improved employee fit with the organization's software process maturity and related values and practices.

## Conclusion

Recent calls have asked IS researchers to look for new and conflicting patterns in data and to utilize multilevel data because it can offer richer perspectives. In particular, Grover et al. (2008, p. 46) urge us to “seek contradictions...or what surprises practitioners.” Our findings challenge prevalent beliefs and therefore position the IS community for needed research regarding realities of software process innovation for the IS professional.
