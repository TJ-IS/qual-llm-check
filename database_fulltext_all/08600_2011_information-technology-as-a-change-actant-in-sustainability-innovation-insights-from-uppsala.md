# Information technology as a change actant in sustainability innovation: Insights from Uppsala

Fredrik Bengtsson, Pär J. Ågerfalk ⇑

Department of Informatics and Media, Uppsala University, P.O. Box 513, SE-751 20 Uppsala, Sweden

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 18 November 2010

Keywords: Sustainability information system Green IT Actor network theory Action research Case study Literature review

## a b s t r a c t

This study investigates the effects of a sustainability initiative in a Swedish municipality viewed through the lens of actor network theory. The focus is on the consequences of committing to the implementation of a reporting and analysis system for IT-supported sustainability. This commitment involves several actor networks, both internal and external to the municipality. The study shows that, to implement successfully a sustainability initiative, a thorough understanding of organizational routines and standards is required to enrol the affected stakeholders. Information systems can play a central role as tools for improving sustainability indicators and routines, thereby constituting important change actants.

## 1. Introduction

Concerns about climate change and an increased environmental awareness have spurred interest in sustainable develop ment and Green IT both in the information systems (IS) field (Melville, 2010; Watson et al., 2010) and among IT practitioners (Webb, 2008; Symantec, 2009). It has been argued that IT holds the promise of being instrumental in combating the negative environmental effects of the world's rapidly developing economies (Erek et al., 20o9), At the same time, the increasing use of IT in recent decades has undoubtedly contributed to increased power consumption and potential overuse of scarce resources (Elliot and Binney, 2008; Hedwig et al., 2009). Green IT research has to date focused primarily on hardware design and energy-efficient use of IT (e.g., Gabriel, 2008; Berl et al., 2009). Although there seems to be a strong public belief in technology as an enabler of greener alternatives (Council Directive 2007/C 306/01, Council Decision 1982/2006/EC). little rigorous research on this subject has been reported (Melville, 2010). In an effort to help remedy this situation, our study focuses on IT as a change actant in sustainability innovation. By using the term “actant" (Akrich and Latour. 1992), we choose to view IT both as a result of human actions and as a force in changing human behaviour through sustainability initiatives. While we position this research as ‘‘Green IT research”, we concur with Melville (2010) and others (e.g., Elliot and Binney, 2008) and argue for the importance of acknowledging a larger strategic context for sustainable development, which includes social and economic factors, in addition to environmental factors (cf. Bengtsson and Edman, 2009). In keeping with Blowfield and Visser (2007), we use the term ‘‘sustainability innovation” when referring to novel initiatives that aim to improve these three factors; i.e., ‘‘innovation explicitly directed at a sustainability goal” (Blowfield and Visser, 2007, p. 3). Such initiatives are driven by an innovation system that involves networked collaboration between several stakeholders, representing governmental interests as well as non-governmental, industrial, academic, and public interests (Freeman, 1987). With this backdrop, the specific research questions addressed are: how can IT serve as a change actant in sustainability innovation and what is the nature of its relation to other human and non-human actants?

These research questions can be understood in the light of Melville’s (2010) suggested research agenda for IS and environmental sustainability, and specifically his research question number seven: what is the association between IS and organizational and sustainability performance? In Melville’s (2010) terms, building on the work of Coleman (1986), the research addresses the role of IT for sustainability in the complex relationships between societal structures and norms and individual actions, and these individual actions’ effects on the behaviour of organizations and social systems.

Drawing on the research framework developed and used by Lyytinen and King (2002) and Yoo et al. (2005), we explore this question by adopting actor network theory (Callon, 1986, 1991; Latour, 1991; Akrich and Latour, 1992) to analyse a sustainability initiative in the municipality of Uppsala, Sweden. The aim is to develop an understanding of the forces that drive and counteract such an initiative and the roles of human and non-human actants in that process. Specifically, we turn our attention to how IT functioned as a change actant in the initiative.

The paper is structured as follows. In the following section, we introduce the area of sustainable development and the driving forces behind it, with a special focus on IS research into sustainability. We then introduce the research approach we adopted and the theoretical framework that guided our analysis. Subsequently, a before-and-after analysis of the municipal sustainability initiative is presented and implications for research and practice are laid out.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Sustainability

In this paper, we adopt a view of sustainability derived from the Brundtland Commission (United Nations, 1987): namely, that something is sustainable if it ‘‘meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.” This line of thought is at the core of sustainable development and a great deal of work has been put into efforts to live up to this goal. Perhaps the best known of these initiatives is the Agenda 21 programme run by the United Nations (United Nations, 1992).

The Agenda 21 programme was revealed at the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development, also known as the Earth Summit, in Rio de Janeiro, 1992. The programme is divided into four sections, covering issues regarding social and economic dimensions, conservation and management of resources for development, strengthening the role of major groups, and means of implementation (United Nations, 1992). Progress on implementing Agenda 21 was reviewed at the United Nations Earth Summit 5 in New York, 1997. A result of the conference was the recognition that, even though positive results had been achieved related to the goals of Agenda 21, the situation was actually worse in 1997 than it had been in 1992 (United Nations, 1997). At the Johannesburg Summit in 2002, marking 10 years since the adoption of Agenda 21, the United Nations once again stated that they should commit to the Agenda 21 programme. Regarding the questions at hand they once again drew the conclusion that, even though progress had been made the downward trend was continuing (United Nations, 2002).

In practice, sustainability refers to the efforts to minimize the negative economic, environmental, and social impacts of an activity (United Nations, 2002), whether by a production company, a service provider, a governmental body, or others. Although there are ways to measure sustainability, many of the factors that are taken into account are difficult to express numerically. Furthermore, the benchmark is not necessarily the same from case to case. Different actors affect sustainability differently and different indicators therefore come into play. There are also laws and regulations that have an impact on the benchmark for good sustainability performance.

The term ‘‘sustainability analysis” was introduced in 1992 by Barry Dalal-Clayton, aiming to broaden the scope of Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) procedures to include all the facets of sustainability, and thereby to create a tool more in line with the content of Agenda 21 (Dalal-Clayton, 1993). EIA is defined by the International Association for Impact Assessment (IAIA & IEA, 1999) as ‘‘the process of identifying, predicting, evaluating, and mitigating the biophysical, social, and other relevant effects of development proposals prior to major decisions being taken and commitments made.” The development of EIAs is a direct result of the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 becoming United States environmental law on January 1, 1970 (NEPA, 1970). Its purpose is:

‘‘To declare a national policy which will encourage productive and enjoyable harmony between man and his [sic.] environment; to promote efforts which will prevent or eliminate damage to the environment and biosphere and stimulate the health and welfare of man; to enrich the understanding of the ecological systems and natural resources important to the Nation; and to establish a Council on Environmental Quality.” (NEPA, 1970)

Sustainability analysis focuses on a set of indicators that are simple and practical to measure, and therefore provides a tool for trend and impact analysis of a project. Dalal-Clayton (1993) presents a set of indicators that span economic, environmental, and social aspects. These indicators have since been refined, as well as expanded with respect to the indicators of social aspects.

The term ‘‘triple bottom line” is often used to capture the criteria for measuring success in the areas of economic, environmental, and social goals of organizations (Elkington, 1997). An important aspect is that triple bottom line reporting argues for a stakeholder view in preference to the more classical shareholder view concerning the organization’s responsibilities.

There are different approaches to the realization of sustainability analysis and triple bottom line reporting, and the application of the results. The sustainability reporting system used in the initiative studied is based on the guidelines developed by the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) (Global Reporting Initiative, 2009). In the GRI case, sustainability analysis is used to facilitate a sustainability-reporting framework, within which organizations can set up a benchmark for their performance against legislation, norms, rules, and self-imposed standards. The benchmark can then be used to compare sustainability aspects over time, thereby identifying areas that need extra attention or that demonstrate their commitment to sustainable development.

## 3. Related research

Terms such as ‘‘Environmental Sustainability of ICT”, ‘‘Green IT” and ‘‘Sustainable Computing” are all used to frame undertakings that link IS and sustainability. Elliot and Binney, 2008 searched eight high-ranked IS journals for this topic and found nothing, Melville (2010) conducted a literature search of papers related to environmental sustainability in 10 top IS and Operations Research journals covering the years 2000 to 2007. There were 35 relevant papers, of which only one, by Heng and de Moor (2003), was published in an IS journal. We conducted a complementary search of the AIS Senior Scholars’ basket of journals (namely EJIS, ISJ, ISR, JAIS, JIT, JMIS, JSIS, and MISQ) for the period January 2008–May 2010, and identified two further papers, by Petrini and Pozzbon (2009) and Watson et al. (2010), in addition to Melville (2010). We can therefore find just four papers on sustainability and IS in the core IS journals over the last decade, as shown in Table 1. To obtain a more complete overview, we conducted a review of the Proceedings of AIS associated conferences,<sup>1</sup> namely ACIS, AMCIS, ECIS, ICIS, and PACIS, covering the period Jan 2000–May 2010. We selected all papers containing the words ‘‘green”, ‘‘sustainable”, ‘‘sustainability”, ‘‘environmental”, or ‘‘environment” in the title, abstract, or keywords, and also examined their content to verify that they were indeed in the area of sustainability and IS.

Table 1 shows the breakdown of the identified papers by source and year. It shows that there are 36 contributions to the field of IS and sustainability represented in premier IS conferences and the AIS basket of journals between 2000 and 2010. For the full list of papers, see Appendix A.

By definition, the concept of sustainability promotes a holistic view that embraces a multitude of human activities related to economic, environmental, and social concerns. However, as shown in Table 1, this is not reflected by the majority of attempts to frame IS research questions in relation to sustainability. Of the three central aspects of sustainability, the environmental aspect, in terms of energy use, is clearly the main or dominant aspect in IS research publications (Courtney et al., 2000: Lodhia, 2002: Courtney, 2006: Pinto et al., 2006: Chaabane et al., 2008: Huang, 2008: Babin and Nicholson, 2009: Butler et al., 2009; Butler and Daly, 2009; Capre and Merlo, 2009; Chen et al., 2009; Erek et al., 2009; Hasan et al., 2009; Hedwig et al., 2009; Molla, 2009; Molla et al., 2009; Sarkar and Young, 2009; Sayeed and Gill, 2009; Vykoukal et al., 2009). A viewpoint shared by many authors is that technical artefacts are the main culprits with respect to carbon dioxide (CO ) emissions. Various approaches to tackling this issue are proposed, such as more energy-efficient computers, more energy-efficient data centres, virtualization of servers, thin clients, and cloud computing (Capre and Merlo, 2009; Mann et al., 2009; Vykoukal et al., 2009), and including more technical aspects that focus on the components used in computer construction (Capre and Merlo, 2009; Hedwig et al., 2009). Other suggested approaches include telecommuting, paperless offices, and changing people’s behaviour (Huang, 2008; Hasan et al., 2009; Mann et al., 2009). Some authors also note the issue of recycling as an aspect of the hardware lifecycle that affects the ecological footprint (Huang, 2008; DesAutels and Berthon, 2009; Molla, 2009; Vykoukal et al., 2009).

Economic goals are also a common theme (Kanungo, 2002; Butler and Daly, 2009; Chen et al., 2009; Sarkar and Young, 2009) but they do not appear to be aligned with the intentions of the Brundtland Commission and Agenda 21. The primary focus here is rather on cost vs. benefit to companies and organizations because of sustainability initiatives. The viewpoint therefore represents the classical view of profit-seeking actors in a business environment. Paquette and Wiseman (2006) even note that economic arguments are often required to legitimize sustainability work

Social aspects of IS in a sustainability setting are mentioned occasionally (Naumann, 2002; Manning, 2007; Butler et al., 2009; Erek et al., 2009; Hasan et al., 2009). Erek et al. (2009) elaborate on all three sustainability aspects in relation to IS organizations, although the data collection and analysis are primarily focused on the environmental aspect. Hasan et al. (2009) discuss what contributes to a more sustainable future, e.g., in terms of the social aspects brought forward by Web 2.0, but the overall theme is yet again the environmental aspect. Molla et al. (2008) provides a more balanced approach when discussing ‘‘G-readiness”.

There could be many reasons for the apparent lack of a social aspect to the majority of Green IT papers within IS to date. One conclusion that can be drawn from the framing of the issue in several contemporary papers is that effects on sustainability in the area of IS/IT are predominantly viewed as caused by technical artefacts (Butler et al., 2009; Capra and Merlo, 2009; Molla et al., 2009; Vykoukal et al., 2009). As a consequence, the term ‘‘Green IT”, with a focus on environmental, and to a lesser extent economic, aspects of IS/IT (Murugesan, 2008) is often used as the definition of how to view sustainability initiatives in IS. Elliot (2007) presents a similar view when defining ‘‘Environmentally Sustainable ICT”.

An environmental focus on IS is important and in line with the common call to save the planet. However, it is equally important not to confuse environmentalism with sustainability. To act in a more environmentally friendly way does not automatically imply a more sustainable economic and social outcome. In follows that sustainability research in IS needs not only to adopt the holistic focus of sustainability, but also to adopt methods and research approaches that can support research via a holistic approach.

In our view, IS as a field has an excellent opportunity to contribute to this emerging area by promoting a holistic view of sustainable development and Green IT, thereby moving from the technocratic view of IT as an environmental villain to a systemic view of the role of IT in the larger context of business and social activity. In line with Melville’s (2010) research agenda for IS and environmental sustainability, we contribute to such a discourse by focusing on IT as a change actant in sustainability innovation. Most of the previous research that does adopt a holistic approach to IS and sustainability is based on conceptual/analytical research and literature reviews (see Table 2). The few empirical studies reported appear to be limited in terms of empirical basis and theoretical framing, which is not surprising, given that all but one are fairly short conference pieces (Courtney et al., 2000; Naumann, 2002; Erek et al., 2009). Petrini and Pozzbon (2009), on the other hand, do provide a useful starting point for researching business intelligence in relation to sustainability, clearly taking the holistic sustainability approach seriously. Our study adds to this body of knowledge by exploring a holistic approach using actor network theory (see below) as a theoretical lens. The lack of rigorous previous research means that our study is, of necessity, explorative. Our hope is that it will serve as food for thought and inspire other researchers to contribute to this emerging area of IS research.

## 4. Research approach

The overall research approach adopted can be understood in terms of the canonical action research method, which sug gests cycles of diagnosing, action planning, action taking, evaluating, and specifying learning (Baskerville and Wood-Harper, 1998). Specifically, the study consisted of three primary phases. Phase I was concerned with achieving an understanding of the initial actor networks and with planning the sustainability initiative (diagnosing and action planning). Phase II concerned the implementation and monitoring of the initiative in the Uppsala municipality (action taking). Finally, Phase III was concerned with characterizing the resulting actor network and analysing the overall effects of the initiative (evaluating and specifying learning).

Data collection in Phase I followed a sequential multi-method approach (Mingers, 2001) that comprised an initial survey and subsequent semi-structured interviews with representatives of participating municipal work-places and external suppliers. The aim of the survey was to achieve an initial understanding of the current logistical situation, the work practices and ordering behaviour, and the sustainability awareness and motivation within the work-places. A questionnaire was distributed to 60 work-places, with 15 useful responses being received; i.e., a response rate of 25%. Although this response rate was quite low, it was by no means exceptional, with response rates as low as 3% having been reported in high-profile IS journals previously (Sivo et al., 2006). More importantly, the survey did not seek to establish statistical generalizability, but to give an initial understanding to be explored further during in-depth interviews. Twelve such interviews were performed with workplace representatives and four with external suppliers with framework agreements (‘‘framework suppliers”). In Phase II, data were continuously collected by means of the redesigned ordering system, the Sustainability Portal (see below), and related ERP systems used by the municipality. In Phase III, 13 further interviews were performed with representatives of municipal work-places. Finally, the data collected throughout the project, together with material from the technical reports produced (Uppsala Municipality, 2008ab, 2009), were coded using the categories suggested by the analytic framework for seed categories (Miles and Huberman, 1994).

## 4.1. Empirical setting

In response to the Agenda 21 programme, the European Union has put forward strategies for strengthening progress in sustainability work among its member states. Aligning with these strategies, the Swedish government has introduced legislation regarding sustainability in Sweden. This has placed pressure on municipalities and other organizations to revise their sustainability strategies.

Responding to these initiatives, the municipality of Uppsala has conducted several projects to investigate how IT can be used to increase efficiency within a sustainability framework. The focal project for this research was concerned with the transportation of goods and ordering routines within local government activities.

In this project, the aim was to reduce the number of transport operations needed to deliver goods for different functions within the municipality and to build an information infrastructure for sustainability reporting and assessment. Aligning with the goals of sustainable development, the main variables examined related to economic, environmental, and social aspects of this activity. The project covered 93 work-places, including day nurseries, schools, libraries, and geriatric care units situated in the southern parts of Uppsala.

## 5. Analytical framework

## 5.1. Actor network theory

In this study, actor network theory was used as an analytical device. Actor network theory was developed by Bruno Latour, Michel Callon and Jon Law and has been used extensively within IS (e.g., Orlikowski et al., 1996; Hanseth and Monteiro, 1997; Walsham, 1997; Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Yoo et al., 2005) and neighbouring disciplines (e.g., Kingshuk et al., 2005; Song and Zahedi, 2005; Volkoff et al., 2007). It is a network theory that broadens the concept of an actor to include human and non-human actors. As discussed above, the impact of a sustainability initiative can be viewed from a triple bottom line perspective. To describe the process of organizational change that takes place when new demands on sustainable awareness are introduced, we need to be able to account for social, economic, and environmental factors. From an actor–network–theory point of view, these three factors are represented and advocated by actors in the sustainability initiative network.

In actor network theory, actors are often referred to as ‘‘actants” (Akrich and Latour, 1992) to distinguish them from the usual interpretation of the word ‘‘actor”. Essentially, actants are anything that takes part in the translation from one configuration of a network to a new, reconfigured network. Therefore, the term ‘‘actant” refers to both human and non-human participants The actants in an actor network could include an information system emplovees legislation standards or municipalities.

Another key concept in actor network theory is that of ‘‘inscription”, which is used to encapsulate the notion that a technological artefact embodies a pattern of use (Akrich and Latour, 1992). Inscription not only refers to the intended use or effect of the artefact, but also to other effects among actants caused by the artefact. Behavioural inscriptions are therefore embedded in the actants of a network. The pattern of use of an object can change as the network evolves, with the inscription caused by the artefact also changing. Business systems and organizational structures are examples of artefacts that cause inscription.

‘‘Translation” is the process in which different actants seek to convince or persuade other actants to align to a network (Callon, 1986). This process is ongoing, with shifting alliances making the network unstable. To be maintained, the network has to ‘‘enrol” the actants. When all the actors within the network are satisfied, according to the interests attributed to them by the focal actor, it has passed an obligatory passage point (Callon, 1986). The focal actor is the actant that engages the other actants within the network to reach its goal. As this goal is reached, the network passes the obligatory passage point and the focal actor is no longer needed. When an actor network has reached this state, the network is stable and irreversible (Callon, 1991). A network can be treated as a ‘‘black box” if the network is stable at the time of study and not itself the focus of the inquiry. Legislation, ISO 14001, sustainability indexes, and other standards are considered black-box actor networks in this study.

## 5.2. Actor networks in sustainability innovation

To facilitate the analysis, we adopted the framework introduced by Lyytinen and King (2002) and subsequently used by Yoo et al. (2005) in a study of the configuration of actor networks involved in the diffusion of broadband mobile services in South Korea. As described below, this general framework, with its dialectic focus on innovation, diffusion, and regulation in relation to standards, policies, directives, and incentives, mapped well to the area of sustainability innovation

The framework (see Fig. 1) describes three realms that correspond to the different types of passage point that the actor networks have to pass through: namely, the innovation system, the marketplace, and the regulatory regime. Each realm has to conform to standards, policies, directives, and incentives as obligatory passage points. Their recognition and adoption is important in the formation of connections between the realms. They also form a critical part of a stabilized network (Yoo et al., 2005).

In the municipal sustainability initiative, the innovation system comprised actor networks that took part in the processes of realization, development, and knowledge creation. Standards, policies, directives, and incentives are all important in forming obligatory passage points, enrolling the actors into sustainable-development actions. Depending on the actor network, some actions may be particularly important, although the innovation system strives to understand better and to put forward a realizable sustainability initiative. The outcome realized can be viewed as the conclusion of an ‘‘innovation cycle” (Yoo et al., 2005). Innovation cycles in a sustainability initiative are linked to the current needs and desires, not only from regulatory actants, but also from social perspectives that perceive sustainability as an important issue.

The marketplace comprises actor networks that are the intended recipients and beneficiaries of the result of the innovation system. For example, employees, work-places, and the public, as well as companies and other organizations affected by the initiative, form these actor networks. A sustainability initiative defines an obligatory passage point to which the marketplace has to adapt. This generates a ‘‘diffusion cycle” (Yoo et al., 2005), a process that describes the willingness to adapt to the sustainability initiative. In a sustainability initiative, this adaptation process may take one of many forms because different actor networks in the marketplace are affected by different aspects of the initiative. For example, employees will face concrete changes in their day-to-day activities, whereas the changes that directly benefit the public are typically non-forced and the beneficiaries may even be unaware of these changes.

A regulatory regime is any type of authority that has the power to impose a set of constraints that inscribes how actor networks can be organized. Because the regulatory regime comprises actor networks that establish standards, policies, directives, and incentives, it has a great impact on the other realms. The ‘‘regulatory cycle” (Yoo et al., 2005) is the process by which the regulatory regime specifies in what way actors may interact.

## 6. The municipal sustainability initiative

The municipality of Uppsala (referred to as the municipality in the following) has identified logistics as an important area for improvement. Part of a project known as ‘‘The Good City” was dedicated to studying transport logistics within parts of the municipality. This study concerned the transportation flow between goods deliverers and recipients. The study covered methods for achieving better co-ordination of transportation by, for example, implementing time restrictions and introducing ‘‘green zones”.

Fig. 1. Analytical framework, adopted from Lyytinen and King (2002).

An important insight from this project was that the focus should be on the actor initiating the transport operation. The same conclusion was also drawn from similar projects in other municipalities (SRA, 2007). The municipality business plan (Uppsala Municipality, 2008a,b) further stresses the importance of an improved strategy for transport logistics, not least as a way to tackle emissions.

Against this backdrop, the municipality decided that two production units within the municipality, namely Uppsala Care & Education (UCE) and Uppsala Direct Services (UDS), should proceed with a project to develop their transport logistics. The project was to employ a triple bottom line perspective to ensure a long-term sustainable logistical operation.

The aim of the project was to refine existing routines related to transport logistics and thereby improve the logistical function itself. Changes on a grander scale, such as transforming existing infrastructure (e.g., roads and buildings) were not part of this project. The focus was on the metropolitan area of Uppsala, and specifically on number of work-places in the southern parts of the city, thereby excluding neighbouring rural areas.

At this stage, the transportation of prefabricated meals was also excluded because of regulatory issues that put special demands on this type of goods, and because it already had an efficient logistical solution compared with the transportation of other types of goods within the municipality. However, future studies might include consideration of this transportation issue.

As mentioned above, the focus of change was on the initiation of a transport operation. To accommodate this, a more structured way of ordering was needed, aided by an IT-based IS, as was a differentiation in service levels with regard to delivery time and security level. Furthermore, the role of the central depot as a consolidation and distribution centre within the municipality needed to be strengthened. To support triple bottom line reporting and to ensure continuous improvements, an IT-based decision support system (the Sustainability Portal) was to be implemented.

Taken together, these changes were seen as instrumental in achieving these intended goals:

Economic goals. The total cost linked to logistics was predicted to decrease by 2% because of a more efficient solution. A decrease in production loss as a result of increased delivery reliability was also expected. Furthermore, a decrease in cash and carry would free up employees’ time and thereby enable them to focus on core duties.

Environmental goals. A reduction in transport operations by 10–15%, resulting in a 10% decrease in ${ \bf C } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ and nitrogen oxide $( \mathsf { N O } _ { X } )$ levels relative to the current state, was expected.

Social goals. The local environment would potentially be safer, because of the decrease in transportation. The living environment would be less polluted by noise, vibration, and exhaust fumes. A decrease in transportation would also contribute to a safer environment for pedestrians and bicycle users. A decreased need for cash handling would contribute to a safer work environment for employees.

## 7. Before the initiative

The work-place survey showed that several different modes of transport and ordering were in operation. Typically, orders were directed to the municipal depot that actually transported the goods. The ordered goods would not always be in stock. In this case, the depot ordered from one of the suppliers that had a framework agreement with the municipality. Sometimes, work-places chose to order directly from a supplier that had a framework agreement with the work-place. There were also examples of work-places that ordered from suppliers without a framework agreement, arguing that this was cheaper than ordering from a supplier with such an agreement. Occasionally, cash and carry purchases were made, which were also typically outside any framework agreements.

Mid-sized and heavy transport vehicles were not seen as a major problem with respect to social issues. When cash and carry purchases were made, it was typically done for practical reasons. In some cases, this was even part of a training programme for users with disabilities, which suggests that it was an institutionalized behaviour within the organization. All of the surveyed work-places knew about the framework agreements and had access to them. With very few exceptions, workplaces did not have formal strategies regarding their logistical activities. Ultimately, the only guiding economic factor that had any focus was the cost of articles. Almost no follow-up or insight regarding cost associated with logistics was expressed at the work-places. Several work-places consciously did not buy from the central depot, because they regarded the depot as too expensive. Some even had the perception that there were internal guidelines stating that they should not order from the central depot. It was also common practice for articles not covered by the framework agreement to be ordered when an order was submitted via a framework supplier’s webpage.

The work-places could see the potential of more efficient ordering and distribution of goods. Several of the work-places reported a need for better ordering routines. One of the proposals to decrease the number of transport movements was to reduce them to once a week from the municipality depot. All work-places said that they could manage with only one delivery per week. Several of the work-places acknowledged the need to factor in other transport-related issues, such as the unpacking of goods, transportation within the work-place, and recycling. The notion that there was a need to address logistical issues from an environmental perspective was also beginning to emerge. Respondents could not describe the service level of their deliveries because there were no follow-up routines to handle orders placed with external suppliers and delivered to the work-places. With few exceptions, the social aspects of logistics were not seen as a priority area

Among the work-places, there was an understanding of the project’s relevance as well as a motivation to take part in the project. Some concerns were raised regarding difficulties in ensuring 24 hour service coverage for those services requiring it. One condition that was seen as important for the success of the project was the usability of web-ordering routines, which were perceived currently as poor. Furthermore, articles covered by a framework agreement should not be considerably more expensive than other suppliers’ articles, and the service quality level should not decrease. Lastly, education of and information to the employees were regarded as vital to achieving changed ordering behaviour.

The supplier interviews revealed that suppliers had structured their logistics in the following three different ways.

\- Nationwide logistics with distribution points that supply the bigger Swedish cities. Local transportation is procured from local firms.

\- Some of the suppliers have chosen to use Posten (the Swedish postal service, which offers messaging and logistical services). The strongest arguments in favour are that they can manage the complete logistical chain, they can deliver to private individuals, and Posten uses wire cages instead of pallets, which reduces the handling of packaging materials by the supplier.

\- One supplier implements all logistics itself.

All of the suppliers offered a web-based solution to which the municipality had access, whereby each work-place had its own customer number and associated delivery address. None of the suppliers generated any form of order confirmation, but they did provide a reference number. All of the suppliers also offered an Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) connection.

With some exceptions regarding special services, the suppliers had not separated the cost of transportation from the cost of goods in their agreement with the municipality. This was primarily for practical reasons, such as reducing the amount of work involved and the need to change internal routines to give special treatment to the municipality. Most of the suppliers could offer extra services at extra cost, such as unpacking and delivery to a specific department, in addition to the agreement Usually, these services were deemed too expensive by the work-places because of budget constraints. All the suppliers had put economic incentives in place to reduce the frequency of small orders. Typically, this involved an additional delivery fee for small orders.

Concerning environmental aspects, the suppliers used ISO 14001 as the benchmark standard. At the national level, all the suppliers used ISO-certified transport operators. However, at the local level, this was not always the case. In some places, the choice of transport operator was very limited, and suppliers had to make do, regardless of certification. The suppliers did not investigate logistical operators other than looking at their programmes for adhering to the ISO certification.

During the interviews, suppliers were asked about their views on joint distribution within the city. Several of the suppliers expressed the opinion that the municipality should take a more active role regarding an integrated strategy for the procurement of logistical services. Some articles were procured jointly between the municipality and the County Council and, as a result, were seen as an additional complication. All of the suppliers could further develop their logistical operations if this was demanded by the municipality. The current solution was a result of the demands set by the municipality when procuring the services. The suppliers were positive with respect to the aim of the project, and did not envision any problems with being involved. Several of the suppliers would handle their transportation differently if they could make bulk deliveries to one address. They could set up temporary solutions during the project, provided these did not result in disgruntled customers.

The suppliers also pointed out that other useful projects of a similar nature might be considered. A fear was expressed that a more integrated local logistical solution could lead to a monopoly situation within the city, which could potentiall skew the market

There were approximately 12,400 transport movements per year that involved the work-places studied, resulting in emissions of $6 3 3 7$ kg ${ \bf C } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ and 25 kg $\mathsf { N O } _ { X }$ per year (about 2.91 kg ${ \bf C } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ and 0.01 kg $\mathsf { N O } _ { X }$ per employee per year)

In summary, the co-ordination of orders was low and there were few articles per order. Many orders were placed at external suppliers, which triggered a transport operation. This indicates that there were considerable savings to be made both in administrative costs and in the physical handling of goods. With better co-ordination of orders and transportation, a reduction in cost would be possible for all parties. The survey also showed that a majority of articles were ordered directly from the supplier, even though they could be ordered from the municipality depot.

## 7.1. Initial actor network configurations

From the perspective of actor network theory, several actor networks can be identified, representing the three realms discussed above. Many of these networks themselves comprise several actor networks, depending on the level of analysis adopted.

A municipality. being headed by a local government, is a large organization formed by a conglomerate of different networks that belong to different realms. Viewed as an actant, the municipality is a strong force in inscribing policies, rules, and standards to enrol its subdivisions, employees, and citizens. The municipality is also an actant that supports and takes an active part in the development and study of phenomena in their realm of interest, and conducted by its different subdivisions. Consequently, the municipality constitutes an important actor network in both the innovation system realm and the regulatory regime realm.

Furthermore, many aspects of Swedish municipalities are regulated to act in the open market in competition with other service providers. This creates the dual roles of both consumer and provider of services. This often results in a situation where the municipalities have to evaluate bids from their own subdivisions in competition with external service providers during a procurement process. Therefore, the municipality is an actor in the market realm as well.

Overall, this makes the municipality very influential in creating a stable environment for actor networks to form, by enforcing legislation, implementing rules, and committing to standards for actor networks to meet. On the other hand, changes in any of these policies will introduce new obligatory passage points that will reshape the affected actor networks.

Within the municipality, there are two actors primarily affected by the sustainability initiative: namely, UCE and UDS. UCE is responsible for the participating work-places, and the study mainly focuses on these as actants, rather than the whole of UCE. UDS is responsible for the logistical activities in the municipality and for the central depot. As part of the municipality, UCE and UDS are part of the innovation realm. Represented by the work-places and the municipal depot, they are also part of the marketplace realm.

At this point, these actor networks were stable with respect to the aspects studied. However, they had not formed in the way that the municipality intended. At the municipality level, as well as at the level of UCE and UDS, there had been actions taken to enrol the work-place ordering routines by inscribing rules such as following framework agreements and placing orders at the municipal depot. The municipality had also implemented different information systems that inscribed actions at all levels within the organization. At the work-place level, networks had formed that perceived these inscriptions as advisory rather than mandatory, thereby enrolling via a more lenient interpretation. The rationale behind this was often that of convenience, but economic, practical, and service aspects were also prevalent. This could be regarded as a compromise reached in the problematization stage of translation, showing that work-places are influential in how they run their activities despite strong pressure to control their work routines.

External suppliers are all found in the marketplace realm. For the most part, they are governed by a framework agreement in their dealings with the municipality, although they are actants in various actor networks that are included in the munic ipality. In the negotiations to reach a framework agreement, they act in one network. As suppliers to the municipal depot, they act in another. In their direct dealings with the work-places, they act in yet another. Their web-based ordering systems, which constitute services to their customers, had enrolled the work-places to do some of their ordering directly from the suppliers’ web pages, which was at variance with the intended routines for work-place actions. This not only extended the enrolment by the municipality inscribed in the framework agreement, it also pointed to the freedom to act autonomously that work-places have.

The first step in the translation process to form the resultant actor network during the sustainability initiative was the commission given to UCE and UDS by the municipal Director of Finance. As a result, UCE, UDS, Uppsala University, and Pondra jointly initiated a study to explore how the affected parties related to the issue focused on by the initiative (of which this paper is an outcome). Effectively, the commission also served to enrol these parties in the initiative itself. Apart from some minor concerns that had to be handled, there were no real objections raised toward work-place involvement, because work-places and the municipal depot were already affiliated to UCE and UDS. The initiative was mainly viewed as something positive that would address important issues.

The external suppliers and their interaction with the municipal depot and the work-places were vital to the realization of the initiative. As they were only bound to what was expressed in the framework agreements, the voluntarism shown from those within the municipality could not be taken for granted. Presumably, the purpose of the initiative and the problems it addressed were of importance to the suppliers. All the external suppliers were positive towards the initiative and were will ing to take part, provided any solutions agreed upon did not have a negative impact on their service level. They also expressed the opinion that the municipality should become a more active actor, creating a uniform strategy for the procurement of logistical services. ## 8. After the initiative

A centralized logistical function at the depot, managed by UDS, has been implemented. This function is responsible fo joint ordering and joint distribution to the various work-places. To accommodate the increased workload, UDS have hired extra ordering and warehousing staff. During the trial period (Phase II), the project team acted as co-ordinator and controller for the joint distribution.

To accommodate the change in routines, the web-based ordering system within the municipality was further developed. A system that extends the existing ERP system was used by UDS to support ordering, warehousing, shipping, and purchasing. The Sustainability Portal was introduced, functioning as a decision support system focused on sustainability reporting and analysis, and based on GRI.

Ordering is handled by a joint ordering system and with common ordering routines, Orders can be placed via a common web-based system, phone, fax, and the framework suppliers’ websites. In the last case, orders are delivered jointly to the municipal depot instead of directly to the individual work-place, which was the previous practice. As a result, all deliveries from external framework agreement suppliers are now made jointly to one central hub, the municipal depot. Summary reports are available monthly via the Sustainability Portal and are sent to the affected parties within the municipality for follow-up of the sustainability indicators.

The change to weekly deliveries has resulted in a decrease of internal delivery runs by 60–80% (depending on the workplace) and the density of goods per transport operation has increased tenfold. Internal transport operations, in this case orders handled by the depot, have increased from 18% to 45%. Several factors contribute to this. The municipal depot has had a 45% increase in orders due to the changed ordering routines. External framework agreement suppliers have seen a decrease of 28%. A large part of the increase in transport operations carried out by the depot is caused by transportation that was previously carried out externally.

Savings were made in the following five areas.

1. An increase in orders placed at framework agreement suppliers.

2. Better options for renegotiating the framework agreements because of their improved utilization, with larger orders and less transportation.

3. New routines pointing to a decrease in cash and carry purchases.

4. A decrease in internal administration and invoices caused by joining orders.

5. A decrease in total cost per package because of more efficient transportation.

All of the interviewed participating work-places felt that the new routines worked well. The change to fixed delivery dates and ordering deadlines helped the work-places to improve their ordering routines. This had positive effects on their main activ ities. Of those interviewees that used the internal web-ordering system, the majority reported that it did not support their activities to the extent they would have liked. The main concern was that the search functions were not as user-friendly as they would have liked. Three of the work-places reported that the statistics were helpful in improving their work activities.

Eleven of the 13 interviewees stated that the project had increased their awareness of the sustainability impact of transportation. The ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ emissions from internal transportation decreased from 824.3 kg during October–December 2007 to 159.0 kg for the same period in 2008 (a decrease of more than 80%). For external transportation, there was an approximate decrease of 36%, based on the decrease in transportation directly to work-places and an overall decrease of transport operations to the central depot by 10%.

All of the participating schools reported that the introduction of fixed delivery dates had had a positive effect. This was also reported by a participating home for the elderly. Five of the interviewees also stated that the initiative had had a positive effect on the awareness among employees of the safety aspects of transportation.

In terms of the project goals stated above, a cost reduction of 2% with respect to logistics and distribution of goods is feasible. There are strong indicators that the service level and quality of transportation have increased. The initiative did not show any significant decrease in the number of cash and carry purchases. Total ${ \bf C } { \bf O } _ { 2 }$ emissions have decreased by 30–40% because of the implemented changes. The local neighbourhoods surrounding preschools and elementary schools have become safer and more secure. The work-places are generally very positive towards the solutions provided during the initiative and would like to see their continuation and scaling up.

## 8.1. Interpreting effects of the initiative

In the initiative, the introduction of sustainable reporting and the changes in logistics were closely linked. The aim was to improve an organization with regard to sustainability goals, and the changes within the organization were introduced in association with an information system to track sustainability improvements. An important change actant during the initiative was therefore the Sustainability Portal, which inscribed the sustainability indicators of GRI. The main change imposed on the other actants was the facilitation of sustainability reporting, which increased the awareness of sustainability issues.

At the municipal depot, the main effect of the initiative was an increased workload because of the joint distribution scheme, which resulted in more orders placed at the facility and an increased volume of goods. This effect was lessened, however, and the depot was enrolled, by increasing the number of staff. Presumably, this also helped employees adapt to changes in the web-ordering system. These changes were a result of the new logistical scheme prompted by the sustainability initiative although sustainability reporting as such was mainly automated via the users’ interaction with the ordering system.

The enrolment of the external framework suppliers was achieved by paying attention to their requirements, as mentioned above (e.g., no negative impact on their service level). The changes were all introduced in line with their normal operations and were easily integrated with their existing systems (via EDI, etc.). However, their enrolment in the initiative did affect their operations because the key sustainability indicators inscribed in the Sustainability Portal had made the municipality more efficient for order placement. This reduced the number of transport operations needed, which in itself was one of the factors that enrolled them to begin with.

The work-places were affected the most. This is in line with the premise of the initiative, which was that a change in ordering routines would be more effective than logistical changes and would have a greater sustainability impact. The changes in logistics are therefore a consequence of the new ordering behaviour. Before the changes to accommodate the initiative were put in place, the overall reactions were positive. When the initiative was launched, it turned out that not all the work-places enrolled to accept the changes. However, some changes to the original set-up of the initiative were made. From the outset, the web-ordering system was thought of as the tool for normal ordering. In practice, a sufficient number of employees at various work-places did not enrol to changing their practice of communicating with the central depot. To enrol them, familiar techniques such as fax and phone were also supported. Consequently, the employees at the central depot had to register the received orders in the web-ordering system. Despite these modifications, some work-places did not enrol, and the changes required to accommodate them would have been too complex to be included in the initiative.

As argued above, the work-places were the actants viewed as central to realizing the envisioned sustainability goals, and the initiative had a major impact on them. Both the Sustainability Portal and the web-ordering system did inscribe new behaviour and, perhaps more importantly for the future, they strengthened the awareness of sustainability issues.

An actor network that is not directly studied as part of the initiative is the public at large. This network represents actors that, to some extent, could be classified as belonging to the market realm, and they are indeed affected by the reduced transportation. At this point, however, transformations in this network because of the initiative would only be speculative. Nevertheless, to study the impact of sustainability innovation in IS on adjacent actants is probably a promising venue for future research. ## 9. Discussion and conclusions

There is a strong belief that we need to act in a way that ‘‘meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs” (United Nations, 1987). This requires change. We have studied the changes induced by committing to a sustainability initiative and the implementation of IS as a strategic part of achieving the envisioned goals. Sustainability is an issue that requires a holistic approach if it is to be viable as a means of securing the future of coming generations. Actor network theory lets us understand better the implications of committing to more sustainable operations.

One important aspect of the studied initiative was to support change that affected all three realms of sustainability; in contrast, many IS research efforts have chosen to focus primarily on the environmental aspect alone. To single out one aspect is to take a big risk, because the sustainability issue is caused by, and intertwined with, all human activities, and the impact of IS therefore needs to be considered.

Our study suggests that there does not have to be much resistance to sustainability issues at the non-committable level. The resistances encountered appear mainly at an organizational level, where required changes are not supported by existing routines and the organizational structure that enforces them. This implies that implementing successfully a sustainability initiative with IT-based reporting and analysis requires a thorough review of organizational routines and standards to find solutions that can enrol all affected stakeholders.

IS. and therefore IT, may have a pivotal role as a change actant in sustainability innovation because these systems inscribe the sustainability indicators that commit an organization to an initiative. In addition, the influence they have on organizations’ participating networks can be substantial. In our study, the initial commitment to use a sustainability reporting system for tracking sustainability indicators had profound implications. Not only was it a commitment to continuous striving for sustainability improvements, it also elevated the issue of educating the employees to enable them to take an active part in the initiative. It furthermore prompted rapid change in well-established routines because sustainability reporting made the sustainability impact more visible to other stakeholders. It became even more important to acquire goods and services from suppliers that were also adhering to sustainability standards because their activities influence the organization’s own sustainability reporting.

## 10. Limitations

Although there are 93 work-places associated with UCE in the southern part of the municipality, these represent only a fraction of its total activity. There are also factors that probably made the transformation of the network easier than it would be for other types of organizations. In a municipality, there is no need to satisfy profit-seeking stakeholders; provided the initiative is within budget, the municipality and its citizens will most likely be satisfied. Another limitation is that the work-places were not strongly forced into taking part. In a situation where the enrolment of all actors was mandatory, the changes required to successfully realize the initiative would probably be different. Given this study’s qualitative nature, no statistical generalization has been attempted and the results should be regarded as illustrative and explorative theoretica propositions (Lee and Baskerville, 2003).

## 11. Implications for research

With an increasing awareness of sustainability issues, IS will play an important part in future efforts. In our study, the changes implemented to accommodate a sustainability initiative caused sustainability improvements in all realms of sustainability. Consequently, it spurred changes in other systems as well. We can conclude that the introduction of an information system can change the sustainability performance of an organization. Inspired by Lyytinen and King (2002) and Yoo et al. (2005), we chose to frame this change in terms of actor network theory and the obligatory passage points imposed by the realms of innovation, regulation, and market. This enabled a holistic analysis that revealed some of the complex relationships involved in sustainability innovation. Specifically, our study emphasizes that a holistic view of IS in a sustainability initiative is important to understand better the social forces involved in such an initiative and IT’s role as a change actant in sustainability innovation. Future research may complement these insights by applying other theoretical lenses, in addition to investigating other empirical contexts. Encouraged by the positive experiences reported in this study, a promising next step would be to extend the initiative to include also other parts of the municipality. Further exploration of the expectations and outcomes in relation to the general public should be part of such an undertaking. On a grander scale, it would be worthwhile also to investigate empirically how the Uppsala case may resonate with wider European and global sustainability initiatives. In a similar vein, replicating the study, or at least the sustainability initiative, in other municipalities within and beyond Sweden could prove to be an important undertaking. both in terms of theoretical development and as an important step forward in keeping with the common call to save the planet. We would hope that further research could also use our results as a foundation for large-scale quantitative empirical work. Although our study has arguably provided rich descriptions and deep understanding of the sustainability initiative in Uppsala, using these insights to refine and test the developed propositions would add further important pieces to the Green IT puzzle. This would certainly reflect our belief in the usefulness of multi-method research in the IS field

## 12. Implications for practice

In practice, the introduction of sustainable reporting within an organization will force an in-depth analysis of the working practices within the organization and its business activities. Organizations also need to express their own view of sustainability. By introducing an information system for sustainability reporting, effort has to be made to interface with existing systems, internal as well as external. Implementation of functionalities that support the sustainability effort is also required. An information system in an organization always inscribes behaviour, and sustainability initiatives are no exceptions. An important fac tor in supporting employees’ enrolment is to help them adapt to the new circumstances brought about by the change. These circumstances will include both system properties and sustainability awareness. An important practical lesson learned is that paying attention to the various stakeholders’ needs and providing alternative solutions and learning processes are important. This is true both in regard to employees who are directly affected by an initiative, and to external actors, such as suppliers.
