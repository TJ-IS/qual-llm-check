# The role of institutional work in the transformation of the IT function: A longitudinal case study in the healthcare sector

## A B S T R A C T

This study aims to provide a deeper understanding of how information technology (IT) functions transform over time in order to align themselves with organizational visions and motivations. We conducted a longitudinal case study in a large mental health institute where an attempt was made to transform the mission and pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the IT function. To orient our case analysis, we combined a typology of IT function archetypes with key concepts from institutional theory. By focusing on key actors’ motives, actions, and decisions, our theoretical propositions suggest, among others, that institutional work that re<sup>fl</sup>ects all the three pillars of rules, norms, and meaning are necessary in order to successfully create and maintain an envisioned IT function archetype. Implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for both theory and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Change management remains one of the most important concerns of managers in organizations. Indeed, private and public companies face internal and external pressures that lead them to implement many changes, small and large. Because of the rapidly evolving digital world, it is becoming increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cers (CIOs) to de<sup>fi</sup>ne what the value of information technology (IT) in organizations should be. Currently, technology is embedded in products and services, customer and supplier relationships, and all of the organization’s processes. Organizations must rapidly adapt to change, and they expect IT (and the IT function) to help them achieve higher performance levels. CIOs are thus under heavy pressure to elicit and then support all aspects of the organization’s existing and future strategies. Faced with this reality, CIOs must develop a strong competency to drive radical IT-based changes. Although they are sensitive to the importance of developing an IT vision for change, planning the change, negotiating the necessary resources, preparing an effective communication strategy, managing stakeholders, and obtaining top management support, it is not always clear how CIOs should proceed. In other words, they must understand how to translate their strategic plans into reality.

Previous information system (IS) studies have provided a wide variety of descriptions of the transformative path taken by IT departments in organizations [4–6]. Still, most studies are atheoretical or based on anecdotal evidence, which prevents the development of enlightening research propositions. Some studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed internal factors and external pressures that contribute to the radical transformation of the IT function in organizations (e.g., [18]). Thus, although the extant literature contributes to our understanding of how IT functions are transformed (macro perspective), it does not reveal much about how and why key actors’ motives, actions, and decisions may in<sup>fl</sup>uence this transformation process (micro perspective). In other words, we do not have a proper understanding of how CIOs and other in<sup>fl</sup>uential actors facilitate or inhibit the transformation of the IT function role and pro<sup>fi</sup>le in organizations. The present study proposes to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap. More precisely, it seeks to provide a deeper understanding of how organizational actors act and interact in order to initiate, develop and consolidate the transformation of the IT function pro<sup>fi</sup>le in organizations.

In line with this objective, we provide answers to the following research questions: What motives, actions, and decisions do institutional actors adopt in order to create, maintain or disrupt the IT function archetype in their organization? How do these motives, actions and decisions in<sup>fl</sup>uence this transformation process? To answer these questions, we conducted a longitudinal case study in a highly institutionalized setting where an attempt was made to transform the IT function. We were able to follow and analyze the institutional work of the key actors over a period of 5 years. As explained in detail later, we did this by combining a recent theoretical typology of IT functions [17] with key concepts from institutional work [25]. By focusing on the key actors’ actions, we provide the foundations for a new theory of the transformation of the IT function in organizations.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. The next section presents the conceptual foundations of this study. Next, the methodological approach is detailed, followed by a presentation and discussion of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. To conclude, we present the study’s limitations and key contributions to both theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Archetypes of the IT function in organizations

Researchers have long examined the role of the IT function in organizations in the interest of developing speci<sup>fi</sup>c pro<sup>fi</sup>les or archetypes (e.g., [38,40–42,45]). In synthesizing this important bulk of knowledge, Guillemette and Paré [15,17] developed a typology of the IT function consisting of <sup>fi</sup>ve ideal archetypes called System Provider, Architecture Builder, Project Coordinator, Partner and Technological Leader. According to these authors, none of these pro<sup>fi</sup>les is deemed inherently superior, as each of them could be well-suited to an organization depending on its particular needs and intentions. Each ideal pro<sup>fi</sup>le represents a unique and coherent combination of properties associated with four fundamental dimensions, namely, the critical activities performed by IT specialists; the most important skills and abilities for IT professionals; the nature of the relationship with business units and external partners; and IT governance issues (i.e., how decisions about IT investments are made and who is accountable for IT success). According to Guillemette and Paré [17], IT functions are transformed over time as one or another of these archetypes is adopted in response to observable changes in organizations and their environments. Below we provide a brief description of each archetype.

First, the System Provider provides the organization with highquality ISs that meet its needs at the lowest possible cost. It therefore places great emphasis on system development, acquisition, and maintenance activities, and has structured methodologies for organizing such activities. In its day-to-day work, the help desk serves as a link with the business units. IT professionals are skilled at communicating with users and have strong technical skills (e.g., programming, networks and development). This archetype is accountable for compliance with budgets, infrastructure reliability, and system quality. Second, the Architecture Builder seeks to design <sup>fl</sup>exible and integrated IT architecture and infrastructure that will provide appropriate support to the business’s present and future activities. It applies best practices and fosters the use of integrated technologies. IT professionals are very proactive, and can quickly grasp the organization’s needs and satisfy them within the existing architecture. This pro<sup>fi</sup>le often takes full responsibility for IT projects and oversees communication between the different units affected by IT projects. Third, the Project Coordinator primarily manages relationships between suppliers and business units in order to meet the organization’s needs. It ensures that current contracts are successful, manages IT projects, and supports business units in this process. IT professionals understand the needs and expectations of business units and external partners and mediate between these two groups. This archetype is thus responsible for controlling IT costs, ensuring system availability and maintaining the level of IT service in the client units. Fourth, the Partner serves as a catalyst for changes to business processes. IT professionals have an excellent understanding of the organization’s processes and can evaluate where and how IT may be deployed to support business objectives. This knowledge is acquired in everyday work through the IT professionals’ close contacts with members of the organization. Decisions are made in collaboration with the business units in multidisciplinary teams where responsibility is shared by all members. Fifth, the mission of the Technological Leader is to identify emerging technologies, <sup>fi</sup>nd innovative applications for them in the organization, and then implement them as required. IT professionals have excellent knowledge of the organization’s business environment and strategy as well as a deep understanding of the competitive dynamic in the industry. The IT function is very proactive and has considerable in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the business units. It therefore often agrees to take responsibility for IT projects it sets in motion, assuming leadership until completion. As a <sup>fi</sup>nal remark, Guillemette and Paré [17] observed that the <sup>fi</sup>ve archetypes are not mutually exclusive and that IT functions that are close to the ideal of any given pro<sup>fi</sup>le seem to be outperforming those with hybrid pro<sup>fi</sup>les (i.e., IT functions having features of several archetypes).

Previous research has shown that this typology can be effectively used in combination with the theory of punctuated equilibria to investigate the transformation of IT functions in organizations [16,18]. In these studies, IT archetypes served as precious anchors that helped researchers characterize the IT function before, during, and after a transformation process, and the theory of punctuated equilibria offers a very insightful, yet macro explanation of the phenomenon under investigation. As mentioned above, we argue herein that the typology developed by Guillemette and Paré [17] can also be combined with key concepts from institutional theory in order to develop a complementary, yet more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained explanation of the transformation of the role and pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the IT function in organizations.

## 2.2. Institutional theory in IS

An institution is a social structure that provides organizations and individuals with lines of action and orientations; simultaneously, this social structure controls and constrains organizations [47]. Institutions are supported by three distinct but inter-related pillars – regulative, normative, and cognitive structures – through which legitimacy is established and maintained. The regulative pillar refers to how some actors have formal control over the behavior of the other actors. Particularly, the normative pillar explains the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on behavior exercised when speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as appropriate in given social situations. Last, the cognitive pillar is based on “shared conceptions that constitute the nature of social reality and the frames through which meaning is made” [47]; p.57). Institutionalized practices may be maintained at any given time through the action of one of the pillars or the combined action of multiple pillars [47]. Under institutional pressures, organizational actors implement strategies in order to earn, maintain or re-establish their legitimacy [51]. Institutional theory is primarily concerned with the tendency towards standardized organizational structures.

The IS literature that has taken an institutional approach was reviewed and analyzed in a paper by [34] that positions IT contributions with respect to the principles of institutional theory and in terms of their levels of analysis. According to this review, the contributions of previous research in this area were essentially related to institutionalization processes, study of institutional pressures on an organization or group, and behaviors observed in response to these similar pressures (legitimation strategies). More precisely, the institutional topics most investigated concern the development and diffusion of technological innovations, outsourcing, power, and IT project management. In more recent studies, IS institutionalists followed the same path; they examined the effect of con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional logics on organizations (e.g., [3,20]), the adoption and diffusion of a technological innovation (e.g., [19,36]), organizational responses to institutional pressures (e.g., [22]), or imitation from IT service vendors (e.g., [43]).

## 2.2.1. Institutional work

As part of institutional theory, the institutional work approach aims to understand how in<sup>fl</sup>uential actors alter or change institutional arrangements rather than maintain them [26]. In other words, institutional work examines the practices of individual actors – also called institutional entrepreneurs – aimed at creating, maintaining, or disrupting institutions [27]. Key actors use the resources at their disposal and deploy various strategies [37,53] to alter the rules, norms, and social practices constraining actions [39]. Although institutional work is becoming the preeminent stream of research on the theory of organizations [55], only a few IS studies have examined it, describing key actors’ roles in large-scale IT infrastructure implementations (e.g., [30]), in the promotion of automated IT professional services (e.g., [56]), in the development of project momentum during customer relationship management (CRM) implementations (e.g., [57]) and to study the introduction of new IT in the management of mega-events such as professional sport championships (e.g., [33]). We believe this theoretical perspective constitutes a relevant and important approach to the study of IT functions in organizations. As mentioned earlier, we believe it has the potential to make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution at a micro level, specifying the type of actions that can be performed to spur change rather than submit more or less passively to institutional pressures. Furthermore, this theoretical perspective will likely provide us with a better understanding of how key actors navigate within a particular context, considering prede<sup>fi</sup>ned institutional logics.

As shown in Table 1, Lawrence and Suddaby [25] have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nine types of institutional work aimed at creating a new institution, as well as six for maintaining and three for disrupting an existing institutional arrangement and they provided, for each, a rich description of the actors and the motives behind their actions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category of work falls under the regulative pillar and is characterized by the creation of rules that either de<sup>fi</sup>ne access to resources for creating the institution (advocacy, de<sup>fi</sup>ning, and vesting), ensure adherence to existing rules for maintaining the institution (enabling work, policing, and deterring) or disconnect sanctions and rewards in order to disrupt institutions. The second work category falls under the normative pillar, and is characterized by actions aimed at either recon<sup>fi</sup>guring the actors belief systems when the institution is being created (constructing identities, changing normative associations, and constructing normative networks); maintaining existing institutions through efforts to reproduce existing standards and systems of thinking (valorizing and demonizing, mythologizing, and embedding and routinizing); or dissociating moral foundations in order to disrupt institutions. Finally, the third category of work falls under the cognitive pillar. It is characterized by actions aimed at modifying categories in order to alter their meaning (mimicry, theorizing and educating) or undermining assumptions and beliefs in order to disrupt existing institutional arrangements. Taken together, the 18 categories of institutional work can help us understand how actors create, maintain, or disrupt institutions by acting on rules, standards, and cognitive structures.

## 3. Methodology

In line with our research objective, a longitudinal case study was conducted. The selected healthcare organization, a large Canadian mental health institute, is not in any major way atypical, extreme, deviant, or intensely unusual with regard to its IT function. However, it provided an information-rich case that manifested the phenomenon of interest. Importantly, healthcare organizations are recognized as being highly institutionalized settings [1,10,48], which makes them a perfect environment to observe de visu institutional work.

## 3.1. Data collection

Multiple data collection methods were combined in three distinct phases taking place over a 5-year period between year 1 (Y1) and year 6 (Y6). The <sup>fi</sup>rst data collection phase, which occurred during the summer of Y1, was mainly intended to characterize the pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the IT function at baseline. Respondents described the archetype of the IT function at that time, as well as the envisioned one. This is particularly relevant to our study, as it gave us an understanding of what the key actors said initially and allowed us to evaluate the strategies that were implemented during the transformation of the IT function. A total of nine formal semistructured interviews with key informants were conducted during the initial phase. Participants were chosen on the basis of their knowledge of the IT function and their role in the organization, mostly developed through their implication in past IT projects. Details of the data collection protocol are provided in Appendix A.

The second phase of data collection was from January of Y2 to May of Y3. Our main objective was to follow the transformation as it progressed over time. One member of the research team was present in the hospital on a weekly basis throughout this period. She attended bi-weekly coordination meetings and monthly IT governance meetings (observation). She also had several ad hoc informal conversations with all key actors. In addition, 10 formal semi-structured interviews were conducted during this period (see Appendix A). Interviewees were asked to identify key events associated with the transformation that was occurring in the IT function and explain the role they played in this process. They were also asked to characterize the IT function archetype as they perceived it. The on-site researcher took several <sup>fi</sup>eld notes that proved to be useful, especially when the participants were monosyllabic in their answers. Lastly, several documents were gathered and analyzed, including consultants’ reports, IT strategic plans, IT project management frameworks, organizational charts, and IT performance reports.

Finally, the third round of data collection took place in the fall of Y6. We were able to conduct retrospective semi-structured interviews with three key actors who had participated actively in the transformation process. Informants recalled and discussed important events that had occurred since the mid of Y3 and characterized the IT function archetype as they perceived it in Y6. Several documents were also gathered and analyzed during this <sup>fi</sup>nal data collection phase (see Appendix A). Overall, the data collection process produced a total of 245 (single-space) pages of verbatim transcripts, 130 pages of <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and 230 pages of documentation.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Data analysis began during data collection. As suggested by Miles and Huberman [35], <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and re<sup>fl</sup>ective remarks were taken to help us understand what was happening during the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork and re<sup>fi</sup>ne our interview protocols. A coding scheme was created based on the 18 categories of institutional work in Table 1 and the <sup>fi</sup>ve archetypes of the IT function. During the entire coding process, we made sure to consider the context of each excerpt and, in particular, the actors’ motives or intentions. In other words, we did not take any verbatim quote out of context, but rather associated each purposeful action of an actor to the code that best mirrored his or her intention behind the action (see Appendix B for examples of the coding process). Two of the investigators independently coded all the interviews, one by one. Subsequently, they met to discuss their coding and reconcile any discrepancies, one interview at a time. Afterward, one member of the research team coded the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and documentation using the same coding scheme.

In order to develop a deeper understanding of the data and the underlying themes and patterns, we used three main strategies to guide our analysis, that is, narratives, visual mapping and temporal bracketing [23]. First, the narrative strategy was used to construct a detailed story from the data collected, by placing actions and events in their context and time. Narration helps bring out the variety and diversity of events, their causes and consequences and its use of speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics (from theory) that helps to understand the data. In this study, we used the narrative strategy as a way to highlight the course of events as well as the actions of key actors in the transformation of the IT function. One of the authors who had participated in data collection wrote the case study, which was then corroborated by a second author. One of the key informants then validated the accuracy of the events described in our narrative, Second, visual mapping is particularly useful to analyses of process data because it allows researchers to easily observe events over time. Events (actions in our case) are modeled using different forms and colors, helping researchers identify patterns in the data and theorize about them. In our study, this method allowed us to see links between the data analyzed and <sup>fi</sup>nd patterns, and this proved very useful. A chronology of events (not shown here) was developed using Visio software. Third, visual mapping was used in combination with temporal bracketing. The latter strategy allows the representation of events over time and decomposes them into successive periods [23]. This method is especially useful for structuring process data It facilitates the creation of comparative units of analysis to explore new theoretical ideas. This organization of data into periods enables to analyze the effects of actors’ motives and actions and the impact of the context of one period on another. Applving this strategy allowed us to understand the considerable amount of information collected. This strategy was particularly helpful for analyzing changes in the IT archetype over time. Time periods were chosen based on key actors’ subjective interpretations of milestone events during the transformation process. Temporal bracketing allowed us to follow the process of transformation through time and evaluate the result of each phase in the form of an IT archetype.

## 4. Case description

## 4.1. Background

Bel<sup>fi</sup>n<sup>1</sup> is a large Canadian hospital that provides mental care services. Its mission is to offer cutting-edge care and services and to advance knowledge in mental health. Bel<sup>fi</sup>n’s staff and managers strongly value excellence, innovation and human potential based on commitment and collaboration. The IT department at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n was created in the late 1960s. At the beginning of this study at Y1, it employed 15 individuals. One of the main activities was to support the technological infrastructure that was distributed across 30 sites connected by optical <sup>fi</sup>bers. The infrastructure included 31 servers, >900 workstations, of which 830 were connected to the network. It also provided technical support to more than a thousand end users. The IT portfolio included >100 applications, 20 of which are clinical ISs. Most of the administrative applications were not well integrated with each other. The help desk received >20,000 calls a year. The following paragraphs describe progress in the transformation of the IT function at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n, with an emphasis on the institutional work of key actors.

Until Y1, the IT function at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n was characterized as a typical System Provider. The primary mission of the IT function was to respond to requests from administrative departments and clinical units [SP]. As a result, the IT function organized its activities around the development (or acquisition), maintenance, and support of IT applications [SP]. IT staff relied primarily on their technical skills [SP], and they interacted with users mainly through the help desk [SP]. A few systems analysts, who had no solid business or clinical background [SP], served as the interface between the user departments and the IT function [SP]. Business units made proposals after consulting with systems analysts [SP], subject to approvals from Mark, the interim IT director. As in most traditional “IT shops,” IT personnel were responsible for the design and installation of IT applications, while managers in the user departments were accountable for the effects of these systems [SP].

In early Y1, the hospital’s CEO gave a mandate to a team of consultants to make recommendations on how the hospital’s IT services should be structured and organized. Several factors led the CEO to undertake this initiative. First, there was perceived dissatisfaction with the IT function among clinicians and staff. Several IT projects had been initiated in clinical units without the involvement of IT personnel. Things were considered “too complicated with [IT staff]” according to a medical director. Clinicians and administrative staff had been questioning the way IT projects were being managed, because a high proportion of systems were delivered late, over budget and without all the required features. Second, the CEO and other executives felt Bel<sup>fi</sup>n was unable to keep pace with recent technological developments and that existing ISs were outdated and did not adequately support the hospital’s core business, that is, clinical activities. Their vision of what should be the role and contribution of the IT function in the organization had started to change.

Third, the CEO and a few in<sup>fl</sup>uential medical directors were not convinced that the interim IT director was the best person to lead the transformation of the IT function. They felt that he did not have the right skills and would be unable to make high-impact decisions when needed and to assume full responsibility for them. However, as they all valued Mark’s strong knowledge of the hospital and his network outside the organization, they wanted him to be involved in the IT function transformation. The team of consultants submitted its report to the CEO in September of Y1.

Following the consultants’ recommendations, two of the hospital’s managers were named joint CIOs in a new structure called the Information Systems Directorate (ISD) in the March of Y2: Katherine, the director of nursing and John, the chief <sup>fi</sup>nancial of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CFO). Katherine had encouraged the hospital’s CEO to create a new structure and recommended to appoint two people with clinical and administrative experience to lead the new department {1}. John had begun to notice that many groups were bypassing established structures (IT advisory and decision-making committees) and establishing their own “IT groups” {17}. Both Katherine and John described the IT department then as reactive and lacking strong leadership. Under the new model, Katherine and John would be responsible for setting priorities among IT projects and recommending approvals to the IT steering committee {1}. User departments would lead IT projects, with the IT staff providing technical support {1}.

## 4.2. Joint management model (Y2–Y3)

The ISD was of<sup>fi</sup>cially created in the March of Y2. Consultants had proposed a vision of a new IT function that corresponded to the Architecture Builder archetype with the creation of an IT department oriented towards the proactive [AB] search of best software solutions on the market; to upgrade and management of an integrated IT architecture; the management of external relationship with equipment and application providers [AB]; and to secure critical information assets. The integration of new key players in the new ISD was deemed important, including clinical and business specialists with good knowledge of best industry practices [AB]. In terms of governance, they wanted to create a single committee that would be responsible for monitoring, reviewing, and prioritizing all technology projects [AB] as well as developing a master plan. Finally, the organization wanted to reduce outsourcing by building ad hoc relationships with external partners. This vision was the basis for the transformation of the IT function that began in the starting of Y2.

Although John and Katherine were appointed joint CIOs, Mark was appointed Head of IT staff {2}. Jane, a registered nurse with some experience in e-health initiatives, also joined the new team {2}. She was asked to work in close collaboration with Mark. From the March of Y2 to April of Y3, Katherine and John invested considerable time and energy to align their visions of the ISD’s role at the hospital {2}. They organized regular meetings with other ISD members to simplify coordination and the transition to the new ISD {10}.

Mark and Jane had to learn how to work together and establish their new roles and responsibilities. Very quickly, they began to experience some problems. Jane was initially responsible for clinical appropriation, change management, implementation and training {2}. Mark was placed in charge of project management, system development as well as the IT staff, the help desk, IT budgets and hiring {2}. In a spirit of true collaboration, Jane and Mark kept each other informed of everything: “In trying to ensure that it would work, we were becoming each other’s clone. We tried to keep each other up to date on everything. It became a bit inefficient. [ . . . ] We were just learning as we went along”, said Mark {10}. This collaboration would lead to the creation of a project coordination of<sup>fi</sup>ce (PCO) {2}. Jane and Mark then began creating a project management framework {8}, which proved dif<sup>fi</sup>cult because their roles and responsibilities had become intertwined. They began to split projects in order to simplify communications: Jane was in charge of projects with considerable clinical content while Mark took care of the non-clinical initiatives {10}. This new arrangement complicated things for systems analysts and project managers to a point where the situation became unmanageable. Jane perceived herself as responsible for change management in IT projects (a role she wanted to play) {2}. She believed that she was to serve as a bridge with the clinicians, managing the department’s relationship with them. Mark perceived himself as responsible for all the IT professionals {2}. He evaluated project requests and assigned a project manager as well as IT staff to each project {2}. Jane and Mark did not fully understand how their roles were complementary { 2}.

At the end of May of Y2, Jane and Mark asked to meet with Katherine and John to clarify their respective roles {1} and to present a preliminary organizational chart for the PCO {2}. Katherine and John quickly rejected the chart, considering it too complex and not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently “client-oriented”. Katherine wanted to integrate best practices into the PCO. It was agreed that the project manager should come from the ISD team {2}. Katherine and John did not, however, agree on the ISD’s accountability for projects, so no decision was taken on this matter { 2}. Following negotiations between the four actors, it was agreed that Jane and Mark’s responsibilities would be major or minor, as initially agreed {2}.

In the July of Y2, Mark began applying a client-based approach. He met with the department’s clinicians (system users) to evaluate the department’s quality of service over the past 5 years {17}. They reported that they were more or less satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the services provided and suggested some improvements. Across the hospital, Mark was still perceived to be managing the IT department, as if there had not been a fundamental change. Mark continued to manage his ITIL projects {11} and wanted to improve client service by creating a position of help desk manager {11} and by implementing various control lists to structure these tasks {15}.

Jane then became a manager, just like Mark {2} but their joint appointment did not work and was abolished in the summer of Y2 {2}. Their statutory meetings ended several weeks later, as requested by Jane, who felt that they were no longer needed {1}. Mark and Jane nevertheless continued to draft a project management framework {8}. In the August of Y2, Katherine and John realized that members of the ISD team had developed a new strategy by which they spoke to either John or Katherine, depending on the answer they sought, to get their agreement on different subjects {10}. They agreed that their management arrangement was inef<sup>fi</sup>cient, that John would become responsible for day-to-day management of the ISD, and they would meet regularly to align their visions {2}.

In the fall of Y2, Mark began a strategic planning project for the ISD and invited Katherine and John to become involved in it {1}. They felt that they did not have the required technological knowledge or background { 9} and thus gave the mandate to an external consultant recommended by Mark {7}. In the February of Y3, a project management framework de<sup>fi</sup>ning roles, responsibilities and procedures was <sup>fi</sup>nally submitted to and approved by the IT steering committee {8}. This marked the of<sup>fi</sup>cial start of the PCO’s work and, by extension, the management of projects unde this new methodology {10}. The availability of various forms (e.g., requests for new projects, client satisfaction, project closing, and change management requests) was announced, making the project management framework operational {15}. The IT department’s project managers were then trained in project management best practices (PMBOK) {9}. The issue of the ISD’s accountability for projects nevertheless re-emerged, and Katherine asked Mark and Jane to send out a clear message that the ISD was 100% accountable for projects {2}.

In the April of Y3, Katherine retired, making John the ISD’s sole director {2}, a temporary situation that became permanent. As part of the IT strategic plan’s implementation, John, Mark and the consultant travelled abroad to visit a large hospital and compare Bel<sup>fi</sup>n to best practices {7}, and Jane visited other Canadian hospitals for the same purpose {7}. They came back encouraged, with John concluding “we aren’t all that bad”. An IT strategic plan submitted in the May of Y3 combined with Katherine’s departure in April, suggested that some major changes could be expected in the ISD. The consultant’s report recommended radically changing the IT infrastructure, replacing or providing intensive training to IT professionals to enhance their skills and knowledge and hiring a specialized <sup>fi</sup>rm to manage this project {1}. The IT strategic plan did not lead to too many changes. Disappointed, Mark started accepting a few mandates outside the hospital {17}, eventually leading him to resign in the December of Y3.

The ISD was unable to successfully implement the vision developed by the team of external consultants. Instead, the ISD operated under a “hybrid” model, composed of elements from both the System Provider and the Partner archetypes. No major efforts were made to improve the hospital’s IT architecture and adapt it to industry best practices, as recommended by the consultants [i.e., an AB pro<sup>fi</sup>le]. Key informants explained that such an initiative was mainly hindered by a lack of local expertise and resources. Consequently, ISD activities were maintained around the development and maintenance of in-house ISs [SP]. The creation of the PCO did not yield the expected outcomes. The PCO turned out to serve as just another way to receive requests from user departments [SP]. However, no one in the ISD group had strong clinical knowledge combined with IT knowledge; skills that would have been necessary to be more proactive with business managers and to propose best practices surrounding the use of IT in support of clinical activities [a characteristic of the AB archetype]. Nevertheless, adding Jane to the team had very positive effects. Because she was involved in each and every IT project that included a clinical component, she transferred part of her clinical knowledge to the IT analysts with whom she interacted [P]. Importantly, the ISD team started to communicate to its clients that it would have to take full accountability for future IT projects. However, this position could not be sustained and only a few months later the ISD team was accountable for technical issues only [SP].

## 4.3. Beginning of a new era (Y4–Y6)

To replace Mark, John formed a selection committee consisting of himself, the consultant who wrote the IT strategic plan and a representative from the human resources department. He asked Jane (who was not a part of the committee) and someone from technological operations to take over Mark’s tasks until a replacement could be found {2}. It took 8 months to <sup>fi</sup>ll the position. During this period, John emphasized the importance of client service and good management of the help desk {2}. Change management became equated with training {2}. In the September of Y4, Brian was hired to manage the IT department in replacement of Mark {2}. Brian had good knowledge of the healthcare sector and IT infrastructure management best practices {9}, but his managerial experience was very limited { 9}. He shared his boss’s (John) vision that the ISD should focus on managing IT infrastructure, providing good client service and reducing IT operating costs {2}. Brian laid off a consultant who had been at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n for many years [16]. He announced this decision at one of his <sup>fi</sup>rst team meetings, saying, “ . . . and let this serve as a lesson to others”. This sent a chill through relations among Brian, Jane and the members of the IT team, as this type of decision was at odds with the existing culture at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n {17}. Jane met with John to discuss the matter {1}, but he was in complete agreement with the direction that Brian was taking and did nothing to stand in his way.

The list of requests received by the PCO led Brian to conclude that most of them did not in fact represent projects, but rather the need to make some improvements. He therefore made a recommendation to John that the PCO be closed {1}. Brian was very uncomfortable with the idea of co-management. He began speaking to John about eliminating joint management at the same time as the PCO would be abolished {1}. Jane’s departure in the May of Y5 opened a door for this change. John hired a new nurse clinician for Jane’s position, but he had her report to Brian and permanently closed the PCO {2}. This made John the sole CIO and Brian the only person responsible for the department, with all the other employees reporting to him. However, the coordinator of the help desk kept his status as team leader.

Following all these changes, Brian introduced a new project management methodology based on PMBOK practices {10}. He had the project managers take several months of training to master this approach {15}. Brian suggested placing clinicians in client roles, whereby they would be responsible for expressing their needs to the ISD {1}. The ISD would then become the sole unit responsible for IT {2}. This new project management structure was presented to Bel<sup>fi</sup>n’s board of directors {9}. Jane held a new position in the organization but was still involved with clinical IT projects from the “business side”. She realized at this presentation that clinicians were unfamiliar with the former structure of the ISD and did not really understand the change. This seemed to be due to the fact that, for several years, hardly any IT projects had been performed with the ISD. They had been told in the October of Y3 that the ISD would concentrate on introducing an electronic medical record (EMR) system and, as a result, it would be much more selective in the new IT projects it would undertake {10}. However, due to pressure from Brian, several non-clinical IT projects considered critical to the organization had been undertaken {1}. “Clinical activities at the Belfin could work very well without IT. Our IT could disappear tomorrow and they wouldn’t notice. But on the administrative side, the need was great, such as in payroll. If, starting tomorrow, payroll production stopped, what would happen? Would the employees still want to work if they weren’t getting paid? [ . . . ] The others didn’t see it this way, but my manager understood what was important”, said Brian.

## 4.4. Epilogue

In the fall of Y6, the ISD was still working on the EMR project and had still not delivered anything. The clinical units had given up waiting for this project and, over the previous few months, had begun acquiring software packages that could meet their needs {17}. Most of these packages had even been implemented without assistance from the ISD team {17}. Some individuals had begun work to establish a clinical project of<sup>fi</sup>ce that would operate outside the formal ISD structure and manage all of the organization’s clinical projects {17}. Simultaneously, Brian began a review of the information resource strategic plan based on a major report submitted by another team of consultants in Y1 that compared the IT department (its organization, the skills of its IT professionals, salaries and infrastructure) with best practices {2}.

Following Katherine’s and Mark’s departure in the April and December of Y3, respectively, and Brian’s arrival in the September of Y4, the ISD shared characteristics of the System Provider and the Architecture Builder archetypes. For one thing, IT activities were mainly focused on the help desk and the development of ISs [SP]. Some efforts were also made to update components of the existing IT infrastructure (e.g., programming languages of existing applications), so that they would be better aligned with industry practices [AB]. The project management skills of IT professionals were also enhanced through professional education and training activities [SP]. Brian also brought with him key industry knowledge in terms of IT infrastructure [AB]. However, the team’s clinical skills and knowledge were embodied solely by Jane and none were left after her departure in Y5. Many clinicians throughout the hospital perceived the IT department as being still reactive [SP]. In terms of governance, as IT personnel before were responsible for the design and deployment of IT applications, while managers were accountable for realizing effects and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [SP]. In summary, the envisioned archetype in the March of Y2 – the AB archetype – was never entirely implemented.

## 5. Case analysis and discussion

This section highlights the roles played by key institutional actors in the somewhat unsuccessful attempt to transform the IT function at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n hospital. As shown in Fig. 1, each actor used several strategies to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the direction and scope of the change. For their part, the transformation process and its resulting outcomes are given in Table 2.

## 5.1. Mark

When, in the summer of Y1, questions began to be raised about the mission or role played by the IT department at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n, Mark’s institutional actions mainly aimed at maintaining the existing structure or pro<sup>fi</sup>le in place. He used valorizing and demonizing {13} institutional work practices. Following the creation of the new ISD archetype, he continued to maintain the IT function’s initial model (represented by the dotted lines in Fig. 1) through actions including policing {11} and embedding and routinizing {15} until August of Y2. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on his initial actions and behaviors, it appears that Mark did not necessarily “resist” the envisioned pro<sup>fi</sup>le because it did not serve his personal interests well, but rather because a clear vision of how things should be done under the new “structure” was missing. As he said, in the meantime he had an “IT department to run”. In fact, Mark actively participated in the creation of the IT function’s new archetype through advocacy {1} and de<sup>fi</sup>ning {2} institutional work. One can also see that, beginning in the December of Y2, Mark’s actions turned toward maintaining this new archetype through enabling work (mainly working in collaboration with Jane) {10}, embedding and routinizing {15} as well as some theorizing {8} and educating {9}. With respect to the creation of the strategic plan, which began in Y3, we see Mark engaged in mimicry behavioral actions {7}. However, once the new strategic plan was submitted in the April of Y3, Mark became gradually disengaged himself from the IT department and started questioning the new archetype through actions that undermined core assumptions and beliefs {18}.

## 5.2. Jane

Jane’s institutional work truly began once the creation of the new directorate was announced. She immediately began work to develop the new archetype for the IT function, mostly through advocacy behaviors {1}, until she was assigned to a management position at the same level as Mark in the June of Y2. Her actions then focused on de<sup>fi</sup>ning her role {2}, accompanied by theorizing {8}, enabling {10}, and embedding and routinizing {15} actions undertaken in collaboration with Mark, particularly with respect to all project management issues. Following Mark’s departure and Brian’s arrival, she clearly adopted advocacy behaviors {1} toward John until her departure from the ISD in the May of Y5.

## 5.3. Katherine

From the outset, Katherine used advocacy {1} to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the consultants’ recommendations on a new archetype for the IT function. As soon as she became co-manager of the function, she engaged in major de<sup>fi</sup>ning actions {2}, sometimes in agreement and sometimes in disagreement with John. In the latter case, she used advocacy {1}, trying to create the new IT function according to her own vision, which was directed at mimicking best practices {7}. Her main actions were de<sup>fi</sup>ning {2} and advocacy {1}. She played only a minor role in actions directed at maintaining the new archetype of the IT function, with the exception of her presence, from March to June of Y2, at the weekly coordination meetings of the ISD’s members {10}.

## 5.4. John

John was the only actor present throughout the entire transformation. Before the “of<sup>fi</sup>cial” creation of the ISD, he used some advocacy {1} to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consultants toward adoption of a new archetype for the IT function. Once the new function had been of<sup>fi</sup>cially announced, he worked tirelessly on de<sup>fi</sup>ning it {2}.

5.6. Towards a new theory of the transformation of the IT function archetype in organizations

In this section, we highlight the institutional work involved in creating, maintaining, and disrupting an IT function archetype. On the basis of the case evidence presented above, we develop a series of research propositions. As shown in Fig. 1, most of the institutional work involved creating new rules. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the preferred types of institutional work were advocacy {1} and de<sup>fi</sup>ning actions {2}. This should not come as a surprise as advocacy work is an important precursor to de<sup>fi</sup>ning the rules that confer status and privileges [25]. These speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions are an essential part of creating shared rules and the formulation of standards and the meaning of what the IT function will be, i.e., its archetype, so that they may be shared by all the key actors. These actions create the basis for a shared vision that will then direct maintaining actions.

However, when new institutional structures are being built (as in this case), these two steps must be reinforced through considerable work at vesting {3}, that is, trying to implement a coercive or regulatory authority that reinforces the preparatory work of advocacy and de<sup>fi</sup>ning [25]. Yet, the Bel<sup>fi</sup>n case provided only super<sup>fi</sup>cial evidence of this important type of institutional work. Indeed, even if the ISD team was formally created in the starting of Y2, the only formal change effected by upper management was to establish its hierarchical structure, and all the details concerning the hierarchy’s roles and responsibilities were left to the discretion of the two appointed CIOs. The day-today organization of the IT function did not bene<sup>fi</sup>t from formal vesting work. In our viewpoint, the absence of vesting {3} seems to have led the key actors to advocate for and try to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the IT function without ever entering into a framework in which a hierarchical power had decided what the IT function should be. Making this decision would have limited advocacy and de<sup>fi</sup>ning actions, and would have helped shared standards emerge. In light of the above, we make the following propositions:

When creating a new IT function archetype, key actors must first ensure that a form of legitimate authority has been clearly established in order to support their vision and efforts. (Proposition #1)

Advocacy, defining and vesting actions work in a mutually reinforcing cycle and, hence, must all be performed in parallel to ensure a successful transformation of the IT function profile. (Proposition #2)

The main goal of activities such as constructing identi<sup>fi</sup>es {4}, changing normative associations {5}, and constructing normative networks {6} is to build the normative structures (i.e., roles, values, and standards) of a new institutional model. As described earlier, despite their importance, such activities were rarely observed in the Bel<sup>fi</sup>n case. We also observed that mimicry {7}, theorizing {8}, and educating {8} were very sporadic throughout the transformation initiative at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n. As mentioned earlier, the main purpose of these forms of institutional work is to modify the cognitive schemes in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the actors’ actions by providing legitimacy of action, by establishing a shared vocabulary and by diffusing the knowledge needed for concerted action [25].

In such a context, it is not surprising that confrontations occurred between individuals who operated under different logics, cultural, or moral foundations and visions. Their respective logics grew out of their personal stories, life experiences and other factors. These allowed key actors to envision an archetype of the IT function that then directed their institutional work. This highlights how con<sup>fl</sup>icting visions can emerge from the actors, directly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the nature of the observed change and the path it can take [14]. In short, the importance given to institution-creating actions (in particular, advocacy and de<sup>fi</sup>ning) suggest the absence of foundations shared by the actors – foundations that would otherwise have led them to work together on creating moral foundations and, from there, to work on maintaining a wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned IT archetype. The continuing advocacy clearly suggests that the key actors did not have a shared vision of what the IT function should be and, as a result, were trying to in<sup>fl</sup>uence other actors to adopt their personal visions. These observations match lessons drawn from Schein [46], which states that beliefs or underlying assumptions determine the behavior of individuals and directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence observed structures or artefacts. In light of the above, we make the following proposition:

In order to ensure a successful transformation of the IT function, institutional actors must engage in actions that not only reflect political work aimed at reconstructing rules, but also build the normative model of the new archetype and alter the cognitive reasoning of organizational members. (Proposition #3)

Institutional work aimed at maintaining an institution has received very little attention in previous research [47,26]. Yet, the issue of how institutions are maintained by actors is an important one [28]. Fig. 1 shows the enabling work {10} that aimed at facilitating and supporting the new ISD structure at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n. For instance, weekly coordination meetings were institutionalized toward the end of Y2 and a new project management methodology based on PMBOK practices was implemented in Y3. However, our case analysis reveals that creating new rules that support the creation of a new IT archetype is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Indeed, the limited institutional work aimed at ensuring adherence or compliance to these rules through enforcement, auditing and monitoring {11} and establishing coercive barriers to institutional change {12} somehow prevented the stabilization of the newly created IT function archetype at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n. Previous research reveals that when they are performed, policing and deterring actions strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of standards and meaning, which act as a mechanism that uni<sup>fi</sup>es change efforts [26]. A similar pattern was observed with regard to institutional work aimed at reproducing existing standards and systems of thinking (i.e., normative pillar). Indeed, as shown in Fig. 1, only embedding and routinizing actions {15} were performed by key actors until Y3. For their part, valorizing {13} and mythologizing {14} actions, which provide discursive resources, were mainly used by Mark in the initial stage of the transformation process with the intent to maintain the System Provider archetype (not the new one) in place. Overall, the absence of continuous normative actions throughout the transformation process, especially embedding and routinizing actions {15}, prevented the stabilization and institutionalization of the envisioned IT function archetype at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n. In light of the above, we make the following proposition:

In order to maintain a newly created IT function archetype, institutional work aimed at ensuring adherence or compliance to new rules and modes of functioning as well as infusing the normative foundations of the new profile into the change targets’ day to day routines and work practices must be continuously performed. (Proposition #4)

The study of the de-institutionalization process in the organizational theory is not very advanced and, beyond propositions made by Oliver [37], the extant literature provides very little evidence of disruptive practices. This type of work requires actors who can quickly understand an organizational context and act in counter-cultural environments, who are able to take original approaches in order to change things, even when under pressure from different governance mechanisms that are already institutionalized [25]. Thus, during an IT function’s transformation initiative, institutional work aimed at undermining the mechanisms that lead organizational members to comply with the new archetype that can only be performed by actors who know the IT function very well, who master the organization’s rules and culture and who will not be directly affected by the organization’s governance mechanisms. Despite the presence of such actors (John and Katherine) at Bel<sup>fi</sup>n, we virtually did not observe this particular type of institutional work. Instead, key actors wrongly believed that the Systems Provider archetype could be disrupted through the gradual development of a new pro<sup>fi</sup>le. In line with the unfreezing stage of Lewin’s [29] change model, we believe that deinstitutionalization must be conceived as a distinct process and, hence, the disruption of IT function archetypes shall involve institutional work that is distinct from that associated with the creation of new institutions. In light of the above, we propose the following:

The successful transformation of the IT function profile in organizations requires that institutional work aimed at deinstitutionalizing or disrupting the existing archetype must either precede or be conducted in parallel to actions and practices aimed at creating the new profile. (Proposition #5)

## 5.7. Summary, limitations and suggestions for future research

Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>eld observations lead us to conclude that Bel<sup>fi</sup>n did not entirely implement the IT function archetype envisioned in Y1. At the outset, the IT function re<sup>fl</sup>ected the System Provider archetype and there was a strong desire to transform it into the Architecture Builder archetype. Ultimately, this transformation was not completed as expected as the IT function had characteristics of various archetypes. Our in-depth case analysis underscores the importance of the role played by institutional work by showing that key actors did not take the speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions needed to disrupt the existing IT function pro<sup>fi</sup>le and create and maintain the envisioned archetype. Indeed, the conceptual lens adopted in this study allows us to understand the importance of taking action in the different categories of institutional work within each of the three pillars in order to create and maintain new institutions. Our observations support the three-stage model of change proposed by Lewin [29] – unfreeze, change, and refreeze – but allow us to go further by showing how key actors perform actions in their day-today work to change and then maintain rules, normative structures and meanings. At Bel<sup>fi</sup>n, the absence of a shared vision of the new IT archetype combined with the absence of certain types of institutional work, including work aimed at disrupting the current pro<sup>fi</sup>le, appeared to have prevented the successful transformation of the IT function archetype. These factors stood in the way of reproducing and strengthening the new pro<sup>fi</sup>le and, in the process, stabilizing it over time.

Even though the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this exploratory case study are not generalizable, they could serve as a basis for comparison when studying other cases. Indeed, although we adopted a rigorous methodological approach, had access to rich and longitudinal data and con<sup>fi</sup>dent about our case analysis and interpretation of the results, additional case studies must be conducted to increase the external validity of the theoretical propositions developed here. Among others, our observations suggest that it may be necessary to accomplish certain types of institutional work before others can perform, pointing toward a stage model of IT function transformation in organizations. Our theoretical propositions would bene<sup>fi</sup>t not only from being validated in other settings and industries but also to be tested against both “successful” and “unsuccessful” transformations of IT functions. Importantly, although we conducted multiple data collection processes over a period of 5 years, real-time data gathering during the entire transformation process was not possible for pragmatic reasons of time and resources. Recognizing that limitation, two tactics were adopted to increase construct validity, namely, reconstruction of events using multiple respondents and key informants reviewing the preliminary version of our report. It is also important to note that our case analysis and <sup>fi</sup>ndings only hold good when the phenomenon under investigation is situated within an institutional theory view. For instance, the adoption of a “dialectical” view [54], which encourages the study of issues such as power, politics and con<sup>fl</sup>icts, would have certainly led to a different set of theoretical propositions. To conclude, as presently constituted our propositions are at least one step short of theory formation. At minimum, they represent what Merton [32] calls empirical generalizations, that is, they summarize observed uniformities of relationships between various forms of institutional work and the dynamic and outcomes of the transformation process. At best, they suggest a rudimentary process model of IT function transformation. To the extent that the results presented in this article are valid and can be supported by the data from future case studies, we believe a solid and realistic theory of IT function transformation in organizations can be built.

## 5.8. Contributions to research and practice

Our study contributes to both theory and practice. With regard to theory, our <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution lies in the juxtaposition of institutional theory with the IS literature. In this study, we applied the recommendations of Mignerat and Rivard as well as Curry and her colleagues related to the use of institutional approach in our <sup>fi</sup>eld by: a) using a longitudinal design to study an institutional phenomenon, when most IS studies use a crosssectional design that is not necessarily relevant for institutional phenomena; b) avoiding the temptation to see institutional pressures as deterministic, but rather to consider other key components such as the roles played by various actors in the process; c) building on new trends of institutional theory in IS; and, most importantly d) tackling a key relevant topic, the transformation of institutions in IT. Second, while the literature on institutional work in organizational contexts is merely emerging, very few studies have focused on institutional work that is not performed by key actors. Likewise, the IS literature offers numerous examples of successful transformations of the IT function, but very few cases of incomplete or “failed” ones. We concur with McGaughey [31] that the “emphasis to date on success, rather than failure, introduces a strong bias in our understanding of institutional entrepreneurship, creating a general image of typically heroic and successful action in regard to achieving intended effects” (p. 92). Yet, many attempts at transforming the IT function either completed partially or do not deliver the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. By presenting a case of partial transformation of the IT function archetype, the present study strives to <sup>fi</sup>ll an important gap in both the IS literature and the institutional theory literature.

With regard to practice, our work reorients the traditional concerns related to changes in the IT function by shifting the focus to understanding key actors and how their actions affect institutions. To this end, we have built on the theory of institutional work and relied on archetypes as a tool to formalize the vision of what should be the IT function. The use of these archetypes may help IS managers develop a clearer vision of the direction of the change they want to implement. These <sup>fi</sup>ve archetypes proposed by Guillemette and Paré [17] present the advantage of clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ning coherent choices about fundamental aspects of the IT function. Moreover, using these archetypes may assist IS managers in the communication of their vision of the new IT function, facilitating in turn the creation of a shared meaning required to ease the transformation journey. Once IS managers have clearly and transparently characterized the current and desired IT function archetypes, they can consciously engage in various types of institutional work needed to create the new management model and maintain it over time. Managers interested in the status quo will be able to <sup>fi</sup>nd concrete tactics for maintaining institutions through institutional work. Those who wish to establish a new IT function can also act on each of our research propositions by: 1) instituting a form of legitimate authority to reinforce efforts to create new rules, such as a governance committee or hierarchical authorization process; 2) ensuring that actions to create shared work practices are recognized and accepted, for instance through some form of reward or performance evaluation; and 3) ensuring that activities aiming at creating the new archetype are well advanced in each of the three pillars, perhaps through a systematic review of the efforts being made not only by managers but also by all the key actors involved in the transformation process.

## 6. Conclusion

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the role of key actors’ motives, actions and decisions in the context of the transformation of the IT function pro<sup>fi</sup>le in organizations. To this end, we developed a conceptual framework that combines elements from institutional theory with a recent typology of IT function archetypes. We conducted a longitudinal case study in a large Canadian mental health institution investigating the transformation of its IT function for >5 years. Our study suggests that all the categories of institutional work regarding rules, norms, and meaning play complementary roles, and that all appear to be necessary, at different periods during the transformation process. Overall, our in-depth investigation makes an original and important contribution to the extant literature through a deeper understanding of the institutional work performed by key actors in the creation of a new IT function archetype and by illustrating how the type of actions and practices that are performed in<sup>fl</sup>uence this process and its resulting outcomes.
