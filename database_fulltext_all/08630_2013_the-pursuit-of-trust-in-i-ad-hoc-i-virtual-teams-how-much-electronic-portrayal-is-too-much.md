RESEARCH ARTICLE

# The pursuit of trust in ad hoc virtual teams: how much electronic portrayal is too much?

Shoshana Altschuller<sup>1</sup> and Raquel Benbunan-Fich<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Systems Department, Iona College, Hagan School of Business, New Rochelle, NY, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Statistics & CIS Department, Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, CUNY, New York, NY, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Shoshana Altschuller, Information Systems Department, Iona College, Hagan School of Business, 715 North Avenue, New Rochelle, NY 10801, U.S.A. Tel: 001 914 633 2407: Fax: 001 914 633 2012; E-mail: saltschuller@iona.edu

## Abstract

This study develops and tests the concept of electronic portrayal in synchronous computer-mediated communication of ad hoc virtual teams. Electronic portrayal is the extent to which a communication system portrays the true identity of its users. A theoretical model is developed based upon which it is hypothesized that increased information available due to electronic portrayal will impact trust in ad hoc virtual teams. An experiment is conducted to test the model by manipulating the graphical identification of users of a system as well as the rehearsability of the system. Rehearsability is the extent to which users can reread and edit their messages before submitting them to the synchronous communication system. The results show that the combination of both factors – identification and rehearsability – impacts trust among team members. Specifically, partial electronic portrayal (only one form of true-to-life representation) has the most positive impact on trust. This effect is moderated by communication-related variables such as self-disclosure, impressions and virtual co-presence. The implication of these results is that too much true identity information negatively impacts trust. This research provides theoretical and practical contributions for understanding the importance of identification and rehearsability in synchronous group communication. ## Introduction

A virtual team is a geographically dispersed work group that communicates electronically and is formed to accomplish specific short-term goals (Kristof et al, 1995; Reid et al, 1996; Powell et al, 2004). The dispersion that exists in virtual teams can be described based on their spatial, temporal, and configurational characteristics (O’Leary & Cummings, 2007). Under these conditions of limited contextual information, where collaborations can potentially be strained, trust could pose as an important regulator of members’ behaviors and responses (Dirks & Ferrin, 2001). In fact, fostering trust among project team members in an organizational setting is crucial to the possibility of productive collaboration as it has been shown that trust has a positive effect on group performance (Klimoski & Karol, 1976; Dirks, 2000). In the current business environment where a global economy, ubiquitous networking, and ever-improving telecommunications systems commonly obligate physically dispersed team members to interact remotely via electronic means, the importance of facilitating trust is amplified (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). However, because of the innate distance present in a computer-mediated environment, the challenge of attaining trust is also magnified in a virtual team setting. This paper makes a contribution to research in this area by taking a specific focus on electronic portrayal and its impact on trust development in virtual teams.

While trust has been studied extensively in the virtual teams literature (Jarvenpaa et al, 1998; Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2002; Piccoli & Ives, 2003; Sarker et al, 2003; Coppola et al, 2004; Jarvenpaa et al, 2004), the influence of personal portrayal has not been addressed. The mechanisms whereby individuals present themselves are likely to impact the ensuing team interaction. Interpersonal trust among team members has been defined as ‘the extent to which a person is confident in, and willing to act on the basis of, the words, actions, and decisions of another’ (McAllister, 1995). In a team environment, this means that trust facilitates the motivation for individuals to join efforts and work well together, thereby boosting the performance level of the team (Dirks, 1999). For a team whose task is to make a joint decision, this means that trust among team members will allow them to easily accept one another’s assertions, thoughts, and experiences as valid input toward the final group decision. In a virtual team, this further means that the electronic communication media in use will play a crucial role in the way that it can convey those assertions, thoughts, and personal experiences, which will influence group output.

Classically, the capacity for interpersonal affections has been considered limited within a setting of attenuated social cues and lacking communication cues (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Sproull & Kiesler, 1986). Since trust has been described as a complex experience that derives from people’s values, attitudes, moods, and emotions (Jones & George, 1998), it was thought to be difficult to attain in a virtual environment. However, empirical evidence of online social information exchange has prompted researchers to affirm that indeed other aspects such as timing, setting and context of interaction can overcome the constraints that media characteristics present (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). Among these aspects, true identity portrayal has rarely been addressed (for an exception, see Walther et al (2001)). It is expected that the extent to which a system is able to portray its users would be essential to the development of trust because in order to build cognitive-based trust, communicants gather information about each other to help them make judgments (Sarker et al, 2003).

The extent to which the system portrays individuals is critical for virtual teams of participants who must interact with each other without ever meeting face to face. It is even more crucial in the case of ad hoc virtual teams who have never worked together before. As newly formed groups, they have no prior information base upon which to formulate the cognitive decision of whether or not to trust. To gain information as inputs to the decision, communicants must look at each others’ messages, skills, and appearances or behaviors to determine the ability, benevolence, and integrity of their teammates. In ad hoc virtual teams, these cues can be transmitted only via the electronic representation of the communicants. Therefore, Electronic portrayal (EP), the ability of a system to expose a user’s ‘true identity’ and portray it to the group, would have profound effects upon the way team members perceive each other and interact.

Although it has never been studied before, electronic portrayal is closely related to the concept of presence which has been described as the ‘subjective feeling of existence in an experienced environment’ (Nicovich et al, 2005). Presence is a broad concept that has relevance in many different areas including virtual reality simulations (Steuer, 1992) and blogging (Panteli et al, 2011), as well as virtual team interactions (Panteli, 2004; Bente et al, 2008). Consequently presence has been conceptualized in a number of different forms (Lombard & Ditton, 1997; Panteli et al, 2011) including physical presence, self presence, and social presence (Lee, 2004). Since ‘socially realistic experiences are more likely to evoke a sense of presence’ (Lombard & Ditton, 1997), the social dimension of presence and true-identity portrayal are closely intertwined. Therefore, ‘presence’ in this research refers to the psychological perception that social counterparts in two-way virtual exchanges are real (Lee, 2004). Electronic portrayal, in these terms, is the ability of a communication system to convey this presence of realistic or true identity.

Studies have shown that more presence leads to better quality task performance and psychological connection (Nicovich et al, 2005; Wrench & Punyanunt-Carter, 2007). The question addressed by this research is whether the ability to fully portray a person’s true identity electronically leads to the most beneficial level of presence, or if, perhaps, it suggests a point on a continuum where the benefits of presence begin to become attenuated and are replaced with detrimental effects on group work and team member relationships. Because portrayal of identity is an aspect of interaction that is integrally intertwined with media characteristics, the current research focuses on electronic portrayal, seeking to discover how a communication medium affects the portrayal of an individual’s identity for the purpose of fostering or discouraging a trusting relationship.

The primary focus of the current research is to investigate the extent to which changes in the characteristics of a synchronous computer-mediated communication (CMC) system related to electronic portrayal affect trust in groups using the system. In other words, will ad hoc virtual teams communicating synchronously using various modes of electronic portrayal in their online discussions differ in their level of trust? Further, which aspects of the communication process act as moderators influencing the impact of the communication medium on trust?

The description of this investigation will progress as follows. First, we present the theoretical background and the development of hypotheses based on existing literature. We next describe the methodology and experimental procedure that was used to test these hypotheses. Presentation of the results of the data analysis is then followed by a discussion and conclusions, including limitations of the study, contributions, and practical implications of this research, and future research directions.

## Theoretical background and hypothesis development

A classic distinction in the literature is cognition-based trust and affect-based trust (Lewis & Weigert, 1985). Cognition-based trust refers to the ‘calculative and rational characteristics demonstrated by trustees’, while affect-based trust ‘involves emotional elements and social skills’ such as care and concern for the other parties. In a temporary work group, focus tends to be a professional task-oriented one, rather than social (Hiltz et al, 1986; Meyerson et al, 1996) and therefore the dominating type of trust is cognitive. Cognitive trust – the conscious choice, based on available information, of whether or not to trust – relies on information and develops through communications among members (McKnight et al, 1988; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2002; Sarker et al, 2003).

Seminal work in sociology indicates that in face-to-face encounters, individuals seek to acquire information about each other to determine expectations and whether or not they may trust each other (Goffman, 1959). In ad hoc acquaintances, this information is gathered based on clues from a person’s conduct and appearance allowing people to apply their previous experience with similar individuals or decide whether they can rely on what the individual says about himself. In electronic communication, trust is developed based on information that is gathered via a system’s electronic portrayal of its users. CMC systems offer ways to transmit information about a person’s appearance (personal representation) and aspects of his/her conduct (related to message formulation).

Clearly, CMC is different from face-to-face communication in its reduced ability to provide the complete set of information that describes the communication environment. In a face-to-face environment communicants can use their senses to obtain information about the communication environment including non-verbal information about their communication partners. However, in a virtual environment communicants are limited to the information that the communication system is able to convey. Communication in a mediated environment that hides some information about its users has actually been found to benefit group work in some ways such as increased task-focus and reduced evaluation apprehension (Nunamaker et al, 1991; Gallupe et al, 1992). Still, CMC researchers have tried to understand the impact of a mediated communication environment on the communication process and outcomes. For example, research studies have shown the reduction in non-verbal cues to impact communicants’ behavior and perceptions (Sproull & Kiesler, 1986; Sussman & Sproull, 1999). More generally, the study of presence in a mediated environment looks at the ‘perceptual illusion of nonmediation (Lombard & Ditton, 1997) or to what extent communicants perceive their environment and social interactions as real rather than mediated.

In fact, the extent to which information about the ‘real’ communication environment is hidden or revealed is relevant to a number of theories and concepts regarding virtual team interactions. Most relevant are the following two concepts. First, reduced non-verbal cues are cited as the cause for Walther’s hyperpersonal model where virtual communicants’ impressions of one another become idealized (Walther, 1997). Categorical information processing, over-attributions on minimal social cues, and idealization of the communication partners in CMC groups are common because in the absence of individuating cues about others, people build stereotypical impressions based on the limited information they have (Lea & Spears, 1992; Walther, 1997). As described by Jarvenpaa et al (1998), idealization of communication partners is a common occurrence because group member tend to ‘assume similarity and tend to reveal factors and cues about themselves that only reinforce this similarity’. Second, presence has been conceptualized as ‘the extent to which a media portrayal is plausible or “true to life” in that it reflects events that do or could occur in the nonmediated world’ (Lombard & Ditton, 1997), or how ‘realistic’ the communication environment and its participants are. For example, ‘the degree to which we as individuals perceive another as a real person impacts the extent to which we feel their presence (Wood & Smith, 2001). (Classically, CMC studies have looked at this concept from the perspective of social presence theory which focuses on how virtual communicants interact based on the non-verbal cues in various media. However. to accommodate the evolution of virtual environments, more current CMC research focuses on a more general conceptualization of presence of which ‘social richness’ is only a part. For a detailed discussion see Lombard & Ditton (1997), Wrench & Punyanunt-Carter (2007), and Walther (2011a).) Further, presence has been associated with intimacy and psychological closeness between virtual communicants (Lombard & Ditton, 1997).

Although computer mediation has been said to attenuate most social context cues (age, appearance, rank, etc.) that are available in a face-to-face environment, a computer-mediated session in which continuity of interaction is essential often provides means for identifying who the participants are so that a meaningful conversation can ensue. Consequently, different communication media encapsulate and portray the identity of their users in different ways. The extent to which all aspects of communicants’ identities (including how they appear and how they behave) are portrayed by the system (electronic portrayal) will determine the strength of the impact of presence and non-verbal cue reduction on their perceptions and interactions.

Prior literature in virtual teams has examined electronic portrayal primarily from the perspective of complete anonymity vs total identification via names and more recently photos. However, in addition to the means of personal identification (or lack thereof), CMC allows members to gather information about each other based on the process of message formulation. Message creation is affected by the level of rehearsability afforded by the system. Rehearsability is the extent to which a communication system allows users to reread and edit their messages before submitting them to be processed by the system and shown to the receivers (Dennis et al, 2008). Typically, synchronous systems are said to provide low rehearsability because they do not give the user much opportunity to reread or edit his message before submitting it. In contrast, asynchronous systems are considered to provide high rehearsability as they allow users to edit their messages, proofread them to remove grammar and spelling errors, and reflect deeper on their contributions to the group. Within synchronous systems, a high rehearsability synchronous setting would enable users to create and edit their messages privately and submit only the final product to the group. In contrast, a low rehearsability synchronous setting would show the rest of the team every keystroke, hesitation and change that a user makes as he types and refines his messages. In this case, since the team is privy to the process of message creation by each of its members, there are additional nonverbal cues that provide sources to gather information about each other for inclusion in the trust equation. For example, typing speed and number of typos can portray a communicant’s fluency or expertise, influencing the teams’ perception of him.

Communication systems transmit information about a person’s appearance (personal electronic portrayal) and aspects of his/her conduct (related to message formulation). Therefore, the way that people are portrayed in an online environment is largely based upon two aspects of their online participation: ‘who they are’ or their Personal Electronic Portrayal, and ‘how they formulate messages’ or their Message-Based Electronic Portrayal. We propose that these two forms of representation comprise the dimensions of electronic portrayal. We further conceptualize electronic portrayal in terms of how close these two dimensions are to their true appearance in real life (Schroeder, 2002, 2006). The extent to which these dimensions of electronic portrayal are ‘true-to-life’ will confound the results of both presence and the hyperpersonal model in terms of the perceived closeness and idealization that they induce, and their corresponding impacts on trust within a virtual team.

## Electronic portrayal continuum

At first glance, the concept of presence and the hyperpersonal model seems to be at odds regarding the impact of increased electronic portrayal on trust. The concept of presence suggests that increased realism of communication creates the psychological impression that virtual teammates are physically present (Short et al, 1976; Lombard & Ditton, 1997; Wood & Smith, 2001). Therefore, in a scenario of increased electronic portrayal we might conclude that because the system is portraying more true information about the communicants, people feel closer to one another and are more likely to trust each other. In contrast, Walther’s hyperpersonal model (Walther, 1996) builds upon Lea & Spears’ (1992) SIDE (Social Identification/Deindividuation Effect) theory and favors an alternative approach. The SIDE theory states that people tend to categorize themselves as either similar or dissimilar to the team based on the impressions formed using limited non-verbal cues. The feelings of similarity with others are reinforced and then contribute to their willingness to cooperate and trust (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). However, as more information about individuals is available, this effect is lessened (Walther, 1997). By becoming aware of individual differences as they gain more information about each other, people are less prone to making stereotypical impressions that influence them to trust. Thus, increased portrayal is not beneficial because it provides additional information that dispels the positive impressions and idealizations resulting from limited context cues. Accordingly, we might expect that trust will decrease as a result of increasing the amount of true identity information that is available about the communicants via electronic portrayal.

In order to reconcile this contradiction, we combine the two ideas and differentiate between low, partial, and high electronic portrayal (Figure 1). Since the realism of presence and the idealization of the hyperpersonal model are both important to the development of trust, we hypothesize that the ideal setting for trust development is that of partial electronic portrayal, where there is at least one form of true-to-life representation and at least one that is less true-to-life. Given some true-to-life non-verbal information to help communicants feel that their partners are really there (as suggested by the conceptualization of presence) in combination with some concealed non-verbal information to preserve the ability to idealize (as indicated by Walther’s hyperpersonal model), trust can actually thrive. Therefore,

## H1: Participants communicating with partial electronic portrayal (only one true-to-life form of representation) will report more trust than participants communicating with low or with high electronic portrayal.

In ad hoc groups, where team members know little about their counterparts other than what they can glean through their electronic communication, electronic portrayal becomes crucial in that it is the only means by which communicants can form judgments. In this light, our first hypothesis predicts that variation in both of a system’s electronic portrayal dimensions – personal electronic portrayal and message-based electronic portrayal – will have an impact on the trust of members of teams using those systems. We expect that participants trust will be higher for groups operating under partial electronic portrayal than for those working with either of the extremes – low or high electronic portrayal. In other words, we predict that there is a continuum associated with electronic portrayal, as suggested above. In this continuum, the relationship between electronic portrayal and trust is positive until such time as too much identifying information begins to have a detrimental effect on participants’ trust of their team members.

The electronic portrayal continuum describes a direct relationship between a communication medium’s portrayal characteristics and trust outcomes via a combination of presence and the hyperpersonal model. However, as mentioned, cognitive trust results from communication among members and relies on the information that they learn and/or assume throughout the course of an interaction. Therefore, the electronic portrayal continuum’s effect on trust is influenced by the communication process.

In order to identify the communication processes that are most influential, we examine in greater detail the models associated with the electronic portrayal continuum and the phenomena that they provoke within a communication to impact trust. The hyperpersonal model states that because of limited cues, virtual teammates assume similarity (SIDE Theory) and behave in a way that supports trust (Walther, 1996), tending ‘to reveal factors and cues about themselves that only reinforce this similarity’ in the course of the interaction (Jarvenpaa et al, 1998, pp. 32–33). Suppression of some true-to-life information supports this behavior by maintaining the opportunity for participants to idealize the missing facts about their partners and disclose of themselves accordingly. Thus, the impressions formed and the self-disclosure that results both emerge in an exaggeratedly positive fashion, promoting trust. At the same time, achieving the benefits of presence calls for the transmission of enough real communication information to promote the awareness of self and others, and the perceptions of co-presence in a virtual medium. Awareness and virtual co-presence perceptions developed during the process of team interaction are also necessary for achieving trust.

From our review of applicable models, four factors (self-disclosure, positive impression formation, public self-awareness and virtual co-presence) influence the level of trust when communicants are portrayed in an electronic medium. As we describe below in detail, the existence of each of these communication process constructs during an interaction is likely to further impact the level of trust created by electronic portrayal.

## Self-disclosure

Computer-mediation seems to be an ideal forum for voluntary disclosure of personal information (self-disclosure). Some studies indicate that people voluntarily disclose personal information more readily in a computer-based medium (Kiesler & Sproull, 1986; Weisband & Kiesler, 1996; Sussman & Sproull, 1999) and suggest that users are more likely to feel the freedom to self-disclose to one another using computers (Weisband & Reinig, 1995), particularly when true identities are hidden (Matheson & Zanna, 1992). In particular, the use of CMC fosters an increased level of self-disclosure compared with telephone and face-to-face communication (Joinson, 2001; Tidwell & Walther, 2002). This phenomenon is attributed to ‘reduced evaluation anxiety, feelings of safety or invulnerability, and less concern with looking good’ (Weisband & Kiesler, 1996).

Closely linked with trust (Wheeless & Grotz, 1977), self-disclosure is one of the communication process constructs that impacts the development of trust in ad hoc virtual teams. In the psychology literature, self-disclosure has been shown to promote reciprocal trust (Miller, 2002; Turner et al, 2007). Because people feel trusted when they are disclosed to, they trust and disclose in kind, causing an escalation in mutual trust. This phenomenon has been studied within computer-mediated relationships with similar results. In online relationships, self-disclosure has proven to display a positive association with trust among Americans (Yum & Hara, 2006) and has been described as one of the foundations of trust in online friendships (Henderson & Gilding, 2004).

In this light, we expect that when self-disclosure occurs during the interaction of ad hoc virtual teams, trust is positively impacted. The open communication environment that is created by high levels of voluntary self-disclosure contributes to an ideal setting for trust development. Therefore, a group setting that contains self-disclosure is one where partial electronic portrayal is allowed to have the greatest impact on trust. Since selfdisclosure is expected to positively influence the relationship between electronic portrayal and trust within the teams, we predict that,

H2: The influence of electronic portrayal on trust will be moderated by self-disclosure, such that the effect will be stronger for high self-disclosure.

## Positive impression formation

Another experience that is common within the interactions of ad hoc teams is impression formation, where the impressions formed based upon the appearances and communication behaviors of team members during the discussion process prove to be crucial to the development of trust (Panteli & Duncan, 2004). In fact, impression formation has been acknowledged as a cornerstone of social cognitive processes (Walther, 1993; Jacobson, 1999; Tidwell & Walther, 2002), such as developing trust.

In ad hoc teams that communicate electronically, impression formation plays an especially important role. Given the amount and type of information that is often absent in virtual communications (i.e, appearance, age, gender, voice, intonations, hesitations, etc.) (Sproull & Kiesler, 1986), communicants are forced to make assumptions based on the limited information that they have (Walther, 1997). This is why, as discussed above, in CMC reduced non-verbal cues have caused virtual communicants’ impressions of one another to become exaggerated. In cases where the available communication and non-verbal cues convey comforting characteristics such as enthusiasm and trustworthiness, these impressions have the potential to intensify as communicants fill in the blanks with equally positive assumptions. These idealizations contribute to a setting that is conducive to trust in which partial electronic portrayal’s full effects can become realized. Therefore, when positive impression formation exists to a great extent the relationship between partial electronic portrayal and trust is positively impacted. Hence, it is appropriate to expect that:

H3: The influence of electronic portrayal on trust will be moderated by positive impression formation, such that the effect will be stronger for high levels of positive impressions.

In addition to the above interaction experiences, members of ad hoc virtual teams are subject to certain perceptions of themselves and their teammates due to the nature of the communication medium and the transient history of the team.

## Public self-awareness

In CMC, the inability to identify the originator of the messages, and the corresponding freedom to create messages that can be posted without personal identification, has been linked to lower public self-awareness (attention to oneself as a social object) (Pinsonneault & Heppel, 1998). However, when a medium is able to relay identifying information, public self-awareness is experienced by communicants. Given the level of electronic portrayal, team members gain additional information about their counterparts and become aware of the information that is available about them; therefore, they experience a shift in their self-focus (Pinsonneault & Heppel, 1998).

Virtual team participants who experience low levels of public self-awareness are not focused on their outward appearance and consequently do not seek to build a relationship with their teammates. However, when communicants are aware of themselves as social objects, they are apt to develop social relationships with their counterparts, which leads them to trust their teammates. Hence it is appropriate to predict the following:

H4: The influence of electronic portrayal on trust will be moderated by public self-awareness, such that the effect will be stronger for high public self-awareness.

## Virtual co-presence

The outcomes of ad hoc team collaboration are also affected by the extent to which users experience copresence, where communication partners are consistently available to one another (Goffman, 1959). For dispersed groups in an electronic setting this translates into the psychological perception that others are present or physically collocated (Short et al, 1976; Daft & Lengel, 1986; Rice, 1987). More specifically, virtual co-presence, defined as the ‘subjective feeling of being together with others in a virtual environment’, conveys this perception (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). The dynamics of the virtual interaction often cultivate virtual co-presence. For example, the speed of interaction and the ability to track what people are doing in real time (Nardi et al, 2000; Ma & Agarwal, 2007) may promote users’ sense of virtual co-presence.

Recent research regarding the impact of presence on trust is in the context of human-computer interaction (Gefen & Straub, 2004), where increasing the levels of social presence on websites and other online interactions has been a major focus (de Vries, 2006). In collaborative contexts, where there is interpersonal interaction, virtual co-presence is the dimension of presence that is important. Whereas presence connotes ‘being there’, co-presence describes ‘being there together’ in what is perceived as a shared space (Lombard & Ditton, 1997; Schroeder, 2006). Increased feelings of co-presence change the way virtual communicants view themselves in relation to their teammates thereby affecting the level of trust between them. Virtual co-presence not only creates a ‘sense of connection’ (Nardi et al, 2000) and ‘closer ties’ (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2005) among team members, it also is closely related and is a prerequisite for interpersonal trust (Cyr et al, 2007; Bente et al, 2008). Therefore, virtual co-presence will positively influence interpersonal trust in virtual team interactions. Furthermore, a virtual team communication setting that is marked by high levels of virtual copresence is one that will maximize the levels of trust generated by partial electronic portrayal. Consequently, we expect that,

H5: The influence of electronic portrayal on trust will be moderated by virtual co-presence, such that the effect will be stronger for high levels of virtual co-presence.

## Methodology

A controlled experiment was conducted to identify the effects of electronic portrayal on trust within group meetings. It follows a 2 - 2 factorial design (Table 1) where the treatments represent two forms of personal electronic portrayal crossed with two types of messagebased electronic portrayal. Each form of portrayal was achieved by controlling the corresponding way that subjects were represented by the system, as follows.

## Personal representation

A communication system provides textual and graphical means to represent the identity of its users. Textual representation refers to the user name or handle which could be the user’s real name, a computer-generated one, or a user chosen screen name. Graphical representation of users often comes in the form of avatars that are either imported by the user or chosen from a selection of possible avatars, sometimes customizable images of people, animals, or cartoons. The chosen form of personal representation can be characterized based upon the extent to which it is ‘true-to-life’. For example, a photograph is a more ‘true-to-life’ personal representation than a fictional avatar. Avatars frequently gain special meaning within a virtual community as a symbol of identity, which users are sometimes able to manipulate with the ability to modify the appearance of the avatar (Kang & Yang, 2006). When controlled by the user, the avatar is a powerful tool for expression of self, or deception thereof (Holtjona & Nah, 2007). It is therefore important to understand the effects that communicating with fictional visual representation has on communication as some researchers have done (Kolko, 1999; Lee et al, 2005).

In this study, the personal representation factor was manipulated at two levels. While some users were represented by a fictional avatar, others were represented by a photograph of themselves. Avatar-identified participants were represented with a picture of a static object (such as a snowflake) that was gender-neutral. In all cases, users were identified with a non-descriptive and generic username chosen by the researchers.

## Message-based representation

In addition to providing ways to identify who the parti cipants are, a system has the ability to present the way the users communicate. While content, tone, and frequency of messages are determined by the user, a system can help portray its users in terms of the way they are creating their messages. This is often manifested in the level of rehearsability afforded by the system. Message-based representation is more ‘true-to-life’ when implemented with a low level of rehearsability than with a high level because the message formulation process is visible to others. For example, communicants are aware of any hesitation or correction made during message formulation as well as the speed of that process.

In this study, the message-based representation factor consists of two different levels of communication based upon the amount of rehearsability provided by the system: low and high. Specifically, a high rehearsability system is a synchronous communication (chat) system where users type one entire message at a time before submitting it to the group. In this case, users could spend a certain amount of time revising their messages before sending them to their counterparts. On the other hand, the low rehearsability system is comparable to the UNIX chat application (Talk) where users could see their partners’ letters appearing on the screen as they are being typed. In this treatment, all members of the conversation are privy to every keystroke, indicating any hesitation or correction.

## Procedures

Participants were recruited from the population of students at a large urban university and were offered a 10-dollar incentive for 1 h of their time. Subjects who volunteered were gathered in a campus laboratory and were randomly organized into groups of three members and each group was randomly assigned to an experimental condition. Each group of participants worked together to solve an ethical decision-making task for which there is no demonstrably correct solution. In a business environment, this type of task is prevalent and reliant upon the level of trust within a group.

The laboratory where the experiment took place was a computer laboratory specially outfitted with the chat software. Although the students were collocated, each individual was assigned to a computer as he walked into the lab. The workstations were all around the perimeter of the room facing the laboratory walls, and were separated from each other by cubicle-like partitions to prevent physical interaction. The task procedure was run in numerous sessions each of which included several groups working simultaneously so that it was not clear who the team members were. In fact, some participants indicated after completing the task that they were not aware that the people they were interacting with were even in the same room.

Participants were asked, as a group, to use the given synchronous communication system to discuss the assigned case and produce a written recommendation. The case presents the situation of a fictional character who is deciding whether or not he should download digital music through unapproved channels. For the actual text of the case see Appendix A.

The software employed for this task was ICQ Pro 2003b, which has the option to turn the rehearsability feature on or off by switching the layout of a real-time chat screen between Vertical/Horizontal/Split Layout (low rehearsability) and IRC-Style Layout (high rehearsability). When the IRC-style layout is chosen, the user types his message into a text box at the bottom of the screen and presses ENTER when he is ready to submit the message for the rest of the group to view. This provides for a higher level of rehearsability because the user can privately make any changes to the message, which is not shared until he makes the conscious decision that it is ready. On the other hand, when the split layout is chosen, the screen is divided into three parts. As each group member types, his keystrokes are displayed in the part designated with his user name. A low level of rehearsability is achieved this way because each participant can view the messages as they are being formulated keystroke by keystroke. There is no opportunity for a participant to go back and revise the message without his teammates seeing. In addition, temporal aspects of message formulation such as typing speed and hesitations are revealed to the entire group.

Subjects were presented with the avatars or photographs of the members of their group (depending on the personal representation manipulation) and then given explicit instructions for how to begin the chat session according to their rehearsability manipulation. See Appendix B for sample group pages for the two types of personal representation. Photo representation was done via actual photos of the participants that were collected in advance of the session. To maintain uniformity, all photos were screened as casual snapshots prior to the session.

After completing the task, participants were directed to an online questionnaire where they answered questions about their experience during the task. Outcomes of group work were measured in terms of trust among team members and individual perceptions of the process. Control variables captured in a pre-task questionnaire include demographics, usage of and comfort with synchronous communication, and propensity to trust. All survey items are based on 7-point Likert or semantic differential scales. Appendix C shows the items that measured each of the constructs in the post-task questionnaire.

## Data analysis and results

We successfully recruited 87 groups of three to discuss the case electronically and write recommendations. Owing to logistics issues (no-shows), a few groups ended up with two members instead of three. The final sample size consists of 251 usable individual observations for testing the hypotheses. These observations are approximately equally distributed among the four conditions (see Table 2).

The sample has a demographic distribution similar to that of the college population that it was taken from, with 50% of each gender and a large percentage of undergraduates ages 18–24. For detailed demographic information, see Table 3. To check for possible confounding effects, we conducted ANOVAs on these individual characteristics across conditions and did not detect any systematic variations.

Even though the survey items for the constructs measured here are adapted from previously validated surveys, we conducted a factor analysis with varimax rotation with all the items included in the post-test questionnaire. The number of factors was not specified in advance. Our factor analysis resulted in usable scales for all of the constructs that we set out to measure. (See Appendix D for the factor loadings.) Each of the scales was then tested for internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha (see Table 4). Only the alpha coefficient of the three items for self-disclosure is below 0.6, which is typically considered the threshold for exploratory research. This coefficient suggests that the items have relatively low internal consistency. Given this result, we further investigated the dimensionality of the self-disclosure scale via confirmatory factor analysis of the three items and found that they all load on a single factor.

The implication of the low reliability of the selfdisclosure scale is that high measurement error is likely to attenuate the correlations between this and other variables. However, ‘[w]hen a measure has other desirable properties, such as meaningful content coverage of some domain and reasonable unidimensionality, this low reliability may not be a major impediment to its use (Schmitt, 1996). Nevertheless, the decision to use a low reliability measure should include caveats about the potential for underestimating the underlying relationships and the interpretations of such relationships.

Trust is an individual-level variable but participants were nested in groups when they received the treatment. Therefore, Hierarchical ANOVAs were conducted to compare individual trust among experimental conditions while taking into account the effects of the group (Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich, 2005). See Walczuch & Watson (2001) for further details about HANOVA models to control for group effects. Using HANOVA, the model for trust is significant at 0.1% (F ¼ 1.75, P-value of 0.001) and explains 48% of the variance. The presence of neither photos nor keystrokes alone produces significant differences in the trust means. However, as expected, the interaction between the factors is significant at the 1% level when the group is used as a separate factor. This significant interaction indicates that the combination of representation and rehearsability modes is critical.

The differences in the trust means among treatments are shown in Table 5. Examining these means, we note that trust is at its lowest level (4.39) when both personal and messaging-based representations are in their less true-to-life states (avatar and no visible keystrokes). As expected, trust increases with the addition of only the ability to see keystrokes (4.86) or with the addition of only a photo (4.82). However, when both dimensions of electronic portrayal are present in true-to-life forms, trust is once again at a lower level (4.64). The difference between the mean of the partial electronic portrayal conditions and the extreme conditions is found (using a contrast analysis) to be significant with a P-value of 0.0071. Thus, H1 is supported.

A graph of the means shows a crossover interaction effect, portrayed in Figure 3. Users in teams with avatar representation experienced elevated levels of trust when the avatar was used in combination with visible keystrokes, as compared with those using a typical chat system with no visible keystrokes. In contrast, those whose true-to-life appearance was represented with a photo experienced decreased levels of inter-team trust when the photo was used in combination with visible keystrokes, as compared with no visible keystrokes. Therefore, the interaction between the two electronic portrayal dimensions has a significant impact on trust, as predicted in the electronic portrayal continuum.

To test the moderator effect hypotheses, H2-H5, we first defined Partial Electronic Portrayal (PEP) as a dichotomous variable, with the value of 1 when there is only one trueto-life form of representation (i.e. photo/no keystrokes and avatar/keystrokes condition), and 0 otherwise, as it is the case for low PEP (avatar/no keystrokes) and high PEP (photo/keystrokes). With this alternative conceptualization of the independent variables, our four initial conditions are examined in terms of whether they provide Partial Electronic Portrayal or not. For each hypothesized moderator, we computed the $R ^ { 2 }$ of a model containing only PEP and the moderator as predictors (Model 1), and then computed the $R ^ { 2 }$ of a model with an additional interaction term to account for the effect of the moderator (Model 2). Each of these models used a HANOVA specification to control for group effects. Moreover, to address concerns of multicollinearity, we centered each moderator around its mean. For each moderator, we determined whether the $\Delta R ^ { 2 }$ was significant using the F-statistic test described in Carte & Russell (2003). The results shown in Table 6 indicate that a moderation effect does exist for three out of the four proposed moderators. Since the F-statistic for public self-awareness is less than one, the $\Delta R ^ { 2 }$ is not significant and public selfawareness cannot be described as a moderator (Carte & Russell, 2003). Therefore, H4 cannot be supported.

To test the other moderator hypotheses (H2, H3, and H5), we analyzed the nature of the found moderation effect. We dichotomized the three centered moderators (self-disclosure, impression, and virtual co-presence) and examined the differences between the trust means of the partial electronic portrayal conditions when the moderator was at a low level vs when it was at a high level. The comparisons shown in Table 7 indicate that all of the differences are positive and significant. These results support H2 at $p < . 0 5$ , and H3 and H5 at po0.0001.

Finally, to estimate the joint effects of all the moderators, we ran a complete HANOVA model based on the original factors (identification and rehearsability), all the moderators, and their respective interaction terms to account for the moderation effects. The results of this comprehensive model (shown in Table 8) confirm the independent analyses of moderator effects. While the moderator effect of public self-awareness is not supported, all the other variables – self-disclosure, positive impression formation, and virtual co-presence – do moderate the relationship between electronic portrayal and trust. Therefore, H2, H3, and H5 are supported.

## Discussion

The results of this research indicate that in ad hoc groups, more revealing communications are not necessarily the key to optimal outcomes, as much CMC research has surmised (Walther, 2011b). In this study, when little information about teammates is available (avatar and no keystrokes), there is too much guesswork and trust suffers. When, however, low rehearsability is included, users sense from seeing the keystrokes that there is really a person behind that fictional avatar, and the additional non-verbal information that is being transmitted helps to boost the users’ inclination to trust. In contrast, when the teammates are portrayed using real photos and they cannot see the keystrokes, they are satisfied that their teammates are real but with limited non-verbal cues being transmitted they can idealize about the messagebased information that is missing. However, when low rehearsability is added, the users realize, based on the visible message formulation process, that their teammates are not all that they were imagined to be. The system has exposed the ‘fool behind the photo’.

The findings also show that while system characteristics are influential in the development of trust, the communication processes that a team undergoes also impact that influence. Our analysis substantiated three moderators of the relationship between partial electronic portrayal and trust: voluntary disclosure of personal information, idealized impressions and feelings of virtual co-presence. These perceptions capture the dynamics and, to a certain extent, the content of team communication. As such, they are bound to amplify the effects of electronic portrayal on trust.

In particular, we can infer from the results of this study that when participants are actively engaged in communication activities that help to promote trust, the benefit of partial electronic portrayal is even more prominent. As the outcome of this study suggests, for example, voluntary disclosure of personal information during an electronic interaction provides an open and sharing environment that is further conducive to trust. When this rapport is established, partial electronic portrayal can allow trust to thrive. In addition, we can deduce that when communicants perceive that they and their peers are interacting as a unit within the same virtual environment, the advantage of partial electronic portrayal is highlighted. The existence of both virtual co-presence and public self-awareness during an interaction are signs of a close personal interrelationship that is ripe for trust development. When communicants perceive the closeness of the group and themselves as part of it, partial electronic portrayal is allowed to exhibit its full impact.

Furthermore, the joint impact of all the moderators indicates that idealization (through positive impressions) as well as realism (through virtual co-presence) coexisted during the process of team communication. We surmise that this is another reason that the effects of partial electronic portrayal on trust were accentuated. This is evidence that the premise of the electronic portrayal continuum extends beyond the modes of representation provided by the communication medium and into the dynamics of the communication process that develop as a result.

The experimental results serve to support the concepts upon which this research is predicated. It appears from the above analysis that high electronic portrayal is a state in which the level of true-to-life identifying information transmitted across the communication channel has a negative impact on interpersonal trust among team members. Partial electronic portrayal is not strong enough to produce this effect. In fact, as long as there is still some room for idealizing about the true identity of the other team members, partial true-to-life information is beneficial in terms of trust. Consequently, it seems that there is a continuum associated with electronic portrayal, as suggested above. In this continuum, the relationship between electronic portrayal and trust is positive until such time as too much identifying information begins to have a detrimental effect on trust. Ultimately, partial electronic portrayal is the ideal configuration of a communication system for the development of trust. See Figure 4 for a graphical representation of this concept based on the results of this study.

Solving the paradoxical predictions of presence and the hyperpersonal model that underlie this research, the electronic portrayal continuum asserts that both realities operate in tandem. At its core, the electronic portrayal continuum accentuates the need for the coexistence of transmission of true-to-life identifying information to achieve presence, and the suppression of other identifying information to achieve idealization, in order to create the optimal setting for trust. In other words, the electronic portrayal continuum prescribes that the communication medium, and ultimately, the interaction process include the ability to simultaneously assume and learn the identity of virtual teammates. In this study, we have seen both idealization and realism – direct consequences of how electronic portrayal conveys presence – concurrently emerge as crucial aspects of a trusting interaction. When the opportunity for idealization and the feelings of realism within the interaction match the same mix as within the media characteristics, the complete effects of the electronic portrayal continuum are achieved.

These results should be interpreted in light of the limitations of this study as they are applicable to newly formed groups where members have no prior knowledge about their teammates and no prior history of working together (zero-history teams). The implications for other kinds of teams are likely to be different. Furthermore, subjects who participated in this experiment were college students in ad hoc teams solving an ethical task relevant to their age group. Different subject pools or tasks may produce different results. As with any other experiment, some external validity was traded off in favor of higher internal validity. These caveats suggest caution in the generalization of these results to other settings and populations. Other limitations stem from the research methods and procedures. Although subjects were randomly assigned to groups and groups to experimental conditions, logistical and technical issues such as noshows and minor data collection glitches resulted in a slightly unbalanced number of subjects across conditions. In addition, the use of a specific synchronous communication software, ICQ Pro 2003b, which supported rehearsability may limit the replication of these results in other systems with different implementations of the rehearsability feature. Despite these limitations, these results provide important implications for practice and contributions for research.

## Contributions to research and practical implications

This research makes significant contributions to the literature as it is the first to conceptualize electronic portrayal in two dimensions (personal and messagebased), and theorize about their interaction and their influence on trust within virtual teams. In addition, this work solves a logical discrepancy (between presence and the hyperpersonal model) in the theoretical implications of the existence of increased descriptive information during text-based virtual communication. Furthermore, this study is the first to focus on the concept of rehearsability within synchronous systems as a manipulation of message-based portrayal, and empirically test its effects. While rehearsability has been discussed as a media characteristic within the context of the Media Synchronicity Theory (Dennis et al, 2008), to the best of our knowledge it has never been empirically tested or even related to a particular system implementation.

The results of this study also have practical implications with respect to the value of incorporating different types of identification and rehearsability into synchronous communication systems for newly formed groups. For managers and software designers alike, it is important to know the impact of various software features. In general, the rule-of-thumb to follow would be to remain aware of electronic portrayal features since excessive levels dampen the trust outcomes of an ad hoc virtual team. Since the general experimental results show that low rehearsability is in fact an important feature in synchronous CMC, it is recommended that low rehearsability be implemented into synchronous collaboration systems, at least as an option, to be used when it helps benefit the group’s level of trust and disabled if it is creating an uncomfortable or detrimental level of ‘true-to-life’ness.

## Conclusions

This study sheds light on the complex relationship between electronic ‘true identity’ portrayal and trust in ad hoc virtual teams. It is theorized that partial electronic portrayal presents the most conducive environment for attaining trust in ad hoc virtual teams. These effects are moderated by perceptions associated with the process of team communication. Future research efforts should investigate the effects of partial electronic portrayal in other research settings, and test new forms of personal and message-based identification. This research exposes the possibility that, despite the tendency in CMC research to recommend more and more communication cues to bridge the distance between communicants, the element of distance, or ‘unrealism’ that exists in CMC is one of its most important characteristics.

## The task

John is a sophomore in college and he is paying for his own education and living expenses in New York. It is customary at the office where he works part time to exchange gifts before the holidays. Strapped for cash, John is unable to spend enough money to buy everybody there a respectable gift. He feels he must conserve as much money as he possibly can to help pay for the textbooks and software that he needs for his classes. He knows that he will be getting a gift from each of his coworkers and doesn’t want to offend them by not returning the gesture. To solve his dilemma, John downloads free but copyrighted MP3 files using one of the popular peer-to-peer sites that allow users to share music among themselves. He burns a few selections for each of his coworkers onto a CD, packages it elegantly and leaves it on their desks. He knows they will appreciate his choices of music. John feels that this is his best option even though he is aware that the source is not approved by the authorities that represent the recording industry.

Based on your knowledge and personal experience, please answer the following questions:

1. Is John right or wrong in his decision to download music and give it as a gift? Why?

2. If John had approached you with his dilemma as his friend, what would you have recommended him to do in his situation (to download or not to download)? Justify your recommendation.
