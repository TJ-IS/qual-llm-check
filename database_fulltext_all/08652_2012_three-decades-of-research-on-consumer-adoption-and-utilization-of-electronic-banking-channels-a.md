## a b s t r a c t

The increased availability of electronically mediated self-service technologies in the banking industry has changed the way banks service their customers. Banking customers today can access, through a variety of different channels, sets of powerful tools which allow them to conduct analyses, make decisions and enact <sup>fi</sup>- nancial transactions via working from their home, of<sup>fi</sup>ce or elsewhere. Following practice, research into the adoption and use of electronic banking channels has grown substantially over the last three decades. However, banks seek further growth in consumer electronic banking. Prior research may not have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed all the issues involved in adoption and use and may be limited in other ways. Scholars face challenges researching this area due to fragmented <sup>fi</sup>ndings and methods over three decades of study. The aim of this paper, therefore, is to empirically determine the ‘state-of-play’ of research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. Using a systematic and comprehensive review of 247 peer-reviewed articles from key research outlets, this paper reveals theories and methods used to study adoption of electronic banking channel at the individual level. Among other things, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate domains and issues which have been well- or under-researched, conceptual frameworks and principles which have been lightly- or substantially-drawn upon, as well as research methods which have been heavily- or under-utilized. In order to advance research in electronic banking, future researchers should consider diversifying their theoretical and methodological approaches using the opportunities uncovered in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past three decades, the proliferation of new information and communication technologies within the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry has impacted the way banks service their customers. In particular, selfservice technologies have enabled banks to pursue an electronically mediated multi-channel strategy [11]. From the consumer's perspective these new technologies provide new modes of data access, analysis and decision-making regarding one's <sup>fi</sup>nancial management [41,60,69].

The <sup>fi</sup>rst self-service technologies in the <sup>fi</sup>nance sector emerged in the 1970s [83] when banks installed the <sup>fi</sup>rst automated teller machines (ATMs) [19]. This was followed by telephone banking services in the 1980s [2], and in the 1990s, with the emergence of the Internet, banks further extended their existing distribution channels by offering web-based banking applications [9,10,91,93]. Over the past decade, the proliferation of mobile technologies such as mobile phones, PDAs, and smart phones have encouraged banks to provide mobile banking applications [7,57,88].

However, usage rates suggest that banks are missing opportunities to move even more customers to electronic banking channels [23]. While each month 73% of all European banking customers use ATM machines, only 30% use Internet banking services [23,25,26]. Similarly, although most North American and Australasian retail banks offer telephone banking and mobile banking services, only 5– 10% of all consumers have used them [33].

Moving clients to e-channels is an important issue for the banking industry in regard to reducing operational costs [32,33,35]. For instance, E\*TRADE's implementation of telephone banking resulted in an estimated cost saving at \$30 million per year [35]. What is more, <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions employ e-banking channels to cross-sell <sup>fi</sup>nancial products and banks actively recommend and advertise <sup>fi</sup>nancial instruments such as investment products, saving products or credit products through e-banking channels [30].

For the consumer, electronic banking channels comprise decision support systems, because they enable individuals to make real-time <sup>fi</sup>nancial decisions conveniently independently of time and location (e.g., from home, at work or on the go) [14,70]. Electronic banking also helps consumers to decide which banking product <sup>fi</sup>ts best to their personal needs [41,60,69].

An extensive body of research has been developed to understand the adoption and use of electronic banking channels at the individual level. It is appropriate to review the existing body of knowledge in this area for two major reasons. First, practitioners should bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a literature synthesis. Many banks still “trial and error” their electronic banking applications [41]. For example, a recent report of Deutsche Bank research indicates that many German banks already incorporate social media tools into their e-banking strategies [26] — yet we found no academic research studying this topic. Based on interviews with several bank managers [41], practitioners would value summative analyses of research in this domain because it allows them to better understand consumer behavior towards innovative e-banking solutions.

Second, scholars should bene<sup>fi</sup>t from this study because we integrate research across four electronic banking channels spanning over three decades. This provides a rich picture because consumers are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by various factors when using each e-banking channel. Our analysis suggests that banks should not adopt a “one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all” strategy and thoroughly consider what drives consumers to use each e-banking channel [54]. We highlight the methodologies used and theoretical frameworks employed to study e-banking use at the individual level over the last three decades.

The goal for this paper is twofold. First, we aim to review, analyze, and synthesize the extant body of literature of the last three decades on e-banking adoption and utilization at the individual level. This helps us to characterize the development of this research stream and show where it is today. Second, and, most importantly, we provide recommendations where effort should be focused in the future and outline future research avenues.

The next section of the paper brie<sup>fl</sup>y de<sup>fi</sup>nes each electronic banking channel, followed by a discussion of the method used to gather and analyze the study data. Next, the results of the analysis are presented, discussed and synthesized. The paper concludes with a summary and recommendations for future research on the adoption of electronic banking channels.

## 2. Electronic banking channels

This research focuses on the consumer adoption and use of four electronic banking channels and their associated transaction- and decisionassistance tools: ATMs, touch-dial telephone banking, Internet banking, and mobile banking. In light of this study, adoption is understood as the initial use of e-banking [48] whereas use behavior refers to the repeated usage of electronic banking technologies [9]. A short de<sup>fi</sup>nition of each electronic banking channel is presented below.

ATM banking includes computerized telecommunication devices that allow customers of <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to directly use a secure method to access cash as well as their bank accounts [19].

Telephone banking services are computer-based keypad response or voice recognition technologies allowing customers to perform banking activities over the telephone [2]. Telephone ‘voice-to-voice conversations between banking customers and bank staff (e.g., callcenter personnel) are considered to be face-to-face branch banking, not telephone banking.

Internet banking is a banking channel that allows consumers to perform a wide range of <sup>fi</sup>nancial and non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial services through a bank's website [10,93].

Mobile banking is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a channel through which customers interact with a bank through non-voice applications such as text- or WAP-based banking services using a mobile device, such as a mobile phone or personal digital assistant (PDA) [42].

The following section describes the research method used for this study.

## 3. Research method

There is an established tradition in information systems research of examining the research literature itself to better understand the ‘state of play’ of research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, and to discern patterns in the development of the <sup>fi</sup>eld [5,74,88,89]. Having a broad portrait of the existing literature on electronic banking adoption at the individual level will facilitate future debate on theoretical and methodological research perspectives in this <sup>fi</sup>eld [5,75,81,88,98].

Thus, following the information systems research tradition, the subsequent steps were followed:

• Identifying, reviewing and analyzing the existing literature on the adoption and utilization of electronic banking applications and tools at the individual level;

• Identifying theoretical and methodological approaches commonly used to investigate the adoption and utilization of such applications and tools at the individual level;

• Identifying research gaps within the existing literature which addresses the adoption and utilization of electronic banking channels and their associated applications and tools at the individual level.

Since the adoption and utilization of electronic banking channels at the individual level have been researched across various disciplines, we anticipated that relevant articles would be spread across various disciplines such as information systems, decision support systems, electronic commerce, information technology, <sup>fi</sup>nance, management and marketing. In order to cover a broad range of journals, we explored the bibliographic databases shown in Table 1.

A keyword search was conducted on these databases in order to identify relevant papers. The search strategy included keywords such as adoption/acceptance/use of electronically mediated selfservice technologies, as well as ATMs, telephone banking, Internet banking, and mobile banking. We limited the search to peerreviewed articles more than two pages in length. This strategy circumvented book reviews, editorials, and opinion statements. Likewise, trade magazine articles and similar ‘non-scholarly’ work were not considered in the search. To cross-check and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the relevance of the results, the articles' abstracts were reviewed. The next step was to scrutinize articles found in the initial search for additional references in this <sup>fi</sup>eld [97].

Articles that did not focus on the adoption and use of electronic banking channels at the individual level (e.g., articles which concentrated on technical issues of banking transactions) were excluded from the study. Similarly, articles that studied the adoption and utilization of electronic banking channels from a business-to-bank perspective were also not included. The search yielded in 247 peerreviewed research publications.

In order to guide the analysis of the research literature the following questions were posed:

• Which electronic banking channel was studied?

• What was the research approach (empirical, non-empirical)?

## 4. Findings (see footnote 1)

## 4.1. Overall research output

Since 1984, there has been a steady increase in research output related to the adoption and use of electronic banking channels. While the number of research publications rose only moderately between 1984 and the late 1990s (mainly articles devoted to ATMs), the emergence of the Internet and the diffusion of mobile technologies have resulted in a rapid increase in research during the last decade. Table 3 summarizes the extent of the research literature on electronic banking between 1984 and 2010.

Most studies researched each electronic banking channel separately. Only 29 research articles have compared and analyzed adoption behavior across multiple channels. For example Laukkanen [52] contrasted the adoption of Internet versus mobile banking applications. Moreover, out of 247 identi<sup>fi</sup>ed articles, 213 were published since 2001, including 117 articles studying Internet banking services.

## 4.2. Research type and methods used

The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed articles were categorized using Alavi and Carlson's [5] research classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme:

## 4.2.1. Non-empirical research

Non-empirical research included articles based either on authors' subjective opinions and/or literature reviews, but did not include empirically collected data [5]. Non-empirical research was further subclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed into literature reviews, conceptual articles, and illustrative contributions.

Overall, 53 articles were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as non-empirical work (21% of all identi<sup>fi</sup>ed articles). Table 4 summarizes this category and depicts the overall number of research articles published on each electronic banking channel.

Literature reviews were commonly based on secondary sources and reported no empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Conceptual articles were nonempirical studies describing frameworks, models, or theories. Generally, these studies aimed to explore possible drivers for the adoption and use of electronic banking applications. Illustrative articles were journalistic-type reports featuring real-life examples of electronic banking.

As demonstrated in Table 4, the largest collection of non-empirical research on electronic banking channels related to mobile banking services (accounting for 41% of articles published on mobile banking services). Considering the novelty of this <sup>fi</sup>eld, a high proportion of non-empirical work was anticipated. Emerging research <sup>fi</sup>elds tend to be initially dominated by non-empirical studies, which are then followed by more empirically-based work [88]. In contrast, just 17 articles were published on Internet banking without reference to empirical data — less than 15% of all articles published on Internet banking services. Considering that the Internet has only been used in banking services for about 15 years, this illustrates how quickly empirical studies come to dominate the research in a new domain.

## 4.2.2. Empirical research

Empirical articles were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as those relying on <sup>fi</sup>eld observations usually captured through a number of methodological research techniques such as <sup>fi</sup>eld surveys, case studies, <sup>fi</sup>eld studies/interviews, as well as laboratory and <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments [5].

Following Alavi and Carlson [5], empirical articles were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as qualitative research if they had an emphasis on the description and understanding of the context and the environment of the research phenomenon. On the other hand, studies using numerical analysis to illustrate the relationship among factors in the phenomenon studied were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as quantitative research. Some of the studies used both quantitative and qualitative methods in which case they were categorized as mixed methods. Most of these mixedmethod studies initially used qualitative research methods before con<sup>fi</sup>rming their conceptualizations through quantitative research (e.g., [55]).

In addition to quantitative and qualitative categories, the articles were also scrutinized for the research method applied: case studies, focus groups, grounded theory studies, interviews, survey questionnaires and experiments.

## 4.2.2.1. Qualitative research

4.2.2.1.1. Case studies. Yin [99] de<sup>fi</sup>ned case study research as: “an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context; when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident; and in which multiple sources of evidence are used”. We used this de<sup>fi</sup>nition to classify articles as case studies.

O'Reilly et al. [76], for example, used a multiple-case study design to explore banks' Internet channel strategies. These researchers interviewed several senior bank managers to discuss the approaches banks pursue to move existing customers to Internet banking applications.

Peffers and Tuunanen [78] applied case study method to investigate what features of mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial services customers would view as essential. The case study design involved a group of 32 research participants working in a Finland-based research and development <sup>fi</sup>rm. Interviews were used to surface the participants' expectations concerning mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial applications.

4.2.2.1.2. Focus groups. Focus groups comprise a group of individuals selected and assembled by researchers to discuss and comment on the topic that is the subject of the research [5]. In this category, the focus groups typically consisted of consumers of electronic banking services.

For instance, Black et al. [11] conducted several focus group discussions involving consumers in the UK in order to investigate consumer choice behavior related to <sup>fi</sup>nancial distribution channels. Based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the authors argued that channel choice is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by consumers' attitudes, channel characteristics, and organizational characteristics, and that consumers would highly value both product–channel interactions and consumer–channel interactions [11].

Howcroft et al. [44] used a series of focus groups to examine consumers' perceptions of <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. To this end, three focus groups were conducted to study banking channel adoption in the UK. Consumer trust emerged as a particularly in<sup>fl</sup>uential factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumers in their use of an electronic banking channel [44].

4.2.2.1.3. Grounded theory studies. Researchers in this category developed a theory that was “grounded in data systematically gathered and analyzed in which theory emerges during actual research, doing so through the continuous interplay between analysis and data collection” [90].

Little [67] used grounded theory techniques to conceptualize consumers' attitudes towards ATM usage in public zones. The author collected empirical data through face-to-face interviews with consumers who used ATM machines on a frequent base. Among others, trust, safety and privacy were recurring concepts impacting on the use and non-use of ATMs in public places [67].

Lichtenstein and Williamson [65] studied adoption of Internet banking services in the Australian banking context. The authors interviewed more than thirty Australian consumers and analyzed the data using grounded theory methods. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study were used to develop a theoretical framework modeling the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumers' intentions to use Internet banking applications [65].

4.2.2.1.4. Interview-based studies. In this category, researchers collected primary data through face-to-face interviews but without reference to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c methodology such as case study or grounded theory method. Researchers would typically interview several consumers concerning their perceptions about electronic banking channels. For instance, Broderick and Vachirapornpuk [13] investigated service quality in Internet banking applications by interviewing consumers via an Internet banking users' community forum. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that consumers expect honest and timely communication, strong interactivity, a good user interface and adequate support when using Internet banking applications [13].

Laukkanen and Lauronen [57] interviewed consumers regarding their perceptions of the value propositions of mobile banking services. This exploratory study used a means–end approach and laddering techniques to facilitate the interview method. The results suggested that safety and convenience were perceived by the respondents as important value drivers for mobile banking adoption [57].

In summary, qualitative research on e-banking demonstrated that researchers found it particularly valuable for obtaining rich data from consumers using electronic banking channels and their associated tools and applications [45,53]. Out of 247 articles, only two studies used case study design [76,78]. This could be explained by the fact that case study research is traditionally employed by organizational research [99] rather than consumer-based studies. Due to the fact that the current literature analysis focuses on consumer adoption, it appears logical that only few studies employed case study research. Two other papers were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed employing grounded theory techniques although both studies did not report in-depth experiences with using this technique for researching e-banking adoption [65,67].

Also, two further studies employed focus group discussions consisting of consumers using electronic banking channels. These articles reported that focus groups were particularly useful to let participants interact with each other and discuss their experiences with e-banking channels [11,44]. Further, both studies investigated several e-banking channels as part of their studies and reported positive experiences with focus group discussions.

Interviews were the most frequently applied qualitative method. Most researchers stated a desire for gathering rich data as the main motivation to apply interview techniques with individuals [45,53]. In many cases researchers used semi-structured interview protocols and our literature sample did not include studies using innovative interview techniques such as narrative inquiry or storytelling [40].

## 4.2.2.2. Quantitative research

4.2.2.2.1. “Survey questionnaires were used by numerous researchers to examine in a structured and standardized way consumers' perceptions and attitudes towards electronic banking channels [9,10,96]”.

For instance, Wan et al. [96] investigated the factors impacting on customers' perceptions regarding branch banking, ATMs, telephone, and Internet banking services. The authors developed a survey questionnaire instrument and collected 314 responses from bank consumers in Hong Kong. To analyze the data, the authors used con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis in order to examine four major factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing channel utilization: convenience, informativeness, user-friendliness, and assurance [96].

Bhattacherjee [9,10] developed a research model predicting customers' continuous intentions to use Internet banking services, and gathered data using a survey to test his model. IS continuance was modeled as a function of customer services satisfaction, perceived usefulness of Internet banking, and loyalty incentives intended to enhance consumers' satisfaction levels with Internet banking. Satisfaction and perceived usefulness were hypothesized as being predicted by consumers' con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of sales, service, and marketing expectations [9]. Among other things, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that IS continuance is an appropriate dependent variable in studies of post-adoption consumer use of Internet banking services.

4.2.2.2.2. Experiments. Studies in this category manipulated one or more variables, and subsequently measured the impact of the manipulation on one or more dependent variables related to electronic banking channels. For example, Dabholkar and Bagozzi [20] extended the TAM model by adding several variables speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the use of ATM machines. To test the research model, the authors conducted an experiment involving college students. The students were exposed to two treatments including perceived waiting time and social anxiety. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that both perceived waiting time and social anxiety would negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' use behavior related to ATM machines [20].

In summary, quantitative research has been chosen frequently by researchers to study the adoption and use of electronic banking channels. Survey research was by far the most widely adopted research method in our literature sample. Most authors chose this form of data collection because it allowed them to apply a well-understood and popular approach for collecting empirical data [4,9,18].

Several authors made use of organizational networks to collect data from e-banking users, and circulated survey questionnaires within organizations [94]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that access to corporations was helpful for the data collection. Some researchers used student samples [49] whereas others collected data through consumer panels [9]. It was also interesting to observe that the statistical techniques used to analyze quantitative data ranged from simple descriptive statistics to multivariate statistics including structural equation modeling [9,92]. Lastly, one study recommended using an experimental research design in combination with a consumer panel [28].

4.3. Theories used to study the adoption, utilization, and resistance of electronic banking channels

The next step was to identify the key theories and models used by the authors to study electronic banking channels. Five theoretical lenses predominate in the literature.

## 4.3.1. Diffusion of innovations (DOI)

Having its origins in sociology, the Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory has been used since the 1960s to explore the adoption of technologies, ranging from agricultural tools to organizational innovation [86]. DOI theory states that innovations are communicated through various channels over time and within a particular social system [86]. Since consumers possess different degrees of willingness to adopt innovations, Rogers [86] segregated consumers into <sup>fi</sup>ve categories of individual innovativeness starting from early to late adopters: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards. Rogers [86] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve factors determining the rate of adoption of innovations: relative advantage, compatibility, trialability, observability, and complexity. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst four factors would generally positively correlate with rate of adoption, complexity would commonly negatively affect the diffusion rate [86]. Moore and Benbasat [73] drew upon DOI theory to develop an instrument to measure the adoption of information technology. Support was found for the following factors impacting on the adoption of IT: relative advantage, ease of use, image, visibility, compatibility, results demonstrability and voluntariness of use [73].

“Much literature on the adoption and utilization of electronic banking channels has been based on diffusion of innovations theory [77,84,101].” For example, Polatoglu and Ekin [80] examined the factors associated with adoption initially identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Rogers [86], namely relative advantage, observability, triability and complexity in relation to Internet banking services in a survey study of Turkish consumers. This study con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that younger and better educated bank customers perceived Internet banking as very useful and easy to use. Further, they believed that Internet banking provides a relative advantage over traditional bank branches [80].

## 4.3.2. Theory of reasoned action (TRA) and theory of planned behavior (TPB)

Theory of reasoned action (TRA) is rooted in psychology and was originally developed by Fishbein and Ajzen [31]. TRA argues that indi vidual behavior is driven by behavioral intentions which can be viewed as a function of individuals' attitudes towards behavior and their subjective norms [31]. Fishbein and Ajzen [31] de<sup>fi</sup>ned attitude towards behavior “as the individual's feelings about performing a behavior”. On the other hand, subjective norm was explained as “an individual's perception of whether the behavior should be performed”. This would be driven by the motivation that an individual has to comply with opinions from people who are important to the individual [31]. Although not focusing on Information Systems specifically, the theory of reasoned action has been frequently used to explain the user intentions for deploying information systems [93].

Subsequent to the original TRA theory, Ajzen [3] extended the TRA theory establishing theory of planned behavior (TPB). TPB added a perceived behavioral control construct to the TRA. Ajzen [3] argued that behavioral intention can <sup>fi</sup>nd expression in behavior only if the behavior in question is under volitional control, (e.g., if the person can decide at will to perform or not perform the behavior). In many instances behavior would be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by non-motivational factors such as availability of resources [3].

A number of other studies applied either the theory of reasoned action [31] or the theory of planned behavior [3] to research consumer behavior towards electronic banking channels [1,61,82,101]. Some studies combined these models with speci<sup>fi</sup>c theoretical constructs from the electronic banking literature — for example, trust in Internet banking solutions [6]. Tan et al. [93] explored the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the adoption of Internet banking and extended Rogers's DOI framework [86] by combining it with TRA and TPB. Their study provided a comprehensive way to understand how an individual's attitude and perceived behavioral control in<sup>fl</sup>uence their intention to use banking services on the Internet [93].

## 4.3.3. Technology acceptance model (TAM)

“One of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant adaptations of TRA theory is the technology acceptance model originally developed by Davis [21].” This theory aims to predict how users accept and use a technology and suggests that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are of primary relevance for computer acceptance behaviors. Perceived usefulness can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” [22]. Perceived ease of use refers to “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort” [22]. Both, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use would determine an individual's intention to use a system. Perceived usefulness was also seen as being directly impacted by perceived ease of use [22]. TAM is often referred to as one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential and most often used theories in information systems [8,95].

“Several research studies investigated consumers' acceptance of various electronic banking channels using the technology acceptance model [22] or various modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of it [49,62,77,79].” Suh and Han [91], for instance, argued that trust would be one of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant determinants of customer acceptance of Internet banking. Integrating this concept into the TAM model, the authors reasoned that trust had a more direct effect on a customer's attitude than perceived ease of use in the Internet banking context. However they found that perceived ease of use had a greater total effect on a customer's actual use.

## 4.3.4. Technology resistance theory

The traditional adoption and acceptance theories have particularly focused on the enabling factors leading to technology adoption. These studies primarily researched the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts individuals gain when using technologies to perform their tasks. More recently, IS researchers have started to shed light on the in<sup>fl</sup>uential role of inhibiting factors that prevent individuals from using a given technology [16,17,51]. Cenfetelli and Schwarz [17] tested the inhibitors of technology usage model (ITU) and de<sup>fi</sup>ned technology inhibitors as “perceptions held by a user about a system's attributes with consequent effects on a decision to use a system.… The important aspect of use inhibitors, in contrast to enablers, is that they act solely to discourage use” [17]. Durkin et al. [29] investigated both enabling and inhibiting factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumer online banking use behavior. The authors suggested lack of face-to-face contact, lack of trust, and lack of security reassurance would negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer's usage with Internet banking portals [29].

Our analysis also suggested a rich literature concerning consumer resistance to using mobile banking applications [56,58,59]. For example, Laukkanen and Kiviniemi [56] conducted a large scale consumer survey in Finland to investigate the role of information in mobile banking resistance. The results showed that the lack of guidance and the lack of information on mobile banking are inhibiting factors for consumers. This study proposed that banks should educate and guide consumers when using mobile banking applications [56]. The study also revealed that consumers perceived mobile banking applications as risky and they struggled to view m-banking as a valuable service.

## 5. Discussion

Our review and analysis of 247 peer-reviewed articles allowed us to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research trends on e-banking adoption over the last three decades. As part of our analysis, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed those research issues that have been frequently studied in the past. This overview enables us to look ahead and highlight opportunities for valuable future research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. We have summarized these <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Table 8.

The top row in Table 8 presents a summary of the research on e-banking channel adoption and use which has been focused at the individual level. In the past, attitudinal beliefs have been explored and researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors such as perceived ease of use, complexity, attitude towards e-banking or fun [18,46,49]. “Further, researchers studying this area have often argued that perceived value propositions are in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors for consumers to use ebanking channels including perceived usefulness, convenience, and service quality [18,46,49].” The literature review also revealed that qualitative and quantitative research methods have been chosen in the past to study consumers' attitudinal beliefs and value propositions of e-banking services.

Also, inhibitors to adoption of e-banking channels have been explored extensively, and research suggests that costs and perceived insecurity of e-banking applications would deter individuals from using self-service technologies [37,45]. In contrast, research started only recently to pay attention to the inhibiting factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals when using technologies in work-environments [17,51]. Given the rich literature base around e-banking consumer resistance [12,58,59], we believe that this body of knowledge could inform studies researching inhibitors beyond technology use at the individual level.

“It appears that the <sup>fi</sup>eld of e-banking is ripe for methodological advances at the individual levels of analysis.” For instance, recent research has employed neuroscienti<sup>fi</sup>c methods such as fMRI imaging to study individuals' perceptions towards computer interfaces [27,85]. By using innovative research designs, these studies have begun to reveal new insights into why individuals appreciate or reject computerized interfaces. Such studies could also explore the cognitive processes associated with decision-making when using such applications.

Also, there seems to be an overemphasis on studying each electronic banking channel separately. Few researchers have attempted to understand how individuals make decisions concerning the use of multiple channels to manage their personal <sup>fi</sup>nances [43,48,63,69,75,100]. This appears to be particularly important since new technologies (e.g., iPads, netbooks, or latest smart phone technologies) provide consumers with additional technologies to access their <sup>fi</sup>nancial information and make decisions about its management [11].

The second row in Table 8 depicts the theoretical lenses used to study e-banking adoption. Out of 247 articles on e-banking adoption, 123 studies were theoretically rooted in DOI, TRA, TAM, and TPB. Instead of continuing to use the traditional adoption theories, we suggest that a wider range of existing theories could be used to better understand consumers' use of electronic banking channels.

For instance, Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) reconceptualized information systems use and argued that system usage involves three major elements that are essential when studying IS usage: a user (individual), an information system (IT artifact), and a task [15]. Our review of the literature on e-banking services indicates that few authors considered all components of IS usage [15]. Most studies investigated e-banking use in general, and little light has been shed on the ways in which banking tasks (e.g., account inquiries, setting up automatic payments or credit card applications) affect the traditional research models. Integrating banking tasks and the IT artifact within e-banking adoption models should lead to more applicable and precise (though perhaps less generalizable) research <sup>fi</sup>ndings [15].

A potential conceptual starting point for such an endeavor would be an augmented task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t [38] model that would provide <sup>fi</sup>ner and more focused design advice for banking managers. Very little is known how this well established construct might be applied to understanding electronic banking channel adoption and use [41,63]. Practitioners developing e-banking applications would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from such <sup>fi</sup>ndings since the research outcomes would be linked to speci<sup>fi</sup>c banking tasks.

Media synchronicity theory (MST) could also be useful here [24]. MST could be valuable in explaining what type of banking transactions can best be supported by which self-service channels. MST appears particularly useful for investigating new media such as instant messaging, chat rooms, or VoIP.

A third theory that could be valuable in studying e-banking is expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory. This theory is widely used in marketing research, and is becoming more frequently applied in IS studies generally as well. For example [9,10], tested expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory in the context of e-banking.

Lastly, when considering the base of empirical e-banking channel research, quantitative research (79%) is currently employed to a much greater extent than qualitative research (12%) or mixed methods (9%). Similarly, survey questionnaires have been utilized much more frequently than available alternatives. This suggests that researchers may learn more about electronic banking adoption in the future by applying methodological approaches that have been under-utilized to date. For instance, none of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed papers reviewed for this study designed e-banking portals and researched individuals' perceptions towards the newly developed applications. Design science research appears to be a well-suited research method to study e-banking adoption [39]. Also, as our understanding of the e-banking phenomenon stabilizes, opportunities for conceptual (nonempirical) studies – which to date have been rare – should improve.

## 6. Conclusions

This article has provided an overview of the adoption and utilization literature on electronic banking channels by individuals. Electronic banking comprises new, powerful mechanisms for consumers to both manage their <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources and to conduct analyses and make decision concerning those resources. In order to better understand the state of research in this domain, a systematic and comprehensive literature analysis consisting of 247 peer-reviewed articles was carried out.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support future theory development in this domain. In addition to providing a summary of the theoretical frameworks that have been used to date in the study of adoption and acceptance of electronic banking applications and services, several research gaps were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, suggesting opportunities for fruitful future research.

Interested researchers should be able to identify additional research opportunities through the information provided here.

This study also supports construct speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and measurement, as it allows researchers to locate validated measurement scales which can be used in future research in this area, thereby making it easier for future researchers to “build on each other's work” [47]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings should also help future researchers to “identify less used but useful alternative theoretical and methodological perspectives” [98].

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings presented here should be also of interest to banks and other <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions intending to distribute their products and services through electronic banking channels. Research on electronic banking is distributed widely across publication outlets in various research disciplines such as marketing, electronic commerce, information systems, and business and management. This study has incorporated research from across these disparate areas to provide an overall, up-to-date ‘state-of-play’ of research on the adoption of electronic banking applications for both transactional and decisionmaking use.

This article also faces some limitations and delimitations. An important delimitation of the paper is that the literature analysis was limited to individual adoption and utilization of e-banking channels. While this includes the large majority of the literature concerning ebanking channel adoption and use, the scope of the present study could be expanded in the future to include organizational adoption and use as well, e.g., business-to-business e-banking channel adoption. While our literature review was extensive and spanned a number of different research domains, it is possible that some articles were missed. Further, the research analysis was limited to six major research questions. Future studies should examine additional issues, for instance, including what speci<sup>fi</sup>c services (both transactional and decision-oriented) are used on electronic banking channels and how such choices are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by age and gender. Also, it should be noted that, due to space limitations, this article does not list all references. However, all 247 references analyzed for this research can be found at the following web site: http://www.box.net (username = ebankinganalysis@gmail.com; password = review1+). Interested researchers are also welcome to e-mail the lead author in order to obtain more detailed information relating to the development of this paper.

Hartmut Hoehle (hartmut.hoehle@anu.edu.au) is a lecturer at the School of Accounting and Business Information Systems, Australian National University. He received his Ph.D. from Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand. His research focuses on understanding the use of technology in society and organizations. Stemming from his professional experiences gained while working at Deutsche Bank, he is particularly interested in how services and products can be distributed through electronically mediated channels. His research has appeared in journals such as the Journal of Computer Information Systems and the International Journal of Electronic Finance.
