## Abstract

Social media firestorms pose a significant challenge for firms in the digital age. Tackling firestorms is difficult because the judgments and responses from social media users are influenced by not only the nature of the transgressions but also by the reactions and opinions of other social media users. Drawing on the heuristic-systematic information processing model, we propose a research model to explain the effects of social impact (the heuristic mode) and argument quality and moral intensity (the systematic mode) on perceptions of firm wrongness (the judgment outcome) as well as the effects of perceptions of firm wrongness on vindictive complaining and patronage reduction. We adopted a mixed methods approach in our investigation, including a survey, an experiment, and a focus group study. Our findings show that the heuristic and systematic modes of information processing exert both direct and interaction effects on individuals’ judgment. Specifically, the heuristic mode of information processing dominates overall and also biases the systematic mode. Our study advances the literature by offering an alternative explanation for the emergence of social media firestorms and identifying a novel context in which the heuristic mode dominates in dual information processing. It also sheds light on the formulation of response strategies to mitigate the adverse impacts resulting from social media firestorms. We conclude our paper with limitations and future research directions.

Keywords: Social Media Firestorms, Heuristic-Systematic Information Processing Model, Social Impact, Moral Intensity, Argument Quality, Firm Wrongness

## 1 Introduction

Social media sites are popular digital platforms for firms to build an online presence, promote products, and engage with customers. However, despite these benefits, social media can expose firms to threats of online firestorms. There have been cases of a sudden burst of outrage on social media, accusing firms of committing transgressions. For instance, H&M, a clothing retail company, faced a firestorm on social media after its website featured a Black child model wearing a sweatshirt printed with “coolest monkey in the jungle,” a slogan considered degrading to people of African descent (Stack, 2018). Audi, an automobile manufacturer, stirred up controversy on social media after posting a potentially sexually suggestive advertisement that included a photo of a little girl leaning against the front of a car and eating a banana (Evans, 2020). Social media transcends geographical barriers and transforms individuals from passive readers and listeners to active speakers and participants. Any negative comment on social media can quickly gain momentum, ignite public outrage, and turn into a firestorm, leading to severe reputational threats and potentially uncontrollable adverse impacts on firms under fire (Hauser et al., 2017). It has been reported that among firms impacted by social media firestorms, 58% suffered from short-term negative brand perceptions, and 40% suffered from long-term adverse impacts (Hansen et al., 2018).

Social media characteristics play an imperative role in fueling firestorms (Pfeffer et al., 2014). Hyperconnectivity, homophily, and filter bubble and echo chamber effects are just a few prominent social media characteristics that lead to the rapid development of firestorms. Social media circulates a vast volume of negative messages concerning a firm’s transgression during firestorms. Individuals are exposed to not only incident-related information about firms’ practices but also accusations from other social media users and their negative reactions (e.g., anger) on the platforms. Without a doubt, processing such a vast volume of information to arrive at a judgment of a firm’s transgressions presents cognitive challenges. Often, given the nature of hedonic social media use, the tendency for individuals to conserve their cognitive resources, coupled with the abundance of heuristic cues in the social network environment, users tend to adopt heuristics learned from their everyday life and social media use to arrive at a judgment. Consequently, firms could be victimized by potentially invalid allegations. For instance, a Twitter user maliciously ignited a firestorm against Uber a few years ago. Uber ended up facing serious allegations and suffered from the #DeleteUber movement, despite the incident being initiated by an individual who feverishly trolled Uber’s service chatbot for a racist reply using the “N-word” (Connor, 2019).

Understanding the relative effects and the interplay between the use of cognitive deliberation and heuristic cues and the biasing effects of the latter on social media, the judgment of users in firestorms thus represents an important research opportunity and managerial issue. The dual-process models of information processing, predominantly the heuristic-systematic model and the elaboration likelihood model, have been widely used by information systems (IS) researchers to explain how individuals assess and adopt information. Most studies have focused on the factors affecting information usefulness and, subsequently, information adoption behaviors in the context of online reviews (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019; Watts & Zhang, 2008). While the application of dual-process models of information processing provides a reasonable theoretical tenet to explain individuals’ information processing behaviors in social media firestorms, established antecedents of the two processing modes (i.e., source credibility and argument quality) and judgment outcomes (i.e., information usefulness and adoption) are insufficient to explain individuals judgments and responses in social media firestorms. Context-specific variables representing the two information processing modes and the judgment outcome in social media firestorms need to be identified to provide a contextualized explanation of the phenomenon. Further, developing effective response strategies requires firms to understand how social media users judge and respond to such firestorms (Scholz & Smith, 2019). Therefore, it is crucial to understand how individuals process social media firestorm information, make judgments, and respond to the targeted firms.

Accordingly, this study aims to address a primary research question: How do individuals process information, judge, and respond to firm transgressions in social media firestorms? The objectives of this study are threefold and focus on the context of social media firestorms: (1) to reveal factors representing the dual modes of information processing, (2) to examine their relative effects on the judgment of firm wrongness, and (3) to investigate the effects of the wrongness judgment on negative responses to targeted firms. We used the heuristic-systematic model of information processing (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012) as the theoretical foundation to develop our research model. We contextualized the model by incorporating variables specific to the information processing modes and judgment outcomes in social media firestorms, including social impact, moral intensity, and firm wrongness, based on the social cognition, moral decision-making, and consumer psychology literatures. We tested our research model using mixed methods, including a survey, an experiment, and a focus group study, to derive richer insights into the current phenomenon.

Our study has important implications for research and practice. From a theoretical perspective, our study identifies the factors representing the heuristic and systematic modes of information processing and their relative influences on judgment and negative responses toward firms in social media firestorms. It also offers a novel theoretical explanation for the occurrence of an emerging technology-mediated phenomenon. From a practical perspective, our findings offer insights into devising corporate response strategies for social media firestorms by considering the relative use of the two information processing modes and the effects of perceptions of firm wrongness on retaliatory and avoidance behaviors.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we review the concept and research status of social media firestorms. Then, we introduce the heuristic-systematic model of information processing, the research model, and the hypotheses. After that, we report the research design, data collection, and data analysis. Finally, we provide a discussion of the implications for research and practice, limitations, and future research directions.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Social Media Firestorms: Definition and Characteristics

Online firestorms refer to situations where a person, group, or institution experiences a sudden surge of negative attention in a digital environment (Pfeffer et al., 2014) and are often triggered by controversial or divisive events. In this study, we focus on social media firestorm situations, where a firm suddenly receives a large amount of negative attention on social media concerning a purported moral transgression. Although it is not uncommon for people to criticize a firm when they feel dissatisfied with the performance of its goods or services, this is particularly salient when they observe what they consider to be an immoral practice on the part of the firm. As consumers and the general public expect firms to act morally and serve as a force for good in society, value-related transgressions tend to provoke stronger negative reactions than performance-related transgressions (e.g., failures with goods and services).

The design artifacts and algorithms of social media can quickly turn negative opinions, criticisms, and complaints into a firestorm (Jontgen, 2020). In most social media environments, the opinion-sharing system does not afford sophisticated or gradualist opinions. Most sharing is designed to be quick and to primarily signify whether a user “likes” or “endorses” content without inviting deliberation (Gruber et al., 2020). Further, the local clustering in social media networks echoes the same negative opinions from various connections, creating the impression that the vast majority of people have already formed the same negative opinions about the firm’s practice (Pfeffer & Carley, 2013). Lastly, homophily in social connections creates a filter bubble that restricts the diversity of opinions that users receive, and echo chambers facilitate the effective persuasion and affirmation of the same negative opinions (Terren & Borge-Bravo, 2021) rather than offering a critical evaluation from alternative perspectives. Consequently, most social media firestorms exhibit a lopsided negative sentiment against the targeted firm (Herhausen et al., 2019; Matook et al., 2022), leading the firm to face considerable risk of reputational damage and financial loss.

## 2.2 Social Media Firestorms: Research Status

Research on social media firestorms is growing, as indicated by the increasing number of scholarly publications on this topic. Previous research on social media firestorms has examined three key aspects of the phenomenon: (1) the nature of firestorms; (2) detection, intervention, and response strategies in relation to firestorms; and (3) the antecedents and outcomes of firestorms (see Table A1 in Appendix A).

Investigations into the nature of social media firestorms have largely focused on the characteristics of firestorms and individuals’ motives and goals for participation. Inductive approaches via content analysis and social media data mining are commonly used to understand themes, triggers, and motivations related to social media firestorms. For instance, based on an analysis of 130 social media firestorms, researchers proposed a typology of social media firestorm stimulants consisting of perceived moral misconduct, perceived incompetence, perceived market misconduct, and perceived violations of honor or reputation (Einwiller et al., 2017). Also, research has found that participation in social media firestorms is driven by multiple different motives (Gassman et al., 2019), such as rectification, dissatisfaction, vilification, amusement, and honor.

Detecting social media firestorms is a top priority for many firms because it “enables” them to proactively deal with incidents before they go viral. Previous studies have explored various proxies for detecting social media firestorms, including measuring the strength of structural ties, linguistic styles, and the overall sentiments of social media posts (e.g., Herhausen et al., 2019). However, the development of detection algorithms has not kept up with the pace of such incidents in the fast-moving social media landscape. As a result, another stream of research has focused on intervention and response strategies for social media firestorms. Various typologies of intervention and response strategies have been proposed, such as proactive response vs. no response (Kintu & Ben-Slimane, 2020), proactive response vs. reactive response (Chen, 2022), assertive style vs. cooperative style (Hauser et al., 2017), and specific justification vs. generic justification (Lappeman et al., 2018). However, research has acknowledged that the effectiveness of these strategies depends on a range of factors, including participants’ characteristics and their relationships with firms, the social structure of the online social communities fueling the firestorms, and the presence and response of moderators and aggressors in conversations (Hauser et al., 2017). Indeed, Herhausen et al. (2019) also highlighted the significant challenges of preventing negative comments from going viral. They suggested that firms could use tailored messages to mitigate their virality by varying the use of empathy and explanation in the messages. Similarly, Hauser et al. (2017) reported that a collaborative conflict management style, characterized by high assertiveness and high cooperativeness, could prevent the escalation and mitigate the harmful outcomes of social media firestorms.

The antecedents and consequences of social media firestorms also represent a prominent research area. Different theoretical perspectives, such as the situational theory of problem-solving (e.g., Gruber et al., 2020), homophily (e.g., Suwandee et al., 2019), social capital theory (e.g., Jontgen, 2020), and the appraisal theory of emotions (e.g., Delgado-Ballester et al., 2021) have been used to guide the examination of the antecedents and consequences related to social media firestorms. For instance, using the lens of homophily, Suwandee et al. (2019) found that high consensus regarding the incident among online community members led to significant attitudinal changes. Hansen et al. (2018) compared the types of firm failures and their negative impacts on firms. They found that compared to firestorms related to communication failures, firestorms related to social failures generated more negative impacts on firms. Research on the consequences of social media firestorms on firms has generated mixed findings. Some studies have found that firms suffered from negative firm perceptions (Hansen et al., 2018) and boycotting behaviors (Lim, 2017); others have reported that fighting back against social media firestorms actually generated positive value for firms (Scholz & Smith, 2019). Although increasing scholarly attention has been devoted to examining social media firestorms, it remains unclear how individuals process information presented in social media firestorms, arrive at a judgment, and respond to firms under fire. This study aims to bridge these research gaps.

## 3 Research Model and Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 The Heuristic-Systematic Model of Information Processing

We build on the literature of dual-process models of information processing to understand how individuals process social media firestorm information, arrive at a judgment, and respond to the firms involved. Dualprocess models of information processing, predominantly the heuristic-systematic model (HSM) and the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), have been widely used by IS researchers to understand how individuals process information in a wide array of digital contexts (e.g., Cheung et al., 2012; Goel et al., 2017; Kwak et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021). A brief overview of the dual-process models of information processing is discussed in Appendix A.

We build on HSM and the literature on social cognition, moral decision-making, and consumer psychology to develop a contextualized research model that captures the antecedents and outcomes of information processing in social media firestorms. HSM postulates that heuristic and systematic modes can influence a judgment outcome independently or interdependently (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993). In particular, HSM posits that an individual’s attitude can be shaped via two modes of informational influence, the heuristic and the systematic modes. The two information processing modes differ in the amount of thoughtful information processing demanded of individual subjects, with the heuristic route involving less cognitive effort. HSM represents a sound and legitimate theoretical lens to explain the emergence of firestorms in social media, where heuristic cues are abundant and communication is fast-paced and stimulating. In particular, HSM upholds the sufficiency principle and assumes that individuals utilize an economy-minded approach to process information. Given the need to conserve cognitive resources to maneuver through the complex social media environment, we assume that individuals tend to use the least cognitive effort possible to process the information encountered. Consequently, the heuristic mode of information processing may dominate when evaluating social media firestorms.

In the context of social media firestorms, the vast majority of transgressions concerned are value related (Einwiller et al., 2017), involving violations of universal or moral expectations and norms (Grappi et al., 2013) such as the tolerance of human rights abuses, support for authoritarian regimes, the infringement of cultural or religious norms, and the exploitation of labor. The judgment of wrongness has been recognized as a salient judgment outcome in the moral judgment paradigm of value-related transgressions. Accordingly, firm wrongness here refers to the extent to which individuals perceive the actions or practices performed by a firm as being morally inappropriate. It represents individuals’ overall judgment outcomes resulting from the heuristic and systematic modes of information processing in social media firestorms. Based on HSM, people arrive at a wrongness judgment via one of two information processing modes: (1) the heuristic mode and (2) the systematic mode. We contextualize our research model by modeling the heuristic mode with social impact. Social impact is shaped by three influencing sources from the social network environment, including the number, strength, and immediacy of the sources (Latané, 1981), which signal the inappropriateness of a firm’s practices from different perspectives. Then, we model the systematic mode with argument quality and moral intensity. Apart from modeling their direct effects, we also propose that the two information processing modes interact and influence the wrongness judgment. Finally, we include vindictive complaining (i.e., a form of retaliatory behavior) and patronage reduction (i.e., a form of avoidance behavior) as two negative responses to the targeted firms in social media firestorms. ## 3.2 The Heuristic Mode of Information Processing

In this study of social media firestorms, the heuristic mode of information processing involves arriving at a wrongness judgment based on the social impact perceived from the social network environment. The heuristic mode of information processing relies on schemas, stereotypes, expectancies, and other forms of prior knowledge that can be imposed on information to assimilate it quickly into one’s existing structures (Bohner et al., 1995). Learned knowledge structures— for instance, “experts are credible,” “my own social group can be trusted,” and “consensus implies correctness”—are used consciously or unconsciously by individuals when evaluating social media firestorms.

## 3.2.1 Social Impact

Social impact refers to the extent to which individuals perceive the overall negative sentiment regarding the firm’s practice in the firestorm. The overall social impact perceived by social media users is determined by three sources of impact (Latané, 1981): the number, the strength, and the immediacy of the sources of impact that users perceive from the social network environment. In line with HSM and the principles of sufficiency, individuals tend to avoid the expenditure of cognitive or behavioral energy, given their limited capacity for attending to and processing information (Bohner et al., 1995). Instead, they apply the heuristics learned from their prior experience to arrive at a judgment.

In the context of social media firestorms, firestorm messages left by individuals who are high in power (e.g., close friends and family members), are knowledgeable (e.g., subject experts), or are charismatic (e.g., online influencers and celebrities) are generally considered to be more credible and thus more persuasive. Further, information about the firm circulates rapidly on social media during firestorms. Recent information is typically viewed as more valuable and impactful than dated information. A large number of messages criticizing a firm, coupled with negative aggregated user reactions (e.g., dislike and anger) about the transgression and the firm, begin to dominate individuals’ newsfeeds. The massive amounts of firestorm information can be overwhelming, thus prompting individuals to rapidly make wrongness judgments based on the overall sentiment perceived from other social media users. Bapna et al. (2019) argued that social media acts like a giant word-of-mouth machine. It brings socially relevant peers into a network, accelerates the diffusion of social information, and subsequently modifies individuals’ thoughts and actions, both consciously and unconsciously. This influence of social information on individuals’ actions and judgments is supported by the prominent phenomenon of observational learning in the psychology literature (Dewan et al., 2017). Hence, the stronger the social impact that individuals perceive, the stronger their perceptions of firm wrongness will be. The reliance on social impact as a heuristic cue in judgment making has been well-documented in previous research. Research has shown that customers use collective opinions and consensus to gauge product quality and firm reputation in online purchases (Amblee & Bui, 2014) and that social media uses opinions and consensus as symbolic anchors to make social movement decisions (Oh et al., 2015). Valentine and Bateman (2011) reported that the perception of the social impact of transgressions intensifies the perception of moral issues. Taken together, these sources suggest that social impact represents the salient situational norms regarding a firm’s transgression and increases users’ wrongness perceptions (Neubaum et al., 2016). Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: Users’ perceptions of social impact, signaling a firm’s inappropriate transgression, positively influence their perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms.

## 3.3 The Systematic Mode of Information Processing

The systematic mode is a comprehensive, analytic orientation in which individuals assess and scrutinize informational input for its relevance, importance, and quality to their judgment tasks (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012). In this study, the systematic mode of information processing involves arriving at a wrongness judgment based on the evaluations of the argument quality of the social media firestorm messages and the moral intensity of the transgression committed by the firm.

## 3.3.1 Argument Quality

Argument quality here refers to the overall assessment of the strength of persuasive argumentation of all the messages embedded in a social media firestorm incident. This comprises all text-based statements from social media users, including the original post and the subsequent comments. Evaluating the plausibility of persuasive argumentation requires a thorough understanding of available information through careful attention, deep thinking, and intensive reasoning (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993). For instance, in a social media firestorm, it may be necessary to scrutinize the logical coherence, the quality of the evidence presented, and the relevance of the arguments in relation to the transgression committed by a firm. Such systematic thinking entails a relatively high degree of cognitive effort. Previous studies have shown that online users engaging in systematic information processing carefully evaluate presented messages and contemplate their validity (Watts & Zhang, 2008). Messages of high argument quality draw recipients’ attention and invoke a reallocation of cognitive resources, eliciting persuaded responses and behaviors (Tam & Ho, 2005).

Firestorm messages of high argument quality contain facts and backing (Petty et al., 1981) and are thereby a strong determinant of persuasion and attitude change (Angst & Agarwal, 2009). When presented with social media firestorm messages of high argument quality, individuals may obtain more information about the transgression of the targeted firm, leading to a higher level of perceived firm wrongness. High message argument quality reduces the ambiguity of a concerning topic or incident and thus reduces uncertainty in judgment making (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019). Hence, if social media users are presented with firestorm messages of high argument quality, they will obtain more facts and evidence regarding the transgression and perceive a higher level of firm wrongness. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Users’ perceptions of message argument quality regarding a firm’s inappropriate transgression positively influence their perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms.

## 3.3.2 Moral Intensity

Moral intensity here refers to the extent to which salient moral attributes are violated in a transgression (Jones, 1991). It is one of the critical factors in moral judgment making (Hashim et al., 2018). Evaluating moral intensity connotes the systematic processing of issue-related information about a firm’s transgression (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012). For instance, one may need to estimate the extent of the harm done by a firm and the probability that the transgression will cause material harm based on relevant tweets and comments from others. Such a systematic mode entails a relatively high degree of mental effort to arrive at a wrongness judgment.

In social media firestorms, when individuals encounter an incident concerning a value-related transgression committed by a firm, a moral judgment-making process will be activated (Frey, 2000; Jones, 1991). Before arriving at a judgment outcome, individuals evaluate various issue-related aspects of the transgression, such as the magnitude of consequences, social consensus, the probability of effect, temporal immediacy, proximity, and the concentration of effect. The issue at hand is a matter of concern because the level of moral intensity varies between “the theft of a few office supplies or the release of a dangerous drug to the market” (Jones, 1991, p. 371). Therefore, when cognitive deliberation reveals a value-related transgression involving severe moral issues and consequences (e.g., a firm causing irreversible damage to the environment and wildlife), individuals will likely perceive a higher level of firm wrongness, as compared with a value relatedtransgression involving minor moral issues and consequences (e.g., a firm leaving a bag of trash unattended on a public walkway). Previous studies have shown that higher levels of perceived moral intensity are associated with stricter or more disapproving moral judgments (e.g., Culiberg & Bajde, 2014). Furthermore, in a meta-analysis of the antecedents of moral judgments, moral intensity had a significant effect on wrongness judgments (Pan & Sparks, 2012). Taken together, we suggest that when social media users perceive a higher level of moral intensity regarding the focal transgression in a social media firestorm, they will likely perceive a higher level of firm wrongness. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: Users’ perceptions of moral intensity regarding a firm’s inappropriate transgression positively influence their perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms.

## 3.4 The Interplay between the Heuristic and Systematic Modes of Information Processing

HSM asserts that there are occasions in which judgments are shaped by an interdependent interplay between the heuristic and systematic processing modes (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2012). Given the salience of social impact in the context of social media firestorms, the heuristic mode of information processing may “influence judgment indirectly by biasing the valence of systematic processing” (Chaiken & Maheswaran, 1994, p. 461).

Specifically, a sudden burst of outrage at a firm committing a transgression is a defining characteristic of social media firestorms. It is typical for such negative sentiments to dominate the social media environment via the local clustering effect, leading individuals to believe that a consensus has been reached regarding the firm’s inappropriate practices. In the context of social media firestorms, the presence of social impact (as a heuristic cue) produces inferences or expectancies about the probable validity of persuasive messages. Hence, the presence of a high social impact leads people to believe that the accusatory messages against the firm are endorsed and thus more credible, convincing, and valid, which inflates their perception of argument quality.

In addition, a higher social impact signals a consensus with one’s social network, enabling informational and normative social influences to take place. Such social influences may intensify individuals’ perceptions of moral intensity regarding the transgression. For instance, in a firestorm against H&M, there was a high social impact signaling the inappropriateness of the firm’s advertisement. The firm was accused of racism by a celebrity, and the accusation was endorsed by many others on social media in a short amount of time. There was a clear affirmation of disapproval against the firm’s practice among social media users. Thus, we propose that the presence of a high social impact could intensify the perceptions of argument quality and moral intensity regarding the transgression. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4: Users’ perceptions of social impact, signaling a firm’s inappropriate transgression, positively influence (a) their perceptions of message argument quality and (b) their perceptions of moral intensity in social media firestorms.

We also expect interaction effects between social impact and argument quality / moral intensity on perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms. Specifically, the social media environment creates an impression that the vast majority of other social media users already have a consensus (i.e., social impact) regarding the transgression. The filter bubble effect limits the diversity of information that reaches the individual, whereas the echo chamber effect heightens effective persuasion and affirmation (Pfeffer et al., 2014). Hence, perceptions of a high social impact regarding the firm’s transgression could reaffirm the cognitive elaboration of the persuasive strength of the argument (i.e., argument quality), the salient moral attributes of the transgression (i.e., moral intensity), and the subsequent judgment outcome (i.e., firm wrongness). In contrast, perceptions of a low social impact regarding the firm’s transgression or weak situational norms could attenuate the systematic mode of processing. Previous research has also shown that under congruent conditions, a heuristic cue strengthens the relationship between the systematic evaluation of attribute information and the evaluation outcome (Mitra, 1995). For instance, employing HSM, London Jr. et al. (2022) found that vividness and sender credibility (representing the heuristic mode) interact with message plausibility (representing the systematic mode) to determine message diagnosticity and novelty among social media users. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5: The heuristic mode of information processing interacts with the systematic mode of information processing in determining judgment outcomes such that users’ perceptions of high (low) social impact, signaling the firm’s inappropriate transgression, strengthen (weaken) (a) the relationship between their perceptions of message argument quality and firm wrongness and (b) the relationship between their perceptions of moral intensity and firm wrongness in social media firestorms.

## 3.5 Negative Responses to Firms: Retaliatory and Avoidance Behavior

In response to social media firestorms, previous research has suggested that individuals may adopt two different types of negative responses to the targeted firm—namely, fight vs. flight (Johnen et al., 2017). Specifically, “fight” responses involve retaliatory behaviors, whereas “flight” responses focus on the avoidance of the firm (Grégoire et al., 2009). In this study, we examine vindictive complaining as a retaliatory behavior and patronage reduction as an avoidance behavior.

## 3.5.1 Vindictive Complaining

Vindictive complaining is a form of retaliatory behavior. It represents a “fight” response because individuals’ actions are intended to retaliate and cause harm to the firm. Individuals hold expectations for firms, even if these expectations are not necessarily agreed upon or met by the firm. Specifically, Montgomery et al. (2018) suggested that these expectations are often unspoken and go beyond tangible and intangible exchanges in a relationship. Previous research has found that individuals value firms that engage in socially responsible activities, both operationally (e.g., engaging in sustainable production) and societally (e.g., donating money to charities) (Lacey et al., 2015). Furthermore, they expect firms to refrain from transgressions because of their potentially substantial societal cost (Johnson et al., 2018). Therefore, firms that violate and betray such expectations would be perceived as wrong (Grappi et al., 2013; Grégoire et al., 2009), setting a stage for retaliatory behaviors on the part of affected individuals. Vindictive complaining represents a retaliatory behavior where individuals take revenge (Grégoire et al., 2009) and seek to restore equity by taking actions against the relevant firm (Kähr et al., 2016). Thus, we hypothesize that:

H6: Users’ perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms positively influence their vindictive complaining response.

## 3.5.2 Patronage Reduction

Patronage reduction is a form of avoidance behavior. It represents a “flight” response because individuals’ actions seek to reduce their patronage of the firm by, for example, switching to alternatives (Grégoire et al., 2009). Firms not only offer goods and services but also create symbolic values for their customers, such as identity construction or self-motive fulfillment (Wolter et al., 2019). Hence, individuals can obtain selfdefining and self-expressive benefits through interacting with a firm, consuming the firm’s products, or engaging with the firm’s initiatives. As a result, individuals establish a shared identity with firms based on the premise of the relational self (Trump, 2014). Perceptions of firm wrongness may lead individuals to withdraw from firm interactions because when their sense of self is intertwined with a firm, perceptions of wrongness can introduce uncertainty and cause individuals to question the value of their shared identity (Fournier, 1998). Since the perception of wrongness gives rise to a feeling of impurity or a degradation of one’s self-concept (Rozin et al., 1999), individuals may engage in avoidance behaviors to disengage from firms in order to avoid psychological discomfort and a loss of status (Cheng et al., 2012). Previous research has suggested that a positive assessment of a firm keeps individuals close to it, whereas a negative perception results in distancing and avoidance behaviors (Zarantonello et al., 2016). Thus, we hypothesize:

H7: Users’ perceptions of firm wrongness in social media firestorms positively influence their patronage reduction.

## 4 Research Method

We tested our research model using three studies, including a survey, an experiment, and a focus group study. We first conducted an online survey to test the proposed research model. Then, we conducted an online experiment to validate the causal effects between the hypotheses concerning the two information processing modes and judgment outcomes. Finally, we conducted a focus group study to derive further insights to enrich and supplement the results of the two quantitative studies.

## 4.1 Survey Design and Data Collection

We conducted an online survey with social media users who have seen or experienced a social media firestorm. The three social media firestorms selected for the study include those concerning the firms (1) Audi, (2) H&M, and (3) Uber. These three chosen social media firestorms involved different business sectors, product categories and brands, and causes of transgressions, enhancing the external validity of the study. Details of the firestorms are presented in Figures B1-B3 in Appendix B.

Measures of focal constructs were adopted from the previous literature with minor modifications to fit the current research context (see Table B1 in Appendix B). We used perceptual scales and measured the responses on a 7-point Likert scale for the constructs in our research model. We used multiple items to assess each construct to ensure construct validity and reliability. We included gender, age, education, ethnicity, social media usage, previous experience with the firm, selfrelevant consumer-brand relationship, and trait moral identity as the control variables in the research model.

We collected data through a market research firm in the UK. Invitations to participate in the study were sent to a nationwide panel of individuals who had previously agreed to take part in research studies. Participation was voluntary and the participants were compensated for their time. The responses for the three firestorms were collected via three separate questionnaires that were distributed after the firestorm occurred on Twitter. Screening questions were asked at the beginning of each survey to identify the target respondents: “Which social media do you use the most?” This screening question offered multiple choices of different social media platforms, such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and Pinterest. Only those who selected “Twitter” as their primary social networking platform in the screening question were allowed to proceed to the next screening question. Then, the screenshots of 10 social media posts were shown to the respondents, and they were asked to indicate the one tweet they were most familiar with. Respondents who selected the social media firestorm post of Audi, H&M, or Uber, respectively, in the corresponding round of data collection were allowed to proceed to the questionnaire. Other respondents were thanked and dismissed. Duplicated responses and those that failed attention-check questions were removed from the datasets, yielding final datasets of 215, 254, and 223 responses for the Audi, H&M, and Uber incidents, respectively. Table B3 in Appendix B summarizes the descriptive statistics of the demographic profiles of the respondents.

## 4.2 Experiment Design and Data Collection

While the survey study aimed to corroborate the associations between firm wrongness and the heuristic and systematic information processing modes, we conducted two independent online experiments to test the causal effects of the heuristic and systematic processing modes on firm wrongness. We conducted two independent experiments with a 2 (social impact, low vs. high) × 2 (argument quality, low vs. high) and a 2 (social impact, low vs. high) × 2 (moral intensity, low vs. high) between-subjects factorial design to test such causal effects.

Social impact was manipulated by the strength of the relationship importance of other social media users who participated in the firestorm, the number of other social media users who responded to the incident, and the recency of their reactions, respectively. Moral intensity was manipulated by the nature of the transgression committed by the firm. Argument quality was manipulated by the presence of claims and backing contained in the firestorm messages and comments. Our experiment involved a simulation of a social media firestorm against a fictitious firm on Twitter using a hypothetical scenario (a vignette). In the current study, we used the hypothetical scenario to rule out potential confounding issues and reverse causality, such as participants forming specific perceptions and attitudes toward the focal firm before the social media firestorm happened. Using the scenario also allowed us to ensure the consistency of the nature and the valence of the relevant transgression, thereby enhancing the internal validity of the study. We conducted a pre-test to examine the effectiveness of the manipulation and ensure the realism of the experimental design. Details of the manipulation, the pre-test, and the experiment conditions are presented in Appendix B (see Experiment Design section).

Two new groups of participants were recruited from the same market research firm. For each experiment, subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions. All experimental conditions mocked up the Twitter environment and mimicked a social media firestorm. The subjects were presented with a hypothetical scenario in which a fictitious environmental group (i.e., denoted by the name “Earth

Watch” and an Earth avatar) ignited a firestorm against a fictitious firm’s (i.e., Food Supermarket) transgression. Negative comments and reactions regarding the transgression from other social media users were shown.

The subjects were debriefed and thanked at the end of the experiment study. 108 subjects were recruited to participate in each of the experiments (with a total of 216 unique subjects for two experiments) to ensure sufficient power (0.8) with a medium effect size (f = 0.30) for both the main effects and the interaction effects. Table B3 in Appendix B summarizes the descriptive statistics of the demographic profile of the respondents.

## 4.3 Focus Group Design and Data Collection

In addition to obtaining quantitative evidence on the associations between and the causal effects of the heuristic and systematic information processing modes on firm wrongness, we conducted a focus group study to derive additional qualitative support and insights to supplement the quantitative findings. A purposive sampling technique was used to recruit a wide range of participants with different sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, occupation, and social class) to resemble the involvement of different social media users in a firestorm. The sampling criterion was a social media user. In each focus group session, one of the authors served as the moderator, while another author facilitated the session.

At the beginning of the study, participants were asked to provide their consent for participation and for the session to be recorded. The images of the three posts that ignited the firestorm against Audi, H&M, and Uber, were shown to the participants. They were first asked to read the posts and share their initial views on the posts. Then, the number of tweets, likes, and comments from other social media users were shown to the participants and they were asked to share their views on the posts again. Finally, the participants were invited to discuss the incident with other participants. The participants were allowed to freely discuss the issue and share their views to simulate natural conversation. A few questions were asked to moderate the discussion, such as “What have you observed from the posts?” “What are your initial perceptions after reading the post?” “Will you spend time reading the post if it was shown on your newsfeed?” “Have you taken any mental shortcut in judging the incident?” and “What would you do if you found this post on your newsfeed?” Participants were debriefed and thanked at the end of the session.

A total of 28 participants were recruited for the focus group study. Seven online focus group sessions, which consisted of four participants per session, were conducted. Each session lasted for approximately one hour. Table B4 in Appendix B presents the details of the participants.

## 5 Data Analysis

## 5.1 Survey Study

We validated the measurement and structural models using partial least squares (PLS) analysis with SmartPLS 4 (version 4.0.8.7). We conducted three analyses to assess the potential threat of common method bias. Results of the tests showed that common method bias was not a major issue in this study (see Appendix C Tests for Common Method Bias). We modeled number, strength, immediacy, argument quality, moral intensity, firm wrongness, vindictive complaining, and patronage reduction as reflective first-ordered constructs, and social impact as a formative second-order construct. Overall, the assessments of the measurement model were satisfactory (see Appendix C, Assessment of Measurement Model section). We performed a bootstrapping analysis with 5,000 subsamples to test the significance levels of the path coefficients in the research model. Figure 2 shows the PLS results.

The research model explained a substantial amount of the variance in the endogenous variables. The obtained path coefficients and levels of significance indicated that the overall research model was supported. In particular, the model explained 10.4% of the variance of argument quality, 36.3% of the variance of moral intensity, 52.8% of the variance of firm wrongness, 15.1% of the variance of vindicative complaining, and 16.4% of the variance of patronage reduction. After running the PLSpredict, we obtained $Q ^ { 2 }$ values that were all above zero for the endogenous variables. Taken together, the results substantiate the predictive validity and predictive relevance of the research model.

Regarding the heuristic mode of information processing, social impact $( \beta = 0 . 4 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ had a positive and significant relationship with firm wrongness, supporting H1. Regarding the systematic mode of information processing, argument quality $( \beta =$ $0 . 3 5 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and moral intensity $( \beta = 0 . 1 3 7 , p <$ 0.01) had positive and significant relationships with firm wrongness, supporting H2 and H3. Regarding the interplay between the two information processing modes, social impact had positive and significant relationships with argument quality $( \beta = 0 . 3 2 2 , p <$ 0.001) and moral intensity $( \beta = 0 . 6 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting H4a and H4b. Furthermore, the standardized beta weights for social impact and moral intensity on firm wrongness were statistically significantly different from each other (Cumming, 2009) (see Appendix C, Assessment of Relative Effect between the Heuristic and Systematic Processing Modes section). Also, social impact positively moderated the relationships between argument quality and firm wrongness $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and between moral intensity and firm wrongness $( \beta = 0 . 1 0 5 , p <$ 0.01), supporting H5a and H5b. Finally, the perception of firm wrongness had positive and significant relationships with vindictive complaining $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 9$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and patronage reduction $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 9 , p <$ 0.001), supporting H6 and H7.

The control variables of gender, age, education, ethnicity, social media usage, and trait moral identity had no significant relationships with endogenous variables. Trait moral identity had a significant relationship with patronage reduction $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 4 , p <$ 0.001). Self-relevant consumer-brand relationship had a significant relationship with vindictive complaining $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . A post hoc analysis, which shows the results for the hypotheses testing across the datasets of Audi, H&M, and Uber, is shown in Table C3 in Appendix C. We discuss the observations in Section 6.1.

No significant differences were found among the subjects randomly assigned to each of the eight experimental conditions. The manipulation check for social impact was performed by asking the subjects three true or false questions about whether the negative comments and reactions were (1) posted by social media users who have a strong relationship with them, (2) made by a large number of social media users, and (3) recently posted or shared on the online social network. The manipulation check for argument quality was performed by asking the subjects one true or false question about whether the negative comments contained claims and backing (i.e., statistics and data). Finally, the manipulation check for moral intensity was performed by asking the subjects one true or false question about whether the practice of the firm included the use of harmful substances in their packaging. The subjects were also asked to answer a perceptual scale of the constructs. All of the manipulations worked as anticipated. Most subjects (95%) were able to provide a correct answer to the true or false questions, and there were significant differences in mean values between the low and high conditions. ## 5.3 Focus Group Study

The recordings from the focus group study were transcribed by one of the authors, resulting in over 15,000 words of data for subsequent analysis. The data was analyzed based on three broad foci of questions posed during the session: (1) participants’ initial observations and perceptions of the incident, (2) their processing mode and evaluation of the firestorm information, and (3) their responses to the incident and the targeted firm. Through the analysis of the transcribed data and observation of the sessions, different findings regarding the three foci above were revealed.

First, considering the application of the heuristic information processing, we found that participants’ evaluations were shaped by the overall sentiments and the heuristics from the posts (e.g., the number of Likes and Retweets), aligning with the findings of the quantitative studies. For instance, in one session, one of the participants did not initially form a negative attitude toward the presented social media firestorm incident. However, the participant’s negative evaluation of the targeted firm was later shaped by the sentiments and reactions from the rest of the participants (three out of four) in the same group. Specifically, Emily (G1) said, “I am annoyed … this H&M incident has given me a very negative impression of the company; it has affected me so much!” After three of the participants expressed similar reactions, Xixi (F4), who originally did not have any issue with the advertisement, changed his mind, “Now it has given me a problem as well … I would show it to everybody and say it’s terrible!” These observations provide direct evidence about the effect of heuristic cues (i.e., social impact in terms of number and immediacy) on the judgment of firm wrongness.

Second, considering the application of systematic information processing, we found that participants evaluations involved deliberate cognitive effort and an attempt to comprehend the messages and posts. Specifically, in one focus group, all participants (four out of four) held strong and negative opinions toward the incidents and were very articulate and clear on what they perceived and how they evaluated the events. For example, Edison (D2) indicated that the advertisement was “utterly offensive and repulsive and it should not be allowed due to the social and ethical controversies embedded in the advert. It is just wrong, simple as [that]!”

Further, reading the comments from the original posts and listening to the discussions from the focus group aided in a thorough evaluation of the wrongness of the firms. For example, Shera (E1) stated: “Looking at these comments, the whole thing made me feel worst because the comments are quite negative. I feel more and more that the companies did something wrong, something that is not politically and culturally correct. And when I see these comments, it makes me even more convinced because it seems that they had made a mistake.” Molly (B3) echoed: “At the start, I did not read the post carefully, and I could see no problem; once I started reading the comments from others then I do feel offended and feel the firm has done something really wrong.” While it was revealed that the Uber incident was due to a malicious social media user, a few participants were still reluctant to accept that Uber had no responsibility for what happened and elaborated on how negligence from Uber hurt the victim and the wider society. Minho (A2) stated: “The Uber incident is just wrong, why do they use that word? The word has been notoriously known to be a contemptuous term for a Black or dark-skinned person.” The comprehension of others’ arguments and the evaluation of the nature of the moral violation of the incident during the focus group studies offered further evidence regarding the direct influences of systematic processing on the judgment of firm wrongness.

Finally, the focus group study also offered an explanation for the nonsignificant relationship between perceptions of firm wrongness and patronage reduction in the Audi dataset. The participants felt that because Audi is a luxury brand, customers may develop a sense of attachment to the brand, making them loath to consider other options. Therefore, a social media firestorm against the brand would not necessarily discourage consumers from purchasing an Audi because of the brand’s symbolic meaning and reputation for product quality. Taken together, we argue that the divergence in the avoidance response could be related to the product categories (e.g., luxury vs. necessity products). Compared with H&M and Uber, representing necessity products, Audi’s products are generally considered to be luxury products and Audi’s (potential) customers may have more difficulty identifying substitutes of similar quality. The results suggest that while individuals’ perceptions of firm wrongness often lead to retaliatory behaviors against firms in social media firestorms, avoidance behaviors (i.e., patronage reduction) are less likely for firms in luxury, highquality product categories, where acceptable alternatives are not always accessible or desired. In addition to discussing negative responses to firms implicated in a firestorm, participants from the focus groups also suggested potential constructive responses. For example, Taz (C4) explained: “I would not take it to social media, but I will contact the companies directly to tell them the error of their ways.” A few participants shared that they would likely respond by starting a conversation with friends and family about the incident instead of criticizing the targeted firm in public.

## 6 Discussion

This study aims to understand how individuals process information, judge firms, and respond to firms in social media firestorms. In the following subsections, we synthesize the findings from the three studies to offer an integrated discussion on the effects of heuristic and systematic information processing on perceptions of firm wrongness and on negative responses to firms in social media firestorms. Then, we highlight the implications for research and practice. We conclude the paper by addressing limitations and suggesting future research directions.

## 6.1 Key Findings

Our studies answered the primary research question and addressed the three research objectives. Specifically, our findings show that social impact, argument quality, and moral intensity positively influence perceptions of firm wrongness. The heuristic mode of information processing not only exerted a stronger influence on judgment outcome, compared to the systematic mode of information processing, but also biased the valence and judgment outcomes of the latter. We also found that the perception of firm wrongness led to two negative responses to the targeted firm. We discuss our key observations and their implications below.

## 6.1.1 The Heuristic Mode of Information Processing in Social Media Firestorms

Our quantitative studies revealed that the heuristic information processing mode played a key role in shaping individual judgment and negative responses to the targeted firm in a social media firestorm. In particular, social impact had a positive and significant effect on firm wrongness, suggesting that individuals rely on heuristic cues (e.g., the number, strength, and immediacy of influencing sources) in their evaluation of encountered social media firestorm incidents. Considering the results in the dataset of H&M, the relationship between social impact and firm wrongness was positive and significant, whereas the one between moral intensity and firm wrongness was nonsignificant. We suspect that these unexpected results with the H&M dataset, compared with those from the alternative datasets, could be attributable to the fact that H&M’s transgression was first publicized by celebrities, leading their followers and other social media users to heuristically respond to the incident under the influence of high social impact. This also replaced the use of cognitive deliberation to analyze the moral intensity of the transgression by the individuals themselves. The analysis of the focus group study provides further qualitative support and insights into the quantitative findings above. Specifically, participants who did not initially form a negative attitude toward the presented social media firestorm incident were shaped by the sentiments and reactions from the rest of the participants and changed their evaluations of the targeted firm.

## 6.1.2 The Systematic Mode of Information Processing in Social Media Firestorms

Our quantitative studies showed that the systematic information processing mode also had a significant effect on individuals’ judgment outcomes concerning firm wrongness, despite being relatively weaker than its heuristic counterpart. Argument quality and moral intensity both had positive and significant effects on firm wrongness, suggesting that individuals devote cognitive resources in order to comprehend the argument quality of firestorm-related messages and to evaluate the salient moral attributes of the valuerelated transgression. The analysis of the focus group study provided additional evidence for the use of the systematic information processing mode in the judgment process and outcomes regarding social media firestorms.

## 6.1.3 The Interplay between the Heuristic and Systematic Mode of Information Processing in Social Media Firestorms

Our quantitative studies demonstrated the relative effects of the two processing modes and how heuristic information processing influences systematic information processing in two ways. Specifically, the survey findings show that the heuristic mode has a stronger effect on judgment-making (as indicated by a stronger standardized beta weight) and can dominate or replace the use of the systematic processing mode. Further, the experiment results showed interaction effects between social impact and moral intensity (argument quality). The findings further demonstrate that the abundance of heuristic cues (i.e., high social impact) in the social media environment can bias the systematic processing mode by exaggerating the valence of systematic assessment and its judgment outcome. The findings are consistent across the three datasets—in each of them, social media users attributed the responsibility to the targeted firm, regardless of the cause of the transgressions.

## 6.1.4 Negative Responses to Firms in Social Media Firestorms

Our quantitative studies showed that the higher the level of firm wrongness that individuals perceived, the higher the likelihood that they would retaliate and avoid the targeted firm. These results suggest that social media firestorms lead to both fight-and-flight responses toward firms. However, about half of the participants claimed that they would not stop buying products from the targeted firm. While firestorms may not have a significant impact on firm sales, the focus group study revealed that social media firestorms do affect firms’ reputations and consumers’ identification with the brand.

## 6.2 Implications for Research

This study advances the literature in several ways. First, drawing on the heuristic-systematic model of information processing, we explain the psychological mechanisms underlying individuals’ information processing and judgment-making in social media firestorms, advancing the social media firestorm literature. The findings support our proposition and confirm the effects of both information processing modes and the salience of the heuristic mode in influencing individuals’ moral judgment and responses toward firms in social media firestorms. Specifically, our findings show a stronger effect of the heuristic mode of information processing on wrongness judgments. We found that the heuristic mode not only directly and strongly influences moral judgment in social media firestorms but also indirectly influences judgment through the systematic mode by biasing the valence of cognitive deliberation in judgment making. The strength of the heuristic mode suggests that social impact in the social media environment serves as well-learned inferential rules that enable social media users to make judgments using mental shortcuts and is thus attributable to the contagion of firestorms. Whereas the existing literature has predominately explained the emergence of social media firestorms via message virality (e.g., Herhausen et al., 2019) and emotional contagion (e.g., Matook et al., 2022) perspectives, our study offers an alternative and novel theoretical explanation for the emergence of social media firestorms from the information processing perspective.

Second, we tested the versatility of HSM in explaining how individuals process information both heuristically and systematically in the context of social media firestorms. HSM was initially developed as a theoretical lens to explain how individuals receive and process persuasive information and messages and the resulting changes in attitudes and behaviors. This theoretical lens has been extensively used by IS researchers to study the adoption of online review messages (e.g., Cheung et al., 2012; Watts & Zhang, 2008), where source credibility and message quality represent the heuristic and systematic modes, respectively. Attempts have also been made to study the theory in other contexts, such as crowdfunding (Wang et al., 2021) and information security (Amblee & Bui, 2014). This study not only confirms the importance of argument quality in the systematic mode but also represents a pioneering attempt to contextualize HSM into the novel context of social media firestorms. We proposed and tested three context-specific variables— namely social impact, moral intensity, and firm wrongness—to represent the two information processing modes. Moreover, we investigated the relative effects of the two information processing modes. Previous studies have found that the systematic mode is consistently more influential and dominates the use of the heuristic mode (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019). In contrast to previous findings, we reveal the dominance of the heuristic mode in processing information in social media firestorms and demonstrate how it biases the valence of systematic processing and its subsequent judgment outcome. Our study thus contributes to the dual information processing literature by directing future studies to reexamine the relative and interaction effects of the two information processing modes in novel contexts where social cues are abundant, such as social media.

Finally, we tested the research model in the context of three well-known social media firestorms, which spanned various product categories, business sectors, and firestorm causes. This approach enhances the external validity of our study. Furthermore, we tested our research model using survey, experiment, and focus group studies, enhancing the overall internal validity. In particular, while the findings concerning the effects of social impact, argument quality, and moral intensity are generally consistent across the survey and experiment studies, a few hypotheses were nonsignificant when the dataset was split into three subsets and tested separately. For instance, the relationship between moral intensity and perceptions of firm wrongness was nonsignificant in the H&M dataset. Further, individuals also responded differently to the firm’s transgression. The nonsignificant effect between perceptions of firm wrongness and patronage reduction in the Audi dataset suggests that individuals may not reduce their interaction and business with a firm targeted in a firestorm, despite the transgression committed by the firm. Our findings suggest that individuals’ judgment and response to a firm’s transgression in a social media firestorm are contingent on other variables, such as the type of transgression, the nature of the brand, and the knowledge and involvement of the individuals. Our findings point to the need for future research to identify boundary conditions of the relative effects of the two information processing modes and their influence on individuals’ judgments and responses.

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

Our research model of social media firestorms explains how individuals process information, arrive at a moral judgment, and respond to value-related transgressions committed by firms. It provides practitioners with a guide to understanding of the emergence of social media firestorms and insights into coping with them. Our findings show that while both information processing modes are used by individuals to evaluate and respond to social media firestorm incidents, the heuristic mode assumes a predominant role. Consistent with the least effort principle, our findings indicate that the effortless heuristic approach appears to be the default mode for individuals to process information in social media firestorms. The abundance of heuristics cues afforded by system artifacts can be readily used by individuals who are inclined to conserve cognitive resources to make a quick wrongness judgment against the targeted firm.

Based on our findings, firm response strategies could be derived from two approaches in relation to an individual’s information processing preference. On the one hand, to address individuals who primarily rely on heuristics to process information on social media platforms, firms should consider well-established crisis communication strategies, such as reducing the offensiveness of the issue and offering corrective actions. These strategies should aim to address the accusation in a timely and relevant manner while keeping the communication concise. Complex explanations are often ineffective in the social media sphere because they require significant cognitive effort to comprehend and can be easily bypassed. By avoiding lengthy and unengaging messages, the core messages can be more readily disseminated and received across the social network.

On the other hand, for existing customers, firms should provide comprehensive communications and explanations internally and individually. This strategy assumes that existing customers will devote cognitive resources to process information shared by the firm because of their involvement with the firm. By addressing the issue and concerns at an individual level, this approach can be particularly effective for transgressions that were unintentional or beyond the firm’s control, such as in the case of Uber. The firm faced backlash on social media after a malicious user intentionally used a derogatory term as their display name, tricking the company’s chatbot into tweeting an inappropriate response. By providing detailed explanations to existing customers, firms can help them better understand the situation and retain their trust in the company.

While social media firestorms may come and go swiftly, their negative impact on firms can persist over time. As our findings show, perceptions of firm wrongness can lead social media users and customers to retaliate against firms through either vindictive complaining, which further deteriorates firms’ corporate image, or patronage reduction, which diminishes sales performance. Vindictive complaining on social media may decrease over time because engagement in retaliatory behaviors requires individuals’ continued effort and resources (Grégoire et al., 2009). Thus, firms should prioritize organizational resources to address the negative impact of reduced patronage. As discussed above, communications that explain the situation to existing customers is a potential strategy that can be employed to retain existing customers and minimize the negative impacts induced by social media firestorms.

This is particularly important for firms of necessity products (e.g., H&M and Uber) whose customers tend to have more bargaining power due to the availability of substitutes. In other words, it is easy for customers to find a substitute for H&M (e.g., Zara) or Uber (e.g., Lyft). However, as suggested by the nonsignificant relationship between firm wrongness and patronage reduction in the Audi dataset, firms selling luxury products appear to be less vulnerable to the threat of reduced patronage. This finding may be due to consumer perceptions that the exclusivity, exquisiteness, and pleasure provided by certain luxury brands is difficult or impossible to replicate via substitution with another brand. Both our quantitative and qualitative findings support this notion.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our study has some limitations that suggest promising research opportunities. First, we tested our research model using self-reported data. Therefore, our datasets captured only a snapshot of the respondents’ perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors in social media firestorms. Future studies could benefit from leveraging objective and log data for the use of natural language processing and social network analysis approaches to analyze how social media users receive and process information and messages related to social media firestorm incidents and how they are influenced by the social heuristics with which they are presented.

Additionally, the datasets were collected in crosssectional settings. Future studies could benefit from using longitudinal studies capable of providing additional insights into both the short-term and longterm detrimental impacts of social media firestorms on targeted firms and their associations with the different modes of information processing. For instance, moral judgments made using heuristic information processing might lead to short-term attitudinal and behavioral changes in individuals, whereas judgments made using systematic information processing might lead to long-term impacts. Identifying the lasting effect of the negative impacts of social media firestorms for firms has significant managerial implications that cannot be easily derived from cross-sectional studies.

Furthermore, we suggest that high social impact takes time to accumulate and its effect on perceptions of firm wrongness will vary across different stages of the development of social media firestorms. Specifically, social impact may have less of an effect on perceptions of firm wrongness at the initial stages of a firestorm, when the number of comments and reactions from users may still be quite low. However, the social impact effect will likely become stronger over time as the number and strength of the impact sources grow. Future studies should endeavor to detect and monitor the development of social media firestorms to observe and track the potential accumulation of social impact and test its longitudinal effects on judgment outcomes across different stages of firestorms.

Finally, while we tested our research model using datasets across three different social media firestorms involving different product categories, business sectors, and firestorm causes, the three chosen firestorms involved Western firms and focused on valued-related transgressions. Generalizations of our findings to other cultures and regions should thus be handled with great care. Specifically, expectations of business ethics are not universal. For instance, Western consumers may have different standards and norms than their Eastern counterparts. Therefore, researchers should also explore differences in individuals judgments and responses to social media firestorms across different cultural orientations (e.g., individualism vs. collectivism), targeting different entities (e.g., firms vs. celebrities vs. ordinary individuals), and involving different transgressions (e.g., value related vs. performance related).

## 7 Conclusion

Drawing on the heuristic-systematic information processing model, we developed and tested a research model explaining how users process social media firestorm information, make judgments, and respond to firms targeted in firestorms. We validated the hypotheses using survey and experiment studies and derived additional insights from a focus group study to provide additional qualitative insights. Our findings show that both heuristic and systematic modes of information processing play important roles in users’ moral judgment making in social media firestorms, with the heuristic mode being predominant. The heuristic mode was also found to interact with the systematic mode and influence judgment making as well as bias the value of systematic processing. Contrary to the existing literature on dual information processing, where the systematic mode typically dominates, our findings show the dominance of the heuristic mode in the context of social media firestorms. These findings have significant implications for research devoted to understanding and intervening in social media firestorms as well as research on the applicability and boundary conditions of the heuristic-systematic model of information processing. Our findings also provide pragmatic guidance for firms and marketers seeking to tackle social media firestorms and mitigate their adverse impacts on firms.

## A Brief Overview of the Dual-Process Models of Information Processing in the Information Systems Literature

HSM and ELM share great similarities in their theoretical premises, positing that an individual’s attitude can be shaped by two routes of informational influence, the heuristic (peripheral) route and the systematic (central) route. The two information processing routes are different in the amount of thoughtful information processing demanded of individuals, with the heuristic route involving less cognitive effort (Majchrzak & Jarvenpaa, 2010). Differences, however, have been observed in the conceptualizations of focal constructs representing these two information processing modes in existing IS studies. Varying conceptualizations of dual-process models of information processing in the IS literature are summarized in Appendix A (see Table A2).

Such differences in conceptualizations have been more evident in discussions of the heuristic route. For example, while source credibility has been modeled to represent the heuristic route in information technology acceptance (e.g., Bhattacherjee et al., 2008) and in online consumer reviews (e.g., Cheung et al., 2012) contexts, a broader set of constructs have been used adopted and contextualized in other contexts, such as source social connectedness, source trustworthiness, source expertise, message framing, message consistency (e.g., Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019) and source factors (e.g., Kim & Benbasat, 2009) in e-commerce contexts, and rules restricting information release in studies of IS security (e.g., Majchrzak & Jarvenpaa, 2010).

Similar patterns have been observed regarding the varying conceptualizations and representation of focal constructs in the systematic route, despite a noticeable exception of the construct, argument quality. It has been used consistently to represent and capture the systematic information processing across different contexts, including information technology acceptance (Bhattacherjee et al., 2008), online consumer reviews (Cheung et al., 2012), and web personalization (Ho & Bodoff, 2014). However, different conceptualizations have also been used to capture the systematic route to fit specific research contexts, including the perceived quality of SAP simulation game in a study of team-based gamified training (Kwak et al., 2018), task dependence (Majchrzak & Jarvenpaa, 2010) and the design of training programs (Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010) in IS security studies. Finally, while some researchers have not proposed a fixed set of constructs to capture the two information processing routes, they have manipulated experimental conditions to manifest such differences, including manipulating the argument framing (Angst & Agarwal, 2009) and profile information (Cummings & Dennis, 2018).

To conclude, while dual process models of information processing such as HSM and ELM have been extensively adopted in existing IS studies, varying conceptualizations have been used to represent and capture these two information processing routes and to contextualize the concerning IS/IT. Similarly, in this study of social media firestorms, we argue that further contextualization is needed to derive specific insights into individual judgments and negative responses to the targeted firms in social media firestorms.

## Experiment Design

We conducted two online experiments with a 2 (social impact, high vs. low) × 2 (argument quality, high vs. low) and 2 (social impact, high vs. low) × 2 (moral intensity, high vs. low) between-subjects factorial design to test the proposed hypotheses. Social impact was manipulated by the strength of the importance of other social media users to the participant, the number of other social media users who have responded to the incident, and the recency of their reactions. Argument quality was manipulated by the quantity of claims and backing contained in the negative comments. Moral intensity was manipulated by the nature of the wrongdoing committed by the firm. Table B3 shows screenshots of the images leading to the fictitious firestorms.

Specifically, in our manipulation, we asked participants to indicate the names of their close friends, family members, or favorite celebrities and politicians to represent high strength, and we asked participants to indicate the names of their acquaintances and strangers to represent low strength. We used 940,000 Likes, 450,000 Retweets, and 300,000 Comments to represent the high number, and we used 9 Likes, 4 Retweets, and 3 Comments to represent the low number. We used the posting time of within a few seconds to represent high recency and the posting time of within a few weeks to represent low recency. We included claims and backing in the negative comments to represent high argument quality and included no claim or backing in the negative comments to represent low argument quality. Finally, we used a purported wrongdoing of a firm packaging food using toxic plastics to represent high moral intensity and used a purported wrongdoing of a firm packaging food using excessive plastics to represent low moral intensity.

We conducted a pilot test with 106 subjects recruited from Prolific prior to the main experiment to assess the appropriateness and realism of the experimental stimuli. Subjects were instructed to go through the scenario and were assigned to one of the eight firestorm posts. They were asked to imagine that the incident was found in their newsfeeds and then rated on the realism of the scenario and judged the severity and timeliness of the incident and the persuasiveness of the comments.

The result showed that the scenario was perceived to be real (mean = 5.60) and that there was a significant difference between the two incidents with respect to perceived moral issues (i.e., use of toxic plastic packaging vs. use of excessive plastic packaging) (t(55.422) = 10.856, p < 0.001), perceived timeliness (i.e., posted a few seconds ago vs. posted a few weeks ago) (t(47.184) = 10.682, p < 0.001) and perceived persuasiveness of the comments (i.e., arguments with claims and backing vs. argument with no claim and backing) $( t ( 1 0 4 ) = 1 3 . 7 4 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

## Appendix C: Analysis of the Survey Study

## Tests for Common Method Bias

First, we conducted a Harman’s single-factor test using a principal component analysis for each dataset. The first factor accounted for less than 50% of the variance. In other words, the items in the dataset loaded significantly onto more than one principal component, indicating no single dominant factor (Harman, 1976). Second, we assessed the correlations between the principal constructs and a marker variable (i.e., running habit in this study) (Lindell & Whitney, 2001). Common method bias exists when all (or most) constructs are highly correlated, including the marker variable, in the correlation matrix. The correlations of the marker variable were trivial or low, suggesting that common method bias did not likely pose a threat to this study. Third, as suggested by Pavlou et al. (2007), we examined the correlation matrix. Extremely high correlations $( \mathbf { e . g . } , r > 0 . 9 )$ typically indicate the threat of common method bias. However, there were no extremely high correlations in the correlation matrixes, and the presence of low correlations indicated that no single factor influenced all of the constructs (see Table C2).

## Justifications for Using PLS-SEM for Data Analysis

PLS is a component-based approach used to produce estimates with minimal restrictions on data distribution. According to Hair et al. (2016), compared to CB-SEM, PLS-SEM has its own merits and capacity to handle the following data and model characteristics. In terms of the data characteristics, PLS-SEM has no distributional assumption because it is a nonparametric method. The methodological literature further highlights that when prior knowledge of the structural model relationships is scarce or when the emphasis of the study is exploratory, PLS-SEM is superior to CB-SEM. Furthermore, the dependent variables in our study were nonnormally distributed, where firm wrongness <sub>Kolmogorov-Smirnov</sub> <sub>test,</sub> $D = 0 . 0 9 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; vindictive complaining <sub>Kolmogorov-Smirnov</sub> <sub>test,</sub> $D = 0 . 0 9 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ patronage reduction <sub>Kolmogorov-Smirnov</sub> <sub>test,</sub> $\mathbf { D } = 0 . 0 9 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Finally, PLS-SEM is well-suited for handling research models with second-order constructs (e.g., social impact in our study). Based on the above methodological guidelines and reasons, we used the PLS-SEM approach to test the research model. Following the two-step analytical approach (Hair et al., 2016), we performed a psychometric assessment of the measurement model, followed by an evaluation of the structural model. This approach ensured that the conclusions of the structural model were drawn from a set of measures with desirable psychometric properties.

## Assessment of Measurement Model

Assessing the measurement model of reflective constructs includes evaluations of reliability, convergent validity, and discrimination validity. Reliability refers to the internal consistency of the measurement items, and it is assessed using (1) Cronbach’s alpha and (2) composite reliability (CR). As shown in Table C1, the Cronbach’s alpha and CR for all of the reflective constructs were above 0.7, meeting the recommended threshold (Hair et al., 2016). Convergent validity is the extent to which the items on a scale are theoretically related. Convergent validity is assessed using two criteria: (1) the average variance extracted (AVE) should be at least 0.5, and (2) all of the item loadings should exceed 0.7 (Hai et al., 2016). As illustrated in Table C1, all of the latent constructs exceeded the recommended thresholds, indicating adequate convergent validity. Discriminant validity is the degree to which a scale measures the variable it intends to measure. Discriminant validity is assessed using two criteria: (1) the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlation (HTMT), and (2) the square root of the AVE for each construct (Hair et al., 2016). The HTMT values for the majority of the constructs were below the conservative threshold value of 0.85. The square roots of each of the AVEs were larger than the correlations between the AVE and all of the other constructs (see Table C2a), indicating adequate discriminant validity.

Assessing the measurement model of the formative second-order construct includes the evaluation of evaluations of convergent validity, multicollinearity, and the significance and relevance of the reflective first-order constructs. The assessment of convergent validity involves redundancy analysis testing to determine whether the formative construct is highly correlated with its reflective global measure. The results of the analysis showed that the path coefficient between formative construct of social impact and its global measure was higher than 0.7, indicating adequate convergent validity. Then, we assessed the collinearity using the value of the variance inflation factor (VIF). As we were dealing with a reflective-formative type of second-order construct, inner VIF values were employed to assess collinearity. We evaluated the constructs of strength, immediacy, and number for collinearity as predictors of socia impact. As illustrated in Table C2b, the VIF values of all the predictor constructs were less than the prescriptive diagnostic of 3.3, therefore collinearity was not an issue. Finally, we assessed the weights of the constructs of strength, immediacy, and number. The results suggest that all of the weights were above the recommended value of 0.1 and the three first-order constructs had significant t-values that provided further support for retaining all of the constructs.

## Assessment of Relative Effect between the Heuristic and Systematic Processing Modes

In order to test the relative effects (1) between social impact and argument quality and (2) between social impact and moral intensity, the standardized beta weights were compared. We conducted a bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples to obtain their corresponding 95% confidence. The beta weights were considered statistically significant from each other when the confidence intervals overlapped by less than 50% (Cumming, 2009). First, we tested the relative effects between social impact and argument quality. Half of the average of the overlapping confidence intervals was calculated (0.0415) and added to the social impact beta weight lower bound estimate (0.372), which yielded 0.413. As the argument quality upper bound estimate of 0.441 exceeded the value of 0.413, the difference between social impact and argument quality $( \Delta \beta = 0 . 0 9 7 )$ was not considered statistically significant. Then, we tested the relative effects between social impact and moral intensity. Half of the average of the overlapping confidence intervals was calculated (0.0402) and added to the social impact beta weight lower bound estimate (0.372), which yielded 0.412. As the moral intensity upper bound estimate of 0.22 exceeded the value of 0.0412, the difference between social impact and moral intensity $( \Delta \beta = 0 . 3 1 3 )$ was considered statistically significant.

## Manipulation Checks

The manipulation check for social impact was performed by asking the subjects three true or false questions on whether the negative comments and reactions were (1) posted by social media users who have a strong relationship with them, (2) made by a large number of social media users, and (3) recently posted or shared on the social network. The subjects in the high social impact condition answered “true,” and all those in the low social impact condition answered “false” to the three questions. The results suggest that the manipulation for social impact was successful. Further, the manipulation check for moral intensity was performed by asking the subjects one true or false question on whether the practice of the firm included the use of harmful substances in their packaging. The subjects in the high moral intensity condition answered “true,” and all those in the low moral intensity condition answered “false” to the question. The results suggest that the manipulation for moral intensity was successful. Finally, the manipulation check for argument quality was performed by asking the subjects one true or false question on whether the negative comments contained any claim and backing. The subjects in the high argument quality condition answered “true,” and all those in the low argument quality condition answered “false” to the question. The results suggest that the manipulation for argument quality was successful.
