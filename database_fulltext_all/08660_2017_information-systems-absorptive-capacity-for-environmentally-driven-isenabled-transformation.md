# Information systems absorptive capacity for environmentally driven IS-enabled transformation

Vanessa Cooper\* & Alemayehu Molla

School of Business IT and Logistics, RMIT University, Melbourne, Australia, email: vanessa. cooper@rmit.edu.au

Abstract. The potential of information systems (IS) to enable environmenta sustainability necessitates an understanding of how organisations can realise this potential. In this study, building on the absorptive capacity theory and following a multi-disciplinary and multi-method approach, we propose that developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mechanism to deliver IS-enabled change that addresses environmental issues. We commenced with a literature review and exploratory interviews to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the IS-environmental absorptive capacity construct. We then developed a model that proposes that sustainable IS triggers, knowledge exposure and prior experience in<sup>fl</sup>uence IS-environmental absorptive capacity, which in turn contributes to the level of environmentally sustainable IS assimilation as well as to the cost saving, operational performance and reputation of organisations. The model was <sup>fi</sup>rst tested through an international survey of 148 senior IS managers. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings support our model regarding the antecedents and value of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. A follow-up case study corroborated the survey results and provided additional insights into the nature and causes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its value. This study, in addition to presenting empirical evidence, de<sup>fi</sup>nes and operationalises the IS-environmental absorptive capacity construct in a theoretically and operationally meaningful way. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: environmentally sustainable IS, green IS, absorptive capacity, IS assimilation

## INTRODUCTION

This paper addresses the capabilities required by organisations to address environmentally driven, information system (IS)-enabled change. IS plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in helping organisations address issues of environmental sustainability (Chen et al., 2008). To date, IS researchers have developed and investigated environmental sustainability-related IS practices such as ‘green information technology (IT)’, ‘green IS’ and energy informatics (Chen et al., 2008; Dedrick, 2010; Watson et al., 2010). IS researchers have also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the organisational and institutional forces that facilitate or inhibit the adoption and diffusion of green IS and green IT (Kuo & Dick, 2009; Chen et al., 2011). Some have developed frameworks to analyse the green IT and green IS capability of <sup>fi</sup>rms (Bose & Luo, 2011; Molla et al., 2011). Research directions and agendas have also been established (Malhotra et al., 2013). This <sup>fi</sup>rst wave of research has been bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to bring the challenges of environmental sustainability to the IS discipline, establish the credibility and relevance of the IS profession in tackling globally important issues and provide useful guidelines to practitioners. However, how organisations develop their capability to leverage IS for environmental sustainability and how environmental sustainability knowledge is assimilated and used by IS departments<sup>1</sup> to add value to both the IS function as well as the wider enterprise need a thorough investigation.

IS departments are at the forefront of providing thoughts, tools and structures that change the way organisations behave and do their business differently (Pawlowski & Robey, 2004). As a result, they are expected to create IS innovations for environmental sustainability ‘that provide the capacity to incorporate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant environmental costs into prices; design corporate sustainability reporting systems that achieve the goal of a more sustainable society; implement ef<sup>fi</sup>cient approaches… to promote green purchasing decisions and develop standards for data processing and storage that minimise their environmental consequences’. [Watson et al., 2012, pp. 29–30] To successfully meet these added responsibilities, IS departments must in<sup>fl</sup>uence organisational environmental initiatives and transformations (Elliot, 2011). They must also incorporate environmental sustainability considerations in the IS infrastructure and practices (Melville. 2010: Watson et al.. 2010: Melville & Whisnant 2014), develop IS innovations that provide environmental bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Watson et al., 2012; Venable et al., 2011) and accumulate green IS capital (Chuang & Huang, 2015). Reviews of the literature reveal that there is a lack of understanding of how such capability can be nurtured and developed and of what variables in<sup>fl</sup>uence the process (Elliot, 2011; Malhotra et al., 2013). Indeed, there is an underlying assumption that existing IS knowledge and skills in system development and design might suf<sup>fi</sup>ce to address environmental sustainability and that IS functions can effectively discharge these obligations which, as we will demonstrate in this study, is not usually the case.

In this paper, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne and operationalise IS-environmental absorptive capacity. This is a speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the concept of absorptive capacity introduced by Cohen & Levinthal (1990) that has been shown to be context speci<sup>fi</sup>c and exist at both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and organisational uni levels (Lane et al., 2006; Roberts et al., 2012). Absorptive capacity has also been used to understand organisations’ ability to respond to the challenges of climate change and innovate for environmental sustainability (Gluch et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2014; Pace, 2015). Our central thesis is that the success of environmentally driven IS-enabled change can be enhanced at the IS and organisational levels when IS departments develop thei ability to learn and effectively use sustainable IS knowledge to transform unsustainable organisational and social practices (Elliot, 2011). This leads to three important research questions: (1) What is IS-environmental absorptive capacity? (2) What in<sup>fl</sup>uences ISenvironmental absorptive capacity? (3) What is the value of developing IS-environmenta absorptive capacity?

In order to answer the research questions, our research draws from three increasingly inter-related <sup>fi</sup>elds – IS, management and environmental sustainability, which we review in the next section. In addition, because the research questions focus on different aspects of the IS-environmental absorptive capacity phenomenon, we adopted a research methodology incorporating three sequential multi-method empirical studies comprising exploratory interviews, a survey and a case study. A multi-method empirical approach combining both exploratory and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory investigations was required because IS for environmental sustainability is a relatively recent undertaking (Corbett, 2013; Chuang & Huang, 2015), poses a major challenge for most organisations in general and for their IS departments in particular and calls for a ‘holistic understanding’ (Venkatesh et al., 2013). The multi-method approach enabled us to develop rich insights into IS-environmental absorptive capacity that could not be fully understood by using either quantitative or qualitative methods alone (Venkatesh et al., 2013). The exploratory interviews were necessary because the practice of IS for environmental sustainability is an emerging phenomenon and there was a lack of understanding of what constitutes absorptive capacity in the context of environmentally sustainable IS. We therefore undertook ‘developmental’ (Venkatesh et al., 2013) exploratory interviews to answer the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, understand the knowledge domain of IS-environmental absorptive capacity and develop research hypotheses to enable the second and third research questions to be answered. To test the theoretical model, a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory approach was required. We undertook both a quantitative survey and a follow-up case study for this purpose. While the survey allowed us to test the hypotheses, the case study corroborated the survey results and provided further insight into the inter-relationships between the hypothesised concepts. Our mixed-method approach therefore enabled us to not only uncover the components of IS-environmental absorptive capacity but also unravel its nature, causes and outcomes.

Theoretically, our research is a notable contribution to a descriptive and analytic theory (Gregor, 2006) of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Although different models of absorptive capacity and its antecedents and outcomes have appeared in previous research (e.g. Lane et al., 2006; Gluch et al., 2009), only a few have focused on describing IS departments’ absorptive capacity for leveraging IS for environmental sustainability and how IS departments learn and utilise environmental sustainability knowledge (Cooper & Molla, 2012b, 2014). The general processes of absorptive capacity could be common, but as our research revealed, the type of IS related environmental knowledge that organisations need to learn and put to use, some of the mechanisms of doing so and the organisational and institutional challenges that in<sup>fl</sup>uence these processes as well as the outcomes are different.

Practically, our mixed-method research produced holistic and valuable insights that foster IS-enabled environmental transformations. The exploratory interviews identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four domains of IS knowledge for environmental sustainability that are crucial to renewing IS departments capability and position them to drive IS-enabled environmental transformations. The global survey showed what facilitates and inhibits the development and value of IS-environmental absorptive capacity proposed in the exploratory interviews. Through the case study, we were able to corroborate the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings and provide additional insights into the nature and causes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its outcomes.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows: <sup>fi</sup>rst, we examine the absorptive capacity literature. Second, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne IS-environmental absorptive capacity and develop a model of its nomological network of antecedents and outcomes. Third, drawing on the results of the literature review and exploratory interviews, we develop hypotheses to test the IS-environmental absorptive capacity construct. Fourth, we describe the research methods and present the results for the survey and the follow-up case study. Fifth, we discuss the results and contributions to theory and practice. We conclude with the limitations and avenues for future research.

## BACKGROUND LITERATURE

In order to foreshadow the development of IS-absorptive capacity for environmental sustainability, we draw insights from literature in the management, environmental sustainability and IS disciplines. The focus, consistent with our three research questions, is to uncover the concept of absorptive capacity and unravel its antecedents and outcomes.

## The concept of absorptive capacity

While scholars have shown a great interest in absorptive capacity, as indicated in Table 1, there is no consensus as to how absorptive capacity should be developed. The absorptive capacity of a <sup>fi</sup>rm is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the ability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to recognize the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends’. [Cohen & Levinthal, 1990, p. 128] Zahra & George (2002) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that absorptive capacity comprises potential absorptive capacity and realised absorptive capacity. Potential absorptive capacity includes a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capability ‘to identify and acquire externally generated knowledge that is critical to its operations … and develop routines and processes that allow it to analyze, process, interpret and understand information obtained from external sources’. [Zahra & George, 2002, p. 189] Realised absorptive capacity includes the transformation and exploitation of existing and the newly acquired and assimilated knowledge (Zahra & George, 2002). Other scholars de<sup>fi</sup>ne three constructs that make up absorptive capacity, For example Lane et al. (2006) identified recognising and understanding new external knowledge (exploratory learning), assimilating valuable external knowledge (transformative learning) and applying assimilated external knowledge (exploitative learning).

Absorptive capacity in the environmental sustainability literature follows the generic process de<sup>fi</sup>nition of external knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation (Gluch et al., 2009; Delmas et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the type of knowledge and competencies of absorptive capacity for environmental sustainability is speci<sup>fi</sup>c. Such knowledge includes managerial awareness and staff know-how in relation to stakeholder environmental demands and legislation, using cleaner technologies (e.g. solar panels, low-emission glass) and environmental practices (e.g. life cycle analysis) and the environmental performance of suppliers and partners (Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999; Del Rio, 2005; Roy & Thein, 2008).

Like the management and environmental sustainability literature, much of the IS literature adopts the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of absorptive capacity as the process of learning about new innovation through external knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation (Roberts et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2013). Some of the IS-related absorptive capacities include the ability to understand, assimilate and effectively use IS functionalities (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006; Park et al., 2007; Lyengar et al., 2015). Others include managerial IS knowledge and communication channels for knowledge transfer (Harrington & Guimaraes, 2005; Lyengar et al., 2015), IS knowledge creation and sharing (Arnold et al., 2010) and task-technology knowledge (Deng et al., 2008). ## Antecedents of absorptive capacity

Management researchers consistently advocate that the breadth and depth of exposure to external knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002; Tortoriello, 2015) as well as <sup>fi</sup>rms’ environmental commitment and culture (Delmas et al., 2011) in<sup>fl</sup>uence the predilection of an organisation to explore new knowledge and develop absorptive capacity. Cultures arise from the interaction of a range of factors including corporate commitment to social responsibility, the presence of environmental advocates or ‘green champions’ and the level of workforce participation, representation and empowerment (Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999).

The environmental sustainability literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es that the existing environmental knowledge base and organisational efforts and culture towards environmental knowledge acquisition (Roy & Thein, 2008) are associated with the formation of absorptive capacity. Similarly, environmental boundary scanners and green champions are associated with developing absorptive capacity (Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999). For many organisations, as environmental innovations often rely on knowledge that is outside of their traditional area of expertise, sourcing external information on environmental technologies and co-producing environmental solutions with business partners is particularly important for building absorptive capacity (Pace, 2015).

A number of factors have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as antecedents of IS-related absorptive capacity, including organisational support and commitment (Park et al., 2007; Hu et al., 2012), corporate culture (Harrington & Guimaraes, 2005) and knowledge sharing and co-ordination processes (e.g. IT–business communication) (Roberts et al., 2012). Other antecedents include pre existing related knowledge, information trading and knowledge brokering and interorganisational knowledge transfer (Malhotra et al., 2005; Lindgren et al., 2008; Lyengar et al., 2015). Notably, the IS literature recognises the role of IS artefacts in the creation of absorptive capacity. Thus, IS artefacts – such as IS-enabled knowledge bases (Deng et al., 2008) and IS that integrate inter-organisational processes – contribute to an organisation’s absorptive capacity and inter-organisational transfer of knowledge (Roberts et al., 2012; Lyengar et al., 2015). Further, IS education factors (e.g. new technology education) and IS capabilities (e.g. investment in IS infrastructure) have been found to increase absorptive capacity (Saraf et al., 2013). Table 2 provides a summary of the antecedents of absorptive capacity taken from a selection of articles across the three domains.

## The outcomes of absorptive capacity

In the management literature, the outcomes of absorptive capacity have been broadly measured in terms of improved levels of innovation (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002; Lane et al., 2006; Tortoriello, 2015), knowledge output (Lane et al., 2006), strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility (Zahra & George, 2002), <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Zahra & George, 2002; Lane et al., 2006) and competitive advantage (Zahra & George, 2002).

In environmental sustainability, Delmas et al. (2011) found that absorptive capacity contributes to proactive environmental strategy, which consists of environmental reporting, regulatory proactivity, operational improvements, environmental partnerships and competitive advantage, such as cost control, enhanced reputation and innovation. Further, absorptive capacity for environmental sustainability improves the responsiveness of companies towards sustainability issues (Roy & Thein, 2008) and leads to both incremental and radical green innovation performance through enhanced exploratory and exploitative green learning (Chen et al., 2014). It has also been found to stimulate process engineering to understand waste and emission-creating processes and undertake ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and waste-minimisation measures (Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999) as well as adopt environmental innovations for energy ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Pace, 2015).

In IS, researchers have consistently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a positive relationship between absorptive capacity and IS assimilation (Roberts et al., 2012; Cooper & Molla, 2014). This is because if an organisation has a high absorptive capacity in a particular domain, this will increase the ability of that organisation to assimilate complex innovations in that same domain (Fichman & Kemerer, 1999). Yet the relationship between absorptive capacity and IS assimilation is dualistic and complex in nature such that IS capabilities enhance absorptive capacity, which in turn mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of IS capabilities on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance – either directly or indirectly by shaping other dynamic capabilities. IS-related absorptive capacity also contributes to knowledge outputs (e.g. IS competence, knowledge conversion and knowledge creation), IS-based outcomes (e.g. IS assimilation, business–IT alignment, rationality in IS planning) (Roberts et al., 2012), <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (e.g. customer service, operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, operational effectiveness and <sup>fi</sup>nancia growth) (Roberts et al., 2012; Lyengar et al., 2015) and developing dynamic capability (Hu et al., 2012).

## IS-ENVIRONMENTAL ABSORPTIVE CAPACITY: CONCEPTUAL MODEL AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

In this section we address our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, that is, what is IS-environmental absorptive capacity and develop the research hypotheses to investigate research questions 2 and 3. As noted in the earlier sections of this paper, what constitutes the content of absorptive capacity is domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c. To uncover the knowledge domain of environmentally sustainable IS, we extracted from our exploratory interviews as reported by Cooper & Molla (2012a, 2012b). Appendix 1 (Table A1) contains the participants’ details used in this paper. The results suggest four sustainable IS knowledge areas as described in Table 4.

We examine IS-environmental absorptive capacity at the IS level and consider it as a sub-set of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s absorptive capacity. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne IS-environmental absorptive capacity as

the capability that organisations and their IS departments continuously develop and nurture to effectively learn and utilise sustainable IS knowledge including the principles of environmental sustainability, and the IS solutions, strategy and evaluation for environmental sustainability to succeed in environmentally driven IS-enabled change.

Thus de<sup>fi</sup>ned, IS-environmental absorptive capacity is a second-order construct composed of four <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs – sustainable IS knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation. Table 5 provides the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of these constructs and points to the items used to operationalise each.

Nomologically, we hypothesise that three sustainable IS antecedents are associated with the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity, which in turn enhances IS and organisational outcomes. Figure 1 depicts the structure of our conceptual model and the ensuing sections develop the research hypotheses.

## Antecedents of IS-environmental absorptive capacity

Three antecedents are related to IS-environmental absorptive capacity – sustainable IS exposure, sustainable IS prior experience and sustainable IS triggers – as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Table 6.

## Sustainable IS exposure

Sustainable IS exposure refers to an IS department’s scope of connections with external sources that bring <sup>fl</sup>ows of information and knowledge on environmental sustainability principles and on IS strategy, solutions and evaluation for environmental sustainability. For many organisations, environmental initiatives have been found to require knowledge from sources other than their regular scanning activities (Roy & Thein, 2008; Pace, 2015). Boundary spanning and exposure to sustainable IS knowledge sources such as research laboratories, universities, governmental agencies, trade associations, competitors and consultants enable recombination of broad knowledge sources within and across unit boundaries and enhance absorptive capacity in general and knowledge acquisition and assimilation in particular (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Van den Bosch et al., 1999).

## Sustainable IS prior experience

Sustainable IS prior experience re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent of knowledge, know-how and skills accumulated by an IS department about the principles of environmental sustainability and the IS strategy, solutions and evaluation for environmental sustainability. Absorptive capacity is path dependent as it is related to prior experience (Zahra & George, 2002). Firms with a greater breadth in related knowledge are more likely to absorb and appreciate the value of any information that is encountered (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Fichman, 2001). Models of absorptive capacity have continued to recognise prior related experience as an antecedent to absorptive capacity and to the knowledge acquisition and assimilation activities in particular (e.g. Zahra & George, 2002; Gluch et al., 2009). Likewise, the existing environmental knowledge base and organisational efforts towards environmental knowledge acquisition are associated with the formation of absorptive capacity in environmental sustainability (Roy & Thein, 2008; Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999). IS departments can gain prior sustainable IS experience from past success, from benchmarking their environmental performance against other organisations (Garvin, 1993), through the conduct of market research (Fahey, 1999) and through collaboration with other <sup>fi</sup>rms (Lane & Lubatkin, 1998). These practices were highlighted in the exploratory interviews, as was the path dependency of IS-environmental absorptive capacity, wherein the success of green IS initiatives motivated further and more challenging initiatives:

We’ve been doing this [sustainable IS] for two years [and] a lot of the easy stuff has sort of been done. So getting rid of say CRT monitors and double sided printing, it’s really basic stuff. Now we’re a little bit challenged…we’re starting to look at how our IT is architected and that type of initiative takes a little bit longer, a little bit more deeper thinking I suppose. [Sustainability Strategy Manager, SSM1]

On the basis of the above we hypothesise the following:

H 2. Sustainable IS prior experience is positively associated with ISenvironmental absorptive capacity.

## Sustainable IS triggers

Sustainable IS triggers refers to the level of external motivation, incentives and coercion on an IS department to learn about environmental sustainability principles and the IS strategy, solutions and evaluation for environmental sustainability. While valuable (or potentially valuable) external environmental sustainability IS-related knowledge may exist, this in itself is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for IS departments to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Research in absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Lane et al., 2006), organisational environmental strategy (Porter & Van der Linde, 1995) and environmentally sustainable IS (Molla & Abareshi, 2012) has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a range of external triggers that compel a <sup>fi</sup>rm to respond to external stimuli. These include policy incentives, legislation and other socioinstitutional pressures (Vickers & Cordey-Hayes, 1999; Delmas et al., 2007). For example the introduction of a carbon tax might compel organisations to implement a carbon management system (Molla & Abareshi, 2012). Technological shifts (Zahra & George, 2002), such as those towards ‘green data centres’, server virtualisation and cloud computing can activate IS departments’ desire to learn about sustainable IS strategy, solutions and evaluation. Competitive and economic opportunities that stem from green marketing and the demand for green products and services (Gluch et al., 2009), such as energy-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient IT products can trigger organisations to learn about sustainable IS. Likewise, the desire to address social and moral obligations (Paulraj, 2008), such as reducing electronic waste and the associated negative impacts on the environment, can trigger sustainable IS learning and action. The importance of each of these external triggers in driving organisations to develop

IS-environmental absorptive capacity was also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the exploratory interviews. For example one manager believed that the introduction of regulation and legislation was required to compel organisations to persist in their endeavours to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity:

Sometimes we lose people [when educating clients about how to use our carbon management system] because it’s like, ‘it’s too hard’. So that is coming back to my initial point about compliance pressures force people to start thinking about it [learning to use IT for green] because it is now voluntary, it is only the progressive companies that are doing something about it. [Operations Manager, OP1]

This leads to the following hypothesis:

H 3. Sustainable IS triggers are positively associated with IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

## The outcomes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity

Theoretically, the value of IS-environmental absorptive capacity can be observed at the IS (through IS-enabled environmental sustainability innovation adoption and assimilation), organisational (through operational and market bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) and bio-spherical (through the quality of the environment as described by Elliot (2011)) levels. In this study. because two of the most widely identi<sup>fi</sup>ed outcomes in the application of absorptive capacity in IS research are IT assimilation and IT business value (Roberts et al., 2012), we focused only on two outcomes, that is, IS (henceforth IS outcomes) and organisational (henceforth organisational performance) levels, as described in Table 7. This choice is consistent with Watson et al.’s (2012, p. 30) recommendations to start environmental sustainability research and action by tackling tractable issues and scaling up as experiences develop. Thus, we provide a foundation for future research to test the relationship between these constructs and environmental leve outcomes.

## IS outcomes

IS outcomes refer to the extent to which environmentally sustainable IS technologies and practices are assimilated both in improving the sustainability of the IS infrastructure (i.e. green IT) and in using IS to improve the sustainability of the wider organisation (i.e. green IS). These technologies and practices include IS that incorporate environmental costs into prices, support corporate sustainability reporting and promote green purchasing decisions (Watson et al., 2012). They also encompass server and storage virtualisation and data centre air<sup>fl</sup>ow and power management (Bose & Luo, 2011; Watson et al., 2010), electronic waste management (Widmer et al., 2005) and personal computer power management (Jenkin et al., 2011). IT policies and procurement practices that consider environmental criteria in both procuring and disposing IT, along with the use of IS for environmental sustainability (Chen et al., 2011), are important re<sup>fl</sup>ections of IS level outcomes. Likewise the diffusion of environmentally sustainable IS innovations for energy, <sup>fl</sup>eet and infrastructure management, for communication and collaboration, for sustainability knowledge exchange and for sustainability performance reporting (Dwyer & Hasan, 2012; Melville & Whisnant, 2014) re<sup>fl</sup>ect the IS level outcomes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

The exploratory interviews indicated that to assimilate IS to improve environmental sustainability, IS departments acquire new external knowledge and skills that extend beyond their traditional focus. This knowledge was acquired through training and consultant engagement and then combined with their existing knowledge to modify their IS practice according to environmental criteria. This practice includes green IT and green IS assessment; how to design IS to monitor, improve and communicate environmental performance and how to redesign or retro<sup>fi</sup>t to green data centres and other applications. This learning was important to assimilate green IT and green IS practices. For example one manager said:

So whether you are procuring IT for yourself or providing it to your client, you are making changes to your thinking. In terms of our own development of our own technology, I think that the main implications for us would be not in the way we develop but what we include in the software. So it is kind of like ‘how do we actually shift the functionality of what the software does to provide the green IT outcome?’ [Operations Manager, OP1]

This leads to

H 4. IS-environmental absorptive capacity is positively related to IS outcomes.

## Organisational performance

Researchers have consistently found empirical support for a positive relationship between absorptive capacity and organisational performance (see section on ‘The outcomes of absorptive capacity’). Likewise, we propose that IS-environmental absorptive capacity enhances two forms of organisational performance, comprising both ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency impacts (operational performance) and competitive impacts (market performance) (Melville et al., 2004). Developing this capability can deliver sustainability value to stakeholders, reduce costs increase pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, enhance legitimacy (Hart & Milstein, 2003) and enable sustained competitive advantage (Dao et al., 2011). It can also spur new green IT/IS products and services that generate increased sales for an organisation (Melville, 2010). This was evidenced in the exploratory interviews. For example the sustainability strategy manager of a large IT <sup>fi</sup>rm, in describing the operational and market bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity, highlighted that their research and development has resulted in new IS-environmental sustainability-oriented products and services that, following internal testing, are offered to external customers:

We apply what we offer to customers internally, just of course to prove it works, and we can then take our own [green IS] experiences and take them out to our customers. [Sustainability Strategy Manager, SSM1]

Thus:

## H 5. IS-environmental absorptive capacity is positively associated with organisational performance.

IS outcomes in<sup>fl</sup>uence business value at both the intermediate process level and the organisation-wide level (Melville et al., 2004; Roberts et al., 2012). Likewise the assimilation of IS for environmental sustainability can directly or indirectly help to reduce the adverse environmental impacts of various business activities (Chen et al., 2008). It can also generate business value and is positively correlated with the market value of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s shares (Nishant et al., 2011). Researchers opine that through intensive use of IS solutions for sustainability, organisations may develop capabilities for using IS in an integrated way to address their operational emissions and develop and deploy processes to build a brand that appeals to environmentally conscious clients (Florida & Davidson, 2001). Evidence from a case study of a leading IT company (SAP) indicates that when organisations assimilate IS for environmenta sustainability, they achieve both operational and market performance (Seidel et al., 2014) Operational performance involves transforming the sustainability of an organisation’s operations, whereas market performance involves developing IT solutions to enable the sustainability of customers (Seidel et al., 2014). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our exploratory interviews provide further evidence that the assimilation of IS for environmental sustainability can contribute to organisational performance:

The three main outcomes [of implementing the company’s green IS programme] is the way we enhance, maintain or uphold our reputation in being not only a sustainable organisation internally, but also a company that can help customers with their sustainability goals. So that’s the reputation outcome. The next one is around our operations internally. So ensuring that we are not wasteful, that the energy that we consume is as ef<sup>fi</sup>cient as possible. So that’ the operational outcome.... and the last one is the way that we innovate and market and position our services and our products to help customers achieve their environment objectives … [Sustainability Strategy Manager, SSM1]

Therefore:

H 6. IS outcomes from IS-environmental absorptive capacity are positively related to organisational performance.

## THE SURVEY RESEARCH

The survey research was intended to empirically test the research model. It involved IS managers in Australia, New Zealand and the USA.

## Generation and evaluation of measurement items

The survey data used in this paper were extracted from a larger survey (Cooper & Molla, 2014). Out of the larger survey, 39 items were developed to measure the constructs used in this paper. To ensure content validity (Straub et al., 2004), the items went through two rounds of card sorting involving four domain experts – three associate professors and a lecturer. The experts were chosen for their research expertise in absorptive capacity, organisational learning, green IS and survey design. Experts were asked to group the items in relation to the variables that they are supposed to measure and identify where revision to the items was required. This process resulted in the revision of the wording of some items. The resulting questionnaire was then submitted for ethics approval to a university ethics committee. The 39 items along with supporting evidence can be found in Appendix 2 (Table A2).

In defining the measurement models, we modelled some constructs as reflective and others as formative. Constructs are neither inherently formative nor re<sup>fl</sup>ective (MacKenzie et al., 2011), and there is no de<sup>fi</sup>nite answer to the question of when to measure a construct re<sup>fl</sup>ectively or formatively. Criteria such as causal priority between an indicator and its latent variable and whether or not indicators represent a sample are adopted in IS research. We therefore modelled sustainable IS exposure as formative on the basis that items were phrased to tap into IS departments’ connection to market forces, professional associations and government that lead to exposure to sustainable IS knowledge. Sustainable IS prior experience was modelled re<sup>fl</sup>ectively as an IS department’s prior experience with sustainable IS causes lessons learned, benchmarks and accumulation of data from market research, and together they represent the consequences of prior experience. Sustainable IS triggers was modelled formatively on the basis that the items tap into the external motivational, incentive and coercion causes of sustainable IS and sustainable IS learning. IS-environmental absorptive capacity was modelled as a second-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective-formative construct. This is because according to the existing literature (section on ‘Background Literature’), absorptive capacity is treated as a second-order construct composed of three or four <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs. IS outcomes was modelled re<sup>fl</sup>ectively as the indicators tap into the extent to which IS departments have developed attitude, policy, practices, technology and governance. Organisational performance is treated as a second-order formative-formative construct because the indicators form two <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs of operational and market outcomes.

Respondents were asked to give responses to each question on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = ‘very low’ to 5 = ‘very high’.

## Sample design and data collection

The sample frame for the survey was organisations with 100 or more employees in Australia, New Zealand and the USA. We targeted large organisations because the exploratory interviews identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that these typically utilised a wider range of formal and informal IS learning processes and would potentially provide more insights into IS-environmental absorptive capacity. In each of the sample organisations, the chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) or senior IT manager or equivalent was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a potential respondent. The list of email contact addresses (1958 from Australia, 542 from New Zealand and 2500 from the USA) was purchased from a commercial database provider OneSource (http://www.onesource.com). After screening the list for compliance to the sampling criteria, a total of 5000 IT managers were invited to participate, but 1092 (21.84%) invitations experienced delivery failure. The initial invitation was followed up with three rounds of reminders, and after 3 months 179 responses were received (109 Australia, 21 New Zealand, 14 USA and 35 not speci<sup>fi</sup>ed), with 148 of these useable. As the response rate was less than 4%, generalisation to the wider population can only be done with caution. Most of the respondents were CIOs (28.4%) or IT managers (52%). ## Data analysis and results

The data analysis proceeded in three stages – data screening, instrument validation and hypothesis testing. We used Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) for validating the instrument and testing the structural relationships. PLS-SEM was chosen because (a) it is suitable for testing complex relationships involving hierarchical component models/higher-order models; (b) we see our work as developing a construct and testing its predictive ability, which is more <sup>fi</sup>tting to the use of PLS; and (c) PLS is often the most preferred structural equation modelling technique when there are a mix of re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative measurement models. We note that Goodhue et al. (2012) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three issues with the application of PLS. These are (a) the use of the 10 or 5 times rule to determine the sample size adequacy; (b) the choice of PLS with the assumption that it is particularly robust for small sample sizes $( n < 9 0 )$ ; and (c) choosing PLS with data that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly vary from normal distribution. In light of our sample size and data distribution, which are detailed in Section S1 in the Supporting Information, these concerns are not issues for our study.

Detailed descriptions of the steps taken to check the quality of the data and validate the psychometric properties are included in the Supporting Information. The instrument validation analyses show that the measurement model is valid and reliable (Table 9). All indicators load to their focal construct higher than to any other construct, and there is no indicator that loads to a second construct with a cross-loading difference smaller than 0.15. The loadings are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. All of the re<sup>fl</sup>ective measures have loadings greater than 0.7, average variances extracted $> 0 . 5$ square root of Average Variance Extracted (AVE) larger than the off-diagonal correlation and composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha values > 0.7. These provide evidence of the validity and internal consistency of the measures. All but one of the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) values of the formative (<sup>fi</sup>rst and higher-order) constructs are less than the criterion of 3.3 as suggested by Petter et al. (2007), and all are less than the criterion of 5 as suggested by Hair et al. (2010). Further, all have a tolerance level > 0.20. Although the outer weights of Trigger1 (TRG1) and Trigger3 (TRG3) are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, they are retained in the model as their absolute contribution (outer loading) is > 0.50 and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (TRG1 = 0.59, $\scriptstyle t = 4 5 . 9 5$ and TRG3 = 0.69, t = 47.43).

The structural model was tested by using PLS-SEM. To estimate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, 5000 sample bootstrappings (Hair et al., 2014) were performed. The mode (Table 10) explains 59%, 61% and 52% of the variance in IS-environmental absorptive capacity, environmentally sustainable IS assimilation (IS outcomes) and organisational outcome (organisational performance) respectively. These $R ^ { 2 }$ values indicate that the model has moderate to substantial power to explain the three endogenous variables. The predictive relevance $( Q ^ { 2 } )$ of the path model was assessed through a PLS-blindfolding procedure. Al $Q ^ { 2 }$ values (Table 10) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from zero, indicating that the model has a large predictive relevance (Hair et al., 2014, p. 184) and a very good utility for explaining IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its IS and organisational outcomes. Additional descriptive results are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S9).

Most of the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients (which are standardised regression weights) are high and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and <sup>fi</sup>ve of the six hypotheses are supported. All the three theorised antecedents, that is, sustainable IS exposure, prior experience and triggers, have been found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (support for H1, H2, H3), of which prior experience has relatively less signi<sup>fi</sup>cance (95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence). The $\boldsymbol { f }$ and $q ^ { 2 }$ values indicate the contribution and predictive relevance of the exogenous variables to their respective endogenous variables respectively. While sustainable IS triggers have a medium effect size and predictive relevance, sustainable IS exposure and prior experience have small effects and predictive relevance. This implies that instead of prior experience from involvement in joint actions and collaboration projects with other organisations and conducting market research on green IT products and services, the desire to address social and moral obligations and the availability of new technology have propelled organisations to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

In terms of the extent to which IS-environmental absorptive capacity routines were developed, the descriptive analysis indicates that the most widely developed routine is IS departments’ ability to acquire external sustainability knowledge from relationships with stakeholders such as customers and suppliers, with 38% of respondents rating it high or very high. On the contrary. the least widely developed routine in IS-environmental absorptive capacity is IS departments’ ability to acquire new external knowledge by sending IS personnel to complete green IT training. This was rated as high or very high by only 15% of respondents. This difference is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Tukey’s Honestly Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant Difference Test: mean difference = 0.69, standard error = 0.2409, p = 0.001).

Two potential outcomes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity were tested in this study – IS and organisational. In terms of the IS outcomes, 45% of survey respondents reported to have developed some sort of governance mechanisms that de<sup>fi</sup>ne the administration of green IT initiatives. Another 45% had policy frameworks to apply environmental sustainability criteria throughout the IT value chain from sourcing through operations and services to end of life (of which less than 10% rated this as high or very high). Sixty-one per cent of respondents had started building an environmentally friendly IT infrastructure (but only 26% rated this outcome as high or very high). Forty-eight per cent had gone beyond policy and implemented green practices in IT sourcing, operations and end-of-life management (again less than 20% considered these practices to be highly or very highly developed). The structural model test <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that IS-environmental absorptive capacity signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contributes to and has a medium effect on and predictive relevance to IS outcomes, that is, the assimilation of environmentally sustainable IS (H4). Further, the assimilation of environmentally sustainable IS is positively related to organisational performance and has a medium effect and predictive relevance (H6).

Further, to explore the relationship between IS-environmental absorptive capacity and organisational performance which was not supported (H5), a post hoc analysis was undertaken. In the post hoc analysis, when the IS outcome variable was removed from the path model, the relationship between IS-environmental absorptive capacity and an organisation’s realisation of cost savings, enhanced operational performance, reputation and market competiveness becomes signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. This indicates that IS outcome fully mediates the relationship between IS-environmental absorptive capacity and organisational outcome.

## FOLLOW-UP CASE STUDY

The follow-up case study was undertaken to corroborate the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings and obtain additional insights into the nature and causes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its outcomes. In our survey, in the interest of parsimony, we focused mainly on triggers and exposure to sources of sustainable IS knowledge outside of an organisation. Therefore, in our follow-up case study, we also wanted to obtain insight into other forms of sustainable IS triggers and exposure that are external to an IS department but internal to an organisation tha an IS department serves.

## Case selection

We sought a case that was consistent with our survey sample that targeted IS departments servicing large organisations. We also sought a case that would exemplify the circumstances of IS departments striving to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity (Yin, 2014; Bryman & Bell, 2011). Therefore, we chose an IS department (henceforth ‘the department’) within a large Australian educational institution ‘EdInst’ (a pseudonym as required by our approved research ethics protocol). The department had a complex IS operation supporting over 40 000 users, with an operational budget of over \$A50 million and consisting of 500 staf members (300 full-time, 200 contractors) and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed our criterion to study a large IS department. It also satis<sup>fi</sup>ed our second case selection criterion as we had prior knowledge of the department’s information and communications technology (ICT) sustainability practices and performance through participation in ICT sustainability community of practice meetings. We were also aware that the department was ranked, based on a benchmark of IS departments in educational institutions in the Asia–Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region, as an average performer in terms of its green practices.<sup>2</sup> Further, EdInst had an environmental sustainability strategy in place, along with a <sup>fl</sup>agship environmental sustainability programme to cut energy and water use and greenhouse gas emissions. The case therefore offered the opportunity to provide substantive insights into an IS department’s journey of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

## Data collection

Case study data were collected through interviews and documents. The interviews were semi structured and conducted with the senior leadership team of the department, namely the CIO and the directors of the six divisions within the department, that is, Applications, Architecture, Client Computing, Infrastructure, Engagement and Delivery and Service Management (see Appendix 3, Table A3). These senior managers were purposively selected because they have in-depth knowledge of both strategic and operational activities across the entire department. In preparation for the interviews, we accessed secondary data sources from the wider institution’s website. We also obtained documents pertaining to the department and its strategy and sustainability orientation. This information provided a form of triangulation with data collected from the interviews. For example participant statements about EdInst’s business and sustainability strategies were veri<sup>fi</sup>ed via organisational documents. A summary of the documents is provided in Appendix 4 (Table A4).

During the interviews participants were provided with the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the key concepts covered in the survey. Interview questions centred on eliciting participants’ assessment of (1) the four domains of sustainable IS knowledge and to what extent and how both the department and their speci<sup>fi</sup>c divisions develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity; (2) the factors that have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the IS-environmental absorptive capacity of the department; and (3) the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (if any) that had been realised from developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity. The interviews lasted between 60 and 80 min each. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed by a professional transcription service provider, generating 168 pages of text.

## Data analysis

Each transcript was analysed via a content analysis (Kripendof, 2012). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a deductive qualitative content analysis was conducted given that our purpose was to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings and provide further explanation of IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its antecedents and outcomes (Mayring. 2000: Elo & Kyngäs. 2008). Each interview was first read in its entirety to make sense of, and become immersed in, the data (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008). Next, three categorisation matrices were constructed to correspond with the three research questions (Mayring, 2000; Elo & Kyngäs, 2008). The initial categories were based on the model of IS-environmenta absorptive capacity (Figure 1) and the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of its constructs (i.e. Tables 5–7). The categorisation matrices were unconstrained, whereby additional matrices and categories were created when new concepts emerged inductively from the data (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008). Extracts from the categorisation matrices are provided in the Supporting Information (Section S4, Tables S10(a)– 10(d)), and sample quotes that connect the categorisation matrices back to the data are incorporated in the case results in the next section.

## Case results

The case study corroborated the model of IS-environmental absorptive capacity (Figure 1) and survey results and provided additional insights.

## IS-environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst

The mangers collectively believed that the four areas of sustainable IS knowledge are relevant However, whereas the principles of environmental sustainability were considered to be highly relevant to all six divisions, the relevance of strategy, solutions and evaluation for environmenta sustainability was dependent on the functions of each division. For example:

So I think that the principles [of environmental sustainability] is just generally something I think that people should be aware of what’s happening and that their IT is a big part of that, and I don’t think that really varies between an Application Group versus Service Management or Infrastructure… [but] the measuring, it would be more relevant for Client Computing and Infrastructure probably than for the Applications who sit on that hardware. [Director, Application Division]

While this functional relevance is perhaps unsurprising, the varied levels of sustainable IS knowledge across divisions impacted the department’s ability to provide environmentally sustainable end-to-end customer solutions. For example a key responsibility of the Architecture Division is to govern the design of IS, which are then delivered and supported by the other divisions. While the Architecture Division believed that it has a relatively high sustainable IS knowledge for the principles, strategy and solutions, this was not the case when it came to evaluation. Consequently, the Architecture Division lacked insight into the outcomes of the systems they design:

I’d like to say it [sustainable IS evaluation] is relevant but that is a little bit inspirationa because often times Architecture don’t get deep enough into the projects to see what happened at the end. [Director, Architecture Division]

The sustainable IS knowledge acquisition, assimilation and utilisation processes were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed as central to developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Generally, the case revealed an ability to acquire externally held, internally held and individually held sustainable IS knowledge and that the processes and mechanisms used to acquire sustainable IS knowledge from these three sources differed. Acquisition of externally held sustainable IS knowledge, consistent with the survey result, was achieved mainly through market research, relationships with external stakeholders and observation of other institutions. In the immediate and short term, the department worked with vendors to acquire knowledge on greening a data centre and virtualising desktops, hired an external consultant to undertake an ICT sustainability benchmarking assessment and ‘insourced expertise’ through contractual agreements to access knowledge and skills. Having acquired the initial expertise, the department endeavoured to build their own capabilities where the capability was required long term:

So one thing we’re quite good at doing as an IT function is buying expertise when we need it because we regularly have to do that for different technology projects because we won’t have the people in-house who are experts in x, y and z and then if it’s something we need over time, then we start building our skill sets. [Director, Engagement and Delivery Division]

Acquisition of internally held sustainable IS knowledge involved collaboration on projects with partners external to the department but internal to EdInst. For example the department had acquired knowledge on incorporating green metrics in software through collaboration with the Strategic Sourcing and Procurement working group during the ‘Green Print Project’. The aim of this project was to improve work<sup>fl</sup>ow, reduce costs and provide a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed, secure print environment. As the collaboration progressed, it became clear to both partners that there were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant environmental bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and the system was designed to report on the environmental impact of printing, including energy use, carbon production and tree consumption. Acquisition of individually held sustainable IS knowledge was achieved by encouraging sustainability interest groups to facilitate the sharing of knowledge that IS personnel have gained because of their passion for, and expertise in, sustainability and from their private-sphere proenvironmental actions (e.g. re-cycling printer cartridges). As one of the managers explained:

The reason why I have that knowledge is because I went, ‘I am interested in this thing. I’m going to look into it. It is something that motivates me. It’s something that I think is important. It’s a responsibility that we all have’ … Then how do I apply it here? [Director, Client Computing Division]

To assimilate the sustainable IS knowledge acquired from these three sources, the department leveraged both established and new routines. Established routines include informa and formal discussions, ‘scrum sessions’, team meetings, post-implementation reviews and internal communication forums such as newsletters and online tools. In leveraging established routines, the environmental sustainability aspect was often ‘piggybacked’ on standard approaches, as described by the Director of the Client Computing Division:

I run a Town Hall meeting every quarter where I get the whole team together and we do something thematic. The theme last time was about sustainability. It was operationa sustainability but it was on Earth Day… So the sustainability was actually tied into environmental sustainability as well [and] we tie in environmental issues into how we operate. [Director, Client Computing Division]

A new routine to enable the assimilation of sustainable IS knowledge is the establishment of an ICT sustainability community of practice. This community of practice began informally out of the convictions of a small group of IS personnel and over time became formally recognised by the department. which provided resources to undertake environmental initiatives. The community also gained formal representation on EdInst’s Sustainability Committee. Nonetheless, with so many competing demands on IS personnel, the community had been stagnating for some time but was seeking to re-launch itself at the time of our data collection in 2015:

The community of practice is not that prominent at the moment, so that ebbs and <sup>fl</sup>ows…[but it] is kind of restarting and been given some momentum again because beforehand it was kind of by volunteer labour and it’s now kind of been legitimised into something that we actually have to assign resource to of<sup>fi</sup>cially. [Director, Architecture Division]

Sustainable IS knowledge utilisation in the department focused on knowledge re-use. This involves leveraging existing competencies and knowledge that has been previously captured (e.g. in documents), packaged (e.g. through development of frameworks) and disseminated (e.g. via team meetings) during the acquisition and assimilation processes. The re-use of knowledge gained about environmental criteria to select vendors was cited as one example of sustainable IS knowledge utilisation:

So if we were to put in a cloud selection processes that has some criteria around a data centre being green, then that set of 10 questions would be re-used for every other cloud selection because the idea is re-use. [Director, Architecture Division]

Sustainable IS utilisation also focused on re<sup>fi</sup>ning and extending existing routines and competencies from a continual service improvement perspective. The director of the Architecture Division went on to explain that while his team focused more on knowledge re-use, he had implemented an after project review process to ensure that his team consistently reviewed their underpinning frameworks and approaches. Quality assurance processes were also used across the department to identify the extent to which learning from projects is integrated into future services. Where such learning had not lead to consistent service provision, the department’s risk register might be updated, triggering additional processes to address the risk:

My team steps in and does QA reviews [for the department], so if we agree sustainability is one of those things, we have learning from actions… then that would be taken across the board and we’ll say ‘well in general have we changed the behaviour?’ If not it may be added to [the department’s] risk register. [Director, Service Management Division]

Generally, the department had demonstrated a great capacity to quickly and effectively acquire sustainable IS knowledge; a reasonable accumulation of stock of sustainable IS knowledge; a reasonable capacity to assimilate the knowledge by leveraging existing routines while new routines obtain traction and established; and limited utilisation of sustainable IS knowledge. In the next section, we discuss the factors that have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the level of ISenvironmental absorptive capacity at EdInst.

## In<sup>fl</sup>uences of sustainable IS-absorptive capacity at EdInst

The three antecedents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the survey were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed to be relevant to contributing to IS-environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst. First, in terms of sustainable IS exposure, the department has strong external connections with vendors and contractors. Nevertheless, interactions with vendors are not always undertaken with environmental sustainability as the main focus, but rather, sustainability is addressed on a need by need basis as part of wider discussions:

We are actively looking for vendors that are either industry leaders or partners and bringing them in and asking them what are your plans, your trends, getting them to present their thoughts and ideas and where the industry is going. [Director, Client Computing Division]

In addition to external connections, because of the distinctive aspect of our investigation of IS-environmental absorptive capacity at an IS departmental, rather than organisational, level, internal connections with sustainability-oriented departments brought <sup>fl</sup>ows of sustainable IS knowledge. Internal connections refer to ties established to knowledge sources outside of the IS department but internal to EdInst, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Such exposure involves a two-way exchange of knowledge that is facilitated by building trust and structured engagement. In particular, the department turned to EdInst’s Sustainability Committee and Property Services Department:

I encourage the guys to go to the other departments within [EdInst] to <sup>fi</sup>nd this knowledge, given you know, we’ve got leaders in this space. [Director, Infrastructure Division]

Second, consistent with the survey results and the level of the IS-environmental absorptive capacity as discussed in the previous section, the contribution of sustainable IS prior experience is limited as indicated by one of the managers:

The exposure and the external triggers would be more prominent than the experience. [Director, Architecture Division]

Third, in terms of sustainable IS triggers, participants reported that the Australian government prior to 2013 had a relatively stronger focus on climate change and signalled the importance of environmental sustainability with the introduction of environmental targets and a price on carbon emissions. This triggered the IS department to contribute to a reduction in the organisation’s tax liability. However, with a change in government and the subsequent removal of the carbon price, the external motivation for developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity had declined.

When the Government was pushing aggressively for targets… we [were] looking at ways [of] reaching targets so it all falls into place. But when that pressure goes away there are so many other pressures on us from all local groups to do all these different things… [Director, Service Management Division]

In addition, participants highlighted the potential role of international IT frameworks to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. The CIO and directors of the Service Management and Applications Divisions suggested that if environmental sustainability were more explicitly embedded into frameworks such as Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL), Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) and Balanced Scorecard approaches, it can legitimise IS’ leadership for sustainability. For example:

Thinking about ITIL and Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (COBIT), IT people love a good framework, so if you’ve got a good framework or process, they’ll just embed it. So there is probably a way to embed some of this [environmental sustainability] without it having to be the leader setting this but just embed it into these frameworks that IT professionals just naturally gravitate towards anyway… [CIO]

While con<sup>fi</sup>rming that sustainable IS exposure, prior experience and external triggers in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst, the participants identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of factors that moderate this in<sup>fl</sup>uence. In the next section, we discuss these factors.

## Moderators of IS-environmental absorptive capacity: contextual factors

Overall, participants perceived that there was a difference between the department’s perceived capacities for vs. its actual acquisition, assimilation and utilisation of sustainable IS knowledge. This difference was attributed to contextual factors at the departmental and organisational levels. In terms of the IS department context, a ‘service provider mind-set’ was evident, whereby the perceived role of the IS department was to deliver projects requested by EdInst as opposed to being a thought leader and initiating projects, including those in the sustainability domain:

From our point of view, we exist to serve, we don’t exist to make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t. We don’t exist for any other reason other than to maintain EdInst’s ICT operations and change it when we’re asked to do so… the customer says go and build an application, we don’t try to have a conversation about sustainability. [Director, Engagement and Delivery Division]

Consequently, the department played a passive rather than proactive role in sustainability. If EdInst did not actively seek environmentally driven IS-enabled change, the department did not prioritise the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. This service provider mind set moderated the levels of in<sup>fl</sup>uence that sustainable IS exposure, experience and triggers have on IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Similarly, the maturity of the department in areas such as risk management, security and customer service impacted the decision to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity:

So if you think about the IT department in terms of it’s on a journey of sort of maturity as al places are and all departments of EdInst are, probably I think you’ll <sup>fi</sup>nd that the departmen was probably well down in that maturity score in 2010, when they started this… so then if you’re needing to do this bulk uplift in terms of where you’re headed, it’s [sustainability] probably one of the things that was de-prioritised in terms of focus. [Director, Infrastructure Division]

Essentially, participants considered that there is a trade-off between developing sustainable IS-environmental absorptive capacity and developing capability in relation to core functions of the IS department. The shared view was that once a satisfactory level of maturity was achieved in the core functions of the department, they could then turn their attention to developing capabilities in areas such as sustainability.

We’re talking about sort of, Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs. I’m in the survivor mode. I’m trying to <sup>fi</sup>nd a house and eat. I’m in the shelter and food and water. At some point I’ll get into the philosophical sphere, which is where the environmental element is unfortunately. It’s very unfortunate. It clashes with my own internal values; the organisation’s priorities. [Director, Client Computing Division]

Thus, the maturity of the IS department moderated the extent to which sustainable IS exposure, experience and triggers in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

The extent to which sustainable IS exposure and triggers contribute to the development of IS environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst is moderated by the organisational sustainability context and, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, organisational sustainability commitment, organisational sustainability performance indicators and organisational sustainability championship. First, participants reported that, even if EdInst had shown a commitment for sustainability by signing several environmental protocols and launching a <sup>fl</sup>agship sustainability programme, IS was not considered a key part of this initiative. The CIO and all six directors, consistent with their perceived role as service providers, were of the view that for the department to take environmental sustainability seriously, sustainability has to be embedded in EdInst’s business strategy:

I think the internal triggers are important. The <sup>fi</sup>rst internal trigger is the business strategy. So for us it is, ‘what are we trying to achieve as an organisation and what is our responsibility as an IT department to help the organisation reach those goals?’ Then it comes down to the internal behavioural drivers… [so] what I more look to do is, ‘do those general industry trends help me gain bene<sup>fi</sup>t to meet those internal business drivers?’ [Director, Client Computing Division]

Second, the CIO and all six directors advocated the importance of EdInst setting clear organisational sustainability performance indicators because without such mechanisms, environmental sustainability would remain low interest in the department. For example the external triggers provide signals to initiate learning about sustainability and IT’s environmental footprint. However, the department needs to be incentivised by the wider organisation to develop the capability to mitigate this:

They [EdInst’s senior management] should be building it [sustainability] into the KPI’s that they’re measuring and take advice off… if it is on their agenda then it will be on the institution’s agenda more widely. [CIO]

Third, top management’s organisational sustainability championship is important. Participants believed that if a sustainability agenda was communicated and championed by the senior leadership level of the organisation, then it was easier to justify sustainability projects and develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity to deliver them. All directors indicated that their divisions had many competing demands, from both internal and external stakeholders, and the selection of which demands could be met was often based on the choice of what was considered important by EdInst’s senior leadership. The CIO echoed this view:

I’ve worked in IT for 23 years and there’s never been any one time in my career when I thought gee we’ve got nothing to do, what will we do today? There’s always far more than we can possibly manage so how do we prioritise and that prioritisation comes from what’s important to the leadership of the organisation. [CIO]

Overall, the case study revealed that the antecedents of IS-environmental absorptive capacity are moderated by contextual factors at the departmental and organisational levels. Next, we describe the outcomes of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst.

## Outcomes

Although the level of IS-environmental absorptive capacity at EdInst was still in an early stage of development, it had yielded some IS level outcomes. Following the ICT sustainability benchmarking assessment, in order to cultivate a positive attitude, the CIO <sup>fl</sup>agged ICT sustainability as a priority area and endeavoured to develop a more systematic approach to the way the department supports sustainability at EdInst:

One of the concerns I’ve got, that we’ve been working on, is we were able to do a lot of the things that we did, because of individuals and people pushing things through. Smart, competent, capable individuals and you can’t rely on that for sustainability, because that’s kind of a one-off great win. So we’re really trying to get more of a system in place. [CIO]

Subsequently, a number of environmentally effective sustainable IS practices emerged and a more environmentally effective IS infrastructure was developed. For example an ICT maxim that stipulates EdInst’s ICT should be environmentally responsible, and a framework for reporting the environmental performance of the data centre were established.

We monitor that [ICT maxim] with basically an environmental utilisation score that we get from our data centre supplier every month. That tells us how ef<sup>fi</sup>cient we have been with our power and cooling and so on which are our biggest environmental contributors within infrastructure.... [and] we have very good scoring on that environmental index. [Director, Infrastructure Division]

In ICT sourcing, environmental factors are given weight and in ICT operation green practices are taking shape:

When we source servers, we’re looking for power consumption, network equipment, virtualisation drives are lower electricity pro<sup>fi</sup>le and therefore carbon footprint. So there’s a number of things, even just desktops and automating. When we did the Windows MOE [Management Operating Environment], little things like we made screen saver time not settable by anyone else but us, so we can control setting screen savings on in ten minutes and then actually shutting the monitors down and things like that. [CIO]

However, there was less evidence to determine the extent to which organisational-level outcomes were realised as a direct result of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity, or indirectly through achieving IS-level outcomes. Some respondents perceived organisational performance had been improved through the realisation of cost savings (e.g. through a reduction in energy consumption):

There’s certainly bene<sup>fi</sup>ts right, because we can see material savings, like I said, in energy costs typically, from the sustainability side, environmental sustainability… So in that regard, yes there’s absolutely bene<sup>fi</sup>ts there in chasing that through. ## DISCUSSION OF IMPLICATIONS FOR RESEARCH AND PRACTICE

This research set out to address three research questions: (1) What is IS-environmental absorptive capacity? (2) What in<sup>fl</sup>uences IS-environmental absorptive capacity? (3) What is the value of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity? We now summarise the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings and discuss the implications for research and practice.

## Key <sup>fi</sup>ndings

Prior green IS research recognises that exploring and exploiting IS to address the uncertainties surrounding what behaviours towards environmental sustainability to change. and how to change these behaviours, is dependent upon organisations’ capability to learn and innovate (Elliot, 2011). The theory of absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) provides insights into general learning and innovation processes. Particular absorptive capacities have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed at the bio-spherical (Mebratu, 1998), regional (e.g. developing nations) (e.g. Walz, 2010), national (e.g. Mowery & Oxley, 1995), industry supply-chain (e.g. Malhotra et al., 2005), interorganisational (e.g. Lane & Lubatkin, 1998), departmental (e.g. Jensen et al., 2005; Tsai, 2010) and individual (Park et al., 2007) levels, but not what, how and why IS departments develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity. We followed a multi-disciplinary and mixedmethod approach, to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the IS-environmental absorptive capacity construct and develop and validate a research model of its antecedents and outcomes. Based on the exploratory interviews, survey and case study <sup>fi</sup>ndings, an extended nomological net of IS-environmental absorptive capacity is provided in Figure 2 and discussed in the next paragraphs.

The key <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study include that IS departments both nurture new capabilities and leverage existing capabilities to acquire, assimilate and utilise sustainable IS knowledge, which includes the principles of and the IS strategy, solutions and evaluation for environmental sustainability. Consistent with the absorptive capacity theory, our survey research shows that sustainable IS exposure, prior experience and triggers in<sup>fl</sup>uence IS-environmental absorptive capacity, which in turn contributes to the level of sustainable IS assimilation as well as to the cost saving, operational performance and the reputation of organisations. Yet, while sustainable IS exposure and triggers provide an initial signal to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity, the case study revealed that contextual factors at the organisational and departmenta levels are required to incentivise the IS department to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity. In particular, at an organisational level, sustainability commitment, performance indicators and championship that link environmental sustainability to economic outcomes through energy bill accountability are important. Further, with regard to sustainable IS exposure, scanning for sustainable IS knowledge requires connectedness not only to knowledge sources external to the organisation but also to other sustainability-oriented departments in the organisation. Similarly, sustainable IS experience is gained from collaboration with sources both internal and external to the organisation. Exposure to. and collaboration with, these sources of sustainable IS knowledge which were not covered in the survey and which were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed during the follow-up case study may account for the unexplained variance in IS-environmental absorptive capacity. At a departmental level, case study <sup>fi</sup>ndings hint that IS departments in different institutional contexts respond differently to IS-environmental absorptive capacity. When an IS department has a low level of maturity in perceived core areas such as security, risk management and customer service and when developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity is viewed as a trade-off between developing capabilities in these core areas, sustainable IS triggers and sustainable IS exposure will have less in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity because developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity will be deprioritised. Further, when the role of the IS department is predominantly perceived and managed as providing a service instead of, for example being perceived as a value and innovation centre, top management sustainability leadership becomes critical for the IS department to prioritise the development of IS. environmental absorptive capacity.

The survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings further suggest that IS-environmental absorptive capacity increases the capability of an organisation to assimilate complex IS innovations for environmental sustainability and green practices that minimise IT’s environmental consequences and that such assimilation tends to mediate the value at the organisational level. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings also con<sup>fi</sup>rm the organisational value of green IT and green IS implementations and remove some of the uncertainties about the outcomes of environmentally driven IS-enabled transformations and the effectiveness of learning about, and with, green IS and technologies. The proportion of variance that was not explained in IS and organisational outcomes as well as the challenges faced by the case organisation to harvest bene<sup>fi</sup>ts suggests the presence of additional variables to fully understand the value of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Nonetheless, the explanatory power of the IS-environmental absorptive capacity theory to environmentally sustainable IS assimilation is higher than the explanatory power reported in a number of other studies. For example our $R ^ { 2 }$ value, being above 0.50, is higher than Chen et al.’s (2011) institutional theory-based model, Molla & Abareshi’s (2012) motivational theory-based model and Gholami et $a / . \boldsymbol { \mathsf { s } }$ (2013) managerial perception and belief-based model. While in comparing the studies one should remain mindful that there are differences in the theories and measures used, and that these studies were conducted in different time frames and in different settings, the model and hypotheses developed in this study can be used in future research on green IS assimilation.

## Contributions to practice

A key contribution of this research lies in the area of practice. First, the four areas of sustainable IS knowledge, that is, sustainable IS principles, strategy, solutions and evaluation, are relevant to prepare an IS department to develop a shared frame of reference internally and to work with others responsible for environmental sustainability externally.

Second, IS departments should pay attention to developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Prior research shows that conventional IS and organisational outcomes. such as operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and cost savings, must be seen to be achievable in order to motivate organisations and IS departments to act in sustainable ways that ultimately bene<sup>fi</sup>t the environment (Molla & Abareshi, 2012). This is typically due to challenges such as the lack of access to <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources, legitimacy and infrastructure for environmental initiatives, risk aversion in forging new collaborations around environmental innovations and scarcity of facilitation skills and skills to facilitate multi-party learning processes (van Kleef & Roome, 2007). Thus, this study provides evidence for IS practitioners that developing IS departments capacity to leverage IS for environmental sustainability can lead to IS and business outcomes.

While the costs associated with developing such capability may still result in some resistance, highlighting the IS and business outcomes might alleviate some of this.

Third, IS practitioners should not assume that their existing knowledge and skill sets are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to provide the IS-enabled change required to address future organisational (and societal) demands for environmental sustainability. IT–business alignment offers a good illustration of how the traditional knowledge and skill sets of IS practitioners and capabilities of IS departments will need to be renewed and extended to address environmenta sustainability. This is because environmental sustainability introduces a distinctive dimension to the IT–business alignment equation, and developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity can facilitate better alignment of IS with business sustainability strategies. Achieving IT– business alignment is a central and ongoing concern for IS practitioners. For organisations to succeed in using IS to drive change that is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to environmental sustainability, there should be a shared understanding amongst a diverse group of stakeholders about environmental challenges (Elliot, 2011), as well as the role of IS in addressing these challenges. The lack of a consistent approach to aligning green IT and green IS with corporate business and sustainability goals is a major problem, and focusing on the capability organisations need to develop to achieve such alignment is an important practical contribution of this research.

While IS professionals already undertake many of the routines identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this research such as desk-based research; developing inter-organisational relationships with customers, suppliers, partners and competitors; developing intra-organisational relationships with other departments in the organisation; boundary spanning and knowledge integration, sharing, and utilisation routines. in the environmental sustainability context both the knowledge content and approaches required are different. IS practitioners will increasingly need to participate in sustainability conferences; become green champions; develop environmental governance mechanisms and ‘green’ career paths; set environmental targets via key performance indicators (KPIs); and establish environmentally sustainable IS communities of practice that create the space and dialogue for environmental sustainability. Such routines require developing capabilities that enable IS professionals to be actively involved with a broader and more diverse network of actors with varied perspectives, interests and cultures spanning different levels and contexts. There are challenges associated with establishing trust and commitment between broader networks as well as the need to understand the role of IS in addressing complex environmental problems. This means developing IS departments absorptive capability for environmental sustainability is a speci<sup>fi</sup>c and complex organisational competence that is different from the development of absorptive capacity for conventiona business outcomes. Therefore, while there is overlap in the processes required to develop ISabsorptive capacity in other contexts and IS-environmental absorptive capacity, our study provides understanding of the need for IS practitioners to pay particular attention to the differences, if they wish to effectively leverage IS for environmental sustainability. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with that of Van Kleef & Roome (2007), who, in comparing the development of capabilities and competence for sustainable business management with conventional business management, found not only an overlap but also a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant divergence between these contexts.

Fourth, in investigating organisations’ use of energy and carbon management systems, Melville & Whisnant (2014) suggested that IS departments might lead efforts to enhance organisational absorptive capacity for sustainability reporting. To this end, our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of IS-environmental absorptive capacity provides insight into what processes IS departments might undertake to do so.

Fifth, to ensure that IS practitioners can deliver environmentally driven IS-enabled change, IS educators and professional associations should ensure that environmental sustainability topics are incorporated into IS curricula. Our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of four key areas of knowledge that should be covered in IS and computer science programmes adds to the work of Watson et al. (2010), Sendall et al. (2010) and Richards & Marrone (2014), who indicated the need for incorporating sustainability topics into undergraduate and graduate IS programmes. For professional associations such as the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) and Association of Information Systems (AIS), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that they are important agents for developing the absorptive capacity of organisations and IS professionals, by developing and disseminating approaches particular to developing and using IS for driving environmental sustainability (Sendall et al., 2010; SFIA Foundation, 2011).

## Contributions to research

Theoretically, our research is a notable contribution to a descriptive and analytic theory of IS environmental absorptive capacity. IS-environmental absorptive capacity and its nomological net can be considered as an analytic and descriptive theory, as it provides ‘a description of the phenomena of interest, analysis of relationships among those constructs, the degree of generalisability in constructs and relationships and the boundaries within which relationships and observations hold’. [Gregor, 2006, p. 619] Although absorptive capacity is widely recognised in the management, IS and environmental sustainability literature, we believe that this is one of the few studies to develop the concept in a green IS context in an operationally meaningful way. While the higher-level processes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity are common, what is different is the type of knowledge that organisations need to learn and put to use, the mechanisms of doing so and the organisational and institutional antecedents that in<sup>fl</sup>uence these processes. Further, as an organisation’s amount of absorptive capacity may differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly from one domain to another, it is important to understand the nuances of absorptive capacity as it applies to speci<sup>fi</sup>c domains. Pitt et al. (2011) suggested that mainstream theories currently used in IS research are inadequate to understand the role of IS in the context of eco-sustainability, and researchers need to look into new theories that can cater to the age of sustainability. To this end, our development of IS-environmenta absorptive capacity provides a valuable theoretical contribution. Further, we demonstrate the centrality of IS-environmental absorptive capacity for achieving both IS and organisational outcomes. The study therefore provides a useful response to Watson et al.’s (2010) call to the IS research community to clarify the role of IS in environmental sustainability and to engage decisively in the investigation of IS capabilities that have the potential to make business and manufacturing processes more environmentally friendly.

Second, the study contributes to IS research’s use of absorptive capacity in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c domain of environmental sustainability. Roberts et al.’s (2012) review of the use of absorptive capacity in the IS literature identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 98 papers, of which many included absorptive capacity as only a minor citation (37 papers) or as theoretical support (43 papers). Fewer papers used absorptive capacity as part of their research model (13 papers) or theoretical base (5 papers), and only two departmental-level studies gave a detailed treatment of absorptive capacity. Our research therefore contributes much-needed understanding of absorptive capacity at an IS department level. We demonstrated that in using absorptive capacity at a departmental level, it would be necessary to make adaptations so that the concepts and constructs re<sup>fl</sup>ect the desired level of analysis. Notably, our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the importance of exposure to knowledge and gaining experience through connections and collaborations with partners outside of the IS department but internal to the organisation is valuable for understanding absorptive capacity at a departmental level, as is the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the IS department context and organisationa sustainability context as moderators of sustainable IS exposure, prior experience and triggers. As there is a scarcity of IS studies that apply absorptive capacity theory at an IS departmental level (Roberts et al., 2012), our <sup>fi</sup>nding that information internal to the <sup>fi</sup>rm could also contribute to IS-environmental absorptive capacity is a useful contribution to research.

Third, our extended model (Figure 2) has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of insights and variables about the antecedents and process of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. For example the extent to which sustainable IS exposure and triggers in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of IS-environmenta absorptive capacity could be strengthened if there is strong organisational sustainability commitment, performance indicators and championship. While exposure to external technology vendors might inform an IS department about general industry trends in environmenta sustainability, the organisational sustainability context would moderate the acquisition, assimilation and utilisation of knowledge. In addition, an IS department’s context in terms of its maturity and perceived role and self-image might have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the importance placed on developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Thus, the very organisational and environmental resources and conditions on which organisations draw in undertaking IS-enabled environmental change can be, at the same time, the basis for shaping and reproducing the organisation’s capabilities. This implies that for environmentally driven ISenabled change to succeed, organisations need to develop IS-environmental absorptive capacity.

Fourth, our study looked beyond the initial adoption of IS for environmental sustainability which dominates existing green IT and green IS research, to understand its assimilation and value. Researchers can therefore use our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to identify what factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence both the adoption and the assimilation of green IT and green IS and what factors are different.

## Limitations of the current study

The current study has a number of limitations. We did not study the environmental outcomes of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Both the survey and case study focused on large organisations, and future research might investigate a variety of other organisations. With respect to the survey, we tested IS-environmental absorptive capacity on the basis of a single sample only and did not have a holdout sample to re-test the model. In addition, our measures of environmentally sustainable IS assimilation focused predominantly on the green IT rather than the green IS component. With respect to the case study, we investigated a single case in a not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t setting and it would be valuable for future case study research to investigate alternative settings. Further, as our case study utilised once-off single interviews, longitudina studies investigating the development of IS-environmental absorptive capacity over time would be valuable.

## Directions for future research

The study stimulates a number of potential extensions and also provides avenues for future research.

Future studies that test and validate the additional variables identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through the follow-up case study and whether or not these variables have a direct, control or moderating effect will be useful. Studies investigating the determinants of organisational change have found that powerful externa and internal forces prevent <sup>fi</sup>rms implementing change even when such change could clearly improve performance (Colombo & Delmastro, 2002). Additional potential internal constraints worth investigation include existing investment (sunk costs) in IT infrastructure and skills of IS personnel that may not be easily transferable to the sustainability agenda, the information constraints of decision makers, internal politics and existing normative agreements on procedures and authority (Hannan & Freeman, 1977). Potential external constraints include legal and <sup>fi</sup>scal barriers, availability of external information, collective rationality and pressures for external legitimacy (Hannan & Freeman, 1977; Molla & Abareshi, 2012).

Our focus of analysis was mainly the IS department within organisations; future studies could focus more on the interaction between IS departmental capability for environmenta sustainability and organisational capability for environmental sustainability.

The ultimate outcome of leveraging IS for environmental sustainability should be its contribution to the quality of the environment. As noted, this paper does not empirically test the environmental outcome. This is an important area that can be scaled from the current work. In seeking to measure environmental outcomes, it is important to acknowledge the numerous complexities. Often the causes of environmental outcomes are ambiguous, and deciding what to measure in the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance is not always straightforward. There are also differing views across stakeholder groups about what constitutes desirable environmental outcomes and metrics. Despite these complexities, future studies might usefully investigate the environmental outcomes of developing IS-environmental absorptive capacity through adopting relevant measures for environmental condition indicators. These indicators include but are not limited to, the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of generating, distributing and using energy at societal levels; reduction in not only energy utilisation but also embedded carbon; and changed human behaviour in sourcing, using and disposing of IT resources in ways that improve the condition of the natural environment through a reduction of electronic waste. To access relevant data, researchers might refer to composite indexes from corporate sustainability reports, ISO 14031 (e.g. environmental condition indicators), third-party reports (e.g. the Carbon Disclosure Project) and country-level indexes (e.g. Environmental Performance Index).

## CONCLUSIONS

Environmental sustainability is recognised as one of the most important and urgent challenges of the 21st century. Climate scientists worldwide have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that human behaviour has contributed to the degradation of the natural environment. Organisations are central to the complex systems that de<sup>fi</sup>ne society’s production and consumption patterns. Therefore, organisations as vehicles for industrialisation and a dominant structure in the modern econom have also played a key role in this environmental degradation.

Yet people and organisations are capable of innovation and change. To tackle the challenges of environmental sustainability, behavioural and organisational change is needed. This invokes questions about the organisational capabilities and competences that are needed to direct innovation and behavioural change towards environmental sustainability and how these can be developed.

We believe that implementing environmentally driven IS-enabled change at an organisationa level depends on the effective development and renewal of IS-environmental absorptive capacity. Developing environmental absorptive capacity is particularly challenging because of the complex and long-term nature of environmental problems and the short-term orientation of many companies. IS-environmental absorptive capacity is a resource-based factor and can yield distinctive resource bundles and capabilities that are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial not only for IS and organisational outcomes but also the health of the biophysical environment. However, unlike market-based resources and capabilities that should be limited to imitation, the ultimate value of IS-environmental absorptive capacity depends on its diffusion. This implies that organisations that are developing valuable IS environmental absorptive capacity should share their experiences and know-how with others so that they can collectively address environmental sustainability challenges.
