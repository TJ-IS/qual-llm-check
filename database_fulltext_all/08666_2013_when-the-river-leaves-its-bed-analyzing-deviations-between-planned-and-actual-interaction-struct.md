# When the river leaves its bed: analyzing deviations between planned and actual interaction structures in IT change processes

Katja Zolper, Daniel Beimborn, Tim Weitzel

University of Bamberg, Bamberg, Germany

Correspondence:

K Zolper, University of Bamberg, An der Weberei 5, D-96045 Bamberg, Germany.

## Abstract

There is ample evidence of deviations between the actual and planned interaction structures between a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT department and business units. Such deviations can hinder senior managers from governing their IT organizations effectively because they do not know how work really gets done. This paper develops an explanation for why actual structures differ from planned structures. Understanding this phenomenon is indispensable for managers to govern the real organization, to uphold compliance with important standards (e.g., ITIL, COBIT), to decide whether the formal or the actual organization is more effective, and, <sup>fi</sup>nally, to identify management actions that support the optimal structure. To develop this understanding, we analyze the interaction structures at the interface between <sup>fi</sup>rms’ business units and IT units in four rich cases, using data from 56 interviews and 47 questionnaires, and applying qualitative methods and social network analysis, which give us deep insights into planned and actual interaction among employees. We test two different explanations for deviations of actual from planned interaction structures and <sup>fi</sup>nd that boundary-spanning theory provides the dominant explanation for such deviations: Inclined to span the business/IT boundary most effectively, the actors involved deviate from planned structures especially when other structures offer better boundary-spanning potential, which is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced primarily by cross-domain knowledge. In addition, relationships also play an important role. On the positive side, relationships provide opportunities for such deviations, while on the negative side, a con<sup>fl</sup>ict-laden relationship might hinder deviations even if they were advantageous.

## Introduction

t is beyond dispute that IT governance needs to be implemented across all relevant organizational levels to be effective. However, there are no guarantees that employees will actually act according to a planned governance structure $( \mathrm { e . g . , \ a }$ de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT change process). The distinction between the ‘formal’ and the ‘informal’ organization of the <sup>fi</sup>rm is one of the oldest in the literature. It hardly needs repeating that, ‘observers who assume <sup>fi</sup>rms to be structured in fact by the of<sup>fi</sup>cial organization chart are sociological babes in the woods (Granovetter, 1985: 502). With regard to the business/IT relationship, Schwarz and Hirschheim (2003) <sup>fi</sup>nd that ‘it is not the presence of a structure, but rather how the structure works within the <sup>fi</sup>rm that helps to create a positive ITbusiness unit relationship’ (143) and that ‘the IT organization is a skeleton, against which the daily life of the employees gives life and <sup>fl</sup>esh to the organic entity of the organization’ (148).

Most executives know that actual work procedures deviate from planned procedures. However, they usually cannot describe exactly what is different, let alone explain why it is different. One CIO we met during our interviews stated: ‘I have a feeling that people actually manage changes [to our information systems] in a different way than the way we planned it. But honestly, this is only a vague feeling. I certainly cannot tell you what is different’ (own emphasis added).

Understanding how actual work procedures differ from the ones planned during strategy implementation is important for effective IT governance. Otherwise, senior managers cannot understand the outcome of their governance activities, that is, how the actors on the front lines really interact, and hence cannot effectively govern their real organizations. With the recent trend for more regulation and the growing need for (IT) organizations to prove compliance with certain standards, for example, Sarbanes Oxley Act (H.R.3763, 2002), ITIL<sup>1</sup> (OGC, 2007), COBIT<sup>2</sup> (ISACA, 2012), knowing about and understanding of actual work procedures has become even more important: If the actual and the formally planned structures differ from one another and the management has no insights into this difference, <sup>fi</sup>rms will not be able to be actually compliant with the above standards. On the other hand, increasing compliance does not automatically lead to the actual organization being more congruent with the formal organization. Contrarily, the ever increasing pressure to react instantly to varying market demands and external regulations have made the informal organization even more important – and often provoke deviations since the formal organization is not <sup>fl</sup>exible enough<sup>3</sup>. Such trends have stimulated recent research works (e.g., Gulati and Puranam, 2009; Ahuja et al., 2012; Kleinbaum, 2012; Soda and Zaheer, 2012) and practitioner-led initiatives to sensitize managers for exploring the ‘invisible organization’ (Farmer, 2008) and balancing it with the formal organization in order to achieve high organizational performance (Goldsmith and Katzenbach, 2007; Reingold and Yang, 2007) and to manage successful changes (Farmer, 2008). Accordingly, very recent research has reminded us that research of the mutual interplay between informal and formal organization is scarce but critical for understanding the organizational functioning (Soda and Zaheer, 2012).

In many ways, interaction structures in <sup>fi</sup>rms resemble <sup>fl</sup>owing water, and the governance challenge is similar to controlling a river. Water, like IT work procedures or business/IT communication <sup>fl</sup>ows, has a tendency to <sup>fi</sup>nd its way through sometimes rapidly changing environments and beyond its intended course. Such deviations can have desirable consequences, such as refertilizing soil with fresh nutrients, but also many negative impacts, such as destroying crops and buildings. Humans have tried to control the <sup>fl</sup>ow of rivers for eons to prevent <sup>fl</sup>oods and improve shipping. While some efforts to redirect <sup>fl</sup>ows have been successful, others have not worked. To direct such <sup>fl</sup>ows purposefully, knowing where the river runs and when it might leave its bed is indispensable.

Imagine a company that has established an IT change process (e.g., according to ITIL) with liaisons responsible for the IT change process on the IT and business sides who are supposed to constitute the interface between the two units and channel all communication between business users and IT developers. Now imagine that in reality these liaisons are largely ignored. Instead, developers and users collaborate directly. Think about what happens when there are major issues with the IT change process. Senior IT management – unaware of how business and IT really interact – will hold the liaisons responsible for problems and will come up with ineffective measures addressing the formally planned but not used process.

In this paper, we narrow down our unit of analysis from general work procedures to interaction structures at the interface between business and IT, which have been found to be of major importance for IT professionals (Agarwal and Sambamurthy, 2002; Chan and Reich, 2007; Seddon et al., 2010). In particular, we analyze the deviations of actual business/IT interaction structures from formally planned structures and aim to answer the following research question:

When and why do differences occur between planned and actual interaction structures at the interface between business and IT?

We test two contrasting explanations by analyzing four rich case studies, using data from 56 interviews and 47 complementary employee questionnaires, which allow us to gain deep insight into the planned and actual interaction structures and driving forces behind them.

Our study improves our understanding of such deviations from planned interaction structures by identifying determinants of deviation and depicting four scenarios based on these determinants. It thus adds to the understanding of drivers of IT service quality and also enriches boundary-spanning theory by re<sup>fi</sup>ning reasons and effects of nominated boundary spanners not becoming boundary spanners in practice. The results will support practitioners by helping them understand when and where deviations are likely to appear and how unwanted deviations can be prevented. Thus, managers can more effectively govern the actual organization rather than the planned but never realized organization.

In the following section, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne important constructs for this research and introduce two different explanations to develop alternative answers to our research question. We then introduce the cases individually and subsequently re<sup>fi</sup>ne the explanation for deviations from planned interaction structures by comparing the cases in light of the two different explanations of deviations proposed in the literature. This research model is further discussed and its contribution to theory and practice is highlighted. Finally, a conclusion is drawn, which indicates future research opportunities.

## Theoretical foundation

## De<sup>fi</sup>nition of important constructs

Since some of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed constructs are not de<sup>fi</sup>ned or only ambiguously de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the existing literature, we will explain our understanding of these constructs before proceeding to the theory development. These constructs are interaction structures, formal and informal structures, as well as the deviations between them.

Interaction structures at the business/IT interface are an important aspect of the IT organization, which focuses (among others) on ‘how IT interacts with, and is related to the business units’ (Schwarz and Hirschheim, 2003: 133). Interaction structures are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the paths along which business and IT employees communicate and whom they involve in such communication.

Unfortunately, the term informal is de<sup>fi</sup>ned ambiguously in the literature: While it always refers to something distinct from the formal (i.e., planned) organization in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social (Johnson et al., 1994) or cultural (McConkie and Boss, 1986) aspects, it is not clear whether it refers to relationshipbased, that is, social aspects (e.g., as in Chan, 2002; Johanson, 2000; Remøe, 1982), or to actual interaction structures, work procedures, or relations (e.g., as in Cobb, 1980; Granovetter, 1985; Nickerson and Zenger, 2002; Santarelli and Sterlacchini, 1990). Some authors even use the term ‘informal’ for both aspects (e.g., Dahl and Pedersen, 2004; Johanson, 2000). This paper uses the following de<sup>fi</sup>nitions:

With regard to what the literature terms ‘informal,’ we distinguish between actual interaction structures and relationship-based structures. Actual interaction structures re<sup>fl</sup>ect the real interactions taking place in the organization; they may – but need not – deviate substantially from the planned ones. For example, Cross et al. (2001) analyzed the information <sup>fl</sup>ow in a petroleum company and found that the most central person was not in a central role on the organization chart. Relationship-based structures manifest in advice and trust networks and connect people who share the same values, norms, and beliefs (Gulati and Puranam, 2009). In our research, the term informal is a superordinate topic summarizing both aspects, relationship-based and actual. Nonetheless, we analyze both aspects separately.

The formal structure is a ‘blueprint for activities’ (Meyer and Rowan, 1977: 342). It formally de<sup>fi</sup>nes the hierarchy and the division of tasks (Johanson, 2000; Chan, 2002). It comprises (among others) reporting relationships, processes, and role descriptions, and represents an organization’s ‘skeleton (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993: 104). To avoid confusion with the ambiguous term ‘informal,’ we will in the following refer to the formal structure by using the term ‘planned structure.

Finally, a deviation of actual from planned structures de<sup>fi</sup>nes a situation in which employees do not follow the planned structure but, for example, involve additional people, or ignore their of<sup>fi</sup>cial counterparts.

## Explanations for deviations of actual from planned structures

In the following, we present two different theoretical explana tions originating from other research contexts that are both transferrable to the business/IT interface and thus can offer an explanation to the deviations of actual from planned interaction structures at the business/IT interface. Yet, they do not provide congruent answers and their predictions might be contradictory. Therefore, our explanatory research needs to determine which of the two explanations does more accurately explain deviations between formal and actual interaction structures at the business/IT interface.

## Relationship-based explanation from informal organization research

We have reviewed the literature around informal organizations because it offers potential explanations why an infor mal (here: actual) organization differs from the formal (here: planned) one. The analysis of informal (i.e., actual and relationship-based) organizations has a long tradition. Roethlisberger and Dickson (1939) found that workers at the Hawthorne plant informally (here: unof<sup>fi</sup>cially) set limits to production outcome to reduce competition among each other and avoid price cuts (Albrecht and Goldman, 1985).

More recently, researchers have analyzed the reasons for deviations between the formally planned organization and relationship-based structures (called ‘informal organizations’; Nickerson and Zenger, 2002: 548). They are caused by the transformation of relationship-based structures naturally lagging behind reorganization (Nickerson and Zenger, 2002; Gulati and Puranam, 2009). These studies explain that people do not adhere to planned interaction structures in reality because they rely on persisting relationship-based structures they have built up previously (Nickerson and Zenger, 2002; Gulati and Puranam, 2009). Relationship-based structures are an organization’s ‘central nervous system driving the collective thought processes, actions, and reactions of its business units (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993: 104). They are ‘used in almost every organization to get things done and to oil the wheels of the formal bureaucracy’ (Strauss, 1962: 174). They are characterized by trust and informal communication, and are indispensable for the functioning of an organization (Nickerson and Zenger, 2002). Employees ful<sup>fi</sup>lling tasks turn to people they know and trust, with whom they have had good personal experiences, because their own experience is their best source of information (Granovetter, 1985).

In IS research, relationships also play an important role, since they are a key success factor for an effective IT function. Building and maintaining a relationship with the business is an imperative for the IT organization (Rockart et al., 1996; Feeny and Willcocks, 1998), and such a relationship is a major antecedent of business/IT alignment (Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Preston and Karahanna, 2009) and IT project success (Gasson, 2006).

Knowing that relationships between business and IT units are vital for the IT function in general and are a primary driver for deviations of actual from planned interaction structures, it is natural to assume that they might also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the deviations of actual from planned interaction structures at the business/IT interface.

Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rst theoretical explanation for such deviations argues that actual interaction structures are influenced by relationship-based structures. Figure 1 shows a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious example of actual structures following relationship-based structures. In this <sup>fi</sup>gure, B and D do not communicate via C as planned (left) but interact directly (right) because they share a strong relationship (center). A and E, however, do not know each other and thus rely on C to intermediate between them. We refer to this explanation henceforth as the relationship-based explanation.

## Boundary-spanning theory

Another explanation for deviations is given by boundaryspanning theory, which explicitly focuses on deviations of actual from planned structures at the interface between organizational units. Boundaries occur between different <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice, such as organizational units, and can be spanned with the help of boundary objects and boundary spanners (Levina and Vaast, 2005). Boundary spanners are ‘capable of translating contrasting coding schemes’ (Tushman, 1977: 591) and thus support knowledge sharing between two groups. Boundary objects are artifacts that preserve a common meaning across all units to support translation (Star and Griesemer, 1989) but that can also serve the local needs of different organizational units and hence be understood by both units. This research will focus on interactions between persons and thus on boundary spanners.

Often, such boundary spanners are of<sup>fi</sup>cially assigned (Nochur and Allen, 1992). However, not all of those designated boundary spanners ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their roles effectively, and other boundary spanners may take over without being of<sup>fi</sup>cially assigned (Levina and Vaast, 2005). To explore this phenomenon, Levina and Vaast (2005) distinguish between nominated boundary spanners and boundary spanners in practice. While the former have been of<sup>fi</sup>cially assigned to span boundaries, the latter actually ful<sup>fi</sup>ll this task.

Three factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effectiveness of nominated boundary spanners. First, boundary spanners need to become legitimate participants of the two fields involved. To do so, they need at least peripheral knowledge of both <sup>fi</sup>elds so that they can show credibly that they can contribute to both <sup>fi</sup>elds. In addition, they need to exchange their own capital from their <sup>fi</sup>elds of origin (e.g., economic, social, or cultural capital) for capital in the other <sup>fi</sup>eld (Levina and Vaast, 2006). Second, they need to become legitimate negotiators on behalf of the <sup>fi</sup>elds they represent, that is, they must show credibly that they are able to shape these <sup>fi</sup>elds according to the results of negotiations with the other <sup>fi</sup>eld (Levina and Vaast, 2006). Third, boundary spanners in practice need to have an inclination to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their role because they expect rewards directly or indirectly associated with that role (Levina and Vaast, 2006).

In summary, boundary-spanning theory proposes that when boundary spanners are legitimate participants and negotiators of both fields and when they are inclined to span the boundary between them, boundary-spanning practices in theory will become effective (Figure 2). While this is an explicit answer to our research question, already foreseeing an interface between organizational units, it contradicts the traditional literature around informal organizations because it does not equally underline the importance of relationships and instead focuses on legitimacy and inclination to span a boundary. Therefore, it will serve as a second theoretical explanation to be tested in this research.

## Combined perspective on both theoretical explanations

The two existent explanations for deviations of actual from planned interaction structures emphasize different reasons for such deviations and inform different hypotheses why actual interaction structures may deviate from planned ones:

Hypothesis 1: Actual interaction structures deviate from planned ones when relationship-based structures favor such deviations (e.g., through stronger relationships outside planned structures).

Hypothesis 2: Actual interaction structures deviate from planned ones when boundary spanners in theory are not legitimate participants or negotiators of both <sup>fi</sup>elds or they are not inclined to span the boundary between them.

In the following, we will compare both explanations based on a set of case studies in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which of the above hypotheses is dominant.

While the <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis concentrates on relationships between actors, the latter sees relationships only as an instrument to get the legitimacy to negotiate for a <sup>fi</sup>eld. However, legitimacy to negotiate for a <sup>fi</sup>eld can also be based on the exchange of other types of capital (Levina and Vaast, 2005).

Similarly, the existence of shared knowledge or cross-domain knowledge is another area of overlap between the two explanations. While boundary-spanning theory argues that a boundary spanner needs to possess at least peripheral knowledge of both <sup>fi</sup>elds to be a legitimate participant in both <sup>fi</sup>elds, shared knowledge is an important aspect of personal relationships and determines the ability to exchange information (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Yet, the relationship-based explanation as developed in the literature around informal organizations does not discuss such shared knowledge or cross-domain knowledge. It rather underlines the importance to know and trust other actors (Bouty, 2000; Strauss, 1962).

Despite their different emphases, both explanations have a basic assumption in common. When other structures are in some way better than planned structures, actual structures tend to deviate from planned ones. It should be noted that the explanations differ in their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘better’ (boundary spanning potential vs relationships).

The matrix in Figure 3 illustrates that when boundary spanning potential is high and relationships are good within planned structures or are low/bad within planned structures while being higher/better outside, both explanations predict the same results. Yet, in other situations, the explanations may predict different outcomes. Therefore, we will in this paper investigate which of the two theoretical explanations has greater explanatory power – especially in the two ambiguous scenarios – and how both explanations interact.

## Research method

Since our research addresses a how-question that requires an intensive examination of a complex, contemporary, real-life phenomenon in its original context and without manipulating the setting, a case study approach is most appropriate (Dubé and Paré, 2003; Yin, 2009). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, since two explanations need to be tested, we follow an explanatory case study approach to determine which of them better explains the deviations of actual from planned structures at the business/IT interface. This approach is analogous to Benbasat et al. (1987) and Markus (1983), and follows the guidelines given by Yin (2009). It increases the likelihood of valid theory creation because it is closely connected to empirical evidence, has a high probability of generating new theory by comparing different situations, and allows us to adapt the interview guidelines in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, thus retaining the most relevant insights (Eisenhardt, 1989).

## Unit of analysis

To compare cases from different companies, we focus on one particular process: the IT change process. We chose this process for several reasons. First, it requires frequent interaction between business and IT to discuss and negotiate requirements and potential solutions. Second, it is quite similar across different industries.<sup>4</sup> Finally, and most importantly, it occurs on the operational level and is distinct from project work. Unlike project work, the IT change process is part of the line organization and thus is established on long-term structures that do not dissolve once a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Compared with interaction at the top management level, IT change procedures involve a larger number of employees and comprise more complex interaction structures. Consequently, these structures are typically regulated more strongly in process de<sup>fi</sup>nitions than are interactions at the top management level. Hence, there is a greater likelihood of observing deviations, the reasons for which can then be analyzed.

We further focus on one potential type of deviation: The bypassing of boundary spanners between business and IT.

Within the IT change process, we analyze changes regarding one particular IT system, delimiting the relevant set of actors. Due to our intention to analyze existing structures with interaction between business and IT, we used an initial interview with the IT manager to identify changes that are not large and complex enough to justify setting up a separately staffed project, yet are too large to be handled within a work<sup>fl</sup>ow-based ticket system without inter-personal business/IT interaction. The management interviews revealed that such ‘medium-size changes’ typically require 5–60 developer days. An example is the introduction of a new insurance product into the sales application so that a work<sup>fl</sup>ow supports sales representatives when selling this product to their customers.

## Data collection and analysis

In 2010 and 2011, we conducted case studies at four companies, which were selected based on a theoretical replication logic (Yin, 2009). Within the matrix of Figure 3, each of the cases matches one cell. Thus, taken together, the cases can be employed to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the propositions made for the unambiguous scenarios and show which of the explanations is dominant in the ambiguous scenarios. Figure 4 shows which case represents which scenario.

We had very good access to these companies; management granted us access to all the interviewees we deemed important for the study, which allowed us to gain deep insights into the sensitive topic of relationship-based structures among employees.

We combined two data collection approaches: interviews, which are the most common data source for case studies (Yin, 2009), and social network data from a related questionnaire since the analysis of such social network data ‘can provide an X-ray’ (Cross et al., 2001) of interaction structures, which are analyzed in this research.

To ensure rich data and a deep understanding of each case, we collected data from multiple informants, including all employees from business and IT who played an active role in the IT change process of the concerned IT application, that is, who implemented changes or requested changes and de<sup>fi</sup>ned requirements. We also collected data from end-users, who worked with the application. When it was not possible to talk to all the developers (because they were too numerous), we made sure that we collected data from all team leads responsible for developers working with the concerned application, as well as from the key players among the developers, who were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed together with their team leads.

Following the guidelines of Yin (2009), we created a detailed case study protocol that was followed rigidly and guided us through a three-step data collection process:

● Initial interviews with IT and business managers to understand the general setting and the of<sup>fi</sup>cially designed IT change process and to identify interviewees for the main stage. These initial interviews were particularly important because there was no of<sup>fi</sup>cial IT change process documentation from which we could derive the planned structure.

However, we ensured that interviewees described the planned structure accurately by talking to managers who had developed the planned structure when it was set up.

● Questionnaires <sup>fi</sup>lled in by interviewees about the interviewees’ actual interactions with the other actors in the involved units allowing us to deeply understand the actual interaction structures instead of planned ones. These questionnaires used existing instruments such as types of interaction (Mintzberg, 1973) to elevate the interaction structure. An outline of this questionnaire can be found in Appendix A.

● Semi-structured interviews comprising (a) a brief discussion of the planned IT change process; (b) a discussion of the actual change practices and deviations from the planned ones (including joint drawings of the interaction structures); and (c) a discussion of the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing these deviations. In accordance with our explanatory case study approach, we paid special attention to factors that mirrored the drivers identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the two explanations presented above. We also followed different avenues proposed by the interviewees and focused in particular on how such drivers in<sup>fl</sup>uence deviations of actual from planned interaction structures. Further details regarding the interview guidelines can be found in Appendix B.

Following the call for greater use of established interviewing techniques in IS research (Schultze and Avital, 2011), we applied an interview approach based on laddering and critical incident technique, combining them to gain maximum insight (Rugg et al., 2002). These techniques were applied especially in part (c) of the interviews, on which we spent the most time.<sup>5</sup> The majority of interviews was conducted in German and the analysis as described below was based on the original non-translated transcripts. Table 1 presents an overview of our interviews.

All interviews were transcribed by students and coded by the <sup>fi</sup>rst author. The coding process built on provisional codes (Saldaña, 2011) derived from the two proposed explanations describing deviations and drivers of deviations proposed in boundary-spanning theory (e.g., legitimacy to negotiate, legitimacy to participate) and the relationship-based explanation (e.g., trust, knowing actors from the other unit). We used a combination of structural and simultaneous coding to highlight text segments dealing with individual drivers of deviations between planned and actual interaction structures and with causal relationships between them (Saldaña, 2011). That is, we structurally coded a text segment with ‘legitimacy to participate,’ when the interviewee discussed this topic, and simultaneously added ‘deviation,’ when he or she described that the <sup>fi</sup>rst in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the latter. Using the above mentioned provisional codes and the subsequent coding procedure (structural and simultaneous codes), we evaluated the constructs from our explanations (e.g., deviation, boundaryspanning potential, relationship). For example, we evaluated the construct relationship based on the answers, interviewees provided to the question: ‘Which words would you use to describe the relationship to the IT unit/business unit/liaison? If answers contained words like trusted, open, friendly, cooperative, we evaluated this relationship as strong; answers containing words like conflict-laden or difficult led to an evaluation as weak or conflict-laden, and words like neutral or work-related led to an evaluation as neutral.

Having evaluated the constructs for every single interview, we aggregated these individual evaluations across all interviews of a case to determine the interview-based evaluation of this construct for the overall case. If we found substantial differences among the interviewees within a case, we went back to these interviewees to clarify what they meant and to resolve contradictions. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings were then compared with the two theoretical explanations to determine which best explained the case. Recurring causal explanations highlighted with simultaneous coding were evaluated to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the research model and its causal relations.

Since the perception of interviewees does not always tell how work is actually done, while social network analysis (SNA) does (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993), we used this method to triangulate interview data regarding the actual interaction structure. On the basis of the data derived from the questionnaire, a network was generated for each case describing actual interaction to measure the degree of deviation. In particular, we used an adopted betweenness centrality measure to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the degree to which the liaison unit was bypassed. Betweenness centrality describes how often a person or group is part of the shortest path between two other persons or groups (Wasserman and Faust, 2007), thus how likely it is that this person or group is involved when these other two persons or groups do communicate (Granovetter, 1973). On the basis of the survey data, we calculated a betweenness centrality measure to determine to which degree communication between business and IT actually involved the liaison unit. This measure was compared with a value for the planned interaction structure, and the difference between the two describes the degree of deviation.

## Individual case analysis

In this section, we will brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe each case individually, by laying out the planned interaction structure, followed by an explanation of boundary-spanning potential and relationships in each case. We will then describe for each case whether the relationship-based explanation and/or boundary spanning theory would predict a deviation from planned structures based on these two factors, and will <sup>fi</sup>nally examine what really happened in this case and what were the driving factors behind that.<sup>7</sup>

## ThisBank

The planned IT change process in ThisBank envisions that users discuss IT-related topics with two liaisons within the business unit. They are supposed to be the ‘main contact persons in that business unit for all IT-related questions’ and, as such, are expected to translate ‘what [users] want to describe functionally into ‘what IT is able to understand’. These liaisons on the business side are responsible for recording change requests in a database and clarifying any missing or unclear information before it is passed on to IT. A liaison in the IT unit takes these records, validates the way in which IT can understand them, and asks one of the developers to estimate the request. Figure 5A shows the overall planned interaction structure with three nominated boundary spanners (the liaisons).

Boundary-spanning potential within the planned interaction structures is above all limited by a lack of cross-domain knowledge with the liaison persons compared with deep expertise in each other’s domains with developers and business users. An interviewee from the business unit explained:

The [liaison persons] can only give very limited answers when I have a question regarding the IT application – limited to no answers. How, could they? They do not work with the application.

According to boundary-spanning theory, this lack of crossdomain knowledge prevents the liaison persons from participating in both <sup>fi</sup>elds and negotiating for their own <sup>fi</sup>eld of origin. It is driven by the fact that the liaison persons are supposed to deal with about 25 IT applications, making it impossible for them to acquire expert knowledge about all these applications. In contrast, users and developers have worked nearly exclusively with the MyAssets system for a long time and share a long common history. This provides developers with deep functional knowledge regarding market data validation and users with deep expertise with the technical aspects of MyAssets. This expertise makes discussions much more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient because long, basic background explanations are unnecessary; users and developers can focus on the issue immediately in an informed discussion of the precise issue at hand. Even more important, users and developers can act as sparring partners. As one user stated:

![](/api/attachments/GV7VCR52/fulltext/images/a923de2222eb46b2fdd4602b83a060257aafdb0c4155a4aacf65e9742d33ee54.jpg)  
Planned and actual interaction structure at ThisBank (schematic).

We debate the issues with the developers. I think that is good. […] I think it is important that colleagues from IT discuss with us and also contribute their ideas. [Such debates sometimes yield] solutions that we would not otherwise have achieved.

Thus, discussions based on cross-domain knowledge improve the boundary-spanning potential and thus IT change process success and IT service quality. Also, relationships are stronger outside the planned interaction structure. While they rarely know the liaison persons, developers and business users have collaborated for a long time. One developer even worked with MyAssets on the business side before joining the IT unit. Their relationship is characterized by trust, openness, and mutual understanding. One interviewee highlighted the advantages of this good relationship for their work with MyAssets:

You can better evaluate the people, you can also evaluate the urgency of what they raise, [and] you know immediately how to handle the other person.

These characteristics of the relationship reduce the probability of con<sup>fl</sup>ict and increase the time spent in constructive discussions on how to resolve issues. The interviewees also highlighted the importance of trust between users and developers, which enables them to discuss issues openly and informally without fear that requests will be shared with others in an early, immature phase.

Therefore, both the relationship-based explanation and boundary-spanning theory unanimously predict a deviation of actual from planned interaction structures.

And indeed, the interviews and the questionnaire data consistently reveal that actors do not follow the planned process. For critical change requests in particular, users contact the developers directly. Only when the developers agree to implement a change request, it is sent to a liaison on the business side, who records it in the database, adopting the exact phrase, hence playing no more than an ‘administrative role (as one interviewee phrased it). Also, clari<sup>fi</sup>cations of details are typically discussed between developers and users directly. Users send only small requests to the liaisons to be initially recorded in the database. One interviewee stated:

[The role of the liaisons in the business unit] … is not clear to me. They record the requests in the request database. What they do apart from that [in the context of the IT change process] is not 100% evident to me.

## Regarding the liaison in the IT unit, he said:

To be honest, I do not interact a lot with him. I know – and this is new information for me – that he receives all the items in the request database centrally and distributes them within IT. That I know by now.

Thus, having the common goal to implement changes ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and with high quality, and therefore to span the business/IT boundary ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, users and developers bypass the liaisons and collaborate directly, thereby enhancing IT change process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality. Even the liaison persons also aiming at good change implementation agree to being bypassed:

We do not want to be a brake pad between business and IT. … If [a well-functioning change management process] has been established and works, you let things slide.

The major driver is the superior cross-domain knowledge outside planned interaction structures. One interviewee stated:

When I have a question, I directly contact the IT unit, because contacting the liaison unit would not be helpful because they do not have enough knowledge of the details. (own emphasis added)

In short, the liaisons who are supposed to channel all communication between business and IT are largely ignored; users and developers collaborate directly. Thus, the actual structures are characterized by a larger degree of interaction between developers and users, that is, lower betweenness centrality of liaisons (Figure 5b). Interviewees emphasize that relationships do not in<sup>fl</sup>uence their choice with whom to interact. Still, relationships enhance the IT change process because they allow business and IT to interact more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. And of course, if business and IT employees did not know their counterparts, they would not be able to contact the respective other unit directly and thus deviate from planned structures. Thus, the existence of a relationship between them enables deviations, while boundary-spanning potential drives it.

## ThisManufacturer

The planned IT change process at ThisManufacturer involves the IT unit, different business units in the headquarter and regional subsidiaries, as well as a liaison unit at the interface. Changes to the MyData system are proposed by key users from the regional subsidiaries or by users in the headquarter’s department of sales reporting. These users are supposed to report the changes to the central key users in the liaison unit, who coordinate these proposals, decide whether they should be implemented, and, if so, compose a change document describing it. This document is handed over to one single IT manager who validates if it contains all needed information. Then, the changes are implemented by the developers and <sup>fi</sup>nally tested within the liaison unit. Communication between the business unit and the IT is intended to be completely channeled via the liaison unit.

Again cross-domain knowledge is a major driver of boundary-spanning role in ThisManufacturer.

With regard to the liaison unit, a user stated that she feels there are:

huge bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from [involving the liaison unit], because they are the experts. They know the user perspective, they know how the system works from a user end, and they are familiar with the data that are in it.<sup>8</sup>

Also, an IT manager stated that the liaison unit is required to translate information so that the business unit can understand it enforcing the planned structure:

We write in an IT language only we understand. […] Needless to say, I do not see the consequences for the user. For me, it is a cube, it has a technical name. I certainly do not know how users out there name these things.

Another factor of boundary-spanning potential in ThisManufacturer is the legitimacy to negotiate for one’s <sup>fi</sup>eld. In ThisManufacturer, every attempt of actors other than the liaison unit members to negotiate with the IT for the business is rejected by the IT unit, thus enforcing the boundaryspanning role of the liaison unit. The IT manager stated:

We do not implement anything without [the liaison unit] knowing it.

This ensures that the liaison unit is informed of and involved in any changes being implemented.

Also, the relationship-based structures closely mirror the planned interaction structures. A user stated:

[I have] a particularly close relationship with [my key contacts in the liaison unit] .... I would be perfectly happy to go to a restaurant or pub or a social situation to hang out with them […] We’ve got mutual respect for one another. They sometimes ask me questions and appreciate my feedback from a [user] perspective.<sup>8</sup>

Another user added that his long and good working history with employees in the liaison unit has helped develop a trustbased relationship that enables them to collaborate more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, without the need to challenge each other’s statements, thus reducing the need for mutual control:

When there were problems, they openly informed us, and we tried to <sup>fi</sup>x it together … . We have helped each other out and thus a sense of communality has developed.

And indeed, as predicted by both explanations, the actual process re<sup>fl</sup>ects the planned one almost perfectly. In most situations, the liaison unit channels interaction between the business units and IT (i.e., it exhibits very high betweenness centrality). A very few users contact the IT manager directly because they have worked with him in the past. However, even these users are redirected to the liaison unit, and consequently the questionnaire data show an actual betweenness centrality of 100% as planned and thus no deviation. The IT manager stated:

Business – for me they are fully represented by the liaison unit

Again, the dominant driver is the boundary-spanning potential, which is enabled by the liaison unit translating between the business and the IT, while relationships do not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the deviation, but rather the IT change process quality.

## ThisInsurance

The planned IT change process at ThisInsurance envisions an IT change process similar to the one in ThisManufacturer with a business department, which contacts a liaison unit to request a change. This liaison unit documents these changes, writes the functional speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, and is then in contact with the IT unit, who implements it. However, in contrast to ThisManufacturer, trilateral meetings between the business, IT, and liaison units are of<sup>fi</sup>cially part of the IT change process.

Boundary-spanning potential is above all driven by crossdomain knowledge. Yet, it is nearly equally distributed across the three units. The liaison unit has been set up with members from the business and IT units; therefore, its members possess a lot of business and IT knowledge. While some are more able to span the boundary to the IT, others communicate better to the business unit

The business does not have as much IT background [as the liaison unit has]. Therefore, the liaison unit sometimes needs to translate certain things for us.

![](/api/attachments/GV7VCR52/fulltext/images/815406951092495a6d1af00cb7a74dca02a81f9558fee270f9ca701d4ef71e97.jpg)  
Planned and actual interaction structure at ThisManufacturer (schematic).

Yet, since the members of the IT unit have also worked with the MyCustomer application for a long time, they also possess a lot of knowledge of the business processes that are supported with MyCustomer and therefore also can span the boundary to the business unit and discuss issues directly with them. Describing his direct interaction with the business unit and the boundary-spanning potential of this immediate boundary, one interviewee from the IT explained:

It depends on whom you talk to. Some of the colleagues from the business unit are more interested in IT and it’s easier with them, while others are not so knowledgeable in IT. But you learn that very fast. When one of them looks puzzled… you realize that very fast and learn what you have to consider when talking to these colleagues. That does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence our collaboration – from my point of view, there are no problems to discuss issues with the colleagues from the business unit.

## An interviewee from the business unit con<sup>fi</sup>rms this view:

Most of the persons I talk to in the IT have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient experience with the requirements of our sales department and they know how MyCustomer is used by the brokers.

Despite the cross-domain knowledge being equally distributed, the boundary-spanning potential, based on this crossdomain knowledge, is even slightly higher when business and IT communicate directly. While these units and the liaison unit are comparably able to participate in the respective other <sup>fi</sup>eld, business and IT unit members are more able to participate in and negotiate for their own <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Relationships are also comparably good within and outside planned interaction structures. All actors involved share a long history and have worked together for years. An employee from the IT explains:

Our friendship-like relationship helps a lot – when there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict or when we have to clarify something. I can then tell them: ‘I could not follow you. Please explain it once again. You can’t do that when you do not know the other person. Yet, with this friendship, they take more time to explain things. […] And you also get more honest explanations – even when there are political issues ….

Thus, while the relationship-based explanation predicts no deviation, boundary-spanning theory does. In fact, the actual interaction structures deviate slightly from planned ones, as can be seen from the questionnaire data, which show more direct interaction between business and IT than planned (deviation of 30.5 percentage points, cf. Table D1 in appendix), and in more detail from the interviews. While direct interaction between business and IT is more frequent than planned and for some changes they even do not involve the liaison unit at all, the liaison unit still is part of the IT change process, whose contribution is appreciated due to the unit’s cross-domain knowledge. An employee from the IT unit explained:

They do a lot of work for us. Sometimes, I just get a call: ‘There was this and that issue; I have discussed it with the business. And you simply have to do X.’ Sometimes, we even don’t need to do anything anymore.

Still, this contribution is smaller than envisioned due to the fact that direct business/IT interaction is more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and due to the higher knowledge of one’s own <sup>fi</sup>eld that resides in the business and the IT unit compared with the liaison unit, that is, the higher direct boundary-spanning potential:

If I would learn everything I need to know from the liaison unit, I would be <sup>fi</sup>ne only talking to the liaison unit. But that is a necessary condition. They have to know exactly what they talk about. Yet, sometimes they do not.

Thus, trilateral meetings (‘to hear it from the horse’s mouth’) and even bilateral meetings only between business and IT are an important aspect of the IT change process. Yet, in contrast to ThisBank, such bilateral meetings and the decision to not involve the liaison even in a whole topic is always jointly agreed upon with the liaison unit:

Sometimes the business unit directly contacts me. Then, I always consult with the liaison unit: ‘Do you want to be involved in the discussions around this change?’ And when they tell me to do it on my own, then we do it, and just keep them informed about what we do.

In a nutshell, the involved actors are inclined to span the boundary between business and IT as ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently as possible to reach their common goal to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently implement changes with high quality. Consequently, they pragmatically adjust the planned interaction structure to make the IT change process more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient by bypassing the liaison unit when they consider this the faster alternative. Yet, the liaison unit is involved in the majority of changes and contributes to them based on its cross-domain knowledge. Again, the driving force is cross-domain knowledge. When asked to compare the importance of cross-domain knowledge with the importance of a strong relationship, one interviewee explained:

De<sup>fi</sup>nitely, knowledge is more important. A strong relationship helps a lot and facilitates a lot, yet, it is knowledge that is indispensable.

Strong relationships increase the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and openness of discussions about changes and thus improve the IT change process. Weak direct relationships between business and IT are a precondition for deviations because actors need to know whom to contact and who may help them with a particular change, that is, they have to know the persons who have cross domain knowledge.

## ThatBank

The planned IT change process at ThatBank is similar to the one at ThisInsurance. The business unit contacts a liaison unit for change requests. The liaison unit is responsible for a functional speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, a <sup>fi</sup>rst cost estimate, and for coordinating between the developers and the business, similar to a project manager. It is the major contact for business and IT, who may interact directly in trilateral meetings to clarify the details of a change request or possible solutions to it.

Boundary-spanning potential is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient within planned structures, while it is very heterogeneous outside these structures, that is, at the immediate business/IT interface. Two factors highlighted by interviewees include cross-domain knowledge in the liaison unit and the legitimacy to negotiate for and participate in business and IT. An employee from the business unit explains:

When I talk to the IT unit and they tell me [what they do], I often cannot follow them. Then, I need the liaison unit to explain to me what will change due to the actions of IT. When I talk to the developer, I only understand half of what he is trying to tell me.

Yet, other actors in the IT unit have more cross-domain knowledge, can translate for their colleagues, and have less problems talking to the business unit.

By and large, business and I, we talk the same language. Of course, I am not from the business and when it becomes too complex, I cannot follow. […]And then they explain it. […] On the other hand, when I have to explain some technical things I should not use too many technical terms …. And I think that works.

The legitimacy to negotiate for and participate in business and IT resides above all in the liaison unit, thus within the planned structures. The liaison unit often enforces its role and insists on the IT unit <sup>fi</sup>rst talking to the liaison unit and not directly to the business.

However, the relationship is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better outside the planned interaction structures than it is within these structures. While there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the IT and the liaison unit, the direct relationship between the business and IT managers is better. The liaison unit manager described:

We all are a little stubborn. And [some of us] are impulsive. And that results in con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

His counterpart in the IT complained:

If one person is nitpicking and wants everything to be perfect, and the other is pragmatic to avoid inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and overhead, then you clearly sometimes end up in quarrel.

And an employee from the IT compares the relationship with the liaison unit with the relationship to the business:

[The relationship to the business unit] is very casual. You don’t have to think about how you say something. […] This way of working together is what makes the collaboration trusted. […] With the liaison unit there is friction. Sometimes the liaison unit thinks they can tell us what we have to do. We don’t like that. We are peers, there is no hierarchy.

![](/api/attachments/GV7VCR52/fulltext/images/215a5b07f43e01a3a14bf738366fe9f7424d66530aac2a2f43d516749067aac4.jpg)  
Planned and actual interaction structures at ThatBank (schematic).

Hence, while the boundary-spanning theory predicts that actual structures follow planned structures, the relationships are better outside planned interaction structures and thus predict a deviation of actual from planned interaction structures.

In fact, our interviews revealed that the planned interaction structure was nearly literally adhered to. One liaison unit employee underlined the importance of boundary-spanning potential and cross-domain knowledge:

The interaction [between IT and business] is always via the liaison unit. I have the impression that the IT beliefs that the liaison unit can answer their questions better.

## And one of his liaison colleagues added:

The business side always tries to contact me when trying to establish contact with the IT. One reason might be that I have known them for ten years. Another reason might be, I think, that the colleagues in the IT answer quite technically.

Yet, also the con<sup>fl</sup>ict contributed to the adherence to planned structures. However, contrary to what we had expected, it did not drive a deviation of actual from planned structures but an adherence to the planned structure. The con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the IT and liaison unit manager was, among other things, about the responsibilities of the liaison and IT unit. The IT manager argued that most of the liaison unit staff (e.g., the business analysts) should be moved to his unit. Therefore, the liaison unit manager enforces his unit’s formal role and prevents the IT from interacting directly with the business unit. An employee from the business described:

The liaison unit manager emphasizes his role. Sometimes IT contacts me directly. Yet then, the liaison unit manager says: ‘Stop, I am the liaison unit, you have to talk to me <sup>fi</sup>rst because I – on behalf of the business – have charged you to implement this. If you have a problem, you contact me <sup>fi</sup>rst. Maybe the problem is with us.’[…]. Hence, I in the business do not interact directly with the IT.

Thus, the con<sup>fl</sup>ict (relationship-based explanation) in<sup>fl</sup>uences the legitimacy to negotiate for IT (boundary-spanning theory).

The ongoing con<sup>fl</sup>ict also results in lower service quality in change implementation due to frequent disputes about whose fault an issue is and due to the IT unit ‘sometimes blocking issues just like that’ when they think that the liaison unit is pushing them.

An exception to this general adherence to planned structures could be seen between a small group of business, IT, and liaison unit actors. This group was able to increase boundary-spanning potential directly between business and IT with trilateral meetings and discussions that enhanced cross-domain knowledge of business and IT. They interacted directly more often than envisioned based on the level of available cross-domain knowledge and the knowledge of their own <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Thus, in ThatBank, boundary-spanning potential was generally better within planned structures. These structures were even further enforced due to the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the liaison unit and the IT unit, and the liaison unit consequently enforcing its formally de<sup>fi</sup>ned role.

## Discussion

## Combined research model

Synthesizing the driving forces of all cases, we can re<sup>fi</sup>ne the theoretical arguments of boundary-spanning theory and the relationship-based explanation and advance the initial research model by combining both explanations. As can be seen from the above individual case descriptions, boundaryspanning theory is dominant, predicting whether or not actual interaction structures deviate from planned structures– or whether a mixture of planned and other structures develops, as in ThisInsurance. While especially cross-domain knowledge causes deviations, weak relationships are an enabler of such deviations, stronger relationships in<sup>fl</sup>uence the resulting service quality, and a con<sup>fl</sup>ict might reinforce planned structures. Figure 11 illustrates the converging theoretical model, which describes the individual factors driving deviations and how they interact. It will be further described in the following.

This research model contains three new constructs, which will be brie<sup>fl</sup>y described before we discuss the causal relations within the research model. The <sup>fi</sup>rst new construct is the locus of legitimization by actual behavior, which describes which actors are actually legitimized to negotiate for and participate in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice. It summarizes both aspects from boundary-spanning theory because in our cases both aspects were always congruent. Opposed to this is the locus of legitimization by formal role, which describes who the nominated boundary spanners are. The difference between the two, the degree of deviation, is the third new construct.

Cross-domain knowledge is a major factor driving boundary-spanning potential. It in<sup>fl</sup>uences the potential service quality because it facilitates the externalization of knowledge and thus the building of constructs and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of hypotheses (Nonaka, 1994)<sup>9</sup>, for example, regarding how a change should be implemented.

On the basis of cross-domain knowledge and shared experiences, actors can more effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently communicate because they can build on a ‘common base of understanding’ (Nonaka, 1994: 24) and may have synchronized their way of thinking (Nonaka, 1994). That is, crossdomain knowledge in<sup>fl</sup>uences the service quality that can be reached within planned structures.

This potential service quality is one of three major factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the locus of legitimization by actual behavior, which stems from the person’s knowledge and their ability to span the boundary and does not require a certain formally assigned role.

As can be seen from the research model, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the potential service quality on the locus of legitimacy by actual role is moderated by the inclination of involved actors to span the boundary effectively and reach IT change process quality. If the potential service quality is bad due to a lack of crossdomain knowledge within planned structures, as, for example, at ThisBank, the involved actors withdraw the legitimacy to span the boundary from the nominated boundary spanners and thus change the locus of legitimacy by actual behavior because they are inclined to reach high service quality. In the cases with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient cross-domain knowledge and thus a good potential service quality, the nominated boundary spanners kept their legitimacy.

Obviously, another factor in<sup>fl</sup>uences the locus of legitimacy by actual role: the cross-domain knowledge by other actors. If no other actors possess superior cross-domain knowledge, no other person can gain legitimacy by actual behavior if it is taken away from nominated boundary spanners. In the cases with deviations, these deviations were always induced by superior cross-domain knowledge by other actors.

![](/api/attachments/GV7VCR52/fulltext/images/a2e15d878c49978b972bce401b0b703731e9d63c73b02394bb85ff85d973f3e7.jpg)  
Combined research model.

Again, the inclination of the involved actors positively moderates this in<sup>fl</sup>uence of cross-domain knowledge on the locus of legitimacy by actual behavior: They are willing to span the boundary and thus search for the best way to do so.

The locus of legitimacy by actual behavior in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences the degree of deviation: If – as in case of ThisBank – the locus of legitimacy by actual behavior is totally disjoint from the locus of legitimacy by formal role (i.e., the nominated boundary spanners), the full deviation scenario will be observed, if both loci are congruent (ThisManufacturer), the organization as planned scenario occurs, and when they only slightly differ (ThisInsurance) a complementary organization scenario will be the result.

Finally, the actual service quality is determined by the degree of deviation, that is, the actual interaction structures, and their boundary-spanning potential (especially crossdomain knowledge), and the relationships within these structures, which, when they are strong, may enhance service quality, as can be seen from ThisManufacturer, ThisBank, and ThisInsurance.

Strong relationships in<sup>fl</sup>uence the IT change process quality by making communication more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and open. Such relationships can be compared with embedded interactions, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘interactions that take place through social relations and networks of relations’ (Uzzi, 1999: 482). Literature highlights that such embeddedness of interaction increases trust and expectations of reciprocity and thus stabilizes interaction (Uzzi and Gillespie, 2002). We found that the embedded, good working relationships fostered ef<sup>fi</sup>cient discussions because trust led to earlier sharing of relevant information, which is typical for such relations (Uzzi, 1997). This upfront information sharing helps the involved actors identify and clarify important issues and avoid rework.

Yet, relationships play a weaker role than envisioned. Besides in<sup>fl</sup>uencing service quality, they moderate the effect of cross-domain knowledge by others on the locus of legitimacy by actual behavior: Across cases, interviewees stated that knowing who the responsible persons within business and IT are, who have superior cross-domain knowledge, and already having talked to the actors in the respective other unit enabled business and IT to interact directly and thus to change the locus of legitimacy by actual behavior. Yet, positive relationships never drive deviations.

Negative relationships, however, may entail a fourth scenario: relationship-based failure. It occurs when there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict within planned structures. As can be seen from ThatBank, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of relationships does not follow the relationship-based explanation, which would predict a deviation from planned structures. In contrast, the con<sup>fl</sup>ict leads to the nominated boundary spanner reinforcing his or her role and thus the planned interaction structures, reducing the degree of deviation. That is, in case of con<sup>fl</sup>ict, the relationship moderates the effect of the locus of legitimization on the degree of deviation, preventing such deviation.

The reason for positive relationships playing a weaker role than envisioned by the relationship-based explanation (i.e., rather in<sup>fl</sup>uencing service quality but only having a minor in<sup>fl</sup>uence on deviations) might lie in the different situations discussed in the extant literature on informal organizations. For example, Albrecht and Goldman (1985) describe a situation from the experiments at the Hawthorne plant (Roethlisberger and Dickson, 1939), in which workers informally organized to reduce their productivity below the goals set by management in order to avoid competitiveness between workers and wage reduction. This scenario differs from our cases in the degree of inclination of employees to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their job in line with management goals. While in our cases all involved actors were inclined to reach IT change process success, the goals of the workers at the Hawthorne plant were not aligned with the goals of their management. While the former wanted to keep wages stable, the latter aimed at more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient production. Yet, the workers limited production by informal rules and norms to keep wages stable (Roethlisberger and Dickson, 1939).

Our results show that cross-domain knowledge is the most important determinant of deviations between actual and planned interaction structures. Cross-domain knowledge within planned structures determines potential service quality, and cross-domain knowledge outside planned structures determines whether other actors can legitimize themselves to be a boundary spanner by actual behavior.

This prominent role of cross-domain knowledge is in line with IS research (e.g., Bassellier and Benbasat, 2004; Curtis et al., 1988; Feeny and Willcocks, 1998) and with the boundary-spanning literature, which even argues that crossdomain knowledge itself can span boundaries and be a boundary object (Carlile, 2002).

Cross-domain knowledge is so important because it is required for knowledge transfer. ‘We can only tell someone about something he or she does not know by making use of something he or she does know’ (Krauss and Fussell, 1990: 112). This is even more relevant because IT and business need to share knowledge about the business processes and about how the users work with the investigated IT application. In all four cases, this knowledge was tacit (Nonaka, 1994), that is, knowledge that cannot be easily formalized and transmitted because of its personal quality and its roots in action and experience (Nonaka, 1994). Integrating this tacit knowledge into an IT application requires business and IT to transfer this knowledge and externalize it, that is, enable its codi<sup>fi</sup>cation. In order to do so, actors need to build on cross-domain knowl edge or ‘redundancy of information’ (Nonaka, 1994: 23) that provides them with a ‘common base for understanding’ (Nonaka, 1994: 24) and enables them to understand and even anticipate what the other is saying. Thus, they can collaboratively develop and test constructs and describe how the IT application needs to be altered to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the business requirements (Nonaka, 1994).

In line with this, interviewees stated that cross-domain expertise allows them to discuss more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently with actors from the other <sup>fi</sup>eld, building on each other’s knowledge and not having to repeat basic information. Thus, communication becomes more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and they are better able to make themselves understood and contribute to the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of change requests.

Due to their inclination to span the boundary, they hence tend to follow structures with superior boundary-spanning potential in terms of cross-domain knowledge and grant actors with cross-domain knowledge legitimacy to span a boundary.

## Contribution to theory

This research has shown that boundary-spanning theory dominantly predicts when deviations occur at the interface between business and IT. Yet, relationships form the basis for such deviations and have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on actual interaction structures between business and IT when there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict within planned interaction structures. Even in this situation, they enforce planned interaction structures instead of causing a deviation as initially predicted by the relationship-based explanation.

With this research explaining the deviations between planned and actual interaction structures at the business/IT interface, we have contributed to IS research and other research areas in several ways outlined in the following.

In the context of IS research, we offer an explanation for low IT service quality, in terms of how well the IT function implements requirements of the business unit, despite favorable IT governance setups. Our research not only explains that but also why such favorable setups are sometimes not adhered to and thus do not yield their intended consequences. By taking the discussion to the operational level and discussing the differences between planned and actual interaction structures, we offer an important contribution to answering the question as to why IT sometimes does not generate the expected business value.

In addition, this research may drive IS researchers to consider the difference between actual and planned interaction structures and to be sensitive to that differentiation. They may then potentially better understand surprising outcomes of research that mixes elements and data from both types of structures – actual and planned.

We also contribute to boundary-spanning theory in several ways: First, we found that cross-domain knowledge is outstanding among the drivers of nominated boundary spanners becoming boundary spanners in practice. While there is no explicit construct in boundary-spanning theory, Levina and Vaast (2005) at least claim that ‘a peripheral understanding (353) is required to acquire the legitimacy to participate in both <sup>fi</sup>elds. However, in our cases, not only peripheral but deep cross-domain expertise showed to be required. This discrepancy with Levina and Vaast (2005) may be rooted in the different situations analyzed. While our cases involved information systems that are critical for the users’ daily work, the systems analyzed in Levina and Vaast’s cases are less integral to day-to-day work, and the distinct collaborating units are less tightly coupled; thus, in their case, knowledge might be explicit and not tacit, and less-detailed shared knowledge might be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for transferring the required information.

Second, we introduced two additional concepts to boundary-spanning theory, which help to analyze boundary situations more holistically: The legitimacy by formal role (in contrast to legitimacy by actual behavior, which corresponds to the legitimacy concepts discussed by Levina and Vaast, 2005), and boundary-spanning structures involving multiple boundary spanners.

We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the importance of legitimacy by formal role, which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the actual boundary-spanning practices because nominated boundary spanners enforce their role. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of such formal roles is not discussed in Levina and Vaast (2005). A reason might be that they do not analyze relationships in detail and that reinforcement of formal legitimacy happens especially when there is a con<sup>fl</sup>ict within the planned structure. We distinguish between formal legitimacy and legitimacy by actual behavior, which interact when the legitimacy by formal role is enforced (e.g., due to con<sup>fl</sup>icts) and limits the degree of deviation even when the legitimacy by actual behavior within planned structures is lower than it could be outside these structures.

In contrast to previous boundary spanning research, we moved the analysis of planned and actual boundary spanning away from individual boundary spanners (Levina and Vaast, 2005) and discussed several boundary spanners simultaneously. Thus, we were able to identify different degrees of deviations of actual from planned boundary-spanning structures. Where previous research found that nominated boundary spanners either do or do not become boundary spanners in practice, our analysis found that the degree of deviation might also lie between these two extremes: some of the nominated boundary spanners may become boundary spanners in practice, some may not, and potentially others may take over their boundary spanning role. This situation occurs when actual and other structures offer comparable boundaryspanning potential. Full replacement of boundary-spanning structures only takes place when the formally planned boundary-spanning structures do not offer any boundaryspanning potential (by actual behavior).

## Implications for practitioners

Our results will help practitioners better address the question whether actual working procedures differ from formally planned ones, and hence govern real rather than planned structures. They can build on the understanding of factors driving such deviations presented in this research and further develop empirical instruments to make actual interaction structures visible and enhance their understanding of actual structures. On the basis of this understanding, they can evaluate the (dis)advantages of such deviations and implement measures to prevent unwanted deviations.

When planning a new organizational structure, practitioners may consult the results of this research and consider the factors that may drive deviations from their planned organizational structure when identifying actors who should occupy certain roles. For example, considering that deep cross-domain expertise is a major enabler of boundary-spanning potential at the business/IT interface, they can nominate boundary spanners who already possess such expertise, knowing that these have a better chance of becoming actual boundary spanners than other actors. Thus, this research enables practitioners to maintain the organizational structures that they set up.

## Limitations

A <sup>fi</sup>rst potential limitation that needs to be discussed in context with the applied case study approach concerns the generalizability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings. A lot of researchers argue that a limited number of case studies restricts generalizability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings (see Table 1 in Lee and Baskerville, 2003).

Yet, case study results cannot be generalized statistically but analytically, building on a careful selection of cases following a replication logic containing literal and theoretical replications, that is, cases that are predicted to duplicate initial <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a <sup>fi</sup>rst case study, and cases for which the analyzed theory predicts contrasting results (Yin, 2009). Also, in case studies, theory validation and/or development and generalizability are not based on the existence of constructs (e.g., cross-domain knowledge or the degree of deviation) or on their correlation. They are based on rich data and descriptions, yielding a deep understanding of causal relationships between constructs that the researcher can gain from multiple in-depth interviews.

Auerbach and Silverstein (2003) argue that a theory can be extended beyond a given sample when researchers are informed about the context of the setting that a theory was developed in. With respect to this context, they can transfer the theoretical constructs of that theory to cases outside the initial set of analyzed cases. While it may be necessary to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the theoretical constructs, they help understand the new case (Auerbach and Silverstein, 2003).

Since we selected our case studies based on a theoretical replication logic based on the two proposed theoretical explanations and validated these explanations based on causal arguments derived from the interviews, and following Auerbach and Silverstein (2003), we argue that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are transferrable to other IT change process settings: Across the cases, the arguments showed to be consistent and the interviews revealed no industry-, country-, or companyspeci<sup>fi</sup>c arguments that affect the validity of these arguments. In addition, there is evidence that the IT organization in general (Tiwana and Konsynski, 2010) and the IT change process in particular (OGC, 2007) are not strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by industry speci<sup>fi</sup>cs.

We also assume that the theoretical arguments are valid for other business/IT interface processes. For example, application development incident management, and problem management can require similar interaction between business and IT units, and it does not matter whether business and IT discuss how to resolve an incident or how to implement a change. The same driving forces are applicable and a lack of cross-domain knowledge will prevent them from developing adequate solutions. If nominated boundary spanners lack cross-domain knowledge but employees are inclined to provide high-quality incident solutions they will also try to <sup>fi</sup>nd other interaction structures that offer better cross-domain knowledge and hence might deviate from planned incident structures, just as it is the case within the IT change process.<sup>10</sup>

Yet, we assume that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings might not be applicable to settings that do not require the exchange of tacit knowledge, for example, when a very structured and easily documented IT application is concerned that supports fully documented and standardized business processes, or when minor changes or incidents are concerned that do not require business/IT interaction because they do not impact the way business works, thus no ‘know-how’ (Orlikowski, 2002: 250) needs to be transferred. In such situations, requirements can potentially be fully codi<sup>fi</sup>ed, hence interaction and cross-domain knowledge will play a less important or even no role, and the research model will not be applicable. Our interviews provided initial support for this hypothesis since even in ThisInsurance, where changes were often handled directly between business and IT or in trilateral meetings, the fully cascaded communication channel via the liaison unit was completely adhered to in case of less complex changes.

A further limitation might be a potential bias of interviewee responses. For example, since interviewees knew that the case study results would be discussed with the management, they might either not have wanted to admit weaknesses or they might have wanted to underline pain points they perceived. However, conducting multiple interviews within each case with interviewees from different hierarchical levels and all involved units, we could develop a comprehensive picture free of contradictions and are thus convinced that we could to the highest degree possible limit bias in interviewees’ answers.

Also, we could not send the SNA questionnaire to all actors, especially those only peripherally involved. However, we made sure that all key actors and also a number of more peripheral actors <sup>fi</sup>lled in the questionnaire and triangulated the data with the interviews. Hence, we are convinced that the network data derived adequately describe the actual communication network at the business/IT interface in the involved companies.

## Conclusion

This paper has developed an explanation for when and why actual interaction structures between business and IT deviate from planned ones. Using boundary-spanning theory and a relationship-based explanation as theoretical lenses, we studied 4 cases with data from 56 interviews, which provided us with in-depth insights into planned and actual interaction structures.

We found that boundary-spanning theory best explained whether or not deviations would occur. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and analyzed factors that drive this deviation. While a river leaves its bed when it <sup>fi</sup>nds an easier path elsewhere, structure deviations at the business/IT interface appear when the boundary-spanning potential, especially in terms of crossdomain knowledge, but also in terms of the legitimacy to negotiate for and participate in business and IT, is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient or even not existent within planned structures; in such situations, planned structures may be complemented or even replaced by other structures with more boundary-spanning potential. Yet, a con<sup>fl</sup>ict within planned structures might reinforce planned structures and prevent deviation.

As is always the case when analyzing a new topic, we had to focus on the most important in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors. Future research might add to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by analyzing additional factors such as the effect of stricter regulations and compliance processes. Companies of<sup>fi</sup>cially putting emphasis on and controlling the fact that formally planned structures are adhered to might see a weaker degree of deviation under comparable circumstances than our cases saw. In an extreme case, stricter compliance processes might lead to a similar situation as relationship-based failure, when compliance processes prevent a strongly required deviation despite the potential service quality being very low and legitimacy by actual behavior outside formally planned structures being higher. Similar effects might be seen in more hierarchical cultures where employees tend to stick more to what is formally prescribed. In both situations, the formal legitimacy to span a boundary might play a much stronger role and might (partly) eliminate the effect that the locus of legitimization by actual behavior might have even in cases without con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

Also, future research can build on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to analyze normatively the circumstances under which deviations of actual from planned structures are positive or negative and how to react to them, that is, whether to change the plan or reinforce the planned interaction structure.

Another interesting area for future research might be the process of deviation. While this research investigated a <sup>fi</sup>xed state, in which the actual structure may or may not deviate from the planned structure, it would be valuable to analyze longitudinally how such deviations take place and how the actual interaction structure evolves.

## Appendix C

## Using critical incident technique and laddering

Motivated by the fact that critical incidents can be more accurately recalled and described than average behavior (Flanagan, 1954) and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the success of the process being studied (Bycio and Allen, 2004), we asked interviewees to describe a recent change that was either handled very positively or very negatively, identifying critical incidents. Focusing on such critical situations, we asked the interviewees for factors that were most important for the eventual deviation of actual from planned interaction structures in these situations and applied the interviewing technique used in laddering to understand the connection between this aspect and the potential deviation of interaction structures in more detail. This technique allowed us to investigate answers more systematically (Rugg and McGeorge, 1995). Questions like: ‘Why didn’t you follow the planned interaction structures?’ or ‘Why in particular did this aspect in<sup>fl</sup>uence your choice of interaction?’ were used to probe upward, respectively, downward the ‘ladder’ of means (drivers) that lead to certain ends (deviation of actual interaction structures) (Reynolds and Gutman, 1988; Rugg and McGeorge, 1995). We also asked the interviewees to describe the regular IT change process, that is, the one they actually followed for regular changes, and compared it with the planned one in a similar fashion.

## Appendix D

## Calculation of deviation measure

The betweenness of a group, such as a liaison unit, is calculated as the percentage of shortest paths between nongroup members of which group members are part (Everett and Borgatti, 1999). Using the survey data, we derived a communication network covering involved people in the business unit, IT unit, and liaison unit. In this network, a tie between two actors means they interact at least once per week in meetings or via phone. Since we are only interested in communication between business and IT (i.e., not the shortest paths within the business unit or within the IT unit), we developed an adapted measure based on the group betweenness measure proposed by Everett and Borgatti (1999):

● Calculation of shortest paths lengths between any pair of business and IT actors;

● Elimination of all liaison actors;

● Recalculation of shortest paths lengths;

● Count of pairs of actors between which the second length is longer or who have become disconnected when eliminating the liaison actors, that is, the pairs of actors whose shortest paths involve at least one liaison person, making the liaison person part of the most probable path along which business and IT exchange information;

● Division by the sum of connected pairs in the original network (before the elimination).

The betweenness centrality value for the planned structure was determined as follows:

● 100% when the liaison person or unit was supposed to fully channel communication;

● 0% when trilateral meetings were an integral part of interaction structures;

● 50% when the liaison unit or person should be the primary contact, yet trilateral meetings or clarifying meetings without the liaison unit or person were part of the planned structure.
