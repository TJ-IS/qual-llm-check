## A B S T R A C T

Previous research has found that social networking applications such as microblogs can develop gratification and addiction among users. Although it is generally believed that gratification reflects the gains through use and that addiction is caused by excessive use of the technology, limited research has been done to empirically verify the relationship between usage patterns and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation versus addiction. This paper attempts to explore the impacts of posting patterns on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction dimensions for microblog platforms. On the basis of existing literature on use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations of media users, we focus on examining the different posting patterns between weekdays and weekends. By using multiple data collection methods, a data set was obtained consisting of 308 microblog users. Our analysis shows that the overall posting frequency is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction levels. Moreover, the posting of microblogs is usually more intensive on weekdays than on weekends. Heavy weekend users tend to feel more socially grati<sup>fi</sup>ed and are most likely to be addicted. In contrast, heavy weekday users are usually not addicted, and reported the lowest levels of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation among all users. Balanced users that do not display distinguishable difference between weekdays and weekends manifest grati<sup>fi</sup>cation levels around the average levels, and are less addicted than weekend users, even if their overall usage amounts are heavy.

## 1. Introduction

Research in the area of information systems (IS) has extensively investigated the positive outcomes resulting from Information Technology (IT) use [1]. Among various theoretical postulations, the use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation (U&G) theory provides a user-centered perspective to explain that the user’s selection and use of certain technologies depends on the grati<sup>fi</sup>cations gained [2–5]. The U&G theory is a well-received theoretical framework that explains the relationships between use and the psychological perceptions of users. It has been developed based on the fundamental assumption that users are goal directed and pursue the psychological needs grati<sup>fi</sup>ed through the use [4,6].

In contrast to the positive outcomes of IT use, the negative impacts have not yet been suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently addressed [1]. Technology addiction, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a user’s psychological state of maladaptive dependency on IT use, is known to be one of the major negative outcomes of overintensive IT use. Addiction is manifested as compulsive IT use at the expense of other important activities [7]. Technology addiction leads to harmful consequences such as reduced work performance, compromised social life, and even severe mental problems [8–10], and has been examined in some IT contexts, such as online games [7,11] and mobile email [12].

It is generally believed that use pattern, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, and addiction are closely related to each other because grati<sup>fi</sup>cation re<sup>fl</sup>ects the gains through usage and addiction is caused by excess use of the technology [5,11]. However, limited research empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>es the relationships between usage patterns and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation versus addiction. It is unclear how usage patterns may relate to the dimensions of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. On the other hand, although the extant literature indicates that addiction is the consequence of excessive use [11,10], little research has empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the positive relationship between them. In other words, the claim that “use is related to the level of addiction” is challengeable even though it is

generally accepted. Therefore, this paper attempts to empirically examine the impacts of posting patterns on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction dimensions.

As one of the most popular and fast growing Internet social media services, microblogs are dramatically changing our methods of communicating and accessing information [13]. Microblog services enable users to exchange messages in 140 characters through various applications. As a result, post volume on microblogs tends to be huge, and excessive use is very common. According to compete.com, more than 40 million unique users in America visited twitter.com in October 2013. As reported in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements, Sina Weibo, a popular microblog platform in China, has about 54 million daily active users in the <sup>fi</sup>rst half of 2013 [14]. The emergence of this phenomenon raises the questions of why microblogs are so attractive to users [2,15], and furthermore, how is the use of this technology-enabled social media associated with user grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and technology addiction.

As the research angel in this paper, we choose to study the differences between weekdays and weekends to explore the relationships between use patterns, grati<sup>fi</sup>cations, and addiction of microblogging. The choice of research angel is rooted in the usepattern studies examined in traditional media research. Research in traditional media has investigated the use-pattern differences between weekdays and weekends in newspapers, radio, and TV [16,17]. For example, newspapers are read more carefully, and for much longer periods of time, on weekends compared with weekdays [52]. Weekend editions of newspapers show differences in design, content, and reader demographics compared with the weekday editions [18]. There were structural and programmatic differences between weekday and weekend TV viewing patterns [17]. So far, most research attention has focused on media readership or viewing, instead of active posting behaviors, rendering many previous studies inadequate for exploring the use of microblogs as a social media application.

Furthermore, posting frequency, represented by daily posting amount, is chosen as a metric of usage in this paper. Compared with records of readership behaviors, which are mechanically limited and unavailable, posting data are available for every individual user, allowing for greater data reliability and validity. Many previous studies have suggested that microblogs play a more effective role as an information production, exchange, and consumption site compared with a traditional social network [19], indicating that the microblog usage could be reasonably re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the posting behavior. Meanwhile, much attention has been focused on exploring the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation concerning the effects of media readership or viewing in traditional media research [16,20], indicating the necessity of treating posting behavior as the further study focus.

Thus far, little research has explored the usage time patterns of online social media. The few studies that address usage patterns of microblogs show that in general, users are primarily active on weekdays [21], but further details are not investigated. On the other hand, it has been largely noticed that social media users may differ in their preferences in use time. For example, Facebook engagement is about 32% higher on weekend than weekday [22]. More attention has been paid to Facebook posts on weekend because they feel more relaxed and happiness sentiments on weekend than on weekdays [23]. Extant literature has revealed that media users tend to show behavioral, cognitive, and psychological differences between weekdays and weekends [17,18,24]. Therefore, if usage behaviors also differ between weekdays and weekends, it is reasonable to deduce that different posting preferences on weekdays and weekends of microblog users re<sup>fl</sup>ect their various grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction levels.

Through multiple data sources, we compiled a data set consisting of user-level microblogging posting data for 308 microblog users. The data on posting patterns are obtained from the three most used microblogging platforms, whereas data on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction dimensions are collected from selfreport questionnaires. Our analyses show that the frequency of microblog posting is not a key factor to user grati<sup>fi</sup>cation or addition, whereas users with different posting time patterns show different levels of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation or addictions. For instance, heavybalanced users, who post frequently on both weekdays and weekends, did not manifest the highest observed grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction levels. However, heavy weekend users who have higher engagement on weekends gain all categories of grati<sup>fi</sup>cations at higher levels and are more addicted in most of the addiction dimensions. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our exploratory research may offer promising directions for future studies.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review related literature and discuss our theoretical foundations. The third section presents our research framework and hypotheses. The fourth section discusses the research methods. The <sup>fi</sup>fth section presents the testing results and, as extended discussions, describes the comparisons of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction dimensions across four groups, which are divided according to users’ posting frequency on weekdays and weekends. The last section concludes the paper by discussing theoretical and practically implications.

## 2. Related literature

Our research is grounded in two theoretical realms related to media and technology use: U&G theory and technology addiction. In this section, we review related literature in these two realms, respectively.

## 2.1. Use and gratification theory

Rooted in mass communication research, the U&G theory provides a user-centered perspective to explain why a speci<sup>fi</sup>c medium or technology that enters the stage of mass communication is chosen over other alternatives [3,25]. A major assumption of U&G is that users are goal oriented and actively select media to gratify their own psychological needs [26]. Considering the personally motivated use, this prospective implies that users’ differences in behavior and cognition are related to a certain technology [6].

Since <sup>fi</sup>rst developed in the 1940s, the U&G theory is regarded as a cohesive way to explore users’ engagement in a certain medium or technology, ranging from traditional media such as radio, newspaper, and television to diverse online applications, such as Internet, blogging, online games, mobile E-book, virtual communities, and social networks. According to Chen [2], this theory is well suited for the study of microblogging, because it is a tool of mass interpersonal communication.

The U&G theory is concerned with purposeful media use. In other words, people want to gratify a range of needs through usage, implying that a main focus in related research is to identify the users’ grati<sup>fi</sup>cations in using a particular technology. Diverse grati<sup>fi</sup>cations have been elicited in reference to different contexts, such as passing time, entertainment, information sharing, meeting friends in SNS [27], self-discovery, entertainment value, social enhancement, and maintaining interconnectivity in virtual community [28].

Current research in U&G has largely focused on the typologies of use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations, indicating that users use media for the content grati<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., Information, entertainment), the process grati<sup>fi</sup>cation through the experience (e.g., escape, sur<sup>fi</sup>ng), and the social grati<sup>fi</sup>cation with regard to the media’s social environ ment (e.g., interaction) [5,29,30,31]. In this study, we adopt the three categories discussed above because these categories allow for the measurement of users’ reactions to the different aspects of general technology use, and can be applied to studies on other technologies. In this regard, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is formatively composed of <sup>fi</sup>ve discrete constructs: perceived relative advantage, perceived enjoyment, social presence, perceived membership, and perceived in<sup>fl</sup>uence. With a vast number of participants distributed across the world, microblog provides users with a plethora of information about newly updated news, resources, opinions, and knowledge [32] in a more convenient way than other media. The one-direction con<sup>fi</sup>rmation in microblog makes it possible for users to exchange ideas and interests in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c community [33]. Moreover, the hashtag, as another special character in microblog use, enables users to share and acquire abundant information in certain categories [34]. Users could gain grati<sup>fi</sup>cation from microblog content by feeling more knowledgeable or experienced. Therefore, perceived relative advantage [53] is applied as the re<sup>fl</sup>ective component of content grati<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Process grati<sup>fi</sup>cation concerns users’ involvement in the use experience [30]. According to Hoffman and Novak [36], users are generally satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the enjoyment of the Internet use processes, which has been widely identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an important component of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation in almost every media context [15,37]. The user derives much enjoyment from the use process, because he/she is able to explore within the self-selected network built according to his/her special interests, and mobile devices facilitate this process. Therefore, in this paper, perceived enjoyment [15,38] is applied as the typical process grati<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Microblog is often regarded as a kind of social communication and interaction channel [39], i.e., a tool for keeping up social connections. Users derive social grati<sup>fi</sup>cation from the interactivity with others through microblog use, which is usually measured by social interaction or social presence in previous studies [6,15]. Besides social presence, which is also provided by SNSs, users gain other aspects of social grati<sup>fi</sup>cations in microblogging platforms. With self-selected networks, users can establish contact with others who have similar interests or backgrounds within certain communities, which provides the possibility of feeling a sense of membership and in<sup>fl</sup>uence. For instance, by following the same favorite star, users are connected, and their sharing in this group can usually get desirable feedback, which provides users with feelings of belonging to certain groups and being able to have an impact on others. Consequently, in the context of microblogging, the three speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of social grati<sup>fi</sup>cations studied in this paper are social presence, perceived membership, and perceived in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

## 2.2. Technology addiction

Regarding its similarities with existing substance-related or behavioral addictions (gambling, eating, drug, etc.), the term technology addiction is adapted to conceptualize a user’s involvement in technology accompanied with severe interference with daily activities, the existence of which has been demonstrated in various technology contexts [7,9,11,12,40].

As a new and quickly developing topic, the concept of technology addiction has not been associated with universally accepted terms and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions [11]. Rather, it has been described in multiple terms with different conceptual scopes [8]. Taking into account that technology addiction is about a user’s status relative to behavior, some terms emphasize the level of use: for example, pathological Internet use, problematic Internet use, and compulsive use [8,11]. Others stress the psychological traits, such as addiction, disorder, and dependency [10]. In this paper, we use the term “addiction” to represent a user’s psychological state of maladaptive dependency manifested through obsessive IT use at the expense of other important activities [1]. As a form of technology addiction, microblog addiction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a user’s psychological dependency on microblog through accumulated use at the expense of other duties.

A prominent issue in technology addiction study is the measurement of a user’s psychological involvement; the range of methods for doing so may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in various studies [41]. Three major approaches have been proposed in the previous studies. On the basis of the criteria for pathological gambling, Young [10] made one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst contributions to technology addiction measurement by putting forward an eightitem questionnaire (DQ) with a cut-off score of 5, and distinctly identifying users as addicted or not. Similarly, according to users answer to “do you think you are addicted”, users are diagnosed into two groups. Despite their popularity and simplicity, these methods are questioned according to their objectivity and accuracy [42].

The second approach treats technology addiction as continuous instead of discrete, arguing that there is no of<sup>fi</sup>cial cut-off point to identify addicted users; instead, all users have a certain level of addiction, with most users having a very low level of addiction [7,43]. Adopting this approach from work on behavioral addictions, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths (1996) treated technology addiction as a unidimensional concept, and summarized six criteria for measurement. The criteria in Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths’ approach is further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being core and peripheral in later studies [1,11].

The third type of literature measures technology addiction as a multidimensional concept [8,44]. Davis et al. [8] introduced online cognition scale to capture Internet addiction that composed of four dimensions: diminished impulse control (inability to independently reduce usage), loneliness and depression (the negative feelings related to restricted usage), distraction (the avoidance and procrastination because of usage), and social comfort (feelings of safety and security as a result of usage), describing various aspects of addiction. In previous studies, users showed differences along the four dimensions [32], indicating the possibility that users’ addiction can be analyzed not only as a whole but also in detailed dimensions.

In this paper, we treat technology addiction as a psychological status with gradual degrees. To explore users’ addiction levels in different aspects, we formulate addiction as a multidimensional construct based on Davis et al.'s [8] work, because of the following reasons. (1) Instead of setting a cut-off point to distinguish between the addicted or non-addicted users, this paper treats addiction as a continuous concept, which is consistent with Davis work. (2) One main purpose of our study is to investigate the relationship between use and addiction. ## 3. Research framework and hypotheses

Despite the increasing research interest, there has been little empirical work in understanding the positive and negative impacts of technology use, which can be represented by grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction, respectively. Notably, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction may be seen as interdependent on each other. Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation can be interpreted as a potential factor that may lead to addiction, whereas addiction may also be considered as an antecedent of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. In this regard, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction represent two aspects of the consequences of usage. In this paper, we empirically examine the impacts of usage on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation types and addiction dimensions (see Fig. 1). First, drawing upon existing literature, we propose research hypotheses in terms of the relationship between the usage frequency and the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction, and the relationship between the use-time preferences and the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. The hypotheses are then tested using data collected from a large microblogging platform. On the basis of posting preferences on weekdays or weekends, users are further divided into four groups. The grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction levels are compared pairwise among groups, to explore the different impacts of weekday/weekend posting patterns on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. Considering that different weekday/weekend usage will lead to different grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction levels, the differences between weekdays and weekends play a role in the same spirit of a moderator. Because “weekday” and “weekend” are two discrete nominal values that do not have inherently relative strengths, we examine their different effects, respectively, instead of explicitly formulating the day types in the form of a moderating construct in a structured equation model.

## 3.1. Use and gratification

As grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the particular dimensions of satisfaction concerning the use experiences [5], it implies a correlation between technology use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation [54]. argued that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is related to one’s Internet use, and according to Melanie et al. [4], grati<sup>fi</sup>cation can be a result of media involvement. Furthermore, the U&G perspective is conceptualized as a causal circulation; the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation based on usage experiences will in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ further usage motivation, indicating continued use, and then strengthened grati<sup>fi</sup>cations [6]. In summary, considering that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation re<sup>fl</sup>ects the gains through use, it can be hypothesized that a positive correlation exists between use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. As an important re<sup>fl</sup>ection of usage, posting frequency should thereby inherit this effect,

H1a. Microblogging posting frequency is positively related to the levels of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation dimensions.

## 3.2. Time pattern and gratification

Media users tend to show differences between weekdays and weekends. According to Towers [18], the weekday newspaper is mostly viewed for Information, whereas the weekend newspaper is also viewed for enjoyment. In the context of the Internet, the weekend version of the online newspaper has a longer lifespan at home, providing the possibility of careful and enjoyable readership, i.e., the content and process grati<sup>fi</sup>cations, respectively [16]. Bronner’s [24] work about mood claims that on weekends, because users have more free time and are in a good mood, users would feel more enjoyment and relaxation in the use process, and therefore gain higher process grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. From the perspective of social grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, users with higher use frequency during weekend prefer to spend time on microblog rather than of<sup>fl</sup>ine weekend activities such as spending time with friends and families, indicating their extraordinary valuation of online social grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. Furthermore, microblog provides users with rich social feelings, particularly on weekends. For example, concert and movie premieres are usually scheduled on weekends, and such a hot topic could be extensively blogged about. As a result, social communication with others who have similar interests induces weekend users’ feelings of social grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. Hereby,

H1b. Microblogging posting frequency on weekends is positively related to all dimensions of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation.

On the other hand, users gain different grati<sup>fi</sup>cation through microblog use on weekdays. The weekday newspaper is used mainly for information [18]. Similarly, the weekday Internet

## 3.3. Use and addiction

Many studies show that addiction is the consequence of intense use. In Theotokis and Doukidis’s [46] work, the time spent on IT use and the variety of use are found to have effects on addiction tendency. Users who spend a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater amount of time are considered addicted [11]. According to Young’s [10] study, addicted users report much larger use frequency. Thus,

H2a. Microblogging posting frequency is positively related to the levels of addiction dimensions.

## 3.4. Time pattern and addiction

People who are trapped in the context of technology tend to rationalize their excessive and problematic usage by interpreting the behavior as functional or positive. Such interpretation reinforces their mindset and induces them to continue their technology use [45]. Hence,

H2b. Microblogging posting frequency on weekends is positively related to the addiction dimensions.

In contrast, users who prefer to post microblogs on weekdays rather than weekends will achieve only content grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, indicating the lower addiction levels. Consequently, no hypothesis is put forward about the relationship between posting frequency on weekdays and addiction.

## 4. Research method

In this section, we will discuss our research method with regard to construct measurement, data collection, and validity and reliability check.

## 4.1. Data collection

Most of the existing research on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction uses self-reported typologies with questionnaires or interviews [46,47], and heavily relies on the interpretation of attitude variables rather than observable behavior. In this paper, a multisource data collection and measurement approach is used. Actual microblog posting data are collected to measure use. A questionnaire survey is used for collecting and measuring grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. In doing so, we attempt to achieve higher reliability and eliminate the possible problem of common method bias.

## 5. Results and discussions

## 5.1. Impacts of overall daily posting

On the basis of use record data, users’ daily posting amounts are calculated to represent the overall microblog use frequency in this study. Ordinary least square regression analyses are conducted to estimate the impact of use frequency on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction measurements. Instead of using aggregate scores for grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction, the relationships between posting and each dimension in these two groups are tested separately. In each regression analysis, overall posting frequency is used as the independent variable and one of the cognitive constructs is used as the dependent variable. The t-values and model signi<sup>fi</sup>cances of the analyses are shown in Table 6.

The results in Table 6 show that the overall posting frequency does not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on most of the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, except for the grati<sup>fi</sup>cation of perceived in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which means the hypothesis H1a is not ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently supported. Although it has been widely implied in the literature that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation can be strengthened through use [5,6], our results suggest that overall posting frequency is not a key factor in determining the levels of diverse grati<sup>fi</sup>cations. Table 6 also illustrates that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between overall posting frequency and addiction dimensions, indicating that the hypothesis H2a is not supported either.

As has been discussed above, addiction is believed to be positively associated with frequency of use [11,12,46], but little empirical evidence has been found. Our results show that the relationships among use frequency, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, and addiction may not be as straightforward as expected. Further analysis should be carried out taking other factors into considerations, such as time patterns.

## 5.2. Posting patterns: weekday and weekend

In the sample, the mean values for the posting frequency on weekdays and weekends are 1.369 and 1.154, with standard deviations of 2.11 and 1.86, respectively, indicating that users tend to post more on weekdays as compared with weekends in general. Furthermore, an independent sample t-test shows that the difference between posting frequency on weekdays and weekends is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at a level of p < 0.01. This result is counterintuitive, as one would expect people to post more on weekends when they have more free time. Alternatively, users do have time preferences on posting, indicating the cognitive and psychological differences between weekdays and weekends.

Table 7 shows the results of the impacts of user weekend (weekday) posting frequency on grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. The results clearly reveal that weekend posting frequency is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly related to two dimensions of social grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and all four dimensions of addiction, whereas weekday posting frequency has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on any dimensions of grati<sup>fi</sup>cations and addiction. In other words, H2b is fully supported, H1b is partly supported, and H1c is not supported at all. To large extent, the results show that the impacts of weekend posting are much more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant than those of weekday posting frequency, which essentially coheres with our theoretical analysis.

## 5.3. Group comparison

To further analyze the impacts of posting patterns, we divide the data sample into four groups according to their posting frequency on weekdays/weekends being higher or lower than the median values of the whole sample. The median value (instead of a mean value) of posting frequency is used in this study to divide the high-value groups from the low-value groups, and to eliminate possible dominating effects of extreme cases. Table 8 shows the descriptive statistics of weekday and weekend postings. As shown in Fig. 2, the four groups identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are labeled as heavy-balanced users (average posting frequency above the median on both weekdays and weekends), weekday users (average posting frequency above the median on weekdays, but below the median on weekends), weekend users (average posting frequency above the median on weekends, but below the median on weekdays), and light-balanced users (average posting frequency below the median on both weekdays and weekends). Fig. 2 shows the sample distribution among the four groups. Although the samples in the weekday and weekend user groups account for a small proportion compared to the balanced user groups, the sample size is more than suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to support our conclusion. It is well documented in the statistics literature ([50,56]) that for symmetric and unimodal distributions, the central limit theorem (CLT) holds within a sample size as low as 4. In fact, even for integer-valued multimodal distributions, the CLT holds within a sample size as low as 40. Because the metrics we used are real valued, within each group, the distribution of each metric is symmetric and unimodal. Therefore, the sample sizes of our four groups are well above the minimum suf<sup>fi</sup>cient size for the CLT to hold in practice.

Future research may consider the attributes of users in each group and collect more data to further validate the differences and discover the causes behind the variation.

## 6. Conclusion

It has been commonly accepted that technology addiction is associated with intense use and the grati<sup>fi</sup>cations gained through heavy use, despite the lack of empirical evidence. In this paper, the relationships among microblog posting, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, and addiction dimensions are empirically explored. Using a mixed datacollection approach, our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that contrary to common understanding, user grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction are not related to overall posting frequency. Furthermore, from the perspective of use-time preference on weekdays or weekends, the user sample is analyzed with groupwise comparisons. Descriptive results show that heavy weekend users tend to be more socially grati<sup>fi</sup>ed and are most likely to be addicted. In contrast, heavy weekday users are usually not grati<sup>fi</sup>ed or addicted. Meanwhile, balanced users who do not show distinguishable difference between weekdays and weekends are less addicted than weekend users, even if their overall use is frequent. Moreover, the possible reasons for the groups’ differences in grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction are discussed using text analysis.

This study offers several contributions to related literature and practice. First, the study contributes to the U&G theory as an important supplement by empirically analyzing the relationships between posting patterns and various grati<sup>fi</sup>cation types. Previous studies on the U&G theory have suggested that use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations are positively correlated ([54,4]; whereas passive behavior (reading, viewing) has been well analyzed, active behavior (posting) was not yet studied. More importantly, previous studies have not examined the relationships between the patterns of use behaviors and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. In this regard, our research offers more in-depth insights on the links between posting pattern and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation, which could allow platform providers to improve their services according to the use

time patterns of the users. Second, this paper contributes to the technology addiction research by investigating the effects of variation in microblog posting time, going beyond the simple and somewhat ambiguous concept of usage frequency that has commonly been used as an aggregate. Our research shows that heavy weekend users are the most likely to be addicted, whereas balanced users are less addicted even if their overall use is frequent. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, future research may further explore a more consolidated model for explaining the levels of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings may offer practical insights to help facilitate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of social media use and reduce the risk of addiction. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms and schools may be advised to encourage the weekday use of microblogging as a knowledge sharing and communication channel but at the same time, intentionally discourage heavy weekend use to lower the possibility of addiction. Third, the validity of this study is increased by the use of actual usage data as opposed to questionnaires, which rely on self-reported typologies and thus potentially generate questionable results [17]. Last but not least, this paper applies weekday/weekend time preference instead of frequency as a new angle for academic research on social media use. Although previous studies have widely discussed the weekday and weekend differences with regard to traditional media, little academic effort has adopted this lens to investigate social media, despite industry awareness of the notably signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences of social media use between weekdays and weekends [22,23]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this paper show the effects of weekday and weekend usage patterns on addiction, which has not been previously discovered in the literature, providing new directions for future research.

One main limitation of this study is that the posting frequency cannot fully describe the use frequency, particularly for those who seldom post although they often use microblogging. It may be challenged whether or not the posting behavior alone is the most suitable indicator for the usage construct. In fact, some people may spend much time in browsing other's microblogs rather than posting. The amounts of browsing are not considered in this paper, largely because the browsing record data cannot be captured online. Alternative, future research may incorporate self-reported use amounts to cross-validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Meanwhile, confounding factors such as user content preferences are not controlled in the current study. This also deserves further investigations in the future.

Another limitation of the research is that the sample size is relatively small, which is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to statistically test the groupwise comparison. Potential future directions include extending our research efforts to analyze other types of usage activities, such as viewing and readership. Studies could also be carried out in the contexts of other popular social networks applications to <sup>fi</sup>nd out whether the use time patterns and research conclusions still hold. Although this study explores the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of posting time preference on weekday/weekend, daytime/night is another important time varying measurement. With a large sample size in possible future studies, data groups could be comparatively analyzed according to daytime/night in addition to weekday weekend, to increase the paper’s accuracy and scope.

Lastly, this paper treats grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction as the positive and negative outcomes of usage separately. Based on the extant literature on technology addiction and U&G theory, grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction may be seen as interdependent on each other. On the one hand, it is intuitively plausible that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation may lead to addiction. On the other hand, it is also reasonable to some extent that addiction may be considered as an antecedent of grati<sup>fi</sup>cation. As our current paper is focused on different impacts of weekday/weekend usage patterns, we have not expanded our theoretical analysis to the relationships between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction, which may be challenging particularly considering the multidimensional nature of both grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction. In this regard, the causal links between grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and addiction may also provide a promising direction for future research.

Her research interests include the technology addiction and social commerce. Her work was presented in prestigious Information Systems conferences such as ICIS, PACIS and HICSS. She has reviewed for numerous IS journals and conferences.

Xunhua Guo received his Ph.D. degree from Tsinghua University and is currently Associate Professor of Information Systems at the School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University. His research interests include electronic commerce, social networks, and business intelligence. He has published in journals including Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, INFORMS Journal on Computing, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, Communications of the ACM, Information Sciences, Decision Support Systems, and Computers in Human Behavior. He serves as Executive Board Member and Vice General-Secretary of China Association for Information Systems (CNAIS). He is on the editorial boards of several journals including Electronic Commerce Research and Applications, Journal of Global Information Management, and Electronic Commerce Research.

Xue Bai is Associate Professor of Management Information Systems in the School of Business of University of Connecticut. She received her Ph.D. degree in Management Information Systems from Carnegie Mellon University. Her research applies quantitative methodologies such as data mining, business analytics, and mathematical modeling to problems in enterprise information systems, online social media, and online platforms. Her research has been published in Management Science, Information Systems Research, INFORMS Journal on Computing, ACM Transactions on Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, among others.
