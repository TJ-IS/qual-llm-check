## Abstract

Prior research on digital ecosystems focuses on the focal firm (e.g., a platform owner) and its ecosystem governance. However, there is a dearth of literature examining the non-focal actor, that is, an ecosystem participant who is at the periphery of a digital ecosystem. This paper proposes a theoretical perspective of the non-focal firm's participation across digital ecosystems for cultivating its innovation habitat through capability search and redeem. Capability search involves the location of external capability deemed valuable for extending the firm's innovation habitat. Capability redeem refers to the firm's use of external capability to develop, distribute, and/or monetize its products and services. We generate and sensitize the proposed perspective in the context of Sony Ericsson's innovation habitat by interpreting the mobile device manufacturer's participation across four digital ecosystems (Visual Basic, Java, Digital Music, and Android). Our findings suggest that the non-focal actor cannot rely on a single ecosystem for addressing all relevant layers of innovation. It benefits from pursuing a pluralistic strategy, operating across digital ecosystems to avoid investing all efforts in the same ecosystem. The model of ecosystem capability search and redeem, which is a result of ideographic research explanation, extends current perspectives on digital ecosystems and contributes to the emerging literature in the digital age.

## Introduction

Digital technology is transforming the business landscape (Evans et al., 2006; Zammuto et al., 2007; Tiwana et al., 2010). As observed by Yoo et al. (2010), inherent properties of digital technology challenge traditional innovation logics, as they separate previously tightly integrated layers of innovation: (a) services from computing devices and (b) contents from its medium. First, the separation of services from computing devices is related to the programmability of digital technology, where the semiotic functional logic of the device operates (almost) independently from the physical embodiment that executes it (Langlois, 2002; Yoo et al., 2010). As an example, programmability allows smartphones to serve as game, navigation aid, heart monitor, remote control, and many other functions, not anticipated by device designers, emerging from manipulation of data through functional logic. Second, the separation of contents from its medium is related to the homogenization of data, where digital technology allows for distribution of audio, video, text, and images over the same networks (Yoo et al., 2010). For instance, as music is digitalized, it is increasingly separated from traditional distribution media such as vinyl records and CDs (Tilson et al., 2013). Because digital data can easily be recombined, heterogeneous data types can co-exist on the same network or medium. Overall, this ongoing separation of layers of innovation hampers the possibility to control the digital innovation process by building and relying on core competence and resources related to a single strategic layer.

It is therefore not surprising that many product-developing firms increasingly abandon singular, firm-centric models of innovation for distributed alternatives (Howells and Malik, 2003; Sawhney et al., 2003; Brusoni, 2005; Boudreau and Lakhani, 2009). This shift towards distributed models of innovation typically involves participation $^{1}$ in digital ecosystems, focusing on service and application development, content production, or device development. Participation in ecosystems offers a way to tap into knowledge flows across organizational boundaries (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Chesbrough et al., 2006; Simard and West, 2006; Basole, 2009; Jonsson et al., 2009; El Sawy et al., 2010; Dougherty and Dunne, 2011), oftentimes even outside the traditional industry boundary (Andersson et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2010). As Moore noted already some 18 years ago, it makes sense to ‘suggest that a company be viewed not as a member of a single industry but as part of a business ecosystem that crosses a variety of industries’ (1993: 76).

Anecdotal evidence suggests that participation in ecosystems is no longer an option but a necessity for the individual firm. Even a large cell phone manufacturer, such as Nokia, needs to cultivate its innovation habitat, carefully identifying different ecosystems that can feed its devices with innovations at the service and content layers. For instance, consider that Nokia's stock price plunged in early 2011 as the global firm showed indecisiveness about which operating system software should power their devices. Ultimately, this decision entailed a commitment to a digital ecosystem (e.g., Android, Windows, or Symbian) generating applications and content with which Nokia's internal innovation would co-evolve in the coming years (cf. Moore, 1993). Indeed, over time, product-developing firms confront many such decisions, and need to navigate between ecosystems to identify pockets of external resources to enhance their products and services.

Looking closer at the literature on this emerging mode of innovation, there is a tendency to focus on the governance of ecosystems (West, 2003; Shah, 2006; West and O'Mahony, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). As a result, the literature largely centers on focal actors such as Apple in the case of iOS, or Google in the case of Android. In addition, similar to Moore's (1993) seminal work, the analysis is confined within the boundaries of a single ecosystem. This is unfortunate since, in the digital age, most firms need to relate to a web of multiple ecosystems, resourcing its innovation habitat across different innovation layers, such as devices, networks, contents, and services (cf. Yoo et al., 2010). A more comprehensive understanding of the non-focal actor's ecosystem participation is therefore vital, not least for those managers who confront the complexity of navigating through this new innovation landscape as representatives of non-focal firms. While the non-focal actor outnumbers the focal actor by far, most, if not all, extant research is targeting the focal one. For the purposes of this paper, we define a non-focal actor as an ecosystem participant who is at the periphery of the digital ecosystem. This definition implies two aspects. First, the digital ecosystem's survival is not dependent on the non-focal actor's participation, although the actor may represent an important actor type for the ecosystem. Second, the non-focal actor's survival is typically not dependent on the specific ecosystem, although the ecosystem may represent an important ecosystem type for the actor. In addition, it should be noted that a specific firm can be focal and non-focal at the same time (although not vis-à-vis the same ecosystem). $^{2}$ For instance, Google is a focal firm of the Android ecosystem. Still, it serves as a non-focal actor when it participates as third-party developer (e.g., Google Voice) in Apple's iOS ecosystem.

Addressing this gap in the literature, we propose a theoretical perspective with which to understand the non-focal firm's innovation habitat and its search and redeem of external capability across digital ecosystems. We view a firm's habitat as 'a suite of resources and environmental conditions' (Weddell, 2002). The habitat defines a firm's scope of innovation in a given moment. The firm's habitat hosts the capabilities for developing, distributing, or monetizing its products or services. For a focal firm, such as Facebook in the case of its social media service, there is significant overlap between the ecosystem and its innovation habitat. However, for the non-focal firm, the distinction between habitat and ecosystem demarcates its own innovation capabilities from the capabilities of the ecosystems in which it participates. Ecosystem participation typically stretches the non-focal firm's habitat, since it may allocate new resources and capabilities necessary for managing technological change. In this regard, firms nurture its habitat by identifying and cultivating pockets of external resources in a web of multiple digital ecosystems (cf. Yoo et al., 2010).

This research has been guided by the following question: How can we understand a non-focal firm's participation across digital ecosystems? Addressing this research question, we extend current views on digital ecosystems by developing a new perspective that zooms out from the platform owner and zooms in on the non-focal actor's ecosystem participation. At the heart of this perspective, the next section presents a model of the non-focal firm's participation across digital ecosystems for cultivating its innovation habitat through capability search and redeem. After describing our methodology in the subsequent section, we sensitize the model in the context of Sony Ericsson's cultivation of its innovation habitat by illustrating the mobile device manufacturer's participation in four different digital ecosystems (Visual Basic, Java, Digital Music, and Android). We conclude the paper by discussing our study as a result of ideographic research explanation that contributes to the literatures on digital ecosystems and innovation in the digital age.

## Theoretical background

Manifesting a variety of theoretical and disciplinary outlooks, prior literature related to distributed innovation takes on slightly different conceptual foci by understanding its underlying structure as networks (Chen, 2004; Simard and West, 2006; Andersson et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2008), communities (Lynn et al., 1996; Lee and Cole, 2003), meta-organizations (Gulati et al., 2012), or bazaars (Demil and Lecoq, 2006). In this paper, we follow the recent trend to adopt an ecosystem metaphor for examining such innovation (El Sawy et al., 2010; Selander et al., 2010; Tiwana et al., 2010). Typically, the notion of ecosystem provides a basis for understanding how capabilities and roles co-evolve and align over time in an innovation or business setting (Moore, 1993).

For the purposes of this paper, we define digital ecosystems as a collective of firms that is inter-linked by a common interest in the prosperity of a digital technology for materializing their own product or service innovation. Essentially, this collective contributes to the technology's survival by (1) reinforcing networks effects (Katz and Shapiro, 1994) to grow its installed base and (2) by aligning disparate sources of intellectual capacity to improve the technology (Yoo et al., 2010). This co-evolution between the technology and ecosystem participants creates self-reinforcing feedback loops that can affect the ecosystem either positively or negatively, making members both collaborators and competitors (Moore, 1993; Walley, 2007).

Prior research on digital ecosystems primarily focuses on focal actors and their ecosystem governance (West, 2003; Shah, 2006; West and O'Mahony, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). For instance, Eaton et al. (2011) examine the decision-making of platform owners, such as Apple and Google, and its role in controlling and cultivating service innovation in digital ecosystems; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson (2013) observe the importance of boundary resources for governing platform ecosystems; West and O'Mahony (2008) develop the notion of participation architecture to describe how sponsors of open source ecosystems can develop 'the opportunity structure extended to potential external contributors' (p. 145). This focus on the focal firm and its ecosystem governance is also manifested in recent research commentaries, where Tiwana et al. (2010), as an example, propose a research program on 'how platform governance influences the evolutionary dynamics of ecosystems' (p. 679).

This one-sided emphasis in the literature is unfortunate. As innovation turns increasingly distributed, an increasing number of ecosystem participants can be characterized as non-focal actors, driven by an entire range of different motives (Shah, 2006). Non-focal actors do not engage in ecosystems to replace established innovation practices, but they seek to extend their existing capabilities by relating to external knowledge and capabilities (Tilson et al., 2010; Tiwana et al., 2010; Rosemann et al., 2011). As a result, they are not bounded to a particular ecosystem. Rather, they draw on the relatively low fixed costs to participate in digital innovation (Yoo et al., 2010; Svahn, 2012) and make continuous reassessments of their engagement and adjust the locus of innovation as they join and leave ecosystems. Non-focal actors recognize that ecosystems follow a somewhat different logic, where inbound and outbound innovation has to be carefully balanced (Chesbrough et al., 2006). Yet, they do not radically transform the basic premises of their innovation processes, since their innovation habitat cannot be reduced to the ecosystem itself. This calls for a new perspective on digital ecosystems and innovation.

## Towards a new ecosystems perspective

We propose a new perspective on ecosystem capability search and redeem. Figure 1 depicts its core constructs and their relationships. The figure is drawn from the viewpoint of a single non-focal actor and its capability search and redeem across digital ecosystems in which it will not assume a focal role in a foreseeable future. As stated earlier, we define a non-focal actor as an ecosystem participant who is at the periphery of the digital ecosystem. Thus, a particular ecosystem's survival is not dependent on the actor's participation, although the actor may represent an important actor type for the ecosystem. For instance, Nokia can be considered a non-focal actor in the Windows Phone ecosystem (Nokia released their first Windows-based phone in October 2011), although this ecosystem would not survive without having any participants on the device layer. Further, the actor's survival is not dependent on the ecosystem. Continuing the Nokia-Windows example, Nokia is not dependent on the Windows Phone ecosystem, although the company would be unlikely to stay competitive without participating in any ecosystem related to contents and services.

We use the notion of innovation habitat to denote the collection of resources and environmental conditions that defines a firm's scope of innovation in a given moment (cf. Weddell, 2002). Reflecting the pervasive integration of digital technology in product and service innovation (Svahn and Henfridsson, 2012), the non-focal firm increasingly attempts to extend its innovation habitat through capability search across digital ecosystems. The ultimate objective of extending the innovation habitat is to increase the likelihood of successful innovation outcome in the form of new products and/or services. We refer to capability search as a firm's activity of locating external capability deemed valuable for extending its innovation habitat. Such search is exploratory since it involves proactive discovery of resources in the hope of generating new possibilities (March, 1991). As indicated in Figure 1, capability search is dynamic rather than linear; it involves iterations where results of the initial search serve as input to new searches. If successful, capability search results in the location of specific resources that eventually may be accessed as factors of production on a semi-permanent basis (Helfat and Peteraf, 2003). We use the notion of pockets of external resources for capturing the subsets of ecosystem resources that allow a non-focal actor to develop new innovation capability. Examples of such resources include specific competences, technologies, and distribution models. However, not every non-focal firm possesses the capability to turn a set of ecosystem resources into a capability that enriches its innovation habitat. As always, the creation of new opportunities is dependent on the capability to deinstitutionalize some of the existing practices in the habitat (Hiatt et al., 2009).

Correspondingly, a non-focal firm not only needs to master capability search but also capability redeem. We refer to capability redeem as a firm's activity of cultivating its innovation habitat with external capability for developing, distributing, and/or monetizing its products and services. Essentially a dynamic process that involves iterations (see Figure 1), such redeem involves exploitation where resources are refined (March, 1991) and used for resource recombination in innovation (Galunic and Rodan, 1998). First, capability redeem may enhance the non-focal firm's capability to develop and niche its product's functionality and meaning (cf. Verganti, 2009) by giving access to, for example, services and/or contents that otherwise would not be accessible within the scope of a firm's habitat. In addition, it may involve new capabilities to distribute the firm's services and/or contents to actors who otherwise would not be accessible within the scope of a firm's habitat. Finally, it may develop the firm's capability to monetize the firm's offer in a way that otherwise would not be possible.

Relying on external resources across ecosystems is a strategy to leverage inbound firm innovation by extending its scope of innovation (West and Gallagher, 2006; Yoo et al., 2010). So, contrary to the focal actors' view echoed in the extant literature, we argue that the non-focal actor actively search for pockets of ecosystem resources. In doing this, it refines, or sometimes even redefines, its innovation habitat. In such habitat refinement, trade-offs, compromises, and constraints (Purser et al., 1995) inevitably evolve as control is distributed dynamically in the attempt to absorb new and unknown knowledge to spur the non-focal firm's innovation (Moore, 1993; Iansiti and Levien, 2004; Adner, 2006). However, the non-focal actor does not commit to a specific ecosystem, but relates to multiple ecosystems in the form of arm's-length, contractually oriented, relationships (cf. Boudreau and Lakhani, 2009) to enable its innovation in a pick-and-choose fashion. Ultimately, this means that a failure to redeem anticipated capabilities from an ecosystem may trigger the firm to leave the ecosystem (Nickerson and zur Muehlen, 2006).

In sum, while established literature provides a detailed view of the focal actor's governance of digital ecosystems (West, 2003; Shah, 2006; West and O'Mahony, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013), it provides little evidence about the process by which non-focal firms cultivate their innovation habitat across digital ecosystems. Addressing this gap, we propose the theoretical perspective outlined above. In what follows, we sensitize this perspective in the context of a case study of Sony Ericsson's capability search and redeem across digital ecosystems over an 8-year period.

This research is designed as a historical case study (Kieser, 1994; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001) in which we gathered data on Sony Ericsson's ecosystem participation between 2001 and 2009. While the use of historical data means that the researcher does not plunge herself into a situated, real-time context, a prime motivation for relying on such inquiry is the large volumes of data that would be impossible using primary data collection techniques alone (Romano et al., 2003). ## Case selection

There were a number of reasons why we selected Sony Ericsson as a case for studying capability search and redeem across digital ecosystems. First of all, during the study period, the firm participated in a multitude of ecosystems in which it did not play any significant role for the prosperity of the technology in focus. Our case story covers four of these ecosystems: Visual Basic, Java, Digital Music, and Android. Second, Sony Ericsson participated in these ecosystems to seek ways to resource their device development across layers of innovation (cf. Yoo et al., 2010) rather than attempting to take on a more focal role of governing the ecosystems. Third, although backed up by multinational enterprises such as Ericsson and Sony, Sony Ericsson has played a relatively minor role on the global market. Over the years, its market share has never exceeded 9% (2007).

We argue that the Sony Ericsson case provides ample basis for analyzing non-focal actors' capability search and redeem process across digital ecosystems. However, it should be noted that being a non-focal actor is not necessarily a permanent state. A firm might change from being in a non-focal position to taking a focal role (and vice versa). In the Sony Ericsson case, we did not see this full transition from non-focal to focal. However, as Sony Ericsson successfully introduced the T610 handset, for instance, it certainly committed the company more to the Java ecosystem without necessarily becoming a focal actor.

## Data collection and analysis

The data collection and analysis of our historical case study followed a hermeneutic process (Klein and Myers, 1999). This means that our theory generation was not linear from data to conceptual model. It emerged through multiple iterations between the emergent theoretical understanding and the data on which it was based. The processes of data collection and analysis were therefore intertwined. However, we separate them analytically below for allowing greater transparency of the research.

## Data collection

We collected a considerable amount of secondary data from three main sources: trade press articles, press releases, and Sony Ericsson's annual reports. First, we collected trade press articles for tracing historical events and providing hints on which events to study more carefully. One useful aspect of this data source is that the sheer volume of data points covering the same event promises multiple interpretations and viewpoints. We used the ProQuest database for eliciting the bulk of the trade press articles. We focused our searches to an 8-year time period (2001–2009), starting the year before the joint venture between Ericsson and Sony. The initial search terms were: Sony Ericsson, Platform, and Community. As we learned more about the case, we added new terms related to events that were deemed relevant. For instance, when we realized how important Sony Ericsson's first Walkman phone, W800i, was, we started to use the model name as a search term. All in all, these searches generated more than 1400 articles from sources such as Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, and Wireless News covering major events and shifts in Sony Ericsson's innovation history.

We also collected Sony Ericsson press releases for the same time period to be included in our case database. Appreciating that press releases are carefully orchestrated to make the firm look good, our objective of using this data source was to trace official product release dates, decisions taken, and the official rationale for decisions taken. All press releases were scanned for content that related to platforms, partnerships, or new product families. In addition to trade press articles and press releases, we lastly collected Sony Ericsson annual reports to gather background material on market shares, sales, and product families. These figures were used for cross-checking against the trade press articles and press releases to ensure data quality.

In addition to these three main data sources, we conducted interviews with two informants from Sony Ericsson to verify key events and our case narratives with people profoundly involved in the process. One of the informants was a co-founder of Sony Ericsson's developer program and application shop (which was initiated in 2003 as Sony Ericsson decided to engage with the Visual Basic ecosystem: see Case Narrative 1), while the other informant had more than 5 years of experience of the developer program. The interviews helped us refine the case timeline and enrich the case narratives with first-hand accounts. As suggested by Hargadon and Douglas (2001), the input from the key informant was not only a useful way to validate our case data but also to sensitize why particular initiatives were taken at a given point in time.

## Data analysis

Reflecting the logic of the hermeneutic circle, the data analysis consisted of iteration between two analytical foci: 'the interdependent meaning of parts and the whole they form' (Klein and Myers, 1999: 72).

Attending to the analytical focus on parts, our data analysis consisted of the following activities. We first reviewed each of the collected trade press articles and press releases to determine whether they provided information related to our ambition to understand Sony Ericsson's ecosystem participation. This screening reduced the material to 60 articles from 30 publication outlets (see the Appendix), and hundreds of press releases down to 61. A key basis for this data reduction was the ambition to focus our analysis on sub-cases with enough data for allowing multiple interpretations of the same events. Using the qualitative analysis software Atlas.ti, we adopted an open coding procedure to discover concepts and their properties and dimensions (Strauss and Corbin, 1998; Charmaz, 2006). The first author of the paper conducted the coding. This procedure yielded an initial set of more than 250 descriptive concepts. To reduce overlap, we then collaboratively reviewed and compared all concepts. In this process, we discovered redundant concepts (e.g., 'third-party developers', 'external developers', 'application developers'), misspelled concepts, and concepts falling outside the initial scope of understanding Sony Ericsson's ecosystem participation (e.g., Sony Ericsson's partnering/sponsoring of the WTA tour). This eventually resulted in a list of 193 mutually exclusive concepts. A typical example of our coding included both abstract codes and codes that were directly reflected in the data, for example:

The lure of Java [code: Java], say proponents, is its flexibility and its widespread support among major handset manufacturers [codes: positive connotations Java; installed base Java] including Nokia, Sony Ericsson, Motorola, Siemens, Samsung and NEC. Developers [codes: developers; Java developers], so the theory goes, can write an application once and distribute it across a variety of different handsets [codes: inherent capability Java; installed base Java]. (The reality is actually more complicated, though developers have reported that the differences from device to device can usually be fixed through small tweaks.) [code: negative connotation Java]

Attending to the analytical focus on the whole that the parts form (cf. Klein and Myers, 1999), we also approached the research problem as a theoretical challenge, generating early conceptual ideas that would make sense of the initial data collection. Stimulated by ideas related to exploration and exploitation (March, 1991), we generated an idea of capability search and redeem early on, which subsequently matured by using it for examining, categorizing, and clustering the descriptive concepts. This process helped us to initially identify five case narratives. Concurring with Pentland's (1999) work on narratives, we created initial case narratives, and a timeline for each of the five episodes by tracing the episodes' fabula and underlying mechanisms. After critical discussions and analysis of data, we soon decided to reduce the narratives to four. We dropped a narrative related to the creation of a common Symbian developer tool together with Nokia, as it did not enable additional insights to the understanding of the whole beyond the initial four ones.

## The Sony Ericsson case

Sony Ericsson's history on the mobile device market started with the merger of Ericsson and Sony's mobile device businesses in 2001. Ericsson had suffered from a downward spiral and needed a new take to regain its former position on the market. While Sony was a giant in consumer electronics, it was a small player in the cell phone market. With a market share of approximately 1% Sony had to grow its business to secure a sustainable presence on the market. For Ericsson, it was Sony's vast experience in consumer electronics and entertainment that made the merger worthwhile. In a press release in April 2001, Sony Ericsson's CEO, Kurt Hellström, argued:

By combining the complementary strengths of Ericsson and Sony, the new company is uniquely positioned to become a world leader in telecommunications, as the industry moves rapidly toward Mobile Internet.

Clearly, Sony and Ericsson saw an opportunity to convert synergy effects into competitive advantage. In August 2001, the two companies finalized the merger with the ambition to become market leader in multimedia handsets. Telecom analysts were cautiously optimistic about the new company's capability to challenge the market leader Nokia. While the new company was founded on expectations to leverage complementary strengths and synergy effects in internal innovation, the merger also marked the initiation of a period where Sony Ericsson attempted to extend its existing innovation habitat through capability search and redeem across ecosystems. In what follows, we describe four narratives, where Sony Ericsson cultivated external capabilities from different ecosystems. The four ecosystems were centered on Visual Basic, Java, Digital Music, and Android (see Table 1 for an overview of case narratives).

## Narrative 1: Visual Basic (2002–2003)

## Innovation habitat

In 2002, the newly formed Sony Ericsson struggled to set up a prospering innovation habitat where the combined strengths of its parent companies would do justice to upcoming product portfolios. Being a market leader in telecom infrastructure, Ericsson injected critical knowledge on low-level radio design and other technical domains. Sony, on the other hand, was well rooted in consumer electronics and offered the new company essential end-user competence from an industry that seemed increasingly relevant for cell phones. Although making up a strong mix of innovation resources and capabilities, Sony Ericsson would not be able to leverage the combined strengths by enforcing an entirely new, integrated innovation process. As noted by Anil Raj in February 2002, corporate executive vice president at Sony Ericsson, the new Symbian operating system (OS) came to be a critical component of the company's common innovation habitat: 'Symbian v.7 OS will be instrumental for Sony Ericsson when launching our competitive mobile multimedia products'. The new software platform would allow for separation of concerns. With innovation revolving around Symbian, creative processes could co-exist at different, relatively independent layers. The legacy of Ericsson could be exercised side by side with consumer electronics skills from Sony.

## Capability search for external resources

As they struggled with the best way to capitalize on its internal resources and capabilities, Sony Ericsson could see that the transition towards mobile Internet and multimedia slowly reshaped the cell phone market. In the late 1990s, Palm had introduced the personal digital assistant. This new device was rapidly considered a powerful technology for recording, tracking, and managing business-related applications such as mail and text documents. Interestingly, Palm's dominant position could not just be explained by internal R&D efforts. Instead, they had consciously engaged external actors in application development. The connection between Palm and its growing community of developers relied on a public application programming interface (API). By continuously maintaining and extending this API, Palm seeded the community with new features, eventually developing into concrete end-user value in the hands of developers. Like many others, Sony Ericsson was impressed by Palm. The co-founder of Sony Ericsson's developer program commented:

We were inspired by Palm. On the handset side, they had successfully opened up their products. They had like 15,000 applications in the late 90's, so they were considered a role model. Everybody knew that they had a technically weak platform but they were still able to capitalize on it.

Palm's strategy was similar to that found in the PC and video game segment. They offered an open operating system (Palm OS), combined with business development, marketing and bundling resources to their developers (Evans et al., 2006). So far they had done it in a surprisingly successful manner. In parallel, Research in Motion (RIM) had successfully introduced the Blackberry pager, in retrospect recognized as the first generation of smartphones. The success of this new device seemed to be linked to its focus on business contexts. Inspired by Palm and RIM, Sony Ericsson identified business users as the primary audience of their upcoming smartphones. This framing clearly involved resources and capabilities that were poorly developed in the established innovation habitat. Their upcoming smartphone strategy would require not only business relevance, but also functional variety and capability to adapt and extend applications over time. It had to be malleable, not just in a technological sense but also from an innovation perspective. Following the example of Palm, Sony Ericsson started to search for an external innovation environment that could complement internal R&D activities to provide these capabilities and resources.

Basically, a business-oriented strategy would make the smartphone a front-end to different enterprise systems. As demonstrated by RIM, such phones made desktop work mobile. Searching for external resources and capabilities that could support such a transition, Sony Ericsson could not ignore Microsoft and its exceptional dominance in the PC desktop market. At this time, Microsoft had launched the programming language Visual Basic and a complementary integrated development environment (IDE) to support third-party development. The relatively easy Visual Basic was designed for rapid development of graphical user interfaces. It offered smooth access to core infrastructure, such as databases, through ActiveX and other data access objects.

Drawing on the wide diffusion of Visual Basic, the US-based software company AppForge had developed a novel solution for mobile application development. AppForge provided a plug-in for Microsoft's Visual Studio IDE, promoted as Crossfire. In combination with a run-time software engine, hosted by the smartphone, AppForge offered unprecedented simplicity in mobile application development. Supporting an increasing number of mobile platforms (Symbian, Windows Mobile, BlackBerry, and Palm OS), their solution also provided for smooth portability of application software. While searching for external resources and capabilities that supported business-oriented application development, the innovation ecosystem emerging around AppForge and Visual Basic became a primary candidate for Sony Ericsson.

## Capability redeem for innovation outcome

Increasingly convinced that Visual Basic was an important enabler for their business-oriented smartphone strategy, Sony Ericsson decided to engage with the ecosystem emerging around AppForge. As later disclosed in a press release, they established a formal partnership to redeem capability from this ecosystem and decided to ship their new product line with the Crossfire run-time software engine. In February 2003, Rikko Sakaguchi, Senior Vice President of Applications and Content at Sony Ericsson noted (in a press release):

We are very excited to introduce our new products to Visual Basic developers worldwide. By enhancing our products with AppForge's mobile application platform, Microsoft developers can write applications directly to the Symbian-based P800 product line without having to use additional technologies.

While Sony Ericsson could see that a prospering ecosystem would be necessary, it was not sufficient. In order to effectively redeem value from external developers they needed a distribution channel through which end-users would be able to purchase smartphone applications. As a response, they decided to set up an online shop to provide over-the-air download of applications.

Just before Christmas 2002, Sony Ericsson launched the P800 smartphone. It was the first model in a series of handsets specifically designed for an external ecosystem. It was equipped with AppForge's run-time software engine and integrated with the new application shop. Overall, the P800 was well received at the market. Extending a firm-centric innovation habitat with external resources and capabilities seemed to be a winning concept. A successful mix of Ericsson's technological strengths and Sony's consumer electronics experiences had provided a robust and attractive handset. In addition, the AppForge ecosystem had offered true malleability on the application side. Such malleability proved critical when struggling for attention in the business segment. During the first quarter (Q1) of 2003 Sony Ericsson shipped 190,000 devices. Although such a figure is insignificant by today's standards, it made Sony Ericsson the second biggest smartphone manufacturer this quarter.

Narrative 2: Java (2003–2004)

## Innovation habitat

In 2003 the telecom industry was focused on the global rollout of the third generation (3G) base station infrastructure. There were several competing solutions, but WCDMA - promoted by Ericsson - was the preferred choice for many of the world's dominant operators. 3G increased bandwidth significantly, but it also included quality of service and other network-based functions, expected to redefine the telecom innovation landscape. End-user functionality would now be enabled in a constant interplay between handsets, network infrastructure, and different service providers. Again, the strong connection to Ericsson seemed to play Sony Ericsson into the hands. Its established innovation habitat was well adapted to these new circumstances; Sony Ericsson had access to the details of 3G network technology, solid consumer electronics skills, and growing experience from distributed software development through its AppForge initiative.

## Capability search for external resources

The telecom industry expected that smarter handsets and 3G infrastructures would feed a wide range of multimedia applications. Remarkable growth of mobile games made an early sign of this. People increasingly played games waiting for the bus, walking around, or just sitting in a park. From a technological point of view, these mobile games were enabled by Java. More precisely, the boom could be traced to a rapid adoption of the Java micro edition (Java ME), and powered by the mobile information device profile (MIDP). MIDP provided a set of APIs defining how software applications interface with cellular phones. Since MIDP 1.0 had been well received by developers, MIDP 2.0 came with great expectations. The main reason was its game API. For Sony Ericsson, it was increasingly obvious that the community forming around Java ME would become a great resource with implications for their smartphone strategy. Together, these independent developers demonstrated outstanding open-ended innovation capability, well beyond any incumbent handset manufacturer. The Java ME environment also offered a unique capability to port applications from one platform to another. This interoperability accelerated innovation as ideas, code, and applications could flow between different settings. Open-ended external innovation capability and interoperability were increasingly seen as missing elements in Sony Ericsson's smartphone strategy.

In contrast to the Visual Basic ecosystem revolving around AppForge, Java development was more loosely coupled. Seeking to locate and, eventually, leverage the resources and capabilities of this ecosystem, Sony Ericsson announced strategic partnerships with Sun Microsystems and Borland Software Corporation. Borland was a dominant supplier of integrated development environments and their products offered a well-established channel for the distribution of a software development kit (SDK) adapted to Sony Ericsson handsets. While it was important to seed the developers in the ecosystem to get valuable returns, the operators could not be left behind. Without influence, these powerful stakeholders would oppose ecosystem-based development of games and other applications. As the Financial Times described in October 2003, Sun Microsystems offered a range of products that enabled mobile operators to manage software and content:

Sun Microsystems has introduced a range of products based on the Java operating system. [...] The company says its content delivery server and device test suite make it easier for operators to deliver and bill for mobile services, such as pictures and polyphonic ring tones, over the air.

## Capability redeem for innovation outcome

Convinced that the growing ecosystem around Java ME would make an essential complement to internal R&D, Sony Ericsson initiated several parallel activities to redeem external resources. First, the firm designed and published the SDK, announced in June 2003 when partnering with Borland:

Under the terms of the agreement, Borland plans to distribute and support the Sony Ericsson Java Software Development Kit (SDK) through the Borland® JBuilder 9 Mobile Edition, accelerating the application development lifecycle for wireless Java™ based devices.

Further, they invested in an initiative labeled Sony Ericsson Developer World. This initiative was an attempt to create a loyal sub-community within the emerging Java ecosystem. By offering detailed knowledge on rollout plans, pre-views of new hardware, and attractive sale channels, Sony Ericsson expected that developers would pay particular attention to their handsets in return.

However, the most important redeem activity was proper Java support for Java in the Symbian-based handsets. Initially, Sony Ericsson engineers designed a solution that primarily resonated with the handset's embedded functionality. As a result, however, the compatibility benefits were partly lost. When pre-released to operators 2003, the new T610 was highly criticized for not supporting de facto standards of Java. In fact, the device could only run about 25% of the Java applications existing at the time. Sony Ericsson therefore soon realized that they needed to better comply with the dominant Java standard. The criticism triggered Sony Ericsson's top management to make drastic decisions on further developing the Java support of the T610 model. The co-founder of Sony Ericsson's developer program commented:

We had the T610 and a poorly implemented Java platform. When the phone reached the operators, who wanted to sell a 'game' phone, they more or less told us that they would withdraw the phone if we didn't improve things. That was in 2003. The top management got involved, pushed in a lot of resources, and initiated a task force. Then, in about a month we improved it substantially and increased performance by a factor 3. [...] We suddenly had the best Java phone on the market. It was a do or die situation.

With the official introduction of the T610 model, Sony Ericsson had turned a serious weakness into strength.

Drawing on its existing innovation habitat the handset manufacturer had adapted the handsets in record time to meet expectations from third-party developers. As witnessed by the co-founder of the developer program this was a critical moment in Sony Ericsson's transition towards an ecosystem strategy for innovation:

With Java we [now] had access to millions of users. [...] This is totally different from Symbian. Programming in Java ME is much like the Java-environment that you have when you work with enterprise solutions on a PC, it's the same programming model. And then we suddenly had approximately ten thousand developers that could sit down and work right away.

The door was now wide open for Sony Ericsson to capitalize on the remarkable momentum in Java-based application development. Customers clearly appreciated the new T610 mobile phone and its orientation towards mobile gaming. Powered by great supply of various games it was well received at the market. 2004 Sony Ericsson dramatically increased sales, which amounted to 42.5 million units.

## Narrative 3: Digital Music (2004–2006)

## Innovation habitat

Apple's successful introduction of the iPod changed the consumer electronics industry. Until this point, PC-based ripping and sharing dominated digital music. The iPod allowed people to bring their growing libraries of digital, mp3-encoded music into a mobile context. Further, Apple had introduced its own online store for music, iTunes, which gave them considerable influence over the growing digital music ecosystem. Frequent releases of new devices and an abundance of digital music pushed Apple's sales figures. When growth peaked 2004–2005, Apple doubled the number of sold iPods each quarter. Clearly, Apple's success challenged the telecom industry. As noted in the Wall Street Journal in April 2005, a strong response was expected from established handset manufacturers:

Despite Apple's domination of the digital music sector the market remains in its infancy. The balance of power could tip suddenly and dramatically, especially if Apple doesn't race to get its music technology into cellphones – an effort that's had some hiccups. Last year, manufacturers sold an estimated seven million MP3 players in the US, a figure dwarfed by the roughly 80 million cellphones sold in the country, according to the Consumer Electronics Association. Most of Apple's profits in the digital music market comes from iPod sales.

Sony Ericsson was determined to take on this challenge and saw an opportunity to benefit from its established innovation habitat. In fact, the mobile device developer followed Apple's success story from a special position. Mobile music was, more or less, invented by Sony, shipping enormous amounts of Walkman portable cassette players in the 1980s and 1990s. Although cassette players and mini discs were history, Sony Ericsson had access to a strong brand and significant experience of portable music technology. The last couple of years had also provided considerable capabilities to engage in and capitalize on open-ended, third-party development in ecosystems. With this innovation habitat as a basis, Sony Ericsson saw an opportunity to build and benefit from a mobile music strategy.

## Capability search for external resources

In 2004, cell phones capable of playing digital music had been around for some time, but they were typically hampered by high costs, limited memory, and large clunky design. Clearly, Sony Ericsson saw an opportunity in being a first-mover towards dedicated, tailored music phones. However, the firm's enthusiasm over this new niche also reflected a reconfiguration of the telecom market. Mobile operators faced a future where revenue streams were likely to shift from speech and data to services. Many of them had started to experiment with service portals to increase end-user value and preconfigured handsets to secure customer loyalty.

To be successful, a music phone had to resonate with the interest of operators. It would require attractive designs, but also a strategy for how to provide content and capitalize on it. To make this move, Sony Ericsson had to follow Apple's example and tap in on an existing ecosystem where users were not just passive consumers only, but also made an important source of contents. The Walkman brand lost most of its appeal in the transition to digital technology. One explanation relates to Sony's reluctance to support increasingly dominant file formats. Although its range of MiniDisc products showed excellent performance characteristics, they eventually failed by following a proprietary file format, which prevented exchange of contents. With this backdrop, Sony Ericsson could see that interoperability was a critical factor when seeking to redeem value from an ecosystem. Ultimately, it was an entry ticket enabling external capability to generate range, diversity, and turnover of digital music. However, to capitalize on content, not just handsets, it would also be necessary to create an instrument for control of transactions in the ecosystem. Somehow Sony Ericsson had to redeem external capability to store and distribute music.

In early 2004, Sony Ericsson decided to frame their upcoming multimedia handset as a music phone. As time went on they were increasingly confident that this strategy was right. The acting product manager recalled in a Rolling Stones interview that 'we knew that music was going to be one of the categories that we wanted to dominate'. Seeking to locate external capabilities to support the implementation of a music strategy Sony Ericsson focused on the only competitive alternative to Apple's QuickTime and iTunes: the RealPlayer suite, provided by RealNetworks. Shipped as an option to the Windows OS, this multimedia software framework was the dominant solution for PCs in 2004. In February the same year, Sony Ericsson initiated a formal partnership with RealNetworks. In particular, the RealPlayer framework would allow for distribution of encoded and standardized audio and video content to users across the world. A partnership with this aggressive actor seemed ideal for Sony Ericsson in their ambition to redeem external resources. RealNetworks could be a catalyzer, allowing them to combine internal resources, such as the Walkman brand and portable music technology skills, with external resources provided by a rapidly growing ecosystem of digital music.

## Capability redeem for innovation outcome

To reinforce a music-oriented user experience, Sony Ericsson gave priority to design issues such as memory and sound. For example, the mobile device manufacturer decided to ship the Walkman phones with a generous 512 Mb expansion card and the HPM-70 headphones, designed to combine hands-free capability with a superior music experience. The firm also introduced the 3.5 mm jack to give customers the opportunity to use headphones of their own preference. These device-centric actions improved music playback experience and would allow Sony Ericsson to redeem value from an abundance of primarily mp3-encoded digital music distributed across people's hard drives. In addition, the integration of RealNetworks' client platform made the phone an integral part of a growing commercial music community. RealNetworks' position in this community had been considerably reinforced with its takeover of the Rhapsody music service. This subscription-based streaming service was now offered under the Helix brand. As indicated in a press release from February 2004, Sony Ericsson would be able to redeem value from this community through state-of-the-art compression and distribution technology.

Sony Ericsson has licensed RealNetwork's technology and will incorporate the RealPlayer® including RealAudio® and RealVideo® as well as 3GPP, in multimedia capable Sony Ericsson phones. The two companies also announced that Sony Ericsson has joined the Helix™ Community. [...] RealNetwork' Helix mobile platform provides mobile operators, content providers and handset manufacturers the tools needed to create and launch mobile media delivery services.

Sony Ericsson released the first Walkman phone in August 2005 – W800i – and followed up with the swivel-designed W600 and W900i. As noted by the former president Miles Hunt in a Financial Times interview from 2006, the Walkman project was risky:

I guess 12 months ago we were a little bit uncertain about exactly what the reaction would be, particularly because we weren't sure that the consumer really thought of the mobile phone as a music device. There had been so much attention on the iPod that we wondered if we could credibly establish the phone as a portable music device.

When the handsets finally were launched all concerns regarding the Walkman brand were put to rest. The new product family became a success, mostly because customers appreciated its inherent focus on digital music. Bringing the renowned Walkman brand to Sony Ericsson was considered one of the greatest achievements during 2005, providing significant growth over the coming period. For instance, the company increased the number of shipped handsets with 28% in Q4 2005, as compared with the same quarter in 2004. Sony Ericsson was able to benefit from the Walkman series throughout 2006 and 2007. In fact, the firm had its most successful year in 2007, selling 103.4 million handsets in total.

Narrative 4: Android (2008–2010)

## Innovation habitat

In 2008, the telecom industry was marked by rapid change. In particular, the character of handsets transformed significantly with the introduction of Android and iOS. Sony Ericsson sales dropped as primarily Apple's iPhone threatened the company's position in the music phone segment. Over the last years, Sony Ericsson had cultivated a largely firm-centric innovation habitat with third-party development of applications and user-generated content, but now these new operating systems seemed to redefine the innovation landscape again. While recognizing the benefits of an open OS handset manufacturers also saw the downside. A non-proprietary OS threatened the control over core software functions and reduce their influence over externally developed applications and content.

In response to this development Nokia acquired Symbian Ltd, made the OS software available open source, and founded a new, non-profit organization called Symbian Foundation. Given its dependence on Symbian, it was a natural choice for Sony Ericsson to join Symbian Foundation in a counterstrike against Google and Apple. After all, its innovation habitat was deeply intertwined with this operating system and the last years had provided complementary capabilities to govern third-party application development as well as peer-production of contents.

However, Nokia's Symbian initiative triggered tensions in the entire industry. The members were all used to be in control of the relationship between OS software and device hardware, and therefore found it difficult to collaborate. The fact that Nokia had a dominant position in the organization further hampered cooperation. The co-founder of Sony Ericsson's developer program explained:

What started off as a collaborative initiative [Symbian] – soon became fragmented and politically infected. More or less all of us were simply sitting there making things hard for each other instead of helping each other out. [...] it was a very limited ecosystem.

## Capability search for external resources

Over time, it became increasingly obvious to Sony Ericsson that Symbian Foundation would not be able to create an innovation hub that could match the emerging initiatives from Google and Apple. Focused on competition rather than cooperation, the organization was incapable of acting as a powerful, joint force, injecting new energy in third-party development. It seemed like Symbian Foundation would slow Sony Ericsson down, rather than supporting the company in the struggle for attention from developers and users. Sony Ericsson would have to look for external resources and capabilities to build a new innovation model where different layers could be inherently disconnected from each other. To remain competitive, the company had to get away from a practice where hardware, OS, applications, and content were closely integrated.

With the intention to redeem such capability, Sony Ericsson decided to join forces with Google, rather than fighting them. In December 2008, the vice president Rikko Sakaguchi declared that the firm applied for membership in the open handset alliance (OHA), reducing its stakes in the Nokia-controlled Symbian Foundation:

Sony Ericsson is excited to announce its membership of the Open Handset Alliance and confirm its intention to develop a handset based on the Android platform. [...] Sony Ericsson is a strong supporter of open operating systems and we believe the Open Handset Alliance offers an exciting opportunity for a new and unique user experience only Sony Ericsson can deliver.

During 2009, Android had achieved what the Symbian foundation was unable to do. A range of different handset manufacturers cooperated within OHA to develop and maintain a free, open source mobile device platform, building on the Linux kernel. Unlike Symbian, the Android platform was open to any device manufacturer but controlled by none of them.

## Capability redeem for innovation outcome

Sony Ericsson was well rewarded for its search of external resources and capabilities through OHA. The Android OS architecture made an efficient template for how to reuse and recombine innovation capability from different layers and the Android ecosystem offered an abundance of relevant applications. Competitive advantage would no longer rely on cutting-edge competence from microelectronics to user experience, but rather on the capability to develop and control resources at one layer to appropriate value at other layers. This triggered Sony Ericsson to rethink and refocus its innovation locus, from functional design to platform enrichment. In designing handsets for Android, Sony Ericsson could pursue a proven third-party developer strategy that opened up for a vast collection of off-the-shelf applications and focused on user experience. Over the coming years, Sony Ericsson successfully developed several uniquely profiled handsets by cultivating its internal innovation habitat with external resources from OHA and the Android community.

A salient example of Sony Ericsson's newly redeemed capability to design innovative Android handsets was the Xperia X10 mini, launched in May 2010. With this product, Sony Ericsson successfully claimed a segment of touchscreen phones that no one had addressed before. However, squeezing Android into a handset equipped with a 2.5-inch screen required some new thinking. For example, Sony Ericsson introduced an original, but intuitive approach to zooming. Rather than a multi-touch gesture, the user pressed a single finger in a spot until it vibrated and, then, moved the finger up or down to zoom in or out. The Xperia X10 mini was awarded the prize as European Mobile Phone 2010–2011.

Using the brand name Xperia, Sony Ericsson released four Android-based handsets in 2010 and 12 in 2011. Owing to the relative success of the Xperia handsets, the company gained 11% market share (2011) in this segment. With 80% of sales now related to smartphones, this made an important leap towards sustainable profitability. Public attention as well as improved financial performance was noted in the 2010 annual report:

2010 was a turnaround year for Sony Ericsson. Our four consecutive quarters of profit reflect the success of our shift towards an Android-based smartphone portfolio. We will celebrate the 10th anniversary of the creation of Sony Ericsson in 2011, and as shown by the recently announced Xperia™ arc, we will continue to focus on delivering the most entertaining smartphones worldwide.

## Discussion

In this paper, we propose a perspective that highlights non-focal actors' participation in digital ecosystems. Such a perspective is relevant, since much of the prior literature is single-handedly focused on the focal actor, typically a platform owner, and its strategies for making other actors participate in their innovation process (West, 2003; Shah, 2006; West and O'Mahony, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Yet, it follows from our definition in the theoretical background that the opulence of a digital ecosystem depends on a collective of firms having a shared interest in its focal technology. A more comprehensive understanding of the non-focal actor's ecosystem participation is therefore vital, not least for those managers who confront the complexity of navigating through this new innovation landscape as representatives of firms that need, or choose, to take on a non-focal role.

Collecting historical data on Sony Ericsson's efforts to improve their position on the handset market through ecosystem participation, our research seeks to make a valid idiographic research explanation. As such, we distinguish generality from recurrent regularities, and look upon our findings as causal tendencies (Tsoukas, 1989). Causal tendencies act transfactually, that is, 'the event or events that they are the powers to instantiate may never actually be instantiated; the powers may remain unactualized, yet these powers remain in existence' (Fleetwood, 2009: 362–363). This means that the model of ecosystem capability search and redeem, which is the main contribution of this research, should be regarded as a model with external validity beyond the Sony Ericsson case even though its explanatory relevance is conditioned (cf. Tsoukas, 1989). We view its main concepts, capability search and capability redeem, as causal tendencies in operation in cases of ecosystem participation involving non-focal actors with contextual conditions similar to those of Sony Ericsson.

Each capturing an episode of capability search and redeem, the four case narratives presented earlier not only sensitize the model but also generate a number of insights. First, the model of capability search and redeem suggests that the non-focal actor cannot rely on a single ecosystem for addressing all relevant layers of innovation. In the Sony Ericsson case, we observed how a single ecosystem was typically incomplete for cultivating the firm's innovation habitat, largely confirming the argument that innovation increasingly takes place across different layers of digital technology (Yoo et al., 2010). Visual Basic allowed for customized extensions in Microsoft's Office programs through scripting. Over time this created an independent layer, providing powerful service innovation in a business setting. Sony Ericsson engagement with AppForge allowed them to address the service layer in a mobile context. Although representing a service layer, Visual Basic had little to offer Sony Ericsson when exploring the gaming market. To redeem value from the upcoming ecosystem on mobile gaming, they realized that adopting the Java technology revolving around Sun Microsystems was a powerful way of becoming part of open-ended game development. Further, the Walkman products marked Sony Ericsson's interest in taking advantage of yet another upcoming innovation layer. As music was digitalized and therefore separated from its medium (Yoo et al., 2010), it gave birth to a new layer of innovation to which the mobile device manufacturer wished to relate. Sony Ericsson did this by identifying a new pocket of external resources, which was redeemed by using RealNetworks software on the Walkman handsets. Finally, Sony Ericsson decision to adopt Android provided an entry key to several innovation layers. Android offered the opportunity to redeem value from a huge service ecosystem of application development, feeding unprecedented functional variety and exceptional demand (Boudreau, 2012). It also offered an informal body of de facto handset standardization, holding significant potential for commoditization and scale advantages (Tiwana et al., 2010).

Second, our model of capability search and redeem suggests that it is imperative that non-focal actors pursue a pluralistic strategy, operating across digital ecosystems and avoiding investing all efforts in the same ecosystem. This implies that the typical non-focal actor continuously reassess its involvement with ecosystems to address different layers of innovation. Unless making such reassessments, the non-focal actor risks becoming a witness of how an ecosystem, which it cannot control, loses its momentum and becomes the subject of a negative self-reinforcing feedback loop (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013). Symbian may well be characterized as an example of such a recession. Sony Ericsson remained loyal to Symbian, ultimately controlled by Nokia, since it promised to challenge Google's Android. As the new initiative lost its momentum, Sony Ericsson gained a lot from the parallel assessments conducted and eventually leading to pursuing an Android path. At the same time, $^{3}$ it should be emphasized that too much distribution of efforts across ecosystems may lead to fragmentation, where internal resources for each initiative would be too thinly sliced (cf. Lyytinen and Rose, 2003). Even though the non-focal actor may not be in a position where it can effectively develop architectural control points (Baldwin and Woodard, 2009), it benefits from a balanced take where it considers the switching costs and lock-in effects (Shapiro and Varian, 1999) for each ecosystem participation effort.

Our study extends prior research on ecosystem governance (West, 2003; Shah, 2006; West and O'Mahony, 2008; Tiwana et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013) by presenting a model of ecosystem capability search and redeem that specifies the relationships between a set of constructs relevant for understanding the non-focal actor. Consistent with a more elaborate understanding of ecosystems (cf. Moore, 1993), we introduce the notion of innovation habitat to give specific attention to the basis from which non-focal actors respond to the strategies of focal actors. While this distinction makes little sense in the case of the focal actor, the reciprocal relationship between the non-focal actor's innovation habitat and the multiple ecosystems in which it participates is essential. For instance, in research on third-party development (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013), significant attention is paid to platform owners' strategic moves to build its ecosystem. However, little attention has been paid to the rationale behind third-party developers' responses to these moves. We propose that our research offers a lens for understanding the non-focal actor by demarcating its innovation habitat, that is, the scope of innovation that a firm has earned through its collection of resources and environmental conditions (Weddell, 2002) as a starting-point for considering participation in digital ecosystems. Having said this, it should be noted talking about third-party developers as a homogenous group may be misleading. In some cases, it can be a small start-up company that is highly dependent on the ecosystem (and therefore not fully compliant with our definition of non-focal actor), while in other cases it may be a powerful service provider (e.g., TomTom) where our ecosystem capability search and redeem perspective is highly relevant.

Our model portrays the notions of capability search and capability redeem as two important causal tendencies of the non-focal actor's ecosystem participation, and we suggest that they enhance our understanding of the stories of digital ecosystems that take the platform owner as starting-point (cf. Tiwana et al., 2010; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Capability search refers to a firm's activity of locating external capability deemed valuable for extending its innovation habitat. Capability redeem refers to a firm's activity of cultivating its innovation habitat with external capability for developing, distributing, and/or monetizing its products and services. Both activities are relevant to continuously nurture a firm's innovation habitat. The competitive position of the individual, non-focal firm is reflected in its ability to evolve its innovation habitat beyond the traditional notion of organizational boundary.

Future studies could address limitations in our work. First, it should be clear that historical data do not provide first-hand accounts of the research situation. It therefore potentially introduces a perceived distance between the research team and the context in which the data originate (cf. Walsham, 1995). While our research design offers longitudinal coverage of the evolution at the case site, primary data collection on activities such as capability search and redeem would potentially enrich their conceptualization as it allows for close-up study of emerging practices and meaning-making of stakeholders involved. Second, our research study is limited to Sony Ericsson, being a mobile device manufacturer. There are obviously many other types of non-focal actors engaging with ecosystems. This particular non-focal actor represents an incumbent firm. Drawing on this work, there is an opportunity for future research to study small, non-focal start-up firms, rather than multinational corporations. Such firms are different in the sense that they do not reinforce or extend an existing innovation habitat, but rather cultivate their innovation habitat on the go. Lastly, we know that the extant literature primarily deals with the governance of a single ecosystem. Still, there are examples of focal actors, who in relation to other ecosystems take on the role as non-focal actor. More research is needed on how focal actors deal with their potential need of engaging in capability search and redeem across ecosystems that they do not control.

## About the authors

Lisen Selander is a Ph.D. and Senior Lecturer at the Department of Applied Information Technology, University of Gothenburg, Sweden. She received her Ph.D. in 2008 from the Institute of Economic Research in Lund, Sweden. Her current research interests are in the areas of Innovation Ecosystems, Social Media and Social Movements, and Information Systems Management Research. The outcome of her research has been published in Information Systems Journal, the Journal of Information Technology Theory and Application, and at several international conferences such as the International Conference on Information Systems and the Academy of Management Conference.

Ola Henfridsson is a Professor of Information Systems and Management at Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, UK. He is also a Visiting Professor at the Department of Applied Information Technology, Chalmers University of Technology, Sweden. His research interests include digital innovation, digital infrastructure, platforms, and technology management. The outcome of this research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and many other journals in the information systems discipline. He is a Senior Editor of Journal of Information Technology and a Senior Editor emeritus of the MIS Quarterly. Ola also serves on the editorial boards of Information Technology and People and Journal of the Association for Information Systems.

Dr. Fredrik Svahn is Postdoctoral Research Associate at the Department of Applied Information Technology, Chalmers University of Technology, Sweden. He is also active at the applied research institute Viktoria Swedish ICT. Fredrik earned his Ph.D. in Informatics at Umeå University 2012 and a M.Sc. in Applied Physics and Electrical Engineering at Linköping Institute of Technology in 1996. He has more than 10 years of professional experience from Defense, Telecom and Automotive industries. Fredrik's research is focused on new patterns of innovation emerging in response to digitalization of manufactured products. In particular, he studies how organizations can architect digital products to improve its generative capability.
