# How does PLM technology support knowledge transfer and translation in new product development? Transparency and boundary spanners in an international context

Valéry Merminod <sup>a,b</sup>, Frantz Rowe <sup>b,c,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> University of Grenoble, IAE-CERAG, France

<sup>b</sup> SKEMA Business School, France

<sup>c</sup> Université de Nantes, IEMN-IAE, LEMNA, France

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 24 November 2010 Received in revised form 10 July 2012 Accepted 17 July 2012 Available online 11 August 2012

## a b s t r a c t

Information Technology is often viewed as imposing too much standardization and limiting <sup>fl</sup>exibility in New Product Development (NPD). This paper aims at understanding how the use of Product Lifecycle Management Technology (PLM) contributes to knowledge sharing in an international NPD environment. The research is based on a longitudinal case study of a consumer goods industry group and involved development teams in Europe and local suppliers in China. Knowledge transfer and translation were observed through the reduction of communication glitches among members and increased NPD work with Chinese suppliers. The results of the case study indicate that (1), with an important codi<sup>fi</sup>cation effort, the use of PLM technology resulted in higher data and network transparency and enhanced knowledge transfer; (2) PLM served as a particularly useful tool for knowledge translation especially for boundary spanners in their work relationships. While PLM can be considered the main mechanism for knowledge transfer in this context, the case suggests that knowledge translation requires a boundary spanner intervention and that, with the use of PLM, they reinforce each other. Particularly noteworthy was a positive shift in the boundary spanners' roles from the project leader to the outsourcing engineer. In turn, this unintended consequence reinforced their credibility and the legitimacy of the use of the system with the Chinese suppliers.

## 1. Introduction

New Product Development (NPD) is increasingly taking place across organizations and geographical borders. The literature dealing with management of knowledge boundaries in inter-organizational development of Information Technologies illustrates the complexity of the phenomenon and is only beginning to address it (Levina & Vaast, 2008). Surprisingly little research has been conducted on examining the effects of IT in international NPD projects on knowledge sharing. When NPD takes place in an international context, knowledge must be exchanged across organizational, geographical, cultural, and language barriers. These multiple barriers to knowledge sharing create high potential for failure (Bjorn & Ngwenyama, 2009) and a consequent degree of risk requiring boundary spanners. More recently, this complex situation has also stimulated a demand for digital solutions, such as Organizational Memory Systems, Cooperative Work Systems, and Project and Resource Management Systems (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006).

The market has responded to the need for integrating these systems with Product Lifecycle Management (PLM) technology, which manage product and project information using object storage and work<sup>fl</sup>ows. Such tools support the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and standardization of work<sup>fl</sup>ows and information objects, as they are produced and used by multiple participants in various functions, thus supporting the development phase from design to industrialization (manufacturing testing and validation) (Batenburg, Helms, & Versendaal, 2004). PLM technology historically comes from Product Data Management (PDM) systems which are dedicated to the management of links between product components and more generally engineering activity (Grieves, 2006; Stark, 2004). It is noteworthy that PLM mainly supports development tasks, and has little to do with creative and ill-de<sup>fi</sup>ned research activities.

Given this technological evolution, this paper contributes to the growing literature in IS and NPD by investigating the effects of PLM on knowledge sharing effectiveness in an inter-organizational and international context. In fact, while PLM enables users to store and modify objects such as product representations, and supports development planning and scheduling, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility ascribed to such integrated technology may not be as high (Barrett & Oborn, 2010) as the literature suggests (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009; Star & Griesemer, 1989), and thus hamper learning (Henderson & Clark, 1990) and mutual understanding (Te'eni, 2001) It is of particular interest to us to examine whether and how PLM can be used and how well knowledge is shared in a context where it crosses many types of boundaries (Romano, Pick, & Roztocki, 2010).

In the New Product Development literature, two opposing streams of literature address the debate on <sup>fl</sup>exibility versus standardization. One school attempts to identify best practices and processes that would enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to increase their control over NPD and thereby reduce the variance in results. Typically, Cooper's (2008) stage gate model is the exemplar for this school of thought. This model consists in de<sup>fi</sup>ning key project milestones and prede<sup>fi</sup>ned deliverables on each milestone so that transparency, i.e. the ability to let everyone see and understand the input and progress made in NPD (Moenaert, Caeldries, Lievens, & Wauters, 2000), is increased. A second school of thought questions the ability to control a complex, multi-faceted, context-dependent process like NPD. Thus, Sethi and Iqbal (2008) show that rigorous adherence to stage-gate activities and criteria are correlated with learning failures, which in turn lead to new product failures. This school calls for <sup>fl</sup>exibility of process and criteria (Krishnan & Ranganathan, 2009; Miner, Bassoff, & Moorman, 2001; Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996; Sethi & Iqbal, 2008). We are questioning the contribution of a standardizing information technology (PLM) to support knowledge sharing effectiveness and <sup>fl</sup>exibility in NPD. The IS literature emphasizes that IT use effects are generally complex and related to structural changes in organizational and work practices (Baxter, 2008; Robey & Boudreau, 1999). This case study sheds light on how PLM facilitates knowledge transfer and translation practices and their effectiveness; in particular it offers insights on how boundary spanners' knowledge translation role bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from PLM. Thus, this paper addresses three research questions. In an international and inter-organizational NPD context:

Q1: To what extent do standardizing IT platforms such as PLM support knowledge transfer and also knowledge translation effectiveness?

Q2: How does PLM use affect knowledge transfer practices and especially those related to transparency?

Q3: How does PLM use affect knowledge translation practices and especially the translation role of boundary spanners?

This research bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the unusual access we had to an industrial group to study 1) the effects of PLM use, for which case studies are lacking, 2) the context of growing European-Chinese supplier relationships which remains under-investigated, and 3) the consumable goods industry which may offer different insights with less radical product innovations than do Gehry's buildings (Boland, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007; Gal, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2008) or the Stealth Bomber (Argyres, 1999) cases.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the literature for understanding how PLM use affects knowledge sharing effectiveness in NPD. In Section 3, we present the organizational context of the case study and our methodology based on participant observations and interviews before and after PLM implementation. In Section 4, we report our empirical results. Finally, the Discussion generalizes results of this case and proposes directions for future research

## 2. Theoretical background

In order to identify research gaps and corresponding research questions, we <sup>fi</sup>rst question the contribution of IT for knowledge transfer and translation. We then introduce the glitch concept (Hoopes & Postrel, 1999) to measure knowledge sharing effectiveness, before tackling transfer and translation practices in an inter national NPD context. We end with the literature on PLM technology. Given the scarcity of PLM papers in IS we introduce its main characteristics and compare its features with those of CAD and ERP systems.

## 2.1. Knowledge sharing in NPD projects and the contribution of IT

NPD is by nature inter-functional so the management of knowledge boundaries is critical. Major streams of research focusing on knowledge sharing such as the Knowledge Based View (KBV) of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Grant, 1996a, 1996b; Kogut & Zander, 1992) and the knowledge creation framework of Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) show that knowledge is embedded in multiple entities such as culture, routines, systems, documents and actors (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Grant, 1996a, 1996b). Such contributions are essential for understanding knowledge in organizations but they do not focus on the speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of knowledge sharing across boundarie in an NPD environment. This is why Carlile has developed a knowledge sharing framework dedicated to the New Product Development process. This framework distinguishes knowledge transfer, translation, and transformation (Carlile, 2004). Knowledge transfer consists in managing syntactic boundaries, knowledge translation focuses on semantic boundaries and knowledge transformation is dedicated to pragmatic boundaries and knowledge creation. Okhuysen and Eisenhardt (2002, p.383) distinguish between knowledge sharing per se – identifying and communicating knowledge – and knowledge creation or transformation. In this paper, we focus on the communication impact on knowledge sharing that encompasses knowledge transfer and knowledge translation. Each of these aspects of knowledge sharing will now be discussed in greater detail.

## 2.1.1. Knowledge transfer and knowledge translation

Knowledge transfer occurs by bridging a syntactic or information processing boundary (Carlile, 2004). The syntactic capacity requires the development of a common lexicon for transferring domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge. Transfer is based on organizational routines with minor evolutions where knowledge sharing is quite easy. This perspective is the primary basis for technological approaches to knowledge sharing which emphasize storage and extraction mechanisms (Davenport, 2005), for knowledge that is seen as external, explicit, and storable (Joshi, Sarker, & Sarker, 2007). A common knowledge repository between participants increases their level of interdependence and the level of information transparency according to conferred access rights. Transfer solves basic problems of knowledge circulation and information access among project members. In fact, the availability of the most recent version of knowledge objects is often an issue in NPD projects. De<sup>fi</sup>nition of a common lexicon between these participants becomes crucial.

Knowledge translation is a more complex type of knowledge sharing which is more consistent with a connectionist perspective, where knowledge is not universal (Joshi et al., 2007). This second perspective incorporates cultural aspects of sharing (Adams, Day, & Dougherty, 1998; Lam, 1997; Lam & Chin, 2005) and relies on conventions between specialized participants with a common knowledge repository. Managing common knowledge is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in inter-organizational development because of differences in organizational and functional expertise due to participants' specializations and their “membership” in different cognitive environments (Carlile, 2004; Hoopes & Postrel, 1999). A common lexicon and transfer rules are not enough. Knowledge translation has a more tacit, situated, and experiential component. The complexity of translating knowledge comes from the need to bridge semantic or interpretive boundaries. This type of knowledge sharing depends on the development of routines to facilitate participants' adaptation (Sambamurthy, Bharadwaj, & Grover, 2003), and is supported by common language de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and experiences (Wenger, Roy, & Brown, 1999). Knowledge translation involves sharing evolving objects that are minimally codi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and a semantic capacity for developing common meanings, and identifying novel differences and dependencies (Carlile, 2002).

## 2.1.2. IT contribution for knowledge sharing across boundaries in NPD

IT may contribute to knowledge sharing through the analysis of boundary spanning activity (Jonsson, Holmstrom, & Lyytinen, 2009; Leonardi & Bailey, 2008). Thus, Jonsson et al. (2009) have studied the case of the use of a remote diagnostic system to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a typology of boundary spanning activities based on three types of boundaries: use pattern boundaries, divisional boundary and contextual boundary. The use pattern boundary corresponds to how the <sup>fl</sup>ow of data enables the provider to gain access to a continuous stream of information (Jonsson et al., 2009). Breaking divisional boundaries consists in understanding how the boundaries between different divisions can be removed with the capability to gather information centrally (Jonsson et al., 2009). Finally, breaking contextual boundaries is possible if knowledge can be retrieved simultaneously from multiple products in many different places (factories) (Jonsson et al., 2009). The PLM system is quite different from remote diagnostic systems in its nature and in its characteristics but the discussion on the three types of boundaries seems also relevant to NPD projects.

IT may also contribute to knowledge sharing through the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of artifacts representing parts of the product and through their exchange across boundaries. In NPD, heterogeneous actors produce and communicate knowledge artifacts which can be viewed as boundary objects (BO) (Star & Griesemer, 1989). BO may be objects, documents or other artifacts created and used during collaborative development, such as schedules, speci<sup>fi</sup>cations or 3D models (Carlile, 2002; Henderson, 1991). To qualify as BO they should both carry a common understanding of the elements that are important for all stakeholders at the boundary of their respective domains, but also be <sup>fl</sup>exible enough that they do not excessively constrain the different actors for pursuing their specialized design role (Carlile, 2002). In the world of design engineers, traditional work practices rely heavily on visual representations, such as sketches and drawings, to allow for the manipulation of tacit knowledge between individuals (Henderson, 1991). These representations offer support for discussion and negotiations (Gal et al., 2008), thus making explicit elements that were only implicit (Yap, Ngwenyama, & Osei-Bryson, 2003). The literature also emphasizes that repositories, standardized forms, objects, and models offer some interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility (Carlile, 2002). To simplify the language, further in this paper, we will refer to objects instead of boundary objects.

Collaborative technologies support the sharing of objects across distance. The NPD literature generally emphasizes knowledge sharing capabilities with measures relative to frequencies and patterns based on face-to-face, telephone, and email media (Subramaniam. 2006: Von Zedtwidtz, Gassmann, & Boutelier. 2004). Some research considers videoconferencing and documentation access (Moenaert et al., 2000) as improving the knowledge sharing infrastructure, but the works remain at a general level of description of media use and do not consider conceptual and theoretical properties of the IT used. Carlile's contribution is essential for better understanding knowledge sharing across boundary dimensions, but he does not focus nor analyze potential IT contributions to leveraging knowledge sharing across boundaries. Moreover, existing papers tend to relegate IT tools to spanning syntactic boundaries only. IT solutions can support asynchronous knowledge transfer (Montoya-Weiss, Massey, & Song, 2001) and facilitate knowledge transfer among the project team through codi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Moenaert et al., 2000). However, some advocate that IT fails at supporting knowledge translation by inadequately conveying contextual cues (Cramton, 2001), not enabling interpretation of new knowledge (Alavi & Tiwana, 2002), enabling sharing of decontextualized knowledge only (Majchrzak, Malhotra, & John, 2005), or limiting knowledge reuse by making knowledge appropriation dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (Kirkman, Rosen, Telsluk, & Gibson, 2004). These perspectives may be too critical of IT capabilities. Our assumption is that IT platforms such as PLM should help bridge both syntactic and semantic boundaries. Given this <sup>fi</sup>rst literature gap, we contend that there is a fundamental need to consider the role that IT solutions, especially PLM, can play in knowledge transfer and also knowledge translation. This brings us to our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question: In an international NPD context, to what extent do standardizing IT platforms such as PLM support knowledge transfer and also knowledge translation effectiveness?

## 2.2. Knowledge sharing effectiveness evaluation through reduction of glitches

To answer such a question we need to measure knowledge sharing effectiveness. By nature, it is hard to measure knowledge sharing (Grant, 1996a; Kogut & Zander, 1992). A good approach for analyzing the contribution of PLM to knowledge sharing is to measure the reduction of glitches that occur during the NPD process (Hoopes & Postrel, 1999). According to Hoopes and Postrel (1999), a glitch is a gap in shared knowledge, that is, an unsatisfactory outcome during a multi-agent project that is directly caused or allowed by a lack of cross-functional or inter-specialty knowledge about problem constraints. The authors posit that glitches can be avoided if participants not only have knowledge, but can also understand and interpret this knowledge. They utilize the glitch to measure the degree of knowledge sharing in the NPD context. In fact, glitches can be used to measure the reduction of the number of errors in communication, because quality of communication improves knowledge sharing (Te'eni, 2001). Glitches can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by critical errors directly linked with ineffective knowledge sharing on projects. Hoopes and Postrel (1999) distinguish several types of “glitches” (Table 1).

• The <sup>fi</sup>rst glitch type is associated with non respect of communication procedures called Serial Product Development Process (SPDP) (Hoopes & Postrel, 1999). This occurs when de<sup>fi</sup>ned sharing rules are not respected (referred to as knowledge transfer according to Carlile's (2004) classi<sup>fi</sup>cation).

• The second glitch type is associated with situations characterized as issue slippage (Hoopes & Postrel, 1999), where procedures are respected in the sense that they were used, but where a key constraint has not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by a participant, (also referred to as knowledge transfer, according to Carlile (2004)).

• The third glitch type is associated with problems that are not solved because they are incorrectly described or understood by a participant. Such problems of communication in NPD happen due to time pressure on projects, integration of several suppliers in projects, and distant design of new products. It is associated with problems of communication on non-critical elements for which one party cannot imagine the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty another party is having in trying to understand the problem. This category is characterized as sticky knowledge (Von Hippel, 1994) by Hoopes and Postrel (1999) (referred to as knowledge translation, according to Carlile (2004)).

## 2.3.1. Knowledge transfer practices in the international new product development context

Transparency of the communication network is the degree to which the communication network is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently clear and accessible such that everyone understands the inputs and progress made (Moenaert et al., 2000). In fact, data transparency is the assurance that data being reported are accessible and can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed for oneself. Codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, a related concept, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the individual and collective processes through which knowledge and experience may be structured and made explicit (Moenaert et al., 2000). The level of knowledge codi<sup>fi</sup>cation is highly dependent on the context (Levina & Vaast, 2005). The inter-organizational environment forces <sup>fi</sup>rms to codify and de<sup>fi</sup>ne a process to support NPD. This is all the more important in an international and inter-organizational context, as geographic distance in product development tasks necessitates high levels of information exchange between distant functions (Moenaert et al., 2000). Contractualization between supplier and customer requires institutionalized mechanisms for knowledge transfer through knowledge codi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Fong Boh, 2006; Moenaert et al., 2000). The implementation of PLM offers a virtual platform for codifying and sharing all project and product explicit knowledge.

To analyze knowledge transparency in NPD projects, we utilize three generative mechanisms which are object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, object visualization and NPD process structuring. Object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation consists in precisely de<sup>fi</sup>ning object content. In the literature, some consider codi<sup>fi</sup>ed objects as rigid shared objects which do not facilitate effective integration of tacit knowing in diverse teams (Barrett & Oborn, 2010; Vaast & Levina, 2006). Others consider that the use of templates increases the effectiveness of knowledge transfer (Jensen & Szulanski, 2007; Jensen, Szulanski, & Casaburi, 2003). Overall, we suggest that the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of key shared object may help to transfer knowledge in international NPD project. Concerning object visualization, existing literature has emphasized the role of 3D tools for managing common representation (Argyres, 1999; Boland et al., 2007). In these studies CAD was used to study object visualization. In this research, we investigate non-CAD visualization practices, and in particular the use of the viewer by non designers. The third generative mechanism of knowledge transparency is NPD process structuring, which is analyzed through the use of rules and in particular through project stage gates and key milestones with prede<sup>fi</sup>ned deliverables (Cooper, 2008).

The extant literature does not explain how PLM use supports transparency practices. In the NPD literature, the most recent works only consider intranets, videoconferencing, email and phone calls (Manolopoulos, Söderquist, & Pearce, 2011; Sosa, Eppinger, Pich, McKendrick, & Stout, 2002). Given this second literature gap, there is a need to investigate whether in such environments, PLM is likely to be bypassed with many workarounds (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006) or whether project members tend to rely on PLM transparency through features use, resulting in higher formalization. This brings us to our second research question: In an international NPD context how does PLM use affect knowledge transfer practice, and especially those related to transparency?

## 2.3.2. Knowledge translation practices in international new product development context

Risks are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant that PLM use will not be effective when NPD team members are geographically dispersed and meet physically only occasionally. Management of dispersed teams is quite complex because of knowledge management problems such as: failure to communicate and retain contextual information, unevenly distributed information, dif<sup>fi</sup>culty communicating and understanding the salience of information, differences in speed of access to information, and dif<sup>fi</sup>culty interpreting the meaning of silence (Cramton, 2001). These problems are exacerbated in international and inter-organizational teams. When cultural distance is high between collaborating organizations, boundary spanners can be nominated to reduce these knowledge management problems. Indeed, these participants also play a key mediating role in ensuring knowledge translation between organizations. The literature on knowledge management has emphasized the importance of relving on individuals to perform boundary spanning roles (Levina & Vaast. 2005: Pawlowski & Robey, 2004). Boundary spanning activities encompass searching, translating, and disseminating (Morrison, 2008). Studying a <sup>fi</sup>rm sending engineering tasks from the US and Mexico to an offshore site in India, Leonardi and Bailey (2008) found that boundary spanners were very important from an ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency viewpoint for routing tasks strategically and for <sup>fi</sup>ltering out poor quality.

Boundary spanners are vital individuals who facilitate the sharing of expertise by linking two or more groups of people separated by location, hierarchy, or function (Levina & Vaast, 2005). Typically, boundary spanners play several roles in everyday relationships in NPD projects, that is, they: (1) bridge lexicon gaps, (2) reconcile interpretive differences by creating shared meanings, and (3) facilitate means through which individuals can jointly transform their local knowledge. Levina and Vaast (2005) also distinguish between nominated boundary spanners and boundary spanners in practice. The latter build a new joint <sup>fi</sup>eld between two <sup>fi</sup>elds, whereas nominated boundary spanners mainly play an institutional role.

In inter-organizational NPD, analyzing boundary spanners is essential in practice because they transform tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge (Pawlowski & Robey, 2004). This is especially important in cross-national NPD collaboration, as there are problems caused by differences in expertise interpretation and national cultures which increase the complexity of project collaboration (Barrett & Oborn, 2010). While searching is not directly affected by PLM, translation and dissemination can potentially gain considerable support from PLM. As in the case of remote diagnostic systems (Jonsson et al., 2009), we contend that PLM goes beyond providing additional means for boundary spanning (Jonsson et al., 2009, p249), but this effect is much more important in an inter-organizational and international context because IT can reinforce the credibility (Joshi et al., 2007; Sussman & Siegal, 2003) of boundary spanners. Given this third literature gap, there is a need to study whether boundary spanners are bypassed after systems implementation or if their role is reinforced and changed. The literature does not provide an answer to our third research question: In an international NPD context how does PLM use affect knowledge translation practices and especially the translation role of boundary spanners?

## 2.4. PLM features and comparisons with other enterprise systems

Surprisingly, we found very few research studies aimed at attempting to understand PLM technology and its effects in the IS literature (Balaji, Ranganathan, & Coleman, 2011; Batenburg et al., 2004; Merminod & Rowe, 2010; Merminod, Rowe, & Watts, 2008). In fact, recently PLM has been studied mostly in the engineering literature (Ameri & Dutta, 2005; Cheung, Matthews, Gao, & Maropoulos, 2008; Young, Gunendran, Cutting-Decelle, & Gruninger, 2007) and in the International Journal of Product Lifecycle Management (IJPLM). These contributions are very useful for understanding technical aspects (Grieves, 2006; Ling, Zhang, Li, & Xie, 2005; Sang, Tae, & Sang, 2010). However, while several papers are focused on knowledge effects (Gomes, Varret, Bluntzer, & Sagot, 2009; Yi & Ming, 2010; Young et al., 2007), the usage context such as the transnational context (Hu, Wang, & Bidanda, 2006) or product complexity and production <sup>fl</sup>ow (Alemanni, Alessia, Tornincasa, & Vezzetti, 2008), most of these contributions only super<sup>fi</sup>cially analyze the relationship between the technology, its context of use, and related effects.

Basic PLM technologies includes an Organizational Memory System with classi<sup>fi</sup>cation features (data organization offering a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned structure, organization of data, tracking, and use cases for components) and a Project and Resource Management System (project planning and monitoring and multi criteria search among project portfolio). When serving NPD processes, Organizational Memory Systems were initially separate from Project and Resource Management Systems, and from Cooperative Work Systems (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). PLM bridges this gap between separate systems by providing a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed enterprise system for project and product follow up. Extended PLM technologies contain a web collaboration solution that enables a real time/synchronous system capable of simultaneous changes by different remote participants on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c object such as a three dimensional plan.

To understand if a standardized platform such as PLM allows for some <sup>fl</sup>exibility and knowledge sharing it may be important to compare its features in a different systems context. Appendix A provides a comparison of the sub systems and their features with those of Computer Aided Design (CAD) and ERP systems. CAD is relevant because PLM is often considered to be an extension of CAD, providing access to drawings and 3D visualizations to employees not in the R&D or styling functions. Using geometric <sup>fi</sup>gures, CAD representations attempt to provide a complete de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the physical structure of a product (Grieves, 2006). The CAD literature analyzes 3D representation effects and shows that 3D CAD solutions offer a higher level of precision for NPD projects (Argyres, 1999) and facilitate <sup>fl</sup>exibility by helping building trading zones (Boland et al., 2007). With CAD solutions, the 2D and 3D representations are often restricted to a limited number of project members (in styling and engineering) whereas with PLM, they are distributed widely among project members so that project members can share common representations from virtual mock ups. This facilitates cohesion and tight coupling (Yoo, Boland, & Lyytinen, 2006) in the sense that different representations or views of product information are possible based on virtual geometrical and mechanical views through the CAD viewer.

The ERP literature is also important for understanding PLM because these two technologies are complementary and address the integration issue. PLM is focused on design & development and engineering, whereas ERP is articulated around production and sales & services functions. However, they are complementary systems. Any information about a product should be created in the PLM system before being referenced in the ERP system. PLM and ERP architectures are based on a single system with a shared database and therefore enable better consistency and singularity (Grieves, 2006). The main objective of PLM is to revamp non viable and sub-optimal processes by unifying disparate, siloed processes into a single integrated NPD process (Nambisan, 2010). While the process scope differs — ERP systems focus on the integration of back of<sup>fi</sup>ce activity (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005) whereas PLM primarily focuses on the integration of NPD process (Balaji et al., 2011), both types of systems aim at integrating multiple departments, involving processes that have strong cross functional interdependencies. Moreover, they rely on a standardization capability which consists in codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of data and implementation of prescriptive rules concerning processes (Kallinikos, 2004). The consequence is that ERP systems are generally considered as enabling a limited <sup>fl</sup>exibility (Lee & Lee, 2000) and are accompanied with workarounds (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006). Boudreau and Robey describe an exception to this view in a case study (2005) showing that while ERP rules do not prevent human agency, they may be seen as too numerous, thus imposing ways of working (Strong & Volkoff, 2010) and enforcing a disciplinary regime (Gosain, 2004), but also paradoxically enabling re<sup>fl</sup>exivity and empowerment (Elmes, Strong, & Volkoff, 2005). PLM is considered as an outcome of lean thinking and aims at eliminating waste and ensuring ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency across different aspects of a product's life (Grieves, 2006), but there is no literature establishing if PLM has a limited <sup>fl</sup>exibility or not.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Study design and focus of analysis

The case study method is an appropriate research strategy for gaining rich empirical insights (Yin & Campbell, 2002) and for understanding PLM's contribution to NPD knowledge sharing effectiveness using a wide variety of data sources. The current case study focuses on showing the changes between effects observed before (which is our baseline case) and after PLM implementation. This was made possible under a CIFRE contract (Klein & Rowe, 2008), which allowed the <sup>fi</sup>rst author of this paper to be hired into an industrial group for three years (2005–2008) while he studied for his PhD. During this time he actively participated in PLM implementations in Asia and Europe within this French group. In 2008, this group managed around 350 projects using PLM. This group was chosen because it had an R&D unit within it which offered an opportunity to develop a revelatory case of the effects of PLM on knowledge sharing in international and inter-organizational NPD projects. The form of R&D organization of this research is an international adaptor unit (Nobel & Birkinshaw. 1998) as the Chinese unit is in charge of ensuring the relation between corporate and research units and local teams and providing support to suppliers and local manufacturing units. This R&D con<sup>fi</sup>guration is especially interesting because according to Nobel and Birkinshaw “international adaptor units will exhibit low levels of formalization” (Nobel & Birkinshaw, 1998, p. 484), a view that is challenged by PLMT implementation.

In this single case study, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings (presented below) re<sup>fl</sup>ect a macro-level synthesis and comparison before and after implementation of a set of NPD projects. We analyze PLM impact on knowledge sharing practices and effectiveness among inter-organizational NPD projects launched for a business unit of this group.

## 3.1.1. Research site and baseline context of the case

This group develops small domestic appliances with international brands (€2.8 bn turnover in 2007). It offers a wide range of products, such as linen care products (steam irons, steam stations, travel irons, etc.), personal care products (hair dryers, lady shavers, hair remove, etc.), home comfort (fans, heathers, air puri<sup>fi</sup>er, etc.) and baby care products (baby phones, blenders, sterilizers, etc.). 540 employees belong to the R&D department located internationally with an R&D budget representing 2% of <sup>fi</sup>rm turnover. More than 200 new products were developed in 2007. This group has a strong tradition of external growth with multiple recent acquisitions in Colombia, China, Italy, and Brazil. The external environment of this group is characterized by strong competition, pressure from large retailers, and important changes in consumer behavior that have occurred since 2000. In this context, a growing number of products have been developed with Chinese suppliers: 40% of <sup>fi</sup>nished products were co-developed with the Chinese in 2007, up from less than 10% in 2000.

Inter-organizational NPD is organized around a three-group structure (Appendix B). The first encompasses eleven development centers with co-located members specialized by product family. These centers are geographically dispersed all over France and Germany, and are relatively small (i.e., approximately 15 people). The cultures and development processes of these centers vary widely, since they were originally separate companies which were acquired for external growth. Within these centers, European project teams are organized around eight to ten members with specialties such as marketing, styling, technical staff, quality, standards, and logistics.

The second group of participants associated with this group's NPD efforts is based in China and ensures trading and development support functions for international adaptation of suppliers. 72 people are dedicated to identifying suppliers, participating in new product development, and supporting logistic and administrative responsibilities. Within this structure, there are technical staff members (20 outsourcing engineers and ten technicians) in charge of project follow-up, as well as quality teams (19 people) in charge of implementing controls for the supplier manufacturing process and <sup>fi</sup>nished products. These members are located in Hong Kong, Shenzhen, or Shanghai.

The third group of participants in the process are suppliers. There are two kinds of suppliers: ongoing trusted suppliers (32 in 2007) and occasional suppliers (61 in 2007). For the ongoing trusted suppliers, a dedicated part time resource from the Asian trading and support structure is located in the supplier's of<sup>fi</sup>ce. This participant, called ‘outsourcing engineer,’ is in charge of following projects from the supplier's side, and has extensive access rights to PLM. Some ongoing trusted suppliers have direct access to PLM software so that they do not need outsourcing engineers to access to project objects. These suppliers have speci<sup>fi</sup>c access right giving them access to a sub-set of the objects of the project. For occasional suppliers, no outsourcing engineers are located in supplier's of<sup>fi</sup>ces, but an outsourcing engineer who shares his time between several occasional suppliers performs the project follow up. For projects with occasional suppliers, they are given access to objects through email or by PLM through the outsourcing engineers' laptops.

Before PLM deployment, the development teams faced several problems. Knowledge on projects was distributed across several IT tools and hence it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain a consolidated view of project objects. Drawings were only accessible through CAD tools, and there were no interfaces between technical databases; thus, most exchanges were performed through email, resulting in problems with exchange tracking and information overload. These problems lead to redundant data collection, errors in multiple databases, and high numbers of email exchanges on each project.

In order to rationalize their inter-organizational development, the group decided to reorganize its NPD process. Teams in China were reorganized around suppliers instead of product family. This decision had important impacts on the objects shared during the design and industrialization phases, as follows. Before this reorganization, each team in charge of a product family had its own rules and documents that were used to develop new products. The reorganization forced the team to de<sup>fi</sup>ne common rules, such as project milestones and procedures, for NPD with Chinese suppliers.

In order to support this reorganization (after a pilot project for internal development), the group decided to implement a PLM tool in 2006. The main objective was to replace heterogeneous processes with an integrated development process based on integrated software: PLM. The new PLM application was used by all intra-organizational project members (e.g., specialists in marketing, styling, project manager, quality, standards, and outsourcing managers). Extended PLM with web conferencing solutions were available only to a limited number of ongoing trusted suppliers. Web conferences were used in order to help in design or to solve problems necessitating help from specialists located in Europe. The chosen PLM solution is TeamCenter from Siemens. The major weakness of this solution is the limited ergonomy of the PLM interface. However, in this group, PLM use resulted in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant task productivity gains measured through time savings (Merminod, Rowe, & Mothe, 2009) that we attribute to knowledge sharing effectiveness improvements due to PLM use, These productivity gains were expected. but organizational consequences around boundary spanner roles were not.

## 3.1.2. Focus of analysis

Our analysis focuses on the linen care business unit. This unit is strategic for this group, with a leadership position in Europe. Most of its very innovative products remain designed and manufactured internally.

Co-developed products were limited to those with less technological innovation. Linen care was the <sup>fi</sup>rst unit to implement PLM in China after a <sup>fi</sup>rst successful adoption. Indeed, the complexity of linen care activity is related to product design more than to the manufacturing process. A steam iron bill of material (BOM) is composed of around one hundred components, and, in comparison with other business units, its products are fairly complex to design due to security constraints. The average lead-time to develop a linen care product is 12 months. The design activity developed rapidly with PLM implementation: from 10 projects in 2005 to 15 in 2007 (Appendix C). Five projects managed with legacy systems and <sup>fi</sup>ve similar projects supported by PLM were chosen for analysis. Beyond the temporal comparison that allowed for comparing systems effects, these projects were also chosen to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the diversity of supplier integration categories in the product development process: black box (design primarily driven by the supplier based on client speci<sup>fi</sup>cation), gray box (joint design with numerous iterations), white box (design primarily driven by the client), and make to print (detailed speci<sup>fi</sup>cations imposed to the supplier with no input from the supplier) (Petersen, Hand<sup>fi</sup>eld, & Ragatz, 2005). Thus we selected one project for each supplier integration category, except for white box for which we had two.

## 3.3.1. Knowledge sharing practices

Measurement of knowledge transfer practices was structured around the analysis of evolution of knowledge transparency in the projects through object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, process structuring and objects visualization. Object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation was measured through two proxies: the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of major project objects with respect to prede<sup>fi</sup>ned codi<sup>fi</sup>cation rules and the adherence to common storage rules in order to facilitate object search. Process structuring was analyzed using four different proxies: the sharing of key deliverables on the project, validation of project milestones, object evolution tracking, and consolidated views on the project (Table 3). To measure key deliverables on the project, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a list of ten key deliverables for the project and the number of those which were transferred to projects. To measure the validation of project milestones, we veri<sup>fi</sup>ed whether there was a real validation of milestones by project managers. For object evolution tracking, we observed object indexes and status. Finally, for a consolidated view on projects, we investigated the dashboards available in Legacy systems before and during PLM implementation. The 3D viewer and the newly codi<sup>fi</sup>ed product technical sheet offered new virtual representation of the future product, completed with components and design constraints. The analysis compared these situations with Legacy systems use before PLM implementation.

The practices associated with knowledge translation activity were analyzed through the role of boundary spanners in the project. In our international inter-organizational projects, the boundary spanning role was mainly performed by the Chinese outsourcing engineer and the project manager located in Europe. The operational roles of the Chinese outsourcing engineer and project manager were analyzed through interviews and observations on selected projects.

## 3.3.2. Knowledge sharing effectiveness

A list of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed glitches was compiled based on observations and interviews, and was then validated by project managers and outsourcing engineers during several meetings. From the exhaustive list of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed NPD projects, problems were collected through project team members, from which we selected those related to glitches. Next, we associated the glitches with the three categories of glitches: SPDP, issue slippage, and sticky knowledge glitches.

Knowledge transfer outcomes were measured as lack of knowledge transfer glitches. Glitches were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through the requests for complementary information generated by project members during the project. The objective was to measure whether a) knowledge was transferred suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently early in the project, and was available for all members, or b) if it was necessary to ask for complementary information due to a lack of knowledge exchange during the project (Table 4). This analysis was based on the triangulation of interviews with project members and the analysis of emails exchanged during the project.

Regarding knowledge translation, the outcome measure corresponds to the knowledge translation glitches identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in sticky knowledge situations. This data analysis enabled us to measure knowledge sharing effectiveness and better understand the nature of knowledge sharing and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms of knowledge transfer and translation. We compared elements collected in interviews with basic statistics from projects and from the PLM system and other legacy systems. Semi-structured interviews focused on problems before and after PLM implementation and on PLM bene<sup>fi</sup>ts/drawbacks and consequential outcomes. The interviews were analyzed using NVivo 7 based on a coding scheme corresponding to simple glitches (SPDP or Issue Slippage glitches) and sticky knowledge glitches.

## 4. Results

This section systematically reports the evolution of knowledge transfer and translation by considering <sup>fi</sup>rst the situation prior to PLM implementation and then after. We present the evolution of knowledge sharing practices and then knowledge sharing effectiveness for these two knowledge sharing issues.

## 4.1. PLM enhances knowledge transfer practices through transparency betterment

Identi<sup>fi</sup>ed problems included simple knowledge transfer problems due to basic communication errors. After PLM implementation, we looked for residual transfer problems to distinguish them from those that seemed to have been resolved with the use of the PLM tool. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two types of knowledge transfer problems that were solved: lack of common syntactic representation with codi<sup>fi</sup>cation issues, and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties due to asymmetric information and lack of transparency.

## 4.1.1. Common object codification for project syntactic rules

PLM contributes to the improvement of the management of a common lexicon between client and supplier. It also facilitates de<sup>fi</sup>nition of preliminary mutual constraints, thanks to the availability of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned object content. This preliminary object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation was critical for improving information processes in NPD projects. During PLM implementation, 40 types of objects (Table 5) were codi<sup>fi</sup>ed in order to facilitate interactions and to increase understanding between participants.

“Before PLM, we had no document with all product characteristics which was understandable by the supplier but also internally. The technical sheet enables to consolidate all product information in one unique document and a huge job was done in order to take into account specific requirements to facilitate information comprehension” (Product Manager).

For the group members located in China, centralized object collection and rules regarding project milestones enabled them to have clear, objective information on the project every day. Before PLM implementation, only 40% of objects were stored with clear rules, whereas more than 90% were stored afterwards using the capabilities of the PLM tool (Table 6). Before PLM implementation, trading and support staff in China found it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain an overall view of projects and their progress. After PLM, participants had access to consolidated views from the supplier and other resources. Outsourcing engineers could easily understand the project context since they had access to information on all projects, with secured access to internal PLM resources.

## 4.1.2. Object visualization

Prior to PLM tool implementation, during the process of design con<sup>fi</sup>guration between the Chinese and French, a cultural dif<sup>fi</sup>culty arose from the way project explicit knowledge was presented. Mutual understanding of product development requirements was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult due to the lack of a common knowledge repository and visualization solutions for key objects. The visualization capabilities of the PLM limited transfer problems with suppliers and especially with the Chinese. In particular, the 3D visualization capabilities helped achieve a common representation of the product design.

“PLM enables having a clear virtual representation of the future product and facilitates problem identification early in the design. Thus, the 3D mock up validation by the European design team enables verification of common design rules and norms and thus eliminates some errors during the industrialization phase” (Chinese Supplier project manager).

The need to facilitate object sharing with Chinese suppliers relied partially on the visualization through objects. A picture with basic explanations was easier for Chinese suppliers to understand than a description with detailed written technical explanations. The preliminary work of pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ning some of the key objects exchanged through the PLM tool helped reduce the number of problem solving situations. In projects involving occasional suppliers, the 3D PLM viewer helped improve knowledge sharing with the outsourcing engineer. In projects with on-going trusted suppliers that had direct access to the PLM tool, this feature enabled reduced issue slippages without the intervention of outsourcing engineers. Moreover, the 3D viewer feature enabled a common and holistic representation of the <sup>fi</sup>nished product for all participants (e.g. from marketing, styling, engineering department, etc.), whereas before PLM, only participants with CAD tools could visualize the design.

Technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and quality plans were totally re-engineered with the PLM project so as to facilitate visualization. Many pictures and drawings were added to support exchanges. Previously, a lot of re-engineering was done on product technical sheets that contained all technical, styling, logistics, and standards information on any speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>nished product variant (cf. Appendix E). Before PLM, this document was produced manually with component collection from all departments involved; in many cases, it was in different formats and information presentation types. With PLM, common group content was de<sup>fi</sup>ned with a clear focus on explicit knowledge ergonomy, such that product colors, technical characteristics, logistics information, and after sales information were produced, which, in turn, facilitated analysis by the Chinese supplier design or logistics team. This work had a positive impact on Chinese suppliers who recognized that the new product speci<sup>fi</sup>cation rules enabled them to reduce problems caused by their misunderstanding of the French team's requirements.

“We work with several business units on different ranges of products. Sometimes, it was difficult to understand detailed product specifications because information was sent separately and the content could be difficult to understand. It's true that the new IS solution enables having clear detailed product technical specifications with clear information. It's easier for us to manage internally” (Chinese supplier).

## 4.1.3. NPD process structuring

Using the PLM database, we conducted an analysis comparing the existence of 10 key deliverables (Table 6) on projects and the extent of validation before and after the implementation of PLM (Table 6). For projects created and managed using PLM, 95% contained these ten key objects, whereas only 60% of the projects managed outside PLM contained these elements. Indeed, the percentage of objects tracked in the system increased considerably with PLM, and each key object was electronically validated through work<sup>fl</sup>ows. PLM enabled structured storage of project objects, and thus pushed participants to mindfully respect templates of key objects. It also reinforced the need to respect structured project milestones. After the PLM implementation, 90% of milestone content was validated, whereas prior to PLM, only 20% of milestone content was validated.

Object content and structure were rede<sup>fi</sup>ned in order to take into account mutual constraints during the PLM project. As a result product and project transparency were improved. Prior to PLM implementation, each functional department used its IT legacy system to store their own objects, that is, the NPD was organized in information silos by functional area. Thus, for the Bill Of Material (BOM), there was a BOM managed by the engineering team, one managed by the industrialization team and one managed by the after sales services. With PLM, there is one unique IT solution, which supports project and product objects.

A huge rede<sup>fi</sup>nition of the objects' work was undertaken to include different representations of product information and avoid duplication of objects by department. The work consisted of taking into account requirements from different functional areas to facilitate interdependent representations of each object. Thus, the product technical sheet clearly integrates views from engineering, marketing, quality, standards, and industrialization project members. Before PLM, several objects had to be consolidated in order to get an overall view of a variant of the <sup>fi</sup>nished product, whereas with the object technical sheet, there is an inter-functional and cohesive view of the product characteristics (Appendix E).

“For a supplier, we had different Finished Product technical sheets structures… It raised problems for quality control…With PLM, technical sheets are clear and errors of interpretations decreased” (Outsourcing engineer).

Under PLM, peripheral project members are not forgotten as occurs after sales specialists. Work<sup>fl</sup>ows serve to reinforce participants' awareness because validation is tracked and knowledge content is readily available (Table 6).

“With PLM, we have noticed a clear change in the respect of project process and validation of key project deliverables. Chinese support teams are very aware of the respect of project process rules and they put pressure on Business Unit teams when they are late or do not validate project documents” (Quality manager).

Naturally, all of this does not imply that PLM monitoring reveals every action in real time. Project members can still release data and knowledge as late as possible, but communication network transparency and its more detailed level of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions enabled by PLM serve to reduce information withholding.

## 4.2. PLM enhances knowledge translation practices through changes in boundary spanners' role

## 4.2.1. PLM reinforces outsourcing engineers' role in knowledge translation

PLM use enabled the outsourcing engineer, who was located in or near supplier plants, to have direct access to project and product knowledge. He was, and remains, in charge of managing operational relations, answering questions and meeting communication needs. Before PLM, these engineers found it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to gather all necessary information on their projects. Thus their credibility, vis à vis Chinese suppliers, was limited.

The project manager was the key participant for project operational follow up, but as he was located in Europe, he was only available by phone, email, or web conferences before PLM. Many operational technical adjustments were partially done or were done with limited or inaccurate information. After PLM implementation, the local outsourcing engineer had access to more information on projects and could thus more effectively deal with operational relations with the supplier(s). His position was key because he was Chinese and used to working with the French and could thus detect and address some potential cultural gaps and reduce glitches. His position near suppliers' factories was an ideal one for observing NPD. For important suppliers, outsourcing engineers are located within supplier factories for 2 to 3 days per week. This positioning decreases some glitches due to cultural misunderstandings, such as continuous changes in planning and priorities by the Chinese supplier. Indeed, we observed the Chinese attitude of constantly modifying and challenging speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and priorities, whereas the French preferred that dates and content engagements were respected. The French also tended to be more direct and could sometimes be perceived as being rude, whereas the Chinese preferred indirect language without loss of face. The outsourcing engineer's role was to preserve good relations in the inter-cultural context.

“Before PLM, I had some difficulties solving problems with suppliers because I didn't have all the information on projects. Sometimes, the project manager made contact directly with the supplier by e-mails and I was not informed. With PLM, I can more easily solve some cultural or technical gaps between the French team and suppliers” (Outsourcing engineer).

Prior to PLM implementation, the outsourcing engineer played the communication role of verifying that all information was correctly transferred among distant participants. After PLM implementation, outsourcing engineers have direct access to all available project and product objects; they could thus easily identify potential semantic gaps between Chinese suppliers and internal information. Moreover, having a 3D viewer was important in their operational relations with the supplier. This feature supported knowledge translation by making it easier to visualize a virtual prototype that, negotiations and decisions centered upon (Table 7).

With this feature the outsourcing engineer played an operational role by validating options and potentially solving problems directly with the supplier, without the intervention of the Europe-based project manager. Thus these boundary spanners earned credibility because they were now able to really manage operational relations with the supplier. Outsourcing engineers reinforced their role after PLM implementation. This helped in daily operational relations with suppliers because outsourcing engineers were technically more autonomous viz à viz European technical teams, thanks to direct access to all technical objects for the project.

“Since PLM implementation, the outsourcing engineer is the real manager of the project and we do not need to exchange any more with the French project manager for operational technical questions related to the product. Two years before, the outsourcing engineer autonomy was partial so his role and value added was often limited for us” (Chinese supplier).

However, local outsourcing engineers were limited in their ability to manage complex technical problems and solve these problems directly with Chinese suppliers. Thus, the Chinese support team aimed at helping European teams understand where the technical expertise lay, rather than building real development skill localized in China.

“PLM enables [us] to reduce simple communication errors between Europe and China but it is not sufficient to ensure project success. Cooperation and [the] technical skills of suppliers are far more important for project lead time than just document sharing” (Project manager).

## 4.3. PLM enhances knowledge sharing effectiveness

## 4.3.1. Knowledge transfer effectiveness

The number of SPDP glitches decreased from an average of 10 by project to an average of 1 to 2 after PLM implementation (Table 8). Due to the distant locations of participants in the NPD context, numerous SPDP glitches were reported before PLM implementation, when most communication occurred via email, resulting in very high exchange volumes. Due to the lack of tracked communications, some tasks were performed using incorrect document versions, or the object was not shared with the appropriate project members. Many misunderstandings were attributed to outsourcing engineers located in China who had only partial information and knowledge of their projects. Knowledge exchange between European and Chinese teams were made dif<sup>fi</sup>cult when Europeans shared knowledge directly with Chinese suppliers, without including the Chinese support team.

An example of SPDP glitches is the communication of technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Before PLM implementation, technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations were shared late with the outsourcing engineer and with the supplier, via email. Consequently, there were delays because the supplier had only partial information at the beginning of the project. With the use of PLM, technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations were available earlier to the supplier and outsourcing engineers, which helped to integrate supplier-side technical constraints earlier.

## 4.3.2. Knowledge translation effectiveness

For glitches related to sticky knowledge, the impact of PLM implementation is less clear. Indeed, the reduction of sticky knowledge moved only from an average of six to four glitches per project (Table 8). In order to explain PLM impact on sticky knowledge situations, we present one example, the development of a new generation of iron with a new soleplate. A Chinese supplier was chosen based on their expertise in this range of products, and their reputation. The project team faced a problem of excess temperature on a component detected on the <sup>fi</sup>rst prototype. The overall product design required modi<sup>fi</sup>cation in order to improve air circulation to decrease the temperature on the targeted component. This modi<sup>fi</sup>cation had implications for the product styling, internal design (internal components aggregation), and supplier constraints (impacts on the industrialization process for supplier). The solution was problematic because technical solutions had impacts on the styling, but the styling department had dif<sup>fi</sup>culty understanding that no technical solutions were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that did not impact the styling. In order to reach an agreement, technical teams demonstrated technical requirements through the 3D viewer. The PLM web conferencing solution was used combined with the real time 3D visualizations in order to simulate modi<sup>fi</sup>cations on the design of the product. The PLM technology enabled making changes in 3D plans by the three project members associated via the web conferencing. Prior to extending the PLM implementation, dealing with real time complex problem solving situations with distant teams would have been impossible.

“In the preliminary design phase, we used the web conferencing tool quite frequently in order to create a common understanding on mutual constrains coming from styling, design, and industrialization. An example: we solved a complex problem due to a component temperature excess thanks to PLM” (Project manager).

## 5. Discussion

This case study allows us to understand to what extent a standardizing IT platform such as PLM supports knowledge sharing effectively in an NPD process which also requires some flexibility, thus answering the first research question. The study also identi<sup>fi</sup>es work practices, thus offering explanations for the reduction of glitches and improvements in knowledge transfer and translation effectiveness. In particular it provides 1) a better understanding of how transparency practices can be more effectively developed with PLM use, and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally how knowledge transfer is improved by enabling the sharing of representations through object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, visualization and process structuring, and 2) a better understanding of how PLM use and the boundary spanner's roles reinforce each other by allowing the outsourcing engineer to be more effective at managing semantic boundaries with the 3D viewer. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings respectively answer the second and third research questions relative to how PLM affects knowledge transfer and translation respectively.

Findings suggest that a standardizing technology such as PLM helps enhance knowledge translation such that it can also support boundary objects and thus be used in a <sup>fl</sup>exible way, provided that boundary spanners use it and work closely with suppliers, in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient manner. Thus, in contrast to knowledge transfer, knowledge translation requires more of a socio-technical adjustment that is embedded in organizational relationships and IT features. These features in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing effectiveness through knowledge sharing practices. For example, PLM features such as data uniqueness and integration, CAD viewer or work<sup>fl</sup>ows enable enhanced knowledge transparency. Moreover, the combination of PLM features use, (e.g. CAD viewer and web conferencing), and boundary spanners has a positive impact on knowledge translation (Fig. 1).

## 5.1. IT features promote greater standardization and transparency for knowledge transfer

In this study, knowledge transfer effectiveness is improved through greater standardization and transparency, supported by IT features. Prior to PLM, the templates and recommended practices were spread over different legacy systems, which did not permit an integrated view of the project development. Thus, in PLM, objects are available for all project members, along with the status of the object; the phase of the NPD process is also available for all project members. PLM integrates all templates and best practices for product development and project management in a unique solution which facilitates knowledge transfer effectiveness when there is adherence to recommended practices (Szulanski & Jensen, 2006). In PLM, the practice of centralizing product and project codi<sup>fi</sup>ed items in a unique database reinforces NPD standardization. The automation of NPD process rules through work<sup>fl</sup>ows and object codi<sup>fi</sup>cation rules facilitate knowledge transfer. This is especially important in inter-organizational con<sup>fi</sup>guration, as some objects are contractually de<sup>fi</sup>ned (Petersen et al., 2005). The combination of data and process transparency reduces the propensity to withhold knowledge among project members, but process structuring and work<sup>fl</sup>ows do not preclude information withholding between participants when the project has not yet reached a milestone. In addition, compared to ERP systems (Kallinikos, 2004), the standardization effect of

PLM does not seem as binding since part of the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation bears on new product or components that are not yet stable and mature. Due to the uncertainty inherent in NPD projects, there is a balance between the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation allowed by PLM features and human agency on the project. This <sup>fi</sup>nding echoes research results on ERP. Indeed ERPs tend to standardize business process practices. However there are still spaces for improvised learning and reinvention (Boudreau & Robey. 2005) and, despite new imposed constraints by the technology, organizational adjustments in use are found (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). For PLM the data uniqueness and integration of validated components through work<sup>fl</sup>ows do not necessarily mean that in the <sup>fi</sup>nal state such components will be part of the <sup>fi</sup>nal product. With PLM there is higher reversibility in actions and outcomes than in ERP. In sum our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that IT constrains activity with standardized processes rules and codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of templates. At the same time, these structuring constraints do not completely inhibit <sup>fl</sup>exibility which is critical to cope with uncertainty in preliminary NPD stages.

Overall, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that, despite the high costs, knowledge codi<sup>fi</sup>cation and communication network transparency are essential for project team communication effectiveness (Moenaert et al., 2000), Thus, we identify the impact of common codi<sup>fi</sup>cation practices (codi<sup>fi</sup>ed objects and common storage rules) and NPD process structuring practices – such as a list of key deliverables on milestones, tracking of object changes, generation of dashboards, and validation of milestones content – on knowledge transfer. With object visualization, these operational mechanisms lead to a more cohesive project and product representation. Previous literature (Moenaert et al., 2000) has only emphasized access and transparency without insisting on functions such as cohesiveness and 3D visualization. Cohesiveness is all the more important that, compared to CAD, PLM makes key objects accessible and used by a greater diversity of participants. Thus, PLM extends boundary spanning effects while enabling all NPD project members to share a common virtual representation of the future products thus detecting potential design problems earlier. Without the support of PLM technology, this would have been managed at a later stage, with additional costs.

Our results also offer insights on the role of IT to generate new forms of boundary spanning activity quali<sup>fi</sup>ed by Jonsson et al. (2009, p.243) as “use pattern boundaries”, “divisional boundary” and “contextual boundary”. First, similar to their paper, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that the introduction of a PLM has reduced the use pattern boundaries. While enhancing transparency, PLM clearly spans pattern boundaries while providing a unique centralized platform for product and project explicit knowledge and so facilitates data <sup>fl</sup>ow between project members. Secondly, PLM features participate to breaking divisional boundaries. Contrary to remote diagnostic systems where the diversity of actors involved in the use of the solution is limited, NPD project involve a wide variety of functions. The integrated perspective of the PLM solution diminishes information silos due to functional and technical specializations. Instead of following a functional expertise logic as in diagnostics systems, PLM proposes a cross functional perspective of explicit knowledge circulation. New connections between functional areas are automatically generated with the introduction of PLM solution, This does not solve all glitches and potential misunderstandings but, at a minimum, it helps reduce simple glitches.

## 5.2. The necessary combination of IT and boundary spanner for knowledge translation

However, Jonsson et al. (2009) of contextual boundaries is restrictive and only focuses on knowledge transfer issues whereas breaking contextual boundaries consists in bridging syntactic but also semantic gaps especially in international NPD con<sup>fi</sup>guration. In this NPD context, the PLM system is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to bridge contextual gaps. The local boundary spanner becomes crucial as he can use metaphors, speci<sup>fi</sup>c objects, and cultural cognitive preferences with Chinese suppliers to solve potentia misunderstandings.

The analysis of the use of PLM solution for NPD project shows that apart from use pattern, divisional and contextual boundaries. there is a fourth type: the credibility boundary. Contrary to Jonsson et al (2009) where the introduction of a remote diagnostic system reduced the technician's visits on site, in the PLM international NPD context, the outsourcing engineers spend more time visiting suppliers while exchanging and improving the operational development process with them. Indeed, PLM increases the knowledge of the designed product for all project members and fosters the credibility of outsourcing engineers for working closely with, and being helpful to, the suppliers. This point is particularly important in Chinese culture, where trust and saving face are fundamental in all types of relationships and can explain the failure of knowledge management systems (Young, Kuo, & Myers, 2012). Beyond what the literature has already described and analyzed (Argyres, 1999; Cramton, 2001), this case study suggests that an active trust development with the supplier (Child & Mollering, 2003) is facilitated by local outsourcing engineers and the PLM-enabled transparency practices. With PLM, these engineers feel and appear as more competent, trustworthy and can more easily engage in closer relationships. Our work shows that there is mutual reinforcement between PLM and the translation role of the outsourcing engineers. They do not need anyone to interact directly with the project manager to get the necessary information to manage daily relations with suppliers. After PLM implementation, outsourcing engineers have direct access to the project and product knowledge in real time. They can focus their efforts in ensuring knowledge translation with Chinese suppliers concerning language, priority management, technical problem interpretation, and con<sup>fl</sup>ict prevention.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that PLM use, combined with other practices, helped improve knowledge translation. Several examples showed that the use of CAD viewer was a necessary but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition to solve a technical problem. Indeed, an outsourcing engineer was needed to help interpret the implicit knowledge embodied in virtual mock ups. The ability to translate knowledge that can be appropriated depends on the location, and on cultural and language abilities of the boundary spanner that go beyond professiona competencies (Leonardi & Bailey, 2008).

PLM has dramatically changed boundary spanners' role. In Leonardi and Bailey (2008), US coordinators were not perceived as completely ensuring their boundary spanning role from the Indian engineers, because they did not have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient expertise. In our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, with PLM, the Chinese outsourcing engineers played an extensive knowledge brokering role because they had suf<sup>fi</sup>cient expertise to bridge technical, organizational and national cultural boundaries to translate contextual, situated knowledge. This improvement in knowledge translation is also related to material features of IT which affect boundary spanning in international NPD context. The work of outsourcing engineers is now much more mediated by product digital representations. This reduces communication impedance in projects (Tushman & Katz, 1980). Thus, PLM visualization features reduce noise in communication through the ability to ensure more complete and tightly coupled representation of virtual product (Yoo et al., 2006).

In international NPD projects, knowledge translation effectiveness results from complex socio-technical activities where human activity and IT resources are completely intertwined. If existing literature insists on the quasi unique human activity of knowledge translation through the boundary spanner (Hayes, 2001; Levina & Vaast, 2006), our results reveal the critical role of PLM technology for the change of boundary spanners' role. With PLM. outsourcing engineers spend less time to transfer knowledge to the supplier's team (Merminod et al., 2009). Practically, they can spend more time on value added tasks such as the control of the project advancement, the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of the understanding of NPD project context and rules and the solving of complex problems that involve knowledge translation. With features such as the 3D viewer and web conferencing this can be done effectively while leaving also space for trading zones and <sup>fl</sup>exibility.

## 6. Conclusion

This study has examined how Product Lifecycle Management (PLM) technology contributes to NPD knowledge transfer and translation in an international context. While previous researchers have found that PLM and similar technologies can have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive impacts on NPD (Andersson, Lindgren, & Henfridsson, 2008; Banker, Bardhan, & Asdemir, 2006; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006), most studies have examined the process when the level of uncertainty is high due to radical innovations (Argyres. 1999: Baxter, 2008; Boland et al., 2007). This study investigates PLM support for incremental innovation in an international con<sup>fi</sup>guration with Chinese-European interactions that faced signi<sup>fi</sup>cant knowledge sharing challenges due to important national culture and inter-organizational differences. In this environment, knowledge translation issues are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage without the use of PLM by a local boundary spanner in charge of managing professional, organizational, and national cultural gaps. Our work shows that there is mutual reinforcement between PLM and local boundary spanners. IT use has reinforced Chinese outsourcing engineers vis à vis the suppliers, and has increased their role in project management. Additionally, their use of PLM reinforces its legitimacy and their own credibility for Chinese suppliers. To further this research, a comparison of projects across different companies or implementation conditions, such as supplier integration, might shed additional light on these results. We call for further research in order to understand under which circumstances and how, with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cultural differences between organizations, PLM and other relevant IT and organizational arrangements can increase knowledge sharing effectiveness in new product development.

Despite the challenges and constraints presented by the context studied here, such as codi<sup>fi</sup>cation effort and limited ergonomy of the PLM interface used, PLM successfully supported numerous relationships and enabled new product development with Chinese suppliers at a considerably higher level than did previous attempts. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that this was partly due to PLM's capacity for reducing the number of problems through its features improving transparency precisely in this context. However, this came up with unintended consequences, such as the positive shift in the boundary spanner roles from the project leader to the outsourcing engineer, which, in turn, reinforced the credibility of the latter and the legitimacy of the use of the system with the Chinese supplier. In the end the system delivered its promises. But, had there been no local boundary spanner, PLM implementation would have had probably very different outcomes.
