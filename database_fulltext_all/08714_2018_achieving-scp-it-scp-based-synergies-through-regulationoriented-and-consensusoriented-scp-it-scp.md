# Achieving IT‐based synergies through regulationoriented and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities

## Abstract

This study aims at exploring the IT governance capabilities that enable organizations to achieve IT‐based synergies. Following existing work on the contextualization of theories and drawing on the resource‐based view of the firm (RBV), we develop an RBV of IT‐based synergies in two steps. First, we adopt existing context‐specific constructs and relationships from prior work on IT governance capabilities, IT relatedness, and synergies to develop a preliminary contextualization of the RBV. Second, to further refine our theoretical framework, we conduct an exploratory field study that includes interviews with 26 CIOs and other IT executives from 21 multibusiness firms. Our findings suggest that IT governance capabilities lead to IT‐based synergies through IT relatedness and business process relatedness. We found regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities (IT roles and IT processes) to increase IT relatedness, while consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities (IT groups and relational capabilities) had a positive effect on business process relatedness. Our results suggest that, in isolation, IT and business process relatedness lead to IT cost synergies, while collectively enabling ITinduced business synergies. Our study is among the first to treat IT relatedness as an endogenous construct and to explicitly integrate business process relatedness into the IT governance domain. Our context‐specific decomposition of IT governance capabilities helps to better explain their links to IT and business process relatedness. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the tension between IT‐based synergies and business‐IT alignment. Decisionmakers are guided in developing IT governance capabilities to achieve IT‐based synergies.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

It is almost a truism that digitization leads to an increasing clock speed of industries, requiring firms to quickl react to customer needs (El Sawy & Pavlou, 2008; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). At the same time, digitization requires firms to break down silos and act as a unified whole to create value for customers based on integrated services and data (Ross, Beath, & Sebastian, 2017; Singh & Hess, 2017). Thus, the long‐recognized trade‐off between differentiation and integration (L awrence & Lorsch. 1967) is even more relevant today because of the ubiquity of IT: On the one hand, IT needs to be highly aligned with the local business requirements across the firm to ensure differentiation (Tallon, 2007). On the other hand, IT needs to enable firms to achieve synergies and create additional value for customers (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005; Tanriverdi, 2006). Whereas the question of how firms can increase business‐IT alignment has been studied extensively (see Gerow, Grover, Thatcher, & Roth, 2014, for a meta‐analysis), considerably less is known about the question of how firms can achieve IT‐based synergies.

Prior scholarly work on the nature of IT‐based synergies has mostly drawn on the resource‐based view of the firm (RBV) (e.g., Tanriverdi, 2006). The RBV sees firms as collections of assets and capabilities, which provide value if they enable firms to implement strategies that improve effectiveness or efficiency (Barney, 1991; Mata, Fuerst, & Barney, 1995). An asset is anything tangible or intangible firms can use to produce and offer goods or services (Sanchez, 2004). Capabilities are the processes and skills needed to make use of assets for creating value (Daft, 2012; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Prior work has studied the link between IT assets and synergies by referring to IT relatedness—i.e., “the extent to which a […] firm uses common IT resources […] across its business units” (Tanriverdi, 2006, p. 59). IT relatedness may lead to synergies in terms of the joint costs/returns of multiple business being lower/higher than the sum of the business units' individual costs/returns (Farjoun, 1994; Milgrom & Roberts, 1995; Robins & Wiersema, 1995).

Although little is known about the capabilities needed to achieve IT relatedness and IT‐based synergies, prior work points to the key role of IT governance (Tanriverdi, 2006). In particular, prior work has consistently mentioned the potential for achieving synergies as a key contingency factor for designing IT governance (Brown & Magill, 1998; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Weill & Ross, 2004). More generally, it is widely acknowledged that the goal of IT governance is “to encourage desirable behavior in the use of IT” (Weill, 2004, p. 3), including its potentially desirable related ness. Yet our knowledge on how IT governance helps achieve synergies is so far inconclusive. Prior work linking IT governance and IT value has mostly focused on the effect of IT governance capabilities on business‐IT alignment (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Wu, Straub, & Liang, 2015)—i.e., “the degree to which the IT mission, objectives, and plans support and are supported by the business mission, objectives, and plans” (Reich & Benbasat, 1996, p. 56). Although business‐IT alignment was found to contribute to IT value in various contexts (Bradley, Pratt, Byrd, Outlay, & Wynn, 2012; Buchwald, Urbach, & Ahlemann, 2014; Chang, Wang, & Chiu, 2008), the alignment of IT with different business requirements on a local, business unit level may often counteract organization‐wide goals, such as IT‐based synergies (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Therefore, it is not clear how IT governance capabilities can contribute to IT‐based synergies. Moreover, prior work has found that IT‐based synergies are often achieved through the relatedness of different, complementary assets (Tanriverdi, 2006). This raises the questions of what assets need to be related to achieve IT-based synergies and how the relatedness of these complementary assets is linked to IT governance capabilities. Against this backdrop, the goal of this study is to explain how IT governance capabilities help achieve ITbased synergies.

We draw on recent advances in theorizing that stress the importance of contextualizing theories in the management and IS disciplines (Hong, Chan, Thong, Chasalow, & Dhillon, 2013; Johns, 2006). In particular, we draw on the RBV and the widely acknowledged relationship between capabilities, assets, and value (e.g., Barney, 1991). We contextualize this general theoretical perspective to explain the effect of IT governance capabilities on IT‐based synergies. Specifically, following recommendations of extant literature, we decompose core constructs and add explanatory variables in two sequential steps (Hong et al., 2013). First, based on existing literature, we deduce a preliminary RBV of IT‐based synergies. Second, we conduct an exploratory interview field study in 21 multibusiness firms, relying on a unique access to Chief Information Officers (CIOs) and other high‐profile I executives. The goal of this second step is to refine our literature‐based preunderstanding through developin and adding context‐specific constructs and relationships between constructs (Hong et al., 2013). Specifically, w develop and add regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities, consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities and business process relatedness, resulting in a theoretical framework that reflects our contextualized RBV of IT‐based synergies.

We expect our study to contribute to the discussion on IT‐based synergies, IT governance capabilities, and IT relatedness (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004; Tanriverdi, 2005; Tanriverdi, 2006; Wu et al., 2015). Our exploratory analysis unveils how firms can achieve IT relatedness and business process relatedness through regulation‐oriented and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities, thereby extending prior work on IT governance capabilities and business‐IT alignment. We also provide evidence for the relationship between I and business process relatedness and IT‐based synergies. Our study is among the first to follow the guidelines for considering context in IS research that were recently put forth (Hong et al., 2013). In line with prior work on qualitative research (Benbasat, Goldstein, & Mead, 1987; Darke, Shanks, & Broadbent, 1998; Keutel, Michalik & Richter, 2014), our study exemplifies that qualitative research is particularly well‐suited to contextualize general theories. The results of this study provide decision‐makers with guidance on establishing the IT governance capabilities needed to achieve resource relatedness and IT‐based synergies. We expect insights related to business process relatedness and how it can be achieved through consensus‐oriented governance capabilities to be particularly valuable for practitioners.

In the following section, we develop our literature‐based preunderstanding of IT governance capabilities and IT‐based synergies, which reflects our initial contextualization of the RBV. We start by introducing the RBV and discussing the contextualization of general theories in the IS discipline. We then introduce prior work on IT‐based synergies, IT relatedness, and IT governance. Next, the design of our empirical study—an exploratory interview fiel study with participants from 21 multibusiness firms—is discussed. Subsequently, the empirical findings are presented, resulting in three propositions and a refined contextualized theoretical framework. Finally, we discuss implications fo research and practice and limitations of our study.

## 2 | BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | The resource‐based view and the role of context

Firms strive for the creation of competitive advantages (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997). The RBV of the firm assumes that competitive advantages stem from tangible and intangible resources that are heterogeneously distributed among firms and that are rare and valuable (Barney, 1991). Resources are valuable if they allow for implementing strategies that improve the organization's effectiveness or efficiency (Barney, 1991; Mata et al., 1995). If, in addition, resources are neither imitable nor substitutable, then competitive advantages building on these resources have the potential to b long‐lasting (Mata et al., 1995). Focusing on managerial actions that enable such resource‐based competitive advantages, extant literature distinguished between assets and capabilities (Itami & Roehl, 1991; Teece et al., 1997) Assets are “anything tangible or intangible the firm can use in its processes for creating, producing and offering its products (goods or services) to a market” (Sanchez, 2004, p. 519). Capabilities, in turn, are “repeatable patterns of actions in the use of assets to create, produce, and/or offer products to a market” (Wade & Hulland, 2004, p. 109) that are reflected by processes, skills, and managerial capabilities (Daft, 2012). In other words, through capabilities, firms are able to create value utilizing their assets.

The RBV can be seen as a grand theory that postulates to be valid in all organizational contexts. However, in a 10‐year retrospective on the resource‐based view, Barney cautioned that

a grand, unified resource‐based theory may not be all that helpful. Rather, what may be more helpful is to understand that the resource‐based view can be applied in several different ways, and that the way it should be applied depends mostly on the empirical context of the application. (Barney, 2001, p. 649)

This is line with recent advances in theorizing in the management and IS disciplines that reinforce the value of contextualizing theories (Hong et al., 2013; Johns, 2006). According to Hong et al. (2013), theory contextualization involves three steps: (1) the identification of a theory to be contextualized, (2) the contextualization of the theor by adding or removing core constructs, and (3) the refinement of the contextualization by decomposing core constructs and by adding contextual factors, e.g., as antecedents or dependent variables. Hong et al. (2013) also provide a number of guidelines for contextualizing theories. For example, the authors recommend to select general theories for contextualization, such as the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) or the RBV. The authors also note that, in many cases, a certain level of contextualization may already be available in the literature. Such existing contextualization should be adopted and further refined. As another guideline, the authors urge researchers to thoroughly evaluate the context under investigation. In particular, it is noted that “the identification of context‐specific factors could be based on past research […] and/or in‐depth analysis […] using qualitative methods such as interviews” (Hong et al., 2013, p. 119)

Existing IS research has extensively drawn on the RBV to examine the capabilities that generally help increase the business value of IT—i.e., “the organizational performance impacts of information technology at both the intermediate process level and the organization‐wide level” (Melville, Kraemer, & Gurbaxani, 2004, p. 287). For example, Mithas et al. studied the impact of information management capabilities on customer‐focused, financial, human resource, and organizational effectiveness results (Mithas, Ramasubbu, & Sambamurthy, 2011). In the context of IT‐based synergies, Tanriverdi drew on a resource‐based perspective to study IT relatedness (Tanriverdi, 2005; Tanriverdi, 2006). In the context of IT governance, prior research studied the effect of IT governance capabilities on business‐IT alignment (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Wu et al., 2015). In what follows, we build on this work on IT relatedness and IT governance capabilities to develop our preliminary RBV of IT‐based synergies, which we further contextualize in a subsequent step based on our empirical study.

## 2.2 | Cross‐unit synergies and IT relatedness

Previous literature has examined IT‐based synergies by referring to the relatedness of IT resources, including the relatedness of IT infrastructure, IT strategy‐making processes, IT vendor management processes, and IT human resource management processes (Tanriverdi, 2006). IT relatedness was found to lead to IT cost synergies (Tanriverdi 2006), which can be defined as subadditive IT expenditures, such as reduced expenditures for infrastructure and software applications acquisition, development, maintenance, and support services (based on Farjoun, 1994; Robin & Wiersema, 1995; Tanriverdi, 2005; Tanriverdi, 2006). Tanriverdi (2006) found these cost synergies to be particularly strong and long‐lasting if different types of resources were related across business units. This suggests complementary effects among the relatedness of different resources in that the synergies resulting from the joint relatedness of different types of resources exceed the synergies resulting from the relatedness of individual resource (Farjoun, 1994; Robins & Wiersema, 1995)

The relatedness of resources may not only lead to cost synergies but may also enable business synergies (Campbell & Goold, 1998). Business synergies have been referred to as “the value that is created, over time, by the sum of the businesses together relative to what, their value would be separately" (Martin & Fisenhardt, 2001 p. H2). Similar IT resources across organizational units may facilitate knowledge sharing and knowledge exploitation, which was suggested to enable business synergies (Manrodt & Vitasek, 2004; Martin & Eisenhardt, 2001; Tanriverdi 2005) More generally firms may achieve higher innovation rates and greater new product success through organizational integration (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005; Troy, Hirunyawipada, & Paswan, 2008). The relatedness of IT resources, such as organization‐wide ERP systems, may contribute to organizational integration (Barki &

Pinsonneault, 2005). Hence, prior research suggests that resource relatedness, and in particular IT relatedness, may help achieve IT cost synergies and IT‐induced business synergies.

## 2.3 | IT governance

IT governance has become a pivotal topic in practice over the last decades (Brown & Grant, 2005; Weill, 2004). This trend was fueled by the enactment of the Sarbanes‐Oxley Act in 2002, which urged organizations toward establishing governance frameworks that explicitly consider IT issues (Brown & Grant, 2005). Although often originally established for compliance reasons, the question of how IT governance can provide value has quickly moved to the foreground (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Weill, 2004). To address this challenge, many organizations rely on best practic frameworks, such as COBIT (Simonsson & Johnson, 2006). Although these frameworks offer templates that guid practitioners in designing IT governance structures and processes, they rely on industry practices and do not aim at explaining antecedents or implications of IT governance.

On the other hand, a large body of scholarly literature on IT governance has emerged throughout the last decades (Brown & Grant, 2005; Webb, Pollard, & Ridley, 2006). Early work identified different forms of IT governance, such as centralized or decentralized responsibility for IT (e.g., Jelinek, 1977; Wetherbe, 1988) as well as federal IT governance modes, where responsibilities are shared between business and IT management (Boynton & Zmud, 1987; Zmud, Boynton, & Jacobs, 1986). Later work focused on contingency factors that explain the salience of different governance modes in organizations, such as the potential for synergies, organizational strategy, firm size, or industry (e.g., Brown & Magill, 1998; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993). More recent research synthesized previous results into comprehensive IT governance frameworks (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Weill, 2004; Weill & Ross, 2004 Willcocks, Feeny, & Olson, 2006).

Although providing important insights, the developed models are not explicitly dealing with the mechanisms though which IT governance is associated with IT value (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009). In an attempt to overcome this gap, previous research examined IT governance capabilities, including the capabilities to institutionalize IT roles, IT groups, and IT processes as well as relational capabilities (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004). IT roles refer to formally defined positions that are tasked with managing the IT function (Brown, 1999; De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004). Examples are CIOs, IT program managers, or IT account managers (Lim, Stratopoulos, & Wirjanto, 2012). IT groups are formally institutionalized collectives of actors that meet regularly to manage the IT function and to ensure coordination within and between organiza tional units (Brown, 1999; De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004). Examples include IT strategy committees, IT steering committees, or IT‐related advisory boards (Huang, Zmud, & Price, 2010).\* IT processes hav been defined as the “formalization and institutionalization of strategic IT decision‐making or monitoring procedures” (Peterson, 2004, p. 15). Examples include IT risk management procedures or IT investment prioritization (Xue, Liang, & Boulton, 2008). Relational capabilities refer to “the active participation of, and collaborative relation ships among, corporate executives, IT management, and business management” (Peterson, 2004, p. 15). Instantiations of relational capabilities include informal meetings between key stakeholders from business and IT management and knowledge management and communication addressing IT topics (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peppard, 2001; Peterson, 2004).

## 2.4 | Summary: Literature‐based contextualization of the resource‐based view

Synthesizing the above discussed literature, we develop a preliminary contextualization of the RBV that reflects th specifics of IT‐based synergies (see Figure 1). Definitions of the constructs are provided in Table 1. Our preliminar framework reflects recent recommendations for contextualizing IS theories (Hong et al., 2013). Specifically, we draw on the RBV as a general theory and adopt existing work that decomposed constructs and added context‐specific factors. Based on prior work on IT governance, we decompose the general IT governance capabilities construct into the capabilities to institutionalize IT roles, IT groups, and IT processes as well as relational capabilities. Borrowing from prior work on synergies and IT relatedness, we decompose the general value construct into IT cost synergies and IT‐induced business synergies and add IT relatedness as a context‐specific mediator. Although prior work allows us to deduce the preliminary framework shown in Figure 1, it is not yet clear how the context‐specific constructs are linked to each other. Specifically, prior work has focused on the influence of IT governance capabilities on business‐IT alignment. However, business‐IT alignment and IT‐based synergies may reflect contradicting goals, and it is not clear how IT governance capabilities contribute to resource relatedness (see question mark 1 in Figure 1). Moreover, prior work has stressed that synergies often depend on the relatedness of complementary resources. However, it is not clear what resources complement IT relatedness (see question mark 2) and how resource relatedness contrib utes to IT‐based synergies (see question mark 3). Following recommendations to contextualize theories (Hong et al., 2013), we further refine our contextualized RBV of IT‐based synergies through a qualitative field study.

## 3 | QUALITATIVE FIELD STUDY: RESEARCH DESIGN

To develop our RBV of IT‐based synergies, we conducted an exploratory qualitative field study (Guillemette & Paré, 2012) in 21 multibusiness organizations that was guided by the literature‐based contextualization shown in Figure 1. We considered large multibusiness organizations to be particularly well‐suited to develop a contextualized RBV of IT‐based synergies: The trade‐off between differentiation and integration and the related challenge to effectively and efficiently use IT are particularly evident in multibusiness firms. On the one hand, hardware infrastructure, software applications, and IT management processes are often tailored to the idiosyncrasies of business units. On the other hand, there is often a significant potential for leveraging IT‐based cross‐unit synergies (Brown & Magill, 1998; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999). For multibusiness firms, the ability to realize IT‐based cross‐unit synergies may therefore be a key differentiator (Campbell & Goold, 1998). The following subsections discuss the process of data selection, collection, and analysis. Appendix A provides more detailed information on our methodological approach.

## 3.1 | Data selection

Table 2 gives an overview of the analyzed organizations and their annual revenues—or balance sheet totals in case of financial institutions—as well as the respective interview partners. Given the size, importance, and reputation of the studied organizations, we refrained from describing the organizations in more detail to maintain anonymity. The 21 multibusiness organizations were selected purposefully. The importance of IT governance capabilities as well as ITbased synergies increases with firm size. Thus, to ensure comparability of findings, it appeared reasonable to focus on large multibusiness firms. Given their large size, most organizations in our sample had acquired smaller firms in their history or were founded through a large merger. Notably, none of the selected organizations went through a large merger or acquisition in its recent past, which increases comparability across the organizations. Although the particular distribution of responsibilities for IT varied across firms, all analyzed organizations had generally implemented a federa mode of IT governance—i.e., business and IT management were both responsible for IT decisions to a certain extent

Despite being active in multiple businesses, many large corporations focus on certain industries, calling for a sam ple of organizations from different sectors to obtain generalizable results. To increase comparability across firms and control for cultural factors, we focused on organizations headquartered in the same European country. For data collection, all firms traded on the national blue chip stock market index as well as not publicly traded firms of comparabl size were approached. We were careful to collect enough data to ensure variance in the study's core constructs, i.e., to apply theoretical replication logic (Yin, 2013).

In line with our research focus, we aimed at interviewing knowledgeable high‐level executives. In particular, we considered members of the organizations' CIO office or organizationally close functions as desirable interview partners because a CIO perspective could be expected to provide deep insights into both IT governance capabilities and IT‐based synergies. Thus, we contacted the CIO offices of all selected organizations. The first written inquiry led to the participation of six organizations. After data were collected at these six sites, the organizations that had not yet reacted to the first written inquiry were reapproached via phone, resulting in an ongoing process of data collection.

In total, we conducted 26 expert interviews. In eight of the selected organizations, we were able to interview the CIO. In the other firms, other high‐profile IT executives such as division CIOs, heads of IT strategy, or heads of the CIO office were interviewed. These interview partners were recommended by the CIO offices as being knowledgeable regarding the constructs of interest. Four firms sent more than one interview partner. The interview partners had an average tenure at the organizations of 12 years; most interview partners were very experienced in their respective organization. One notable exception is the interviewee at RETAIL, who was appointed head of IT around one year prior to the interview. Given the interesting and relevant case and the knowledge the head of IT already had about the challenges at RETAIL (s/he was hired specifically to introduce IT governance capabilities and had talked to all stakeholders), we included this case in our sample.

## 3.2 | Data collection

Data were gathered via semistructured interviews that followed a preformulated interview guideline while leaving enough room for ad hoc modifications whenever interesting and relevant themes emerged (Yin, 2013). Data gathering and analysis evolved over time (Yin, 2013), which is reflected by the initial and later versions of the interview guideline (see Appendix B). The initial guideline was based on our preliminary framework and focused on more open and genera questions regarding IT governance capabilities, IT relatedness, and IT‐based synergies. The later version more directly reflected the constructs and relationships that emerged from our analysis

Except for RETAIL, all interviews were performed in person and took place on‐site. Most conversations were held in the national language and had an average length of 90 minutes, varying between 60 and 180 minutes. Except for TELCO and GOVBANK, we recorded and transcribed all interviews.<sup>†</sup> All transcripts were sent to the case organizations for inspection and were returned with minor or no modifications.

In addition to the expert interviews, most case organizations provided access to internal documents, such as IT governance manuals and guidelines, presentations, organizational charts, or quality manuals. These documents complemented the insights from the conversations and increased the internal validity of the study (Yin, 2013). Th interview transcripts together with the provided documents resulted in more than 130 000 words of qualitative data

After data collection and analysis were completed, we presented our results to approximately 50 participant (CIOs and other senior IT managers) of a workshop we organized. This workshop was attended by participants and non‐participants of our study. The reactions and discussions increased our confidence in the internal and externa validity of our RBV of IT‐based synergies as well as in the value of our insights for decision‐makers (Rosemann & Vessey, 2008).

## 3.3 | Data analysis

Data analysis began with coding the a priori constructs of our literature‐based contextualization of the RBV—i.e., we coded for IT governance capabilities (IT roles, IT groups, IT processes, and relational capabilities), IT relatedness, I cost synergies, and IT‐induced business synergies using the software tool NVivo (version 10). Based on the definitions of the a priori constructs and our empirical data, we developed indicators for low, moderate, and high values of thes constructs (see Tables 1, C1, and D1).

While coding our data, we were looking for other dimensions of resource relatedness in our context (see question mark 2 in Figure 1). That is, borrowing from coding approaches in grounded theory (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Urquhart Lehmann, & Myers, 2010), we followed an open coding approach to identify additional dimensions of resource relatedness, In this process, business process relatedness emerged as an important context-specific factor that appeared to be related to IT‐based synergies and IT governance capabilities. Going back and forth between our data and existing literature (i.e., through pattern clarification; Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994), we developed a definition of business process relatedness (see Section 4) and related indicators for low, moderate, and high levels of business process relatedness that enabled us to code the entire qualitative material (see Table C2).

To increase the reliability of our coding, empirical data were redundantly coded by two authors (one of the coders did not participate in the interviews), resulting in an intercoder reliability of 78% (Holsti, 1969). In case of diverging assessments, the text fragments were discussed by the authors. As most of the differences were based on misunderstandings or a different reading of text fragments by the author who did not participate in the interviews, all conflicting assessments could be resolved easily.

After this variable‐oriented strategy to identify and code constructs, we moved to a case‐oriented strategy (Miles & Huberman, 1994) to identify relationships between constructs. We did so by relying on Eisenhardt's (1989) strategy to examine “within‐group similarities coupled with intergroup differences” (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 540). We started this process with our dependent variable IT‐based synergies with its two dimensions IT cost synergies and IT‐induced business synergies. This analysis revealed that those firms that enjoyed high IT‐induced business synergies tended to be high on both IT relatedness and business process relatedness, whereas firms with lower IT‐induced business synergies tended to be lower on one or both of the two types of resource relatedness. Thus, high IT‐induced business synergies seemed to require both high IT relatedness and high business process relatedness, and a lower level of either IT or business process relatedness seemed to constrain IT‐induced business synergies (see Figure 5). The analysis also showed that firms with high IT cost synergies were high on IT relatedness or business process relatedness or both, whereas those firms with lower IT cost synergies had neither high IT nor high business process relatedness. This pointed to independent positive effects of IT and business process relatedness on IT cost synergies (see Figures 3B and 4B). In a next step we examined the cases within the respective groups for plausible explanations for these observed regularities (Eisenhardt, 1989). We present these explanations in Section 4 when developing the respective propositions

We followed a similar approach for the left side of our theoretical framework—i.e., the link between IT governance capabilities and resource relatedness. Eisenhardt's (1989) within‐group and intergroup analyses suggested that IT relatedness and business process relatedness increase with higher levels of IT governance capabilities. Beyond this rather general observation, however, within‐group and intergroup analyses did not reveal deeper insights regarding distinct effects of particular IT governance capabilities. Yet, from our knowledge of the case organizations and our acquaintance with the interview data, we expected that considering the longitudinal nature of the case narratives would reveal more fine‐grained insights: Although firms that had developed IT governance capabilities had done so over a certain period of time, at the time of our interviews, there was a tendency for firms to have similar levels of all IT governance capabilities—thus concealing effects of distinct IT governance capabilities when conducting within‐group and intergroup comparisons. Therefore, to distil possible distinct effects of the different IT governance capabilities, we further explored the cases in more detail.

We did so by relying on two techniques from grounded theory: axial coding and memo writing (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Urquhart et al., 2010). In this process, we heavily relied on the retrospective nature of our data (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) and also on causal statements by interviewees (Dibbern, Winkler, & Heinzl, 2008; Miles & Huberman, 1994). This analysis revealed that IT relatedness was rather achieved through establishing regulations in the organization, whereas business process relatedness hinged on consensus among actors. The analysis also revealed that firms tended to establish regulations by means of IT roles and IT processes and achieve consensus with the help of IT groups and relational capabilities. We therefore decomposed IT governance capabilities int regulation‐oriented capabilities (IT roles and IT processes) and consensus‐oriented capabilities (IT groups and relational capabilities) (see also Figure 2). Again, the explanations for the identified relationships are outlined in the respective part of Section 4

As it is common in interview studies, some interviews were more insightful with regard to the effects of regulation‐oriented and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities than others—mostly because of the interviewees varying awareness of events happening in the past and the varying explicitness and richness of the provided information. To counteract the consequences of being “overly influenced by the vividness” of some interviews or by “more elite respondents” (such as CIOs in our case) (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 540), we “compare[d] systematically the emergent frame with the evidence from each case in order to assess how well or poorly it fits with case data” (Eisenhardt, 1989 p. 541). We did so with the help of the data displays (Miles & Huberman, 1994) shown in the paper (see Figures 3A and 4A). Although the relationships between regulation‐oriented and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities and IT and business process relatedness did not emerge from these data displays (see above), they helped us to prob the identified relationships (Yin, 2013). Based on the data displays, we were unable to falsify the identified relationships, which increased our confidence in the propositions

## 4 | RESULTS

This section presents the results of our interview field study and the development of our refined RBV of IT‐based syn ergies (shown in Figure 2). As can be seen in Figure 2, we decomposed the IT governance capabilities construct into the two novel context‐specific factors regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities. We also added business process relatedness as a context‐specific mediator. The context‐specific decomposition and the addition of context‐specific factors helped us to “make the relationships between the constituent dimensions of core constructs and dependent variables clearer” (Hong et al., 2013, p. 116). Specifically, we pro pose that IT relatedness mediates the relationship between regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and IT cost synergies (Proposition 1), that business process relatedness mediates the relationship between consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities and IT cost synergies (Proposition 2), and that IT relatedness and business process relatedness jointly enable IT‐induced business synergies (Proposition 3).

We present the results of our empirical analysis and theorizing effort in two ways. First, to provide vivid empirical accounts, we substantiate the theoretical relationships with brief narratives and quotes from organizations that form polar examples regarding the proposed links (Langley, 1999; Yin, 2013)—i.e., high and low values for the respective constructs. Second, to show that the set of propositions holds across our sample of organizations, we show data displays for each proposition (Figures 3-5). The results of the coding for all constructs are provided in Table D1.

## 4.1 | The effect of regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities on IT cost synergies

A subset of the IT governance capabilities identified in previous literature led to IT cost synergies through IT related ness. Specifically, IT roles and IT processes led to more common IT resources across an organization. As opposed to IT groups and relational capabilities that put more emphasis on finding consensus among stakeholders, IT roles and IT processes emphasize clear accountabilities and rules for IT decision‐making. Based on prior work, we refer to IT roles and IT processes that clarify accountabilities, decision rights, and decision procedures to encourage desirable behaviour in the use of IT as regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004; Weill, 2004). Next, we present empirical evidence for the effect of the two identified regula tion‐oriented IT governance capabilities on IT cost synergies via IT relatedness. Figure 3 shows the relationships between regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities—measured as the average value coded for IT roles and IT processes—and IT relatedness (Figure 3A), as well as between IT relatedness and IT cost synergies for al studied organizations (Figure 3B)

## 4.1.1 | Regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and IT relatedness

The institutionalization of clear roles for IT‐related decisions, such as a powerful CIO office or IT decision‐makers in the business units, increased IT relatedness. Because of the personal accountabilities associated with clear IT roles decision‐makers were more likely to look for cost savings through cross‐unit synergies and have an organization‐wid optimization in mind.

The example of PROPERTY illustrates how the institutionalization of IT roles led to a high degree of IT related ness. Reflecting on the historic role of IT at PROPERTY, the head of the CIO office commented that “the original state in the business units was a typical candy shop situation. But a simple withdrawal of decision rights would not have worked. We need the business units to be involved in the redesign of the software stack.” Interestingly, the primary way to address this challenge was not to establish groups and committees for horizontal coordination, but rather to define strong IT roles in the business units. “I need strong promoters in the business units. These managers need to understand the daily routines in the business units and they need to know how to negotiate with the local managers. If they are weak, IT governance won't work. […] We came to understand one thing: Establishing such a proces bottom‐up is impossible. These mechanisms need to be implemented top‐down, promoted by the right people” (head of CIO office).

## 4.1.2 | IT relatedness and IT cost synergies

By using similar hardware and software landscapes across business units, the studied organizations benefited from lower costs for purchasing or developing software and for providing maintenance and support services. For example, SUPPLY “realized huge synergies through global licensing and pooled procurement [...]. Achieving these synergies would have never been possible if the local departments [were still acting] independently.” Similarly, the head of the CIO office at PROPERTY reported efficiency gains associated with IT relatedness The interviewee stated that in this context, efficiency means “decreased costs of running our IT and good maintainability.”

Whereas high levels of regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities resulted in IT relatedness and IT cost synergies, IT relatedness and IT cost synergies were limited in the absence of regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities. For example, at CAR—a large automotive manufacturer—IT governance had not been a significant topic. The company had only very few and rather weak IT roles and IT processes in place—i.e., regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities were lacking. As a result, IT relatedness was low at CAR. Several business units were still running their own hardware and applications. Most projects were approached individually and independent of each other. While the IT department was introducing an SAP solution for logistics and order management at the time of our interview, the business units were planning own approaches and solutions for HR, finance, and management accounting. Consequently, only low IT cost synergies were reported at CAR. For example, CAR's division CIO stated that “we have three [different] SAP systems [running, doing the same thing] and wanted to harmonize these systems, but the business side was not positive. [...] This would have saved us two thirds of our IT costs.”

Taken together, our analysis suggests the following mediated relationship:

Proposition 1. IT relatedness mediates the positive effect of regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities (i.e., IT roles and IT processes) on IT cost synergies.

## 4.2 | The effect of consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities on IT cost synergies

IT governance capabilities also contributed to IT cost synergies in a second important way: IT governance capabilities not only influenced the relatedness of IT resources but also of business processes. A business process is a “set o logically related tasks performed to achieve a defined business outcome” (Davenport & Short, 1990, p. 12). Based on this definition and on previous literature on different types of resource relatedness (e.g., Chatterjee & Wernerfelt, 1991; Farjoun, 1994; Tanriverdi, 2006), we refer to business process relatedness as the degree to which sequences o tasks to achieve business goals are similar across different business units. In our sample of multibusiness firms, business process relatedness was associated with certain IT governance capabilities: IT groups and relationa capabilities led to increased business process relatedness, which in turn resulted in IT cost synergies. Given that both IT groups (e.g., IT steering committees) and relational capabilities (e.g., informal meetings with intensive communica tion) help achieve agreement between among stakeholders regarding IT decisions, we refer to this subset of capabil ities as consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities. More formally, we define consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities as IT groups and relational capabilities that enable horizontal coordination and aim at achieving agreement between business and IT management (based on Brown, 1999; De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004) Figure 4 shows the link between consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities—measured as the average value coded for IT groups and relational capabilities—and business process relatedness (Figure 4A), as well as between business process relatedness and IT cost synergies (Figure 4B)

## 4.2.1 | Consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities and business process relatedness

Business process relatedness was achieved through institutionalizing teams and committees for coordination and decision‐making—i.e., through IT groups. By means of IT groups, the studied multibusiness firms ensured that both business and IT managers were involved when important IT decisions were made. The discussions within these groups about the ability of particular systems to map different business processes made business managers aware of the affordances of IT systems and of potential process enhancements. Also, collaborative decision‐making within institutionalized groups helped the studied organizations ensure that IT management was not bypassed when business processes were changed. In fact, in many cases, IT management was one of few, if not the only unit within the firms that was able to overlook the entire company regarding the applied business processes. Moreover, through interacting with IT and other business managers within institutionalized groups, business managers learned about best practices for business processes, thus enhancing business process relatedness.

The example of HITECH, a market leader in various industries that had a long‐lasting tradition in engineering, illustrates this link. HITECH had engaged in the topic of IT governance almost 20 years ago by defining I processes and roles. This resulted in a high degree of IT relatedness. However, as the CIO of HITECH explained cross‐unit IT cost synergies could still not be fully leveraged. “IT governance assured that [HITECH] was running only SAP on a unified hardware, but the business units were defining their processes themselves, and gradually the SA R/3 was not standard anymore, but merely a platform for various highly customized proprietary solutions [... which] basically represent very expensive tailored software solutions.” Therefore, the CIO office refined IT governance wit a special emphasis on consensus‐oriented capabilities. The CIO explicated: “It is not only about decision power or even about enforcing unpopular decisions. Often, it is simply about knowing what is possible with our running solu tions; in the past, this knowledge was often missing in the business units, leading to a process design that enforced unneeded new software applications. If a department has a request, we first analyze if we can map this request t our platform. If not, we try to redesign the process. If this is not possible, we are happy to build a new solution.” The CIO of HITECH reported that such requests as well as the affordances of existing solutions are discussed in numerous standing committees and temporary special expertise groups on current topics with representatives from business and IT management. The CIO stressed that these institutionalized IT groups are particularly beneficia because they link business and IT management at all hierarchical levels. This enabled a “constant dialog both on a management level and on a business process level.” The CIO added that “we want the business areas to talk about processes with us, not about IT.”

In addition to IT groups as a form of IT governance structure, we found empirical evidence that relationa capabilities led to a higher degree of business process relatedness. Whereas IT relatedness could be achieved by means of more formal, regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities, the interviews revealed that prompting business units to rely on similar business processes often required informal communication and negotiation, activ conflict resolution, and environments that help develop a shared understanding. Through such relational capabilities managers of business units could often be convinced of the ability of standardized systems to map existing busines processes or of the necessity to align processes with company‐wide best practices

The case of CHEMICAL illustrates this link between relational capabilities and business process relatedness. At CHEMICAL, “cost and process efficiencies are the central topics of our IT governance” (CIO). Consequently, IT governance was designed in a way that business units cannot bypass IT management in the design phase of processes However, the CIO stressed that this does not mean that decision rights had been transferred to the IT organization or the CIO office. By contrast, s/he stated that a centralized approach, with IT as a key authority, would have resulted in inefficiencies. “What we are looking for is […] transparency and involvement of all parties […]. Some things have to be promoted with certain imperialism, but in balance with the involved stakeholder groups. A collaborative structure generates more efficiency and effectiveness than a centralized structure.” Through this approach, CHEM-ICAL was able to establish a considerable degree of business process relatedness across its various business units Despite major frictions, particularly related to international subsidiaries, the company was able to achieve business process relatedness owing to an informal approach based on dialogue. “We discuss with many stakeholders on various topics related to IT and business processes in order to address personal concerns and increase acceptance. This is not about technology, it's more about people that may be against certain initiatives. […] There is a lot of emotions involved” (CIO). In fact, CHEMICAL had actively established an environment for such a dialogue between IT and business management in the form of a “community” that has the goal to develop company‐wide best practice business processes. Members participate voluntarily because “it [best practice processes] is better for everybody” (Head of IT services).

In addition to the relational capabilities mentioned in previous studies (Brown, 1999; De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004), the conducted interviews suggest that establishing IT departments with a hig degree of business knowledge helps organizations achieve business process relatedness (Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004; Peppard, 2001). For example, HITECH's CIO talked about the difficulty of convincing the business side to use best practice processes. In the past, business units had often refused process harmonization by flatly referring to business‐related nonfeasibility. To address this challenge, HITECH's CIO office had started hiring people with strong process knowledge from the business units. “Many of our staff came from business units. In some cases, we have more process knowledge in our organization [i.e., the IT department] than the business units themselves do.” The CIO stressed that specific knowledge from HITECH's business units was still highly important when designing business processes. However, this specific knowledge only became useful because the IT department was able to understand and apply such outside knowledge. The importance of the IT department's ability to understand and apply context‐specific business knowledge was also underlined by the head of the CIO office at LIFE. “I need people in my I department that are able to understand the highly specific processes we run.[.1 If not, there is no need to have them in‐house, we could just as well outsource our IT. [...] If you want to implement an integration process with people that are only technically qualified, you are lost.”

## 4.2.2 | Business process relatedness and IT cost synergies

Similar to IT relatedness, business process relatedness led to IT cost synergies. In particular, business process relatedness reduced the need to develop specific solutions, customize existing applications, or purchase additiona hardware or software, which in turn decreased costs for maintenance and support services. For example, HITECH's

CIO stressed that “the process owner has to talk to IT management, because if he doesn't, IT needs to be adjusted to map the process. This is what was done in the past with our old SAP R/3 solution. The effect was that it consisted of 50% customizing in the end. This was financially not feasible anymore. [… Today,] we ensure that uni fied processes are used for similar problems. [...] The benefit is that now we can offer many standard solutions for many business areas. […] The more processes are harmonized, the easier it gets to manage IT.” Similarly, the CIO of CHEMICAL explained that for those processes that were not yet shared across business units, “[w]e constantly get overlapping and clashing change requests. The system becomes extremely complex and costly to maintain. […] The definition of requirements, in whatever corner of this company, has eventually an effect on the operation [of IT] and if everything that the business units ask for is implemented, then we end up with a system that no one can run or maintain anymore.”

In contrast to HITECH and CHEMICAL, RETAIL—a retail company with a large number of stores all over Europe— had no IT groups or relational capabilities established at the time of the interview. In fact, RETAIL's head of IT was hired “because […] there was a culture of completely decentralized governance, or let's say, no governance at all.” RETAIL's head of IT commented that the organization's “business processes evolved in a decentralized way over the last 15 years. […] Every department, branch, and subsidiary has its own processes.” According to the head of IT, the reason for this was that “[t]here were no incentives for shared business processes in the past.” Along with RETAIL's low IT relatedness (see Figure 3A), low business process relatedness at RETAIL led to low IT cost synergies. This was confirmed by the head of IT who stated that “in the past and right now it's all about keeping the company running.” However, s/he emphasized that “we expect massive cost savings and process improvements once the first harmonization projects are completed.”

To sum up, the following proposition is put forth:

Proposition 2. Business process relatedness mediates the positive effect of consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities (i.e., IT groups and relational capabilities) on IT cost synergies.

The mediation effects developed in Propositions 1 and 2 imply that firms can achieve (1) IT relatedness through regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and (2) business process relatedness through consensus‐oriented I governance capabilities. Notably, we did not find evidence for a relationship between regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and business process relatedness. The actual implementation of business processes is usuall outside the control of IT units. Therefore, environments for finding consensus among business and IT management may be inevitable for achieving business process relatedness. Similarly, we did not find evidence for a link between consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities and IT relatedness. The reason for this may be that consulting man different stakeholders with the goal of achieving consensus may overly complicate the process of defining standard ized IT resources.

## 4.3 | The effect of IT and business process relatedness on IT‐induced business synergies

Those firms in our sample that had achieved high degrees of both IT relatedness and business process relatedness enioved IT-induced business synergies in terms of effectiveness gains and innovation, building on resources from mul tiple business units. Prior work has referred to firms' ability to integrate and utilize resources from different organizational units as organizational integration and stressed that organizational integration may be difficult to achiev because of different skills, knowledge, and procedures (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005; Hitt, Hoskisson, & Nixon 1993). Our findings suggest that organizations can overcome some of these barriers by means of IT governance capa bilities that lead to IT relatedness and business process relatedness. The link between the two types of relatednes and IT-induced business synergies is illustrated with the example of AUTOMATE

AUTOMATE is an engineering company that manufactures machines, which are used in various industries At AUTOMATE, common IT infrastructure and business processes across divisions enabled the establishment of an entirely new line of business. Clients often have thousands of products from AUTOMATE running in different production plants. Because of the high number of product variations offered by AUTOMATE, clients had to deal with various AUTOMATE divisions for service and support. When the IT unit of AUTOMATE started merging databases across divisions and installed a unified customer relationship management system, the firm realized that it possessed data precisely showing the installed based, i.e., which client ran which product at which plant. Using this information and unifying the support process across all divisions, AUTOMATE was abl to offer an entirely new service to its clients. AUTOMATE remotely monitored all devices at the clients' plants not only ensuring immediate replacement of the correct device in case of failure (one assembly line can utiliz dozens of different AUTOMATE products) but also providing predictive analytics of how long a certain aggregate could run before breaking down. Thus, downtimes of production facilities could be substantially reduced As a result of these innovative services, customer satisfaction, customer loyalty, and device sales increase substantially.

By contrast, interviewees from organizations with either low IT relatedness or low business process relatedness or both did not mention such IT‐induced business synergies. Organizations with a high degree of IT relatedness wer able to exchange knowledge and data, but without common business processes, business units were lacking th procedures and interfaces to collaborate. In case of rather similar business processes across units but heterogeneous IT resources, firms lacked the technological infrastructure to integrate knowledge and comprehensively access and apply information from different units.

This joint effect of IT and business process relatedness is illustrated in Figure 5. Our empirical findings suggest that IT‐induced business synergies are enabled by the joint relatedness of IT resources and business processes. If either IT relatedness or business process relatedness were moderate or low, then this moderate or low level of IT relatedness or business process relatedness drove and constrained IT‐induced business synergies—i.e., IT‐induced business synergies were also moderate or low. In line with this argument, Figure 5 links the minimum value coded for the two types of relatedness with IT‐induced business synergies. For example, the joint related ness of IT and business processes (measured as the minimum value of IT and business process relatedness) of HEAVY is low in Figure 5 in line with HEAVY's low level of business process relatedness, despite its high level of IT relatedness.<sup>‡</sup>

Taken together, our analysis suggests the following relationship:

Proposition 3. IT relatedness and business process relatedness collectively enable IT‐induced business synergies.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

The goal of this study was to provide a deeper understanding of the effect of IT governance capabilities on IT‐based synergies. We followed recent advances in contextualizing general theories to develop a RBV of IT‐based synergies We drew on existing work on IT governance capabilities, IT relatedness, and IT‐based synergies to develop a preliminary, literature‐based contextualization of the RBV (see Figure 1). The preliminary framework was then furthe refined based on an interview field study within 21 multibusiness firms. The resulting RBV of IT‐based synergies (see Figure 5) considers the novel context‐specific constructs regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities, consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities, and business process relatedness. Regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities are proposed to lead to IT cost synergies via IT relatedness (Proposition 1) and business process relatedness (Proposition 2). IT relatedness and business process relatedness are proposed to jointly enable IT‐induced business synergies (Proposition 3). These findings have severa implications for research and practice.

## 5.1 | Research contributions and implications

Our study complements existing work on IT governance capabilities that has so far mostly focused on business‐IT alignment as the dependent variable (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004; Wu et al., 2015). Our findings show that IT governance capabilities not only affect business‐IT alignment but also have distinct effects on resource relatedness (recall question mark 1 in Figure 1). An increased awareness for IT costs and the personal accountabilities associated with IT processes and IT roles result in more common hardware and software landscapes within organizations. IT groups that bring together stakeholders from IT and business management as well as relationa capabilities enable firms to more effectively harmonize business processes. With regard to relational capabilities, our study underscores the importance of business knowledge within IT units (Preston & Karahanna, 2009). Our results suggest that establishing business knowledge within IT units may be particularly important for achieving business process relatedness because business knowledge enables IT managers to make educated judgments about the need for unique variations of business processes. Our findings on the distinct effects of IT governance capabilities contribute to prior work, which has often distinguished structural, procedural, and relational IT governance capabilities (De Haes & Van Grembergen, 2009; Peterson, 2004; Wu et al., 2015). When studying the effects of IT governance capabilities on resource relatedness, conflating IT roles and IT groups into structural capabilities conceals their distinct effects on IT relatedness and business process relatedness—underlining the value of our context‐specific decomposition of I governance capabilities into regulation‐oriented (including IT roles) and consensus‐oriented (including IT groups) capabilities.

Our results also contribute to extant literature on IT relatedness by shedding light on the question of how IT relatedness can be achieved and on the complementary effects of resource relatedness (Milgrom & Roberts, 1995; Tanriverdi, 2005; Tanriverdi, 2006). Whereas previous work has treated the degree of IT relatedness across business units as given, this study is among the first to explicitly consider IT relatedness as an endogenous variable that can potentially be altered through managerial action in the form of establishing IT governance capabilities (question mark 1 in Figure 1). In particular, our findings link regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities to IT relatedness. In addition to these findings on the antecedents of IT relatedness, our results also add to the discussion on its implications (recall question mark 3 in Figure 1). Our results substantiate the finding of previous work that IT relatedness leads to IT cost synergies (Tanriverdi, 2005). In addition, our findings suggest that IT‐induced business synergies may arise as a joint result of IT relatedness and business process relatedness (recall question mark 2 in Figure 1). Thus, we provide evidence that IT relatedness and business process relatedness complement each other in achieving organizational integration and IT‐induced business synergies (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005; Campbell & Goold, 1998).

By theorizing and providing empirical evidence on the effects of IT governance capabilities on resource relatedness and IT‐based synergies, our study contributes to recent work that advocates a more nuanced treatment of business‐IT alignment (Ravishankar, Pan, & Leidner, 2011; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Prior work has ofte examined the alignment of IT with general business requirements of an organization. Departing from such a simplistic view, recent work acknowledged idiosyncrasies of different business units and considered alignment at the level of business units and at the corporate level (Ravishankar et al., 2011; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015); in fact, alignment at the corporate level may often translate to achieving IT‐based synergies (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Based on such more nuanced view and inspired by platform‐based organizations, such as Apple or Google (Tiwana, Konsynski, & Bush, 2010), Reynolds and Yetton (2015) recommend an “IT corporate platform strategy” for addressing the long‐recognized tension between integration and differentiation (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967). Such an internal platform strategy may help firms achieve integration through standardization and at the same time enable business units t develop their respective “IT application portfolio strategy” (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Our results are in line with these assertions but extend them by adding the critical dimension of business process relatedness. Business process relatedness is an important construct in the domain of IT governance because harmonizing business processes may reduce the need for IT solutions that are specific to local requirements—with important consequences for achieving IT‐based synergies. Although certain unique business processes will likely remain necessary for creating value and responding to customer needs, consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities can help harmonize those business processes that are unnecessarily dissimilar across the organization. To the extent that business processes can be har monized without hampering local value creation, consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities may act as “integra tive devices” that help firms achieve both integration and differentiation (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967)

Future research may build on our and Reynolds and Yetton's (2015) work to study emergent questions in more detail. If firms are able to harmonize business processes—and to a certain extent adapt business processes to the capabilities of standardized information systems (Strong & Volkoff, 2010)—it may be beneficial to rely on similar IT resources to benefit from IT cost synergies and IT‐induced business synergies. For some business areas, IT relatedness and business process relatedness may only be feasible to a lower extent, which may reinforce the need for a firm‐internal platform strategy (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). For some products and services, it may even be reasonable to rely on a firm‐external platform strategy and benefit from outside innovation, similar to the strategy of established platform companies such as Apple and Google—for example, if business process relatedness is not feasible or if the benefits derived from business process relatedness are outweighed by the value derived from large‐scale innovativeness (Tilson, Lyytinen, & Sørensen, 2010). The situational factors influencing the managerial choices related to the design of IT governance and IT platforms are so far not well‐understood, providing ample opportunities for future research.

Further, our findings contribute to the large body of knowledge on the RBV (e.g., Barney, 1991; Mata et al., 1995; Wade & Hulland, 2004) and to the recent discussion on contextualizing theories in management and IS (Hong et al., 2013; Johns, 2006). The RBV is a grand theory that postulates to be valid in all organizational contexts but may lack the specificity to provide fine‐grained explanations for causal effects in a particular context. We believe that our study provides a valuable contextualization of the more general RBV for the specific context of IT‐based synergies. B decomposing general constructs and adding context‐specific ones, we were able to capture context‐specific relation ships. These relationships would have remained opaque without contextualizing the general constructs and relation ships of the RBV.

Similar to our reliance on prior work for a preliminary contextualization of the RBV, future work may build on our results and study IT governance capabilities, resource relatedness, and resulting synergies in other contexts. For example, future research may focus on the virtue of regulation‐oriented and consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities during and after large mergers and acquisitions. Although this was not the focus of our study, our decomposition o constructs may be a promising starting point for further contextualizing our theory in the particular context of post‐merger integration (Henningsson & Kettinger, 2016). As another example, extant literature suggests that achieving synergies and organizational integration are also relevant goals across firm boundaries (Barki & Pinsonneault 2005). In fact, IT governance capabilities, IT relatedness, and business process relatedness are not confined to intraorganizational contexts but may also play an important role in interorganizational networks. Hence, the findings of this study may be the basis for future research in the promising area of IT governance in interorganizational net works (Trang Opitz & Kolbe 2013: Xiao Xie & Hu 2013)

Our study is among the first in the IS literature to follow the recent recommendations on theory contextualization (Hong et al., 2013). In particular, we adopt existing contextualized constructs and relationships and rely on qualitative research to further refine the contextualization Thus, we add to prior work on contextualization whose focus was mostly on deductive and confirmatory theory testing (Hong et al., 2013). Our approach to theory contextualizing ech oes classic writings on qualitative research, which consistently stressed its value for “capturing the contextual complexity” of IS-related phenomena (Benbasat et al 1987, p. 374). Based on our promising results, we call for future research contextualizing the RBV and other grand theories in IS‐related contexts, relying on both quantitative and qualitative research (Venkatesh, Brown, & Bala, 2013)

## 5.2 | Managerial implications

Our study has several implications for decision‐makers. First, our results show that different IT governance capabilities have distinct effects on resource relatedness and IT‐based synergies. When considering the implementation or adjust ment of IT governance capabilities, managers should first evaluate the current level of IT relatedness and business process relatedness. Firms that identify a potential for synergies, which stems from a lack of IT relatedness, should rather aim at developing regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities. By contrast, if business processes seem unnecessarily tailored to business units, or if the benefits from specific processes do not outweigh unrealized syner gies, then decision‐makers should aim at developing consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities. We identified th development of business knowledge in the IT area to be one important foundation for consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities. IT human resource management should offer trainings on business knowledge to IT staff or activel hire individuals with knowledge on both business and IT. These findings also underline the value of graduates from educational programs that integrate knowledge on technology and management (Hustad & Olsen, 2014)

Based on our results, we suggest a framework for establishing IT governance capabilities to achieve IT‐based synergies in practice (see Figure 6). Generally, regulation‐oriented capabilities seem easier and faster to establish than consensus‐oriented capabilities, which require stakeholders to develop informal links and shared communication habits. Therefore, firms that have not realized any IT cost synergies yet should start by implementing clear IT roles and IT processes (see left side of Figure 6). This will likely increase IT relatedness and result in initial IT cost synergies To achieve business process relatedness and additional IT‐based synergies, firms also require consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities, including IT groups and relational capabilities. Because the development of relational capabilities that help business and IT to find consensus will likely take time, firms should start forming IT groups and developing business knowledge in the IT area as early as possible (see centre of Figure 6). The developed consensusoriented IT governance capabilities will later help firms to achieve a balance between local business‐IT alignment and the harmonization of business processes. To the extent that both IT relatedness and business process relatedness can be achieved, firms will likely enjoy additional IT cost synergies resulting from the relatedness of business processes as well as IT‐induced business synergies, building on the relatedness of both IT resources and business processes (see right‐hand side of Figure 6).

These insights have wider implications for IT managers who are faced with the challenge that IT is sometimes seen as cost driver and other times expected to deliver additional value. If an organization is striving for efficienc and cost leadership, IT governance should be geared toward control, which can often be achieved in rather short time by means of regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities. However, in the digital age, IT is often expected to deliver value through enabling the recombination of core competencies, thereby fostering innovation. IT managers learn from our findings that IT‐based innovation often requires consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities. Althoug developing consensus‐oriented capabilities in addition to regulation‐oriented ones will likely require long‐term efforts—such as developing business knowledge in the IT area and nurturing a good relationship between business and IT—it enables firms to simultaneously achieve IT and business relatedness and, as a consequence, innovate and provide holistic customer experiences through breaking down silos. With increasing digitization, this is likely a key differentiator in many industries

## 5.3 | Limitations

When interpreting and applying the results of this study, some limitations have to be taken into account. First, our field study relied on interviews with CIOs and other IT executives of multibusiness organizations as key informants. These IT managers may be role‐biased toward a positive and strong performance impact of IT and IT governance. Complementing our study with data from the business side may thus provide valuable additional insights. We would like to note, however, that we felt that our data selection was justified by the focus on understanding relationships between IT governance capabilities and IT‐based synergies instead of explaining more general variations in business performance. CIOs and other IT executives had a comprehensive overview of the constructs of interest of this study that managers in the individual business units would likely not have—they would know about IT resources and business process in their own unit but would likely not be aware of IT relatedness, business process relatedness, and IT‐based synergies in the entire firm. Our focus on CIOs and other IT executives also seemed justified from a pragmatic point of view by the unique access to our high‐profile interview partners. We would also like to note that our study is not normative in that it would recommend institutionalizing as many IT roles, processes, or groups as possible or harmonizing all IT resources and business processes. Instead, our goal was to explain implications of IT governance capabilities on IT‐based synergies above and beyond aligning IT with local business requirements. We believe that based on this knowledge, organizations are better equipped to make educated choices of capabilities that help balance loca business‐IT alignment and the entrepreneurial spirit of business units on the one hand, and the potential cost savings and value gains of IT‐based synergies on the other hand.

Second, when interpreting our results, it is important to bear in mind that we relied on a variance‐based logic to capture effects of IT governance capabilities on resource relatedness and IT-based synergies, We believe that this approach was well‐justified because of the nature of our research question and our data. Also, even though we collected data through a limited number of interviews over a limited time period, our interviews and the secondar material were retrospective in nature and we considered these long‐term insights when developing our proposition (see Section 3). However, it has to be noted that our results suggest that IT governance capabilities, resource related ness, and IT‐based synergies evolve over time, reflecting a rather complex process. It would be interesting to complement our study with in‐depth, longitudinal case studies on selected organizations. The empirical context of organizations that emerged through M&A activities discussed above could be a setting in which such an in‐depth case study is feasible and promising.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Our goal in this study was to contextualize the more general RBV to be able to explain the link between IT governanc capabilities and IT‐based synergies. We adopted an initial contextualization from prior work and further refined our framework based on a qualitative field study of 21 multibusiness firms. Our findings suggest that regulation‐oriented IT governance capabilities lead to IT cost synergies via increases in IT relatedness, while consensus‐oriented IT governance capabilities help achieve IT cost synergies through increasing business process relatedness. Our findings also suggest that IT relatedness and business process relatedness collectively enable IT‐induced business synergies, which are of ever‐increasing importance in the digital age. The findings of our study contribute to prior literature on IT governance, IT relatedness, and the RBV. Practitioners are guided in achieving IT‐based synergies through estab lishing IT governance capabilities.

## APPENDIX B

## Initial interview guideline

Information regarding the interview partner and the case company

• Interviewee's position, educational background, and career?

• Organization's main business area, structure, employees, revenues?

## IT in the organization

• Relevance of IT for the corporation?

• Size, structure, and role of IT organization? Allocation and distribution of tasks? Performance and reputation of IT organization?

## IT strategy

• Specific IT strategy in place? How often adapted in the past?

• Responsibility for contents, goals, and attainment of IT strategy?

## Corporate governance

• Interviewee's and firm's understanding of corporate governance?

• Relation between corporate governance and IT governance?

## IT governance

• Interviewee's and firm's understanding of IT governance? IT governance on the agenda? Since when? What were the drivers? Which barriers were observed during IT governance rollout?

• Who is responsible for IT governance? Who has which IT decision and IT input rights?

• Which IT governance capabilities are in place (structures, processes, relational mechanisms)?

• Positive impacts of IT governance capabilities? On IT? On strategic IT goals? On strategic corporate goals? On IT processes? On business processes?

• Negative impacts of IT governance?

• Relation between IT governance and risk management? Relation between IT governance and compliance?

## Interview guideline for later stage of theorizing process

Information regarding the interview partner and the case company

• Interviewee's position, educational background, and career?

• Organization's main business area, structure, employees, revenues?

## IT in the organization

• Relevance of IT for the corporation?

• Size, structure, and role of IT organization? Allocation and distribution of tasks? Performance and reputatio of IT organization?

• Context‐specific knowledge of the IT department?

• Ability of the IT department to understand knowledge from outside IT?

## IT governance capabilities

• Which IT governance structures are in place? Operating efficiently and effectively?

• Which IT governance processes are in place? Operating efficiently and effectively?

• Which IT governance relational mechanisms are in place? Operating efficiently and effectively?

• How long have these mechanisms been in place? Is IT governance in a mature stage?

## IT relatedness

• Degree of harmonization of the IT and communication infrastructure? Shared IT demand and supply processes?

• Extent to which applications are common or comparable?

• Shared IT management practices?

## Business process relatedness

• Shared business processes?

• Shared managerial practices?

## Cross‐unit synergies

• Changes in IT costs?

• Changes in profits or return on investments?

• Changes in revenues or market shares?

• Changes in quality or reputation?

• New technological developments and/or innovations in business operations?

• Changed time to market?

For each of the above questions: If yes, related to IT/a specific business process/combination of business processes?
