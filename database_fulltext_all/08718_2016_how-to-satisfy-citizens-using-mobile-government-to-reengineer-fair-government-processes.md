# How to satisfy citizens? Using mobile government to reengineer fair government processes

Zhen-Jiao Chen <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Douglas Vogel <sup>b</sup>, Zhao-Hua Wang

<sup>a</sup> School of Management and Economics, Beijing Institute of Technology, 5 South Zhong Guan Cun Street, Hai Dian District, Beijing, PR China <sup>b</sup> School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, 92 West Dazhi Street, Nangang District, Harbin, Heilongjiang Province, PR China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 December 2013 Received in revised form 30 November 2015 Accepted 30 November 2015 Available online 11 December 2015

Keywords: mGovernment Mobile functions Procedural fairness User satisfaction

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines how mobile and wireless technology (MWT) characteristics affect user satisfaction in the mGovernment field to which mGovernment literature thus far has paid little attention. Specifically, we identify seven MWT functions of mGovernment and explore the underlying process through which the seven MWT functions affect user satisfaction. By integrating the task-technology fit perspective and theory of procedural fairness, we develop a research model to demonstrate that the seven MWT functions of mGovernment reshape three characteristics of fair decision procedures and increase the procedural fairness of governmental services, which, in turn, improve user satisfaction. A total of 449 experienced users in China responded to a survey. The results indicate that 1) two time-critical functions of mGovernment improve transparency, 2) a locationsensitive function and a mobile multimedia function promote information accuracy and 3) three personal control functions increase voice opportunity. Further, the three fair procedural characteristics (i.e., transparency, information accuracy and voice) increase procedural fairness of governmental services, which, in turn, increases user satisfaction. This study results in a theoretical contribution to e/mGovernment and mobile service literature by explaining “which” and “how” MWT functions affect user satisfaction. Moreover, it applies and complements theory of procedural fairness in the context of mGovernment. This study also suggests guidelines for government agencies and system developers on selecting a suitable portfolio of MWT functions to fit government's various task needs and to improve user satisfaction.

## 1. Introduction

The ubiquity and rapid development of mobile and wireless technology (MWT),<sup>1</sup> such as mobile phones, Wi-Fi, and 4G, has created potential opportunities for improving mobile government (mGovernment), which refers to government's use of MWT to deliver its services. Although mGovernment is a subsection of electronic government (eGovernment), it is possible that mGovernment services will become more popular than eGovernment services [3,8,48,54]. Statistics indicate that the usage of M-internet (mobile internet) has overtaken that of PCinternet (Personal computer internet) [48], which offers mGovernment a bigger user base than does eGovernment. Moreover, the mobility of mGovernment facilitates citizen access to ubiquitous services, which eGovernment cannot provide [33]. With mobile penetration rates exceeding 100% in more than 30 countries, it is imperative for governments to re-evaluate the advantages of using M-internet.

Governments are vigorously developing mGovernment services. Both First-time adoption and continued post-adoption usage of these services are pivotal to mGovernment success [2]. However, extant mGovernment literature focuses more on first-time adoption issues rather than on continued post-adoption usage issues [25,27, 36,48,50]. To fill this gap, this study focuses on user satisfaction with mGovernment services, which is a critical predictor of postadoption usage of mGovernment services [10].

An increasing number of studies discuss user satisfaction with eGovernment [2,13,18,40,57]. However, the results cannot be transferred to mGovernment, as mGovernment has unique features [59]. These studies identify various social issues (e.g., information quality, trust, perceived risk and social value) as antecedents of user satisfaction toward eGovernment [2,13,18,40,57], but ignore the effects of technological functions (e.g., user interface design) on user satisfaction [59]. In mobile service literature, several studies adopt the “utilitarian value” perspective to identify various economic values of mobile services as predictors of user satisfaction, but they rarely pay attention to the effects of functional values, therefore deviating a little from the perspective of the IS discipline [6]. To fill this gap, this study explores “how MWT characteristics of mGovernment affect user satisfaction.”

An important objective of mGovernment application in many countries (e.g., China) is using MWT to reengineer the processes of governmental services (also called “administrative reform” or “xing zheng ti zhi gai $g e "$ in Chinese) in order to improve citizen satisfaction toward governments [37]. Specifically speaking, the “rule of man” in traditional processes of governmental services gives rise to kickbacks, corruption, unfair decisions and resource distribution, thus incurring citizen dissatisfaction [8,9]. Information services of mGovernment are expected to facilitate information flow between government agencies and citizens. This is critical for creating a fair and transparent decision procedure [8, 9,37]. It is the procedural fairness of government decision making that is expected to improve user satisfaction. Although mGovernment services include information and transaction, this study focuses on the former, as mGovernment in many countries- especially in developing countries (e.g., China) — is still at the embryonic stage, mainly providing information services [48,54,59].

Some IS researchers propose that the use of ICTs in public administration benefits fairness of government decisions and services [8,9], while others imply that the user fairness perception may improve satisfaction with IS [14,15]. However, none of the above studies has empirically examined how technical functions improve user satisfaction through reengineering fair decision procedures. This paper thus investigates two research questions: 1) What are the MWT functions of mGovernment? 2) From the perspective of reshaping procedural fairness, what is the mediating process through which the MWT functions improve user satisfaction with mGovernment?

In order to address these research questions, we borrow the tenets of the task-technology fit (TTF) and theory of procedural fairness (TPF) to propose that the MWT functions of mGovernment promote the formation of the fair procedural characteristics of mGovernment services, which, in turn, improve procedural fairness of governmental services and user satisfaction toward mGovernment. First, TTF suggests that technology features of an IS system should match with the characteristics of the tasks that the IS system supports [22]. In the mGovernment context, an important task of MWT is to shape fair procedures of governmental services. Thus, MWT functions (technological characteristics) are expected to improve the formation of the characteristics of fair governmental procedures (task characteristics). We adapted a traditional classification of mobile functions [4,61] to identify seven MWT functions of mGovernment, and adopted TPF [34,47] to identify three procedural characteristics of mGovernment services (i.e., transparency, information accuracy and voice opportunity). Based on the tenet of TTF, we tested whether the seven MWT functions improve the three fair procedural characteristics. Second, based on the tenet of TPF, which claims that several characteristics of fair procedures affect procedural fairness and individual attitudes, we hypothesized that the three procedural characteristics improve both the procedural fairness and user satisfaction of mGovernment.

This research contributes to literature in three major ways. First, prior mGovernment research focuses mainly on first-adoption, rather than on post-adoption, issues. To fill this gap, this study focuses on user satisfaction, a typical post-adoption issue. Second, prior eGovernment or mobile services literature primarily identifies behavioral, rather than technical, issues as antecedents of user satisfaction. This study fills this gap and identifies MWT technical factors as antecedents of user satisfaction toward mGovernment, thus broadening the scope of antecedents that predict user satisfaction toward eGovernment and mobile services. Third, this pioneering study applies TPF and sheds light on the underlying processes through which MWT artifacts affect outcomes from the perspective of procedural fairness in the mGovernment context. Finally, we identify seven MWT functions as predictors of three procedural characteristics to complement TPF. In addition, our results will enlighten government agencies and system developers in designing mGovernment services that satisfy users.

The remainder of this study is organized as follows. Section 2 is a comprehensive literature review and theoretical background.

Section 3 presents the research model and hypotheses. Section 4 describes the research method and Section 5 reports data analysis and findings. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed in Section 6 and Section 7 concludes the study.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical background

## 2.1. TTF perspective

TTF refers to the congruence between IT functionalities and the requirements of tasks that IT supports [22]. The TTF perspective can be broadly applied to any situation in which technology is used to accomplish specific tasks [39]. For instance, Maruping and Agarwal [39] adopted the TTF perspective to explore how various IT functionalities (e.g., immediacy of feedback) match the characteristics of different team tasks (e.g., conflict management) in virtual teams. Zigurs and Buckland [63] developed propositions that link the fit between IT functions and task characteristics in the context of group decision systems. Given our emphasis on mGovernment, we adopt the TTF perspective to develop hypotheses that link the fit between MWT functions and task characteristics of mGovernment.

How does technology fit a task in the mGovernment context? A task of MWT is to assist government officials in reengineering fair procedures of services in the mGovernment context [37]. The task of reengineering fair procedures is characterized by specific characteristics, such as increasing transparency [34,47]. As these task characteristics vary in their nature, MWT functions differ in the extent to which they are able to support these characteristics. For instance, transparency might be improved by MWT functions that enable real-time information exchange. This suggests that specific MWT functions are expected to improve different procedural characteristics in the mGovernment context.

However, the TTF perspective does not specify technical and task characteristics in mGovernment. In addition to identifying MWT functions based on the literature review, this study draws on TPF to identify the characteristics of reengineering fair procedures. According to the TTF perspective and TPF, we test the effects of seven MWT functions on three specific procedural characteristics.

## 2.2. mGovernment services

Despite the rapid growth of mGovernment becoming more popular than eGovernment, extant literature focuses less on mGovernment than on eGovernment [3,8,36,54,57,59]. Among the few mGovernment studies, researchers focus mainly on the determinants of initial adoption or usage of mGovernment [27,36,50]. For instance, Hung, Chang and Kuo [27] identified perceived usefulness, ease of use, trust, interactivity, attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavior control as antecedents of first-time adoption of mGovernment. Liu and colleagues [36] found that ease of use, long-term usefulness and social influence improves the intention to use mGovernment services. However, understanding continued post-adoption usage of mGovernment is more important than initialadoption for mGovernment service providers because the cost of acquiring a new user is several times more than that of retaining an existing user in the mobile service industry [16]. In spite of the importance of user continued post-adoption usage research, the determinants of postadoption usage has received little attention in the mGovernment field. This study fills this gap and focuses on user satisfaction toward mGovernment because user satisfaction is the most important determinant of user continued post-adoption usage of mGovernment [10].

In eGovernment literature, several studies [2,13,18,40,57] have explored the predictors of user satisfaction; for instance, Alawneh and colleagues [2] proposed that security, privacy, trust, accessibility and service quality may affect satisfaction toward eGovernment. Welch, Hinnant and Moon [57] found that transaction, transparency and interactivity directly affect satisfaction toward eGovernment. Carter and Belanger [13] report that relative advantage, image and compatibility are antecedents of user attitudes. The results of these studies might be related to mGovernment but cannot be transferred to mGovernment, considering the fact that users have different requirements on mGovernment services, such as mobility, time-criticality, location discovery, convenience and personalization [16,59]. Different requirements of users cause the predictors of satisfaction with mGovernment to vary from that of eGovernment. In addition, the above studies mainly identify social issues as predictors of user attitudes, and no IT artifacts (i.e., application of IT to enable delivery of government services) are presented as predictors in these studies.

Benbasat and Zmud [6] criticize that many IT studies are firmly based on behavior discipline and thus they call for IS research to explore how IT artifacts (i.e., IT functions) are constructed and how they affect context. Wu et al. [59] also call for studies to explore the effects of technical functions of mGovernment, as it is an important issue for ensuring the success of mGovernment. In response to these calls, this study aims to identify the MWT functions of mGovernment and explores their effects on user satisfaction toward mGovernment.

In mobile services literature, many researchers have adopted a “utilitarian values perspective” to demonstrate that different utilitarian values significantly predict user satisfaction for different types of mobile services [16,52,62]. These studies focus on various mobile services (e.g., mobile stock, mobile TV, mobile games and mobile banking) except for mGovernment services. For instance, Tojib and Tsarenko [52] found that experienced economic value significantly improves user satisfaction toward advanced mobile services. Zhao and colleagues [62] demonstrated that, in the context of mobile commerce (mCommerce), interactional fairness and distributive fairness significantly improve customer satisfaction toward mCommerce services; however, procedural fairness has no significant effect on customer satisfaction. Our application of the perspective of utilitarian values is consistent with this line of research, but it varies from the above studies in two ways. First, in the context of mGovernment, we identify procedural fairness as an important economic value of the mGovernment services that are expected to predict user satisfaction toward mGovernment. Second, utilitarian values include economic values and functional values [16]. Extant empirical studies focus mainly on the former rather than on the latter. Our study fills this gap by testing the effects of seven mobile functions on user satisfaction toward mGovernment services.

How do these MWT functions affect user satisfaction? In the IS and public administration field, researchers imply that ICTs can improve user satisfaction through promoting fairness. Specifically, some public administration researchers recognize the important role of perceived fairness in the relationship between public administration and citizen attitudes [51,53]. Other researchers also propose that information and communication technologies (ICTs) used in public administration may benefit citizens' perceived fairness of government services [8,9]. Several IS researchers (e.g., [14,15]) confirm the importance of user fairness perception in their assessment of user satisfaction with various IT systems, such as online shopping. However, no empirical study has examined the mediating role of fairness between ICTs and user satisfaction of an IS. This study fills this gap. We adopt TPF to empirically examine the mediating processes through which the MWT functions affect user satisfaction in the context of mGovernment.

## 2.3. MWT functions of mGovernment

A literature review shows that traditional classification of mobile application functions include three dimensions: time-critical function, location-sensitive function and personal control function, referring to the extent to which the functions are time critical, location sensitive and controlled by users, respectively [4,61]. In addition, Hsu and colleagues [25] highlighted that the mobile multimedia message function becomes an indispensible function for mobile applications - given the increasing need for mobile communication [25] - as it allows mobile device users to deliver multimedia information that makes mobile communication more expressive than is traditional text-based communication. Our study focuses on information services of mGovernment, and combines the mobile multimedia message function with the other three traditional mobile functions to categorize the MWT functions of mGovernment systems into four dimensions: time-critical function, location-sensitive function, multimedia message service (MMS) function and personal control functions.

Time-critical functions: On-time information delivery denotes the timely and voluntary delivery of current government information by mGovernment systems to related citizens [43]. Response timeliness refers to the degree to which mGovernment systems offer timely responses to inquiries by citizens [58].

Location-sensitive function: Location-based service refers to the ability to identify and locate targets exactly, including location-tracking services and location-aware services [31,61]. The former is defined as mGovernment with the use of object and location identification technology providing information about users' whereabouts to entities (e.g., location of communal facilities, destinations and traffic) other than the user, while location-aware services supply the user (i.e., information requester) with personal data of location [31].

MMS function is a standard way to send messages with multimedia content to and from mobile phones. MMS enables mGovernment systems to convey information that combines text, pictures, voice recordings, animation and video clips [25]. The communication device should be capable of delivering and receiving messages through a third generation (3G) network, since multimedia messages are usually large. Multimedia messaging service is an extension of the short messaging service (SMS).

Personal control functions: Portability denotes that mGovernment service is ubiquitous. An mGovernment system enables people to contact public agencies and gives them access to government services, regardless of distance and location [43]. Ease of use refers to the degree to which users believe that using mGovernment systems will be easy because of personalization of handy mobile devices and customization of mGovernment systems [43]. Active control describes user ability to voluntarily participate in, and instrumentally influence, two-way communication between governments and themselves by means of mGovernment systems [35].

## 2.4. Characteristic of fair decision procedures

Derived from the field of organizational behavior across several IT settings, TPF has been validated as a well-defined theory to explain overall satisfaction [14,15]. Procedural fairness stems from research on dispute resolution processes, and has been adapted to various contexts, including online shopping, e-learning, management and employee selection [14,15,21]. For example, in a Web-based learning context, Chiu and colleagues [15] defined procedural fairness as perceived fairness of grading procedures. In the online shopping context, Chiu and colleagues [14] defined procedural fairness as perceived fairness of online transaction procedures. In the mGovernment context, the current study defines procedural fairness as perceived fairness of procedures related to government services, actions and decisions. Chiu and colleagues [14] indicated that perceived fairness is more important than is actual fairness in determining people's attitudes. Procedural fairness is found to improve various desirable outcomes, including IT user trust, satisfaction and continuance use intention [14,15].

TPF claims that in the field of public administration, procedural fairness of decision making is affected by four procedural rules: 1) transparency, which reflects decision makers providing adequate information of decision making to the stakeholders who are affected by the decisions, 2) information accuracy, which refers to correctness and reliability of the information, 3) voice opportunity, which represents the fact that stakeholders have the opportunity to provide feedback regarding the decisions to the decision makers, and 4) neutrality, which relates to selflessness of the decision makers in decision making [34,47]. Obviously, all of these fair procedural rules reflect effective information exchange between decision makers and stakeholders, except for neutrality.

Our study focuses on information services of mGovernment, i.e., using mobile functions to facilitate information exchange between (decision makers) and users (stakeholders). Thus, these fair procedural rules of mGovernment services should reflect effective information exchange. Accordingly, we identify three procedural rules reflecting information exchange (transparency, information accuracy and voice opportunity) as three antecedents which are expected to improve user perception of procedural fairness of governmental decision making [8,9,37]. Furthermore, we test how the MWT functions promote the formation of the three fair procedural rules of mGovernment services.

In the mGovernment context, the three factors describe bidirectional information exchange between government and citizens. Transparency reflects that a government agency adopts an mGovernment system to synchronously deliver the most current information to related citizens [42]. Information accuracy refers to understandability, correctness and reliability information delivered by mGovernment systems [58], thus reflecting information quality. Voice opportunity relates to citizens using mGovernment systems to obtain more opportunities for expressing their opinions to government agencies [55].

The three types of procedural characteristics are subject to the influence of varied external MWT functions. As Bhatnagar [9] suggests, ICTs promote transparency and fairness by disseminating government information and allowing citizens to participate directly in government activities through various online portals. First, transparency reflects that government agencies adopt mGovernment systems to deliver information to related citizens [42]. This is a government–citizen information flow. Only the MWT functions that facilitate government agencies use of mGovernment will affect transparency. Thus, current research identifies governmental real-time information dissemination and governmental response timeliness as two antecedents of transparency.

Second, voice reflects that citizens use the mGovernment system to deliver information to government, reflective of the citizen-government information flow [55]. Only the MWT functions that facilitate citizens' use of mGovernment systems will predict citizens' voice opportunity. Therefore, citizens' personal control of mGovernment is essential for users to use mGovernment and then have more voice opportunity. Portability, active control and ease of use are identified as three antecedents of voice opportunity.

Finally, MMS and location-based services relate to enhancement of information quality and are expected to be two antecedents of information accuracy. The three procedural rules further increase user perception of fairness and satisfaction with mGovernment services.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Borrowing the tenet of TTF, which claims that technology should fit task characteristics, we develop a research model to test whether the seven MWT functions of mGovernment (technology) promote the formation of three characteristics of fair procedures of mGovernment services (task characteristics). The seven MWT functions are analyzed along four dimensions: time-critical functions, location-sensitive function, MMS function and personal control functions. The three fair procedural characteristics of mGovernment services are transparency, information accuracy and voice. Furthermore, our research model adopts TPF, which claims that several fair procedural characteristics affect procedural fairness and individual attitudes, to test whether the three procedural characteristics improve both the procedural fairness and user satisfaction of mGovernment. Fig. 1 displays the theoretical framework and research model of this study.

Why is procedural fairness important for individual satisfaction? From a utilitarian perspective, individuals prefer procedural fairness because fair procedures increase the likelihood of individuals obtaining desirable outcomes, such as participation in decision-making activities fair resource allocation and so on [53,38]. Such outcomes elicit individual positive attitudes [34]. In traditional governments, citizen satisfaction toward governmental services gradually decreases over time as a result of unfair working procedures that lead to autocracy, corruption and unfair practices in public services [53]. If the mGovernment can ensure the fairness of governmental procedures, citizens can expect more desirable outcomes, such as real-time information, high-quality services, desired resources distribution, democracy and so on [38]. The instrumentality of procedural fairness is expected to increase user satisfaction for mGovernment systems.

Consistent with our arguments, previous studies on business management have reported that procedural fairness positively affects individual satisfaction with various services, including complaint handling, as well as service recovery and pay (for a review, see [15]). Following this literature, we use user satisfaction toward mGovernment services as the dependent variable to capture the positive effect of perception of procedural fairness. This leads to our first hypothesis.

H1. Perception of procedural fairness is positively related to user satisfaction with mGovernment services.

Bertot and colleagues [8] propose that transparency can reflect whether a government agency is adopting an mGovernment system to synchronously deliver the most current information to concerned citizens. Disseminated information is related to citizen rights, governmental rules, governmental services, governmental transaction and decisions, assets of civil servants, as well as how governmental decisions affect citizens, among others. As such, transparency, reflecting that mGovernment systems synchronously deliver information regarding governmental services and actions, should increase user perceived procedural fairness of a government agency's services and actions.

Wixom and Todd [58] define information accuracy as the understandability, correctness and reliability of information. If an mGovernment system provides users with reliable, correct and understandable information regarding governmental decision-making procedures, actions and services, the users are more likely to believe that governmental decision-making procedures, actions and services are fair.

Consistent with this argument, Magner and colleagues [38] propose that information accuracy is positively related to people's perception of procedural fairness in local governments' budgets and tax decision making.

Voice opportunity refers to citizens' use of online communication channels on an mGovernment system to express their opinions regarding a certain governmental decision to a government agency, and perhaps this would influence the governmental agency's decision making [47,55]. For instance, the state council of China always releases the drafts of administrative regulations closely linked with citizen life on an online communication system (called “Xing zheng fa gui cao an yi jian zheng ji xi tong” in Chinese) of its website. Chinese citizens can log onto the system by mobile phones to provide suggestions on these drafts, and reasonable suggestions will be adopted in enacting the formal regulations. The online communication system provides voice opportunities to citizens. If an mGovernment system can provide more voice opportunities for users to participate and influence governmental decision making, the users are more likely to believe that governmental decision-making procedures and services are fair. This argument is consistent with the findings of Magner and colleagues' research [38], indicating that voice (i.e., process control) is positively related to the perception of procedural fairness of governmental decision making. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2. Transparency (H2a), information accuracy (H2b) and voice opportunity (H2c) are positively related to perception of procedural fairness.

As mentioned above, the transparency of an mGovernment system is defined as a government agency adopting mGovernment to synchronously deliver the most current and timely information to concerned citizens [42]. Only the MWT functions that facilitate governmental agencies' usage of mGovernment system should affect transparency. We identify governmental real-time information dissemination and response timeliness as two antecedents of transparency. Both factors facilitate governmental usage of an mGovernment system: the former reflects that an mGovernment system used by a governmental agency volunteer information to concerned citizens in a timely manner (e.g., information push), and the latter, response timeliness, denotes that an mGovernment system used by the governmental agency promptly responds to citizen inquiries about special problems.

Moreover, previous studies imply that timely information delivery is a pivotal predictor of transparency. For instance, Balkin [5] identifies truthful and substantial information as an important element of transparency. Heise [23] denotes that transparent organizations should release information in a manner that is accurate, timely, balanced and unequivocal. In line with this argument, Rawlins [49] shows that substantial and timely information has a significant and positive relationship with transparency. Yavuz and Welch [60] demonstrate that technical capability of information dissemination is positively related to higher governmental website openness. In the context of mGovernment, if MWT functions facilitated governments to disseminate information or responded to key public audiences in a more timely fashion, the public could synchronously monitor the government, thereby increasing government transparency [46]. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3. Real-time information dissemination (H3a) and response timeliness (H3b) are positively related to transparency.

Information accuracy refers to the understandability, correctness and reliability of the information delivered by mGovernment. Location-based services include location-tracking and locationawareness services. Location-tracking services of mGovernment provide reliable and accurate information regarding traffic routes and locations of communal facilities [31,43]. For example, an mGovernment mobile application called “Beijing Real-Time Bus” provides users with information regarding the location of a bus and calculates when the bus will arrive in the station. In addition, location-awareness services of mGovernment can provide users with personal location data. An example is a navigation system on an mGovernment system that assists users in obtaining real-time directions and helps them calculate route plans. In addition, location-awareness services allow users to target themselves so that governments can directly provide their services to them [43]. For instance, users in dangerous situations can locate themselves in the 110 Alarm and Command system-a typical mGovernment system-so that police can be dispatched quickly to aid them directly.

With location and identification technology, locatability is a unique capability of mGovernment. Location-based services improve the accuracy, correctness and reliability of information regarding locations of public resources and individuals. Accurate location information facilitates user fair opportunity in effectively using various public resources (e.g., communal facilities, public traffic and emergency public services), and thus it is more likely to enable users to perceive fairness of governmental services and resource distribution.

MMS has reshaped mobile information communication by making it more versatile and more expressive than ever before [25]. MMS facilitates mGovernment to send complete government information with colorful pictures, voice, video and animated characters. Compared with text-based information, the colorful pictures, voice or videos of MMS clearly convey more vivid and graphic information, which is also more understandable than words. Moreover, text-based information is a textual description of a thing. The correctness and reliability of textbased information might deteriorate when the writer records it with subjective perception bias. However, colorful pictures, voice and video present a thing in an accurate manner, which should be more reliable and correct [25]. Thus, we expect that MMS increases information accuracy. Thus we hypothesized the following:

H4. MMS (H4a) and location-based services (H4b) are positively related to information accuracy.

Voice opportunity refers to citizens' use of online communication platforms of mGovernment to have more opportunities to express their opinions, such that their opinions are more likely to be considered by government agencies [55]. If citizens want to express their opinions to government officials through mGovernment but cannot use its voice platform, they would have no voice opportunity on the mGovernment platform. Therefore, only the MWT functions that facilitate citizen usage of mGovernment would improve citizens' voice opportunities on the mGovernment system.

The theory of planned behavior (TPB) clarifies that personal control is an important antecedent of intention to use an IS system [1]. Considerable empirical studies based on TPB have found that personal controls significantly influence an individual's intention to use new IS, such as eGovernment [26,29]. Thus, personal control (e.g., text editing or information sending) of online voice platforms on mGovernment should increase citizen opportunities to deliver information to government agencies.

Personal control implies that these online voice platforms are easy to use [29]. We identify three MWT artifacts (portability, ease of use and active control) to reflect personal control of mGovernment. First, portability will improve voice opportunity, as it increases ubiquity, decreases time limitation, and therefore facilitates citizens easily in using voice platforms of mGovernment, anytime and anywhere. Second, previous studies have confirmed that ease of use significantly influences individual intention to use new IS services, such as eGovernment [13,29]. Thus, ease of use of online voice platforms is expected to increase citizen opportunities to express their opinions on mGovernment. Finally, only users have the ability to instrumentally control the two-way communication between a government agency and themselves on the online voice platforms; users can gain more opportunities to use the platforms to express themselves clearly, and user suggestions may be given consideration by government agencies. In this light, active control is expected to substantially increase citizen voice opportunities on mGovernment. The above arguments are captured in the following hypotheses:

H5. Portability (H5a), ease of use (H5b), and active control (H5c) are positively related to voice opportunity.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection procedure

Since the “Wireless City” program was implemented in 2009, MWT has developed rapidly in China. In July 2013, the total number of China's Wi-Fi users reached 591 million, and over 464 (78.5%) million of these were M-internet users. Sufficient infrastructures today ensure that the Chinese government can use advanced MWT functions to deliver various mGovernment services to citizens (G2C). In China, G2C mGovernment applications include mGovernment websites, government official micro-blogs, mGovernment applications, government we-chats and so on. Until 2013, the usage rates of mGovernment applications at Chinese ministries, provincial, municipal and district governments were 25%, 31%, 11% and 3.8%, respectively. There are currently 3000 governmental we-chat accounts in China.

Beijing and Shanghai mGovernment services rank high in China.<sup>2</sup> For instance, “Beijing Capital Window” and “Shanghai of China” are two mGovernment web portals. The portals also provide mGovernment applications to citizens, such as “Shanghai mobile client,” “Beijing Real-Time Bus,” “Beijing City” and “Beijing Services for You,” etc. These mGovernment services are mainly information services. For instance, “Beijing Services for You” provides four governmental information query services (e.g., about real estate transactions, marriage registration booking.) and one mayor's e-mail services. These services facilitate information exchange between government administrators and citizens. Because of Chinese mGovernment development, China is selected as an ideal site to conduct the survey.

The data were obtained from a sample of experienced users of mGovernment services in Beijing and Shanghai to increase the generalizability of the results. The introduction to the survey included the definition of mGovernment information services and offered several popular mGovernment information services as examples. The questionnaire asked the respondents whether they had used mGovernment information services. If no, they were excluded from the survey; if yes, they were asked to write down the name of the mGovernment service they had used the last time, and to fill in the questionnaire by assessing the service. However, a small number of participants had written down other services (e.g., eCommerce services) rather than mGovernment services and assessed these services in the questionnaires. We screened out these invalid questionnaires.

A total of 593 individuals were invited to participate in this survey, and 449 valid cases were obtained, representing a final response rate of 76%. In the sample, 54.8% were male and 45.2% female. Of the respondents, 50.1% comprised the 29 or younger age group and 49.5% of the respondents were between 30 and 49 years old. As for years of using mobile devices, 24.9% had used mobile devices for two to six years, 43.0% for six to ten years, and 27.6% for ten or more years. With regard to education, the sample comprised of 86.4% university MPA (Master of Public Administration) alumni or alumnae and 13.6% citizen without MPA degrees to aid in isolation of any MPA degree effects.

A number of empirical studies have identified MPA participants as an appropriate sample for e/mGovernment research (e.g., [12,28,56]). For instance, Welch and Feeney [56] used both MPA participants and citizens for the sample. They conducted empirical tests and found university MPA participants have no biased evaluation of eGovernment compared with citizens. These eGovernment studies (e.g., [12,28,56]) further proposed that MPA participants were suitable samples for eGovernment research. There are two-fold reasons: first, MPA participants are citizens and also need eGovernment services; second, all of them had taken eGovernment courses, and therefore would have a better understanding of eGovernment services [28].

More importantly, we followed previous research [17] to run an independent sample t-test on the differences of mean and standard deviation to check whether the sample consisting of MPA alumni represented the target population (i.e., citizens using mGovernment). Data were divided into two groups: one consisted of respondents with MPA degrees (N = 388) and the other of citizens without MPA degrees (N = 61). The results revealed no significant differences between the participants in the two groups in terms of all studied variables (see Table 1). The results confirmed that sample bias could not be corroborated in this study.

## 4.2. Measures

Most items of the studied variables were adapted from the literature (described below), while three measures were self-developed. The questionnaire was translated into Chinese by means of a backtranslation procedure [41]. The measures of the studied variable included 41 questions. All items were measured by a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree.

Specifically, three items were developed in this study to measure real-time information dissemination. Three items for the response timeliness construct were adapted from Wixom and Todd's [58] timeliness scale. MMS was measured by a self-developed three-item scale. Three items were adapted from Yuan et al.’s [61] study to measure locationbased services, and three items used to assess portability were selfdeveloped. Ease of use was measured by three items adapted from Wixom and Todd's [58] ease of use scale. Active control was measured by a four-item scale adapted from Liu [35]. Transparency was measured using three items adapted from Rawlins' [49] study. Information accuracy was measured by a three-item scale adapted from Wixom and Todd's [58] scale. Voice opportunity was measured by a four-item scale adapted from the voice measure used in Conlon and Fasolo's [20] study. Five items used to measure procedural fairness were adapted from Blader and Tyler's [11] scale. User satisfaction was measured by a four-item scale adapted from Bhattacherjee [10].

A pilot test of 47 subjects was conducted to test the wording of the instruments. The Cronbach's alpha of all studied variables ranged from 0.80 to 0.93, which shows that the studied variables had acceptable reliabilities (the cutoff was 0.70) [19,44]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess the validity of all measures. Each item had a statistically significant loading $\left( \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ of substantial size (N0.07) on the hypothesized factor. Subsequently, minor changes were made on marginal items. Appendix A lists the final items used in the questionnaire.

## 5.2. Structural model

The structural model analysis was conducted to test the hypotheses. We controlled the MPA as a dummy variable that was coded as MPA degree holders = 1 (Others = 0), the results indicating that the MPA degree had no significant effect on satisfaction $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 , n . s . )$ which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [12,56]. The hypothesized structural model, including twelve-factors, yielded a good fit: χ<sup>2</sup>/df is 2.22, p b 0.005. CFI is 0.97, NNFI is 0.96, IFI is 0.97, RMSEA is 0.052. Hence, the fit indices of the structural model exceeded the recommended values: $\chi ^ { 2 } / d f \leq 3 . 0 0 , \mathrm { C F I } \geq 0 . 9 0 ,$ , NNFI ≥ 0.90, IFI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08.

Fig. 2 displays the results of hypotheses testing. As expected, procedural fairness was positively related to user satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 , p <$ 0.01), and thus H1 was supported. The positive effects of transparency $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and information accuracy $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ on procedural fairness were stronger than that of voice opportunity on procedural fairness $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, H2a, b and c were supported. Real-time information dissemination $( \beta = 0 . 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) has a stronger positive effect on transparency than has response timeliness $( \beta = 0 . 3 3$ $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ ), and thus, H3a and b were supported. MMS $( \beta = 0 . 3 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and location-based services $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) were found to significantly increase information accuracy. Thus, H4a and b were supported. Finally, results also show that ease of use $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ has a stronger positive effect on voice opportunity than do portability $( \beta =$ $0 . 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and active control $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 , \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Altogether, 40% of the variance in transparency was explained by real-time information dissemination and response timeliness. 25% of the variance in information accuracy was explained by MMS and location-based services. 23% of the variance in voice opportunity was explained by portability, ease of use and active control. Transparency, information accuracy and voice opportunity accounted for 31% of the variance in procedural fairness. Finally, procedural fairness explained 15% of the variance in user satisfaction (see Fig. 2).

## 6. Discussion

This study has adapted TPF to examine how seven MWT functions reshape a fair procedure of governmental services, which, in turn, increases user satisfaction in the context of mGovernment. Previous research on TPF focuses mainly on organizational behavior or e-commerce fields. This study is a pioneering one with respect to applying TPF, confirming TPF theoretical notions in the newly emerging context of mGovernment services. All the hypothesized relationships are supported.

Our results are consistent with the findings of several studies [24] conducted in the context of public policy services, indicating that procedural fairness is positively related to citizen satisfaction; it differs, however, from the ndings of several studies conducted in the context of e/mCommerce [14,62] showing that procedural fairness does not affect customer satisfaction toward e/mCommerce services. Our findings imply that procedural fairness is one of the most important predictors for individual satisfaction in public administration rather than in business administration.

In addition, transparency, information accuracy and voice opportunity have significant and positive effects on procedural fairness, which further significantly increases user satisfaction. This result is consistent with previous research in the health care industry [55]. More interestingly, we show that the effects of transparency and information accuracy on procedural fairness are stronger than are those of voice opportunity. Compared with citizen-to-government information delivery, receiving accurate information from governments is more effective in improving citizens' perceived fairness of governments.

Furthermore, according to prior literature [4,61], mobile technical functionalities can be categorized into four dimensions: time criticality, location sensitivity, MMS and personal control. The results of the current study further demonstrate that the four categories of MWT functions affect different consequences, respectively, in the context of mGovernment. Specifically, our findings show that real-time information dissemination and response timeliness have positive influences on transparency. These results are consistent with the findings of previous research [49,60] conducted in both traditional organizations and eGovernment contexts, indicating that substantial information and technical capability of information dissemination have significant and positive relationships with transparency. MMS is found to have a positive influence on information accuracy, which supports Hsu and colleagues' [25] argument that multimedia information presents things in a more reliable and accurate manner. Moreover, location-based services also significantly improve information accuracy. Finally, ease of use, portability and active control have positive effects on voice opportunity. These findings are in line with the empirical results of considerable TPB-triggered studies, indicating that personal controls significantly influence an individual's intention to use new IS, such as eGovernment [26,29].

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

First, this study has provided an explanation of the post-adoption issue of mGovernment. Prior literature on mGovernment has focused mostly on the first-adoption stage. At present, most countries, including developing countries, have already passed the first-adoption stage. This study identifies MWT functions, transparency, information accuracy, voice and procedural fairness as drivers for user satisfaction, which directly predicts post-adoption behaviors.

Second, many eGovernment and mobile service studies have identified behavior factors, rather than technical factors, as antecedents of user satisfaction, thus deviating from IS research [6]. This study has developed a comprehensive framework to describe MWT artifacts and to examine their effects on user satisfaction toward mGovernment. This feature has contributed to mGovernment literature by broadening the scope of “what” for predicting user satisfaction with mGovernment, and initiating a new research direction for eGovernment and mobile services. Future research could continue to examine the effects of IT or MWT functions on user attitudes toward eGovernment and other mobile services.

Third, rare empirical mGovernment studies have examined the underlying processes through which MWT functions of mGovernment affect user responses. Our research is a pioneering study that enriches mGovernment literature by opening the “black box” between MWT functions of mGovernment and user satisfaction from a perspective of procedural fairness. Our research adopts TPF and demonstrates that fair procedural characteristics and procedural fairness link seven MWT functions with user satisfaction. Future researchers could continue adopting the “mediation approach” to explore other mediators between technical functions of e/mGovernment and various outcomes from other perspectives of fairness. An example would be to test whether outcome fairness and interactional fairness mediate the effects of IT or

MWT artifacts on user attitudes toward e/mGovernment. This can shed important new light on the underlying dynamics about “how” IT or MWT artifacts of e/mGovernment affect the context.

Fourth, this study also contributes to TPF. It is the first study adopting TPF to explain the mGovernment phenomena, thus confirming the rationale of TPF in mGovernment context. Moreover, TPF only claims to show how a series of procedural characteristics affect procedural fairness, but does not explain what shapes these procedural characteristics. Our research identifies seven MWT functions as the antecedents of three characteristics of fair procedures, which is a complement to TPF in the mGovernment context.

## 6.2. Practical implications

This research provides several important implications for government agencies and system developers. First, to improve user satisfaction with mGovernment services, we suggest that government agencies should improve procedural fairness of mGovernment services. Procedural fairness can be increased by improving transparency, information accuracy and voice opportunity. Among them, transparency and information accuracy are more critical in improving procedural fairness. In addition, previous studies have suggested that procedural fairness of mGovernment services can be enhanced by promoting 1) the opportunity to perform, 2) reconsideration opportunity and 3) interpersonal effectiveness of officials [21].

Second, government agencies can select a suitable portfolio of mobile applications to fit their specific needs. For example, if government agencies need to facilitate citizen voice through mGovernment systems, our study suggests that they should optimize personal control functions of the systems in sequence, namely, ease of use, portability and active control. If government agencies hope to increase transparency by using mGovernment systems, they should optimize time-critical functions of the systems, such as real-time information dissemination and response timeliness. Information accuracy of mGovernment systems should be improved by strengthening location-based and MMS functions.

Our study provides system developers with a service evaluation framework for assessing mGovernment systems. First, the seven important antecedent factors (i.e., real-time information dissemination, response timeliness, MMS, location-tracking, portability, ease of use and active control) of user satisfaction with mGovernment systems have been identified. Accordingly, to evaluate mGovernment systems effectively, we suggest that system developers monitor these seven mobile technological functions as indicators. Second, our study can help system developers create suitable products to meet the different needs of government agencies. For instance, important determinants of transparency of mGovernment services are real-time information dissemination and response timeliness. Accordingly, to improve transparency, we suggest that system developers can adopt various instant messaging software, such as SMS, MMS, micro-message, e-mail and information notification technology, to design mGovernment systems [25]. To improve information accuracy of mGovernment services, we suggest that system developers embed various multimedia elements (e.g., video, audio and animation software) [25] or location and identification technologies (e.g., radio frequency identification and global positioning system technology) [61].

## 6.3. Limitation

We would like to acknowledge the limitations associated with this study. First, this study is cross-sectional, and data are self-reported. The CFA of competing models shows that common method bias is unlikely to be a threat to our results; however, future research could design longitudinal studies to replicate our research findings. Second, mGovernment services include M-Information service and Mtransaction service. We have focused only on M-Information of mGovernment services recognizing that the M-transaction aspect of mGovernment services is currently immature in many countries, especially in developing countries [48,54,59]. Future research could also examine which mobile functions can improve fairness of Mtransaction services and user satisfaction. The final limitation is that one item of our location-based service scale has relatively low loading (=0.57). Although prior research suggests 0.50 is a cut-off criterion of factor loading [19,32] and the results of measurement model comparison demonstrate that our measures have good validity, further research could use a finer measure of location-based services to explore whether our results can be replicated.

## 7. Conclusion

We successfully identified seven important MWT functions of mGovernment services and provided constructs to measure them. We also adopted the TTF perspective and TPF to explore how the seven MWT functions of mGovernment shape a fair procedure of governmental services, which, in turn, increase user satisfaction with mGovernment. Through a survey of experienced mGovernment service users in China, our study proved that (1) perceived procedural fairness significantly increases user satisfaction with mGovernment services; (2) time-critical functions (real-time information dissemination and response timeliness) improve procedural fairness through transparency; (3) location-sensitive functions and MMS improve procedural fairness through information accuracy; and (4) personal control functions (portability, ease of use and active control) improve procedural fairness through voice opportunity. Our study presents theoretical contributions to the literature of e/ mGovernment and fairness, provides insights into government agencies, and gives recommendations for system developers on how to design mGovernment services to ensure user satisfaction.
