# Drivers of context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT use across work and nonwork domains: A boundary theory perspective

K.L. Uthpala Senarathne Tennakoon <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Giovani J.C. da Silveira <sup>b,1</sup>, Daphne G. Taras <sup>c,2</sup>

## a r t i c l e i n f o

## a b s t r a c t

Most theories predicting technology use were conceived when information and communication technology (ICT) devices were less portable, had less functionality, and individuals experienced less permeability between work and nonwork domains. Thus, while individuals today widely use ICT for both professional and personal needs, there is limited knowledge about the validity of traditional models predicting ICT use within and across work and nonwork domains. Using a model based on boundary theory, border theory, and human agency perspective, this study empirically examines the predictors of ICT use across four contexts, namely work use on work and nonwork days, and nonwork use on work and nonwork days. The results, based on data collected from managers and professionals from different Canadian industries, suggest that different drivers may be affecting the four contexts of ICT use. While perceived usefulness of ICT is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor for both withindomain and across-domain ICT use, there are speci<sup>fi</sup>c work and individual characteristics that drive across-domain ICT use. Using theories from work/nonwork literature, this paper presents a renewed view to predict context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT use beyond the traditional models.

## 1. Introduction

Information and communication technology (ICT) has become ubiquitous and rapid technological advances have resulted in devices with sophisticated capabilities, multiple functionalities, and portability. In the past ICT was primarily associated with work-related use at work places, however today ICT enables the performance of nonwork tasks almost at equal intensity. Also, there is a substantial amount of cross-domain use of ICT, for work tasks during nonwork time and for nonwork tasks during work time. This article investigates whether we need to rethink the traditional models in order to predict these context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT uses.

There has been recognition that ICT now routinely straddles work and nonwork domains (Cousins & Robey, 2005; Fenner & Renn, 2010; Golden & Geisler, 2007; Schlosser, 2002). Some studies focused on speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies such as mobile phones (Hislop & Axtell. 2011: Waicman. Bittman. & Brown. 2008). BlackBerry® (Middleton & Cukier, 2006; Schlosser, 2002), and personal digitals assistants (PDA) (Golden & Geisler, 2007). Other studies focused on a combination of ICTs (Boswell & Olson-Buchanan, 2007; Chesley, 2005; Senarathne Tennakoon, 2011). While most early studies focused on technology use at work, more recent research have extended the early models into the nonwork realm (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005; Dwivedi, Williams, & Venkatesh, 2008; Venkatesh & Brown, 2001). Some recent studies have addressed a third, ‘hybrid’ type of ICT use across work and nonwork domains. For example, a survey by Muhl (2003) found that nearly 84% of American respondents regularly sent out nonwork-related e-mails from work. Another study reported that 64% of respondents spent at least one hour at work on nonwork-related matters, often supported by ICT (Salary.com, 2009). Likewise, work encroaches into the nonwork domain via ICT. Managers and professionals are known to routinely take their work into their private hours with no additional compensation, and 80% of all people who brought work home used ICT (United States Department of Labor, 2005). And the evidence of ICT facilitating the intrusion of work into private lives via buzzing smart phones, email during vacations, and so on, is growing signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly (Chesley, 2005; Cousins & Robey, 2005; Golden & Geisler, 2007; Golden, Veiga, & Simsek, 2006; Perry, O'Hara, Sellen, Brown, & Harper, 2001; Richardson & Benbunan-Fich, 2011; Schlosser, 2002).

The literature in the past few years has recognized that ICT is used across work and nonwork domains and the same multi-functional devices are carried with the person, reducing transaction costs and eliminating access issues. However, we were unable to locate a major study that addressed predictors of ICT across multiple contexts of use, to understand whether speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors might explain usage in different contexts simultaneously. Most theories predicting technology use were conceived and tested when ICT was not readily portable, when ICT devices had less functionality, and when individuals experienced less permeability between work and nonwork domains. Therefore, the traditional models of predicting ICT use have limited knowledge and predictive capabilities when it comes to such cross-domain ICT use.

As ICT becomes increasingly pervasive in every domain of human activity, and the boundaries between work and nonwork turn fuzzier, there is a need for research to assess predictors of ICT use across multiple domains. Thus, our study builds and tests a predictive model of ICT use across four different contexts, namely (i) work-related use on work days, (ii) work-related use on nonwork days, (iii) nonwork-related use on work days, and (iv) nonwork-related use on nonwork days.

Building on evidence that ICT is blurring boundaries between work and nonwork (Aoki & Downes, 2003; Chesley, 2005; Dinger, Thatcher, & Stepina, 2010; Gant & Kiesler, 2001; Senarathne Tennakoon & Taras, 2008), our <sup>fi</sup>rst objective is to identify the impact of perceived ICT utility, a key predictor in the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), on all four contexts of ICT use. Second, we explore the role of external in<sup>fl</sup>uences – work demands, nonwork demands, and work <sup>fl</sup>exibility – in driving speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts of ICT use. ICTs that help people do their jobs are also loaded with applications that may enrich lives. With so many functions being performed by ICT devices, including applications for personal use, it is necessary to test if external predictors explain ICT use at work, outside work, and across work and nonwork domains. Finally, we examine the impact of individual preference for role segmentation on ICT use within and across work and nonwork domains. In summary, this study builds on the phenomenon of dissolving life–work boundaries to determine the antecedents of ICT use within and across work and nonwork domains.

From a management perspective, knowing the predictors of ICT use for work and nonwork may help to design policies that maximize work productivity, reduce cyberslacking (Johnson & Indvik, 2004) and cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (Lim, 2002), and minimize burnout (Salanova & Schaufeli, 2000). Using ICT to promote “24/7 work cultures” (Patel, 2002) may increase productivity and availability in the short term, but also lead to stress and reduced work–life balance. Better understanding of predictors of modern day ICT use would lead to more effective ICT policies being developed in business organizations.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Theoretical perspectives of work and nonwork boundary negotiations

The theoretical background follows propositions from work–family border theory (Clark, 2000), boundary theory (Ashforth, Kreiner, & Fugate, 2000), and human agency perspective (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). Human agency theory addresses the interplay between individuals' actions and the structural environment, arguing that observed behaviors are created through volition, via the interplay of habit, imagination, and judgment (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998: 970). Both boundary theory and border theory resonate well with the human agency perspective. Border theory is based on a similar premise that people through their interaction with and across work and nonwork domains shape their environments and, in turn, are shaped by them (Clark, 2000). Similarly, boundary theory discusses how individuals create or dismantle boundaries across work/nonwork domains to foster segmentation or integration based on their desires, previous experiences, social domain, and contexts (Ashforth et al., 2000). In the following section we discuss these theories in more detail and how these theories shape our theoretical framework.

Conceptualized and presented independently in 2000, both boundary and border theories focus on the integration and blurring of the work/nonwork boundary, and advocate the active role individuals play in shaping the role boundary and boundary transactions. Except for few minor differences identi<sup>fi</sup>ed below, these theories have many similarities in their underlying arguments and propositions. Work–family border theory focuses on work and family domains and the tangible and psychological boundaries across domains. The outcome focus is work–family balance (Clark, 2000). On the other hand, boundary theory is a general cognitive theory of social classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and the focus is on the outcomes of work–nonwork interactions, for example, the meanings people assign to work and nonwork (Desrochers & Sargent, 2002).

Both theories describe <sup>fl</sup>exibility and boundary permeability as key conditions that facilitate boundary crossing by individuals. Flexibility determines the ability for individuals to transfer between multiple roles they may be playing during daily activities by shifting the temporal and spatial boundaries such as work time and work location (Ashforth et al., 2000). For example, a teleworking woman may have to shift between being a mother and a professional throughout the work day, and may sometimes shift the “work time” to coincide with “time when children are asleep.” On the other hand, in<sup>fl</sup>exible boundaries can restrict such free movement across multiple roles of an individual (e.g., a security guard is forbidden from allowing attention to waver from the work).

Permeability determines the ability of individuals to psychologically or behaviorally shift to work or nonwork domain while being physically located in the other domain (Ashforth et al., 2000). When high levels of border permeability are present individuals may easily shift mental gears to engage in nonwork-related tasks while at work or vice-versa.

Most individuals use <sup>fl</sup>exibility and permeability of the border to facilitate border crossings in their daily activities (Clark, 2000). While each domain has unique demands involving goals, focus, language, and behavior, there are many bridges and cues that allow individuals to make transitions across work/ nonwork border in an intermittent manner (Zhao, Lee, & Soman, 2012). When the phone rings, the individual can almost instantaneously adjust to the demands of a work supervisor or to a distressed child. Boundary theory distinguished between “macro” and “micro” transitions. Macro transitions are infrequent and more permanent in nature (e.g., retirement), whereas micro role transitions are recurring moves between work and nonwork domains (Ashforth et al., 2000). The attention in the current study is on recurring transitions from work to nonwork and vice-versa with the aid of ICT, and thus we focus on micro transitions across the work/nonwork border.

## 2.2. Context-specific and continuing ICT use

Highly functional and extremely portable modern ICT tools provide almost seamless use for both work and nonwork purposes (Hill, Ferris, & Martinson, 2003). Thus, ICT use today is commonplace be it at work, away from work, or across the work–nonwork border (Chesley, 2005; Gant & Kiesler, 2001; Schlosser, 2002;

Senarathne Tennakoon & Taras, 2008). Therefore, we examined ICT use under four distinct contexts. Following the model adopted by Cousins and Robey (2005), who used a human agency perspective to present the nomadic computing environment of a group of loan of<sup>fi</sup>cers, we also propose a three-dimensional context to examine ICT use of individuals.

First we consider the domain of use, i.e., whether use happens on a work or nonwork day. Second, we consider the purpose of use, i.e., work- or nonwork-related. These two dimensions together create a matrix of four usage contexts; namely work use on work days (Wk\_WD), work use on nonwork days (Wk\_NWD), nonwork use on work days (NWk\_WD), and nonwork use on nonwork days (NWk\_NWD). On a third, temporal dimension, we focus on the continued use of a group of technologies, with current use as a reference point. Let us clarify this further.

Human agency perspective identi<sup>fi</sup>es three temporal orientations: iterational element, projective element, and practical-evaluative element (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). The iterational element deals with habits, norms, and logical sequences of thoughts that have driven individual actions over time until the present moment (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). The projective element allows people to make plans for the future. The practical-evaluative element is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the “capacity for actors to make practical and normative judgment among alternative possible trajectories of action, in response to the emerging demands, dilemmas, and ambiguities of presently evolving situations” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998: 971). Therefore, when individuals report “ICT use on a typical day during last week”, we are most likely gathering a simple snap-shot of their practical-evaluative of ICT use, based on their continued use of ICT overtime (i.e., iterational element). This is in contrast to previous models predicting technology use where the focus has been on the intention to use technology (e.g., TAM, Davis, 1989). Fig. 1 shows the conceptualization of context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT use in this three-dimensional scenario.

We propose that drivers for each context of ICT use may be different. Therefore, using border theory, boundary theory, and human agency perspective we develop a set of hypotheses to examine this proposition. The following section details our arguments.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Perceived usefulness

Perceived utility, <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced as a key predictor in the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), is now a well-established predictor of work-related ICT adoption and use. TAM identi<sup>fi</sup>es perceived utility (usefulness) as to how much individuals believe that technology enhances job performance (Davis, 1989). There have been several enhancements to TAM over the years. TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000) added social in<sup>fl</sup>uence processes (i.e., subjective norms, voluntariness, and image) and cognitive instrumental processes to the original model. The more comprehensive TAM3 (Venkatesh & Bala, 2008)

A meta-analysis of 88 studies (King & He, 2006) found TAM to be a powerful and robust predictive model; the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived utility on behavioral intention for technology use was profound, with a correlation of .49.

In more recent studies, perceived utility has been recognized as a predictor not only of work-related ICT usage, but also of computer usage at home (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005). This double association suggests that if individuals see a particular technology as useful, they will be more inclined to use it in either organizational or non-organizational environments (Green & Pearson, 2011). Human agency perspective suggests that the present behavior of an individual is derived from past experience (iterational element) and future expectation (projective element) (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). Therefore, if individuals consider ICT as useful in work settings, they may expect it to be similarly useful in nonwork settings. Further, if individuals perceive ICT to be useful in work and nonwork settings, they may be more inclined to use it across work and nonwork domains. As individuals can proactively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the nature of their work/nonwork border to suit their needs (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000), they may use ICTs to increase their <sup>fl</sup>exibility and permeability. Thus, we argue that if individuals have a positive perception towards the usefulness of technology, they will be more inclined to use technology anytime, anywhere. This leads to our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Perceived utility of ICT will relate positively with ICT use in all four contexts, namely work-related use on work days, work-related use on nonwork days, nonwork-related use on work days, and nonwork-related use on nonwork days.

## 3.2. Work/nonwork segmentation

Nippert-Eng (1996) argued that boundaries between work and nonwork were along a continuum ranging from anchors of (i) fully integrated and indistinguishable, to (ii) fully segmented and distinct from each other. There appears to be a strong empirical support for increased integration of the work/nonwork domain with the use of ICT (Aoki & Downes, 2003; Gant & Kiesler, 2001; Olson-Buchanan & Boswell, 2006; Rakow & Navarro, 1993). For example, teleworkers, who are heavy users of ICT for work, have reported inability to separate between work and family domains (Ellison, 1999; Hill, Miller, Weiner, & Colihan, 1998). The issue is determining to what extent individuals or organizations <sup>fi</sup>ercely maintain segmentation, or whether there are points in-between that allow for some integration by blurring the boundaries (Ashforth et al., 2000; Edwards & Rothbard, 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996).

For example, people segmenting the two domains could keep different calendars for work and nonwork activities, use separate rings for work and home keys, and have separate wardrobes for work and nonwork clothes (D'Abate, 2005). Those integrating more could allow work interactions to follow home and vice versa. Rothbard, Phillips, and Dumas (2005) compared the complete segmentation of roles by an exotic dancer who would not disclose her job to friends or family, to complete integration of a nun having life and job in a convent. While these are extreme situations, most individuals operate at an intermediary position of segmentation or integration across the work and nonwork boundary (Nippert-Eng, 1996).

This separation may be also observed in the use of technology. People could regulate the boundary by using two cellular phones, keeping separate e-mail addresses for work and nonwork (Senarathne Tennakoon & Taras, 2008), earmarking certain devices only for work or switching off devices while away from work (Schlosser, 2002). However, the more common observations have been that segmentation of the two domains will happen at an intermediary rather than extreme position in a continuous scale (Ashforth et al., 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996). Also, as discussed, individuals' preferences, organizational requirements, and the dynamics of private lifestyles all will determine the intensity of effort to maintain segmentation or allow permeability. When individuals attempt to keep work and private domains apart, they are engaged in a strategy of segmentation, in which they limit the micro-transitions that cross the work/nonwork border. Thus, it is argued that individuals with a high preference for segmentation would limit their cross domain ICT use, leading to our next hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. Segmentation will relate negatively with cross-domain ICT use. In other words, higher levels of segmentation will be associated with lower levels of work ICT use on nonwork days and nonwork ICT use on work days.

## 3.3. Work flexibility

Both border theory and boundary theory have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fl</sup>exibility as an important characteristic in boundary management. Flexibility promotes border crossing (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000), and ICT acts as a tool that create bridges across these borders (Clark, 2000).

Work <sup>fl</sup>exibility includes the autonomy to reallocate work hours (<sup>fl</sup>ex-time) and choose where work is done (<sup>fl</sup>ex-place) (Hill, Hawkins, Ferris, & Weitzman, 2001). The ability to perform work-related tasks from a nonwork location using ICT is determined by the characteristics of the work. For example, whereas a junior engineer may be able to attend to a system fault remotely by logging into an IT network from home, a senior surgeon cannot (to this day, at least) perform a surgery without being physically present at the hospital theater.

Telework literature has examined relationships between work-<sup>fl</sup>exibility and ICT use. Studies have reported that ICT facilitates work <sup>fl</sup>exibility in teleworking (Hill, Hawkins, & Miller, 1996) and that work-<sup>fl</sup>exibility and control relate positively with supplementary work performed away from work via ICT (Venkatesh & Vitalari, 1992). In proposing a model for assessing predictors for technology-assisted supplemental work (i.e., doing work-related tasks in nonwork time) Fenner and Renn (2004) stressed the importance of supporting organizational climate, where work-<sup>fl</sup>exibility formed an important attribute.

The ubiquity of ICT may result not only in work being performed at home, but also in shifting nonwork activities to the work domain (Senarathne Tennakoon & Taras, 2008). These nonwork activities may include both relevant, time saving tasks such as online banking, and non-essential and unproductive tasks, such as gaming or social networking (see Young & Case, 2004 for a review). To carry out nonwork activities at work, workers may require a certain amount of <sup>fl</sup>exibility, especially <sup>fl</sup>ex-time. While this phenomenon might occur at all levels of the organizational hierarchy. Garrett and Danziger (2008) found that “cyberslacking" (the term used by them to describe any nonwork activity during working hours) was more frequent among workers with greater status and work autonomy, likely because these workers have <sup>fl</sup>exibility to regulate their work pace. Furthermore, studies have indicated that facilitating conditions such as individual's autonomy to use IT, whenever and however they preferred, related positively with continuous IT use (Bhattacherjee, Perols, & Sanford, 2008).

In summary, even though ICT provides the potential to perform tasks anytime anywhere, work-<sup>fl</sup>exibility is still required to allow individuals to perform work-related tasks on nonworking days, and nonwork-related tasks on working days. As identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the border theory (Clark, 2000) and the boundary theory (Ashforth et al., 2000), <sup>fl</sup>exibility is a critical factor required to facilitate cross-border intonations. This is also well aligned with the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) framework (Dishaw & Strong, 1999), which matches technology capabilities to job characteristics (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). It posits that ICT will be used only when its functionality <sup>fi</sup>ts the work design (Dishaw & Strong, 1999). ICT, with its ability to create access anytime, anywhere, will thus facilitate across-domain functioning as long as it is made possible by work <sup>fl</sup>exibility. Therefore, we hypothesize,

Hypothesis 3. Work <sup>fl</sup>exibility will relate positively with work-related ICT use on nonwork days and with nonwork-related ICT use on work days.

## 3.4. Work demands

Work demands re<sup>fl</sup>ect the expectations and time pressures an individual faces at work (Kinicki & Vecchio, 1994; Matteson & Ivancevich, 1987). An individual facing heavy workload and time pressure may be more inclined to bring work home via ICT. This has been called “technology assisted supplemental work” (Fenner & Renn, 2004, p.179; Fenner & Renn, 2010, p.63). For example, a survey by the United States Department of Labor (2005) found that that the majority of the respondents brought work home to “finish or catch up on their work." Mazmanian. Orlikowski. and Yates (2006) outlined individuals' experiences and outcomes of using BlackBerry® devices for e-mail purposes to cope with autonomous, <sup>fl</sup>exible, and demanding work schedules. They suggested that such individuals were likely to use these devices to attend to work-related matters during nonwork hours. Senarathne Tennakoon and Taras (2008) similarly found that when work demands were high, ICT helped individuals transfer work to the nonwork domain, to ease the burden of heavy workloads.

The border theory and the boundary theory suggest that individuals could act proactively to shape the work/nonwork boundary to suit needs at hand. Faced with high work demands, they could use ICT tools to attend to work matters during nonwork days and ease the burden placed on their work domain. This is also in line with the human agency perspective (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998) which suggests that when individuals have high demands, dilemmas, and ambiguities, they make practical and normative choices among alternative trajectories of action (p. 971). With ICTs' ability to facilitate working from anywhere, anytime we argue that individuals with high work demands may (i) use ICT to perform work tasks on nonwork days and (ii) use ICTs more during work days. This argument is also supported by the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t framework (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995) which matches the use of technologies to the characteristics of the tasks at hand. In this case, high work demands could be better met with more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ICT use during work days, and performing work-related ICT use during nonwork days. Therefore, it is proposed:

Hypothesis 4. Work demands will relate positively with work-related ICT use on work days and nonwork days.

## 3.5. Nonwork demands

In balancing day to day activities, individuals are also faced with numerous nonwork demands such as family, caregiving, leisure, and recreation. These may require individuals' attention also during work hours. While some activities may require a physical presence, many might be completed via ICT means. Using a similar argument presented for work demands earlier, we propose that when individuals are faced with high nonwork demands, they tend to cross the work/nonwork border via ICT to ease the burden of that particular domain. Therefore, individuals with high nonwork demands could also be high users of ICT for nonwork on work days, leading to the next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5. Nonwork demands will relate positively with nonwork-related ICT use on work days and nonwork days.

## 4. Data

Study data were collected in 2008 as part of a broader multi-country web-based survey on ICT use and work–life balance. The data for this study is from Canadian managers and professionals' use of ICT for work and nonwork purposes. We use only one country to eliminate confounds due to differences in culture, labor legislation, and work practices. As the behavior under scrutiny involved boundary crossing, we sought a sample in which there would be a high adoption of ICT devices, greater autonomy in regulating work tasks and pace, and a higher likelihood of owning or carrying portable technologies that travel from work to home and back. Their compensation structures relate more to speci<sup>fi</sup>c duties and responsibilities than to prescribed hours of work. These workers often face a heavy workload despite the modern managerial rhetoric that encourages “work–life balance.” Although this population limits the generalizability of our study (Johns, 2006), it is fertile terrain upon which we develop explanations for technology use at work and at home. Furthermore, collecting responses from actual workers improves the reliability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings. While many studies of IT adoption have used student samples, King and He (2006) found that these samples might systematically distort results because of youth effects and less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant work or family obligations.

Target respondents were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed via professional organizations, university alumni lists, and personal contacts of two of the researchers. Prospective participants received an invitation e-mail containing a link to the web-based survey, and they were encouraged to forward the study details to friends and colleagues meeting selection criteria for the study. Even though this method led to a non-random sample, it helped obtain a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large number of responses for statistical testing without loss of power and with good reliability. It was not possible to calculate a response rate because the actual number of recipients of the invitation e-mail is not known. However, the web-based survey system provided an estimate that about 80% of the individuals who logged in did complete the questionnaire. The online survey had eight sections and included questions on respondents' work characteristics, individual characteristics, work and nonwork interaction, and ICT use. The respondents were provided with assurances of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and, as an incentive for participation, were offered the option to enlist in a draw to win three CDN \$100 prizes in the form of cash or as a delivery of <sup>fl</sup>owers to their recipient of choice.

The survey yielded 425 valid cases from Canadian participants, representing a wide range of industries including oil and gas (16%), <sup>fi</sup>nancial services (16%), government (16%), consultancy and professional services (12%), IT and telecommunication (9%), and education and research (7%). Other represented industries include manufacturing, power and utilities, healthcare, legal, transportation, and non-for pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Thus, while there was a wide representation of industries, no industry appeared to be clearly dominant. Participants were from an array of divisions within organizations such as general management (16%), sales and marketing (15%), <sup>fi</sup>nance (12%), accounting (10%), human resources (9%), and operations (9%). Other represented divisions include engineering, IT, legal, and self-employed individuals. Most respondents had a high level of education (Table 1) and were relatively heavy users of technology. Individuals also reported their annual household income, by selecting one of <sup>fi</sup>ve ranges in Canadian dollars. For the analysis this was incorporated as an ordinal variable of 5 steps, ranging from “less than \$60,000” to “greater than \$180,000” with increments of \$40,000 between steps.

## 5. Measures

In the following we describe the development of dependent, independent, and control variables. Variable scales and items are transcribed in Appendix A. All of the scales were validated using re<sup>fl</sup>ective approaches involving extensive pre-testing because of conceptual overlaps between their constituent items (Bollen & Lennox, 1991).

## 5.1. Dependent variables

Dependent variables depicted ICT use by individuals in four different contexts. Each one was based on the sum of the respondents' estimate of the number of hours of using three different ICT functions (e-mail, Internet, and portable communication devices) during typical working and nonworking days (Appendix A). Similar to other studies that have used an index to represent ICT use (Boswell & Olson-Buchanan, 2007;

Richardson & Benbunan-Fich, 2011), the summed <sup>fi</sup>gure measures the respondents' estimate of actua hours of ICT use. We also required scoring of work and nonwork ICT use, consistent with the rationale in previous studies (e.g., Klaus, Gyires, & Wen, 2003; Prasopoulou, Pouloudi, & Panteli, 2006). We also queried within- and across-domain ICT use in order to create four dependent variables to account for different contexts of ICT use, namely work-related use on work days (Wk WD). work-related use on nonwork days (Wk\_NWD), nonwork-related use on work days (NWk\_WD), and nonwork-related use on nonwork days (NWk\_NWD). To check the reliability of the responses, we estimated correlations between the quantitative responses (the dependent variables used in our study) and average scores given to a separate survey question on the perceived use of e-mail, Internet, laptop, cell phone, and PDA/BlackBerry® for work and nonwork tasks: “How often do vou use the following types of technologies/devices for [1 work-related; 2. private/nonwork-related] activities?” (responses were provided using a seven-point scale with endpoints “Never” [1] and “All the time” [7]). Perceived use of ICT for work correlated positively with Wk\_WD (r = .51, p b .001) and Wk\_NWD (r = .42, p b .001); perceived use of ICT for nonwork correlated positively with NWk\_WD (r = .44, p b .001) and NWk\_NWD (r = .40, p b .001).

## 5.2. Independent variables

Studies have used alternative scales to assess the “perceived utility” of ICT often based on Davis's (1989) seminal work. Whereas Davis (1989) de<sup>fi</sup>ned utility in relation to “job performance,” our study assessed the level of ICT support to both work and nonwork applications. Thus, perceived utility of ICT (ICTP) was measured by the six-item scale adopted from Chesley (2005), which assessed perception of utility in both work and nonwork domains.

The respondents rated statements regarding technology support to work and life responsibilities. The Cronbach's alpha of the six-item scale was .60, which is below the recommended levels for established scales (Hair, Black, Babin, Anderson, & Tatham, 2010). Exploratory factor analysis with Varimax rotation indicated that two of the six items (ICTP4 and ICTP5) load on a different component (see Appendix B for details). In effect, these items referred to ICT in<sup>fl</sup>uence on exigency aspects (pace of life and amount of work) that contrasted sharply with “quality of life” and work effectiveness aspects in the remaining items. Thus, the ICTP scale was derived by the average responses to items ICTP1, ICTP2, ICTP3, and ICTP6, which had a Cronbach's alpha of .77.

The four-item scale of segmentation (also known as work/nonwork blurring scale) was adopted from work–family blurring scale (Desrochers, Hilton, & Larwood, 2005) and segmentation scale (Sumer & Knight, 2001). The responses were on a seven-point scale with anchors “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree” with two reverse coded items (i.e., items 1 and 3) where high values represented high integration. The Cronbach's alpha for the four-item scale was only .40. Close examination of the items and responses suggested that item 4 appears to be misaligned with the rest of the items and was removed. The three-item scale showed improved reliability at .58, but suggested the removal of item 3 (which was supported by the low loading at the CFA). It appears that the respondents have interpreted SEG3 slightly differently from the other items in the scale. The removal of SEG3 and SEG4 resulted in a segmentation scale of two items with an alpha of .61.

The assessment of work demands (DEM) was based on average responses to items in the time pressure subscale from the Stress Diagnostic Survey by Matteson and Ivancevich (1987). ## 5.3. Measurement model

We used con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis in IBM® SPSS® Amos 19.0.0 (Arbuckle, 2010) to test the validity and reliability of measures following procedures in Fornell and Larcker (1981) and Hair et al. (2010). The model included the eight dependent and independent variables, and the categorical variable nonwork demands. Dependent variables were speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as latent scales with single observed variables, and their error term variances were constrained to zero (Brown, 2006, p.139). Independent variables were speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as latent scales with their respective items as observed variables.

In the initial model, four of the seventeen standardized regression weights (λ) between re<sup>fl</sup>ective items and independent scales were below .6 (Steenkamp & van Trijp, 1991). FLEX3 (.38), SEG3 (.34), and SEG4 (.07) had very low λ values and was dropped. ICTP1 had a somewhat low λ (.54), but was retained for theoretical reasons, as it was the only ICTP item focused exclusively on ICT support to work tasks (as opposed to life or work–life tasks). The re<sup>fi</sup>ned model had a good evidence of validity and reliability (Table 2). All perceptual scales had an average variance extracted (AVE) close to or above .50, which is an evidence of convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hair et al., 2010), and composite reliability (CR) estimates above .6 (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2000). Correlations between latent variables were all below AVE square roots, indicating appropriate discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

We carried out two methods prescribed by Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, and Podsakoff (2003) to identify the potential for common method bias (CMB) in our data. First, a model testing Harman's one factor hypothesis was speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by linking all observed variables to a single latent scale [the variance of the scale was set to one as no marker indicator was used; see, for example, Hair et al. (2010)]. Fit estimates were quite poor $[ \chi ^ { 2 } / D F = 1 2 . 0 5 ;$ ; NFI = .41; TLI = .25; CFI = .42; $R M S E A = . 1 6 ~ ( 9 0 \% ~ \mathrm { C I } = . 1 5 \mathrm { - } . 1 7 ) ]$ , indicating the inadequacy of a single-factor structure.

The second test controlled for a CMB factor. The latent CMB factor was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by all observed variables in a model that maintained the latent scales. We carried out four separate tests, one for each dependent variable and the remaining independent scales. Results showed that all coef<sup>fi</sup>cients from latent to observed variables remained positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\left( p < . 0 5 \right)$ in all four models, suggesting no CMB in our data (MacKenzie, Podsakoff, & Paine, 1999; Podsakoff et al., 2003).

## 6.1. Robustness tests

We performed robustness tests under alternative modeling procedures to further check the reliability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, recall that we used the EM method to replace missing values of the quantitative variables. Therefore, we carried out a new set of regressions using straight listwise deletion of cases with missing values (listwise n ranged from 339 to 345). To approximate residuals to normal distribution, we again transformed outcome variables to natural logarithms. The analysis broadly supported the previous results with a few exceptions. The FLEX coef<sup>fi</sup>cient in Wk\_NWD changed from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to near signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p = .09). The SEG coef<sup>fi</sup>cient changed from non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to near signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = . 0 6 )$ in the NWk\_WD model. ICTP coef<sup>fi</sup>cient in the Wk\_WD model changed from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\left( p < . 0 5 \right)$ to near signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = . 0 6 )$

We also tested speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (after EM substitution) excluding the four co-variants $( n = 4 2 5 )$ . Results regarding the NWk\_NWD and Wk\_NWD models were unchanged in regard to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance or nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cance of predictors. However, in the Wk\_WD model, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for work demands changed from nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant to near signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = . 0 6 )$ . Also, in the NWk\_WD model, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for nonwork demands changed from nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant to near signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = . 0 9 )$ . Overall, results from the original analysis appeared to be robust in regard to alternative data and model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations except for the differences indicated above.

## 7. Discussion

We developed and tested a predictive model of ICT use in four speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts, namely work use on work days (Wk\_WD), work use on nonwork days (Wk\_NWD), nonwork use on work days (NWk\_WD), and nonwork use on nonwork days (NWk\_NWD). Our analysis included <sup>fi</sup>ve predictors, including two individual perceptions (perceived utility of ICT and segmentation) and three external in<sup>fl</sup>uences (work <sup>fl</sup>exibility, work demands, and nonwork demands). Even with a limited number of predictors, our study has much to contribute to the literature.

The results indicate that different factors may indeed explain different contexts of ICT use. For example, while work <sup>fl</sup>exibility was a strong predictor of cross-domain ICT use in both directions (i.e., work use in nonwork days and nonwork use in work days), it had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in within domain ICT use. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are well aligned with the theoretical framework since the border theory (Ashforth et al., 2000) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed role <sup>fl</sup>exibility as a key feature in<sup>fl</sup>uencing micro role transitions. ICT thus demonstrated the ability to create such micro transitions by acting as a border-crossing bridge (Clark, 2000). The selected predictors were far more successful in predicting cross-domain ICT use compared to within-domain ICT use. In addition, the study focused on continued ICT use, compared to the intention to use these technologies. Therefore, the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be useful in assessing the current usage patterns of ICT users, and based on the human agency perspective, such information could be used to predict behavior beyond the current scenarios to future usage.

This study added to the substantial prior empirical work that dealt with rational calculations of the utility of ICT in determining subsequent ICT adoption and usage (Davis, 1989; Davis, Bagozzi, & Warshaw, 1989; King & He, 2006; Venkatesh, 2000; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). We found that a person's perception towards ICT was a predictor of not only work-related ICT use on work settings but also in nonwork and cross-domain settings. The information systems (IS) literature supports that perceived usefulness of ICT predicts work-related use and nonwork-related use (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005). This paper adds to this literature by establishing the validity of perceived usefulness of ICT as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributor towards cross-domain ICT use. In other words, there is generalizability of an important component of the TAM model into both within- and across-domain ICT use.

One of our most intriguing <sup>fi</sup>ndings involved the impact of segmentation. People with a strong preference for segmentation tend to prevent work from <sup>fl</sup>owing into their home lives, but they do allow permeability of nonwork tasks into their working days. Nippert-Eng (1996) argued that individuals with a high segmentation preference tend to keep work and nonwork separate and try to limit nonwork elements during the work day and vice versa. As expected, work use on nonwork days was strongly and negatively related to segmentation. It appears that individuals who segment work and nonwork would limit their work encroaching to the nonwork domain by limiting their ICT use for work purposes during nonwork times. However segmentation was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the model predicting nonwork ICT use on work days. Previous studies found that the family boundary was more absorptive of work demands than vice-versa (Frone, Russell, & Cooper, 1992; Gutek, Searle, & Klepa, 1991; Hall & Richter, 1988): i.e., work is more likely to intrude into the nonwork domain than vice-versa. It is possible that individuals with a high segmentation preference are more mindful of this encroachment and are more cognizant of limiting work encroachment into the nonwork domain rather than the reverse direction. We can only speculate that people who indicate that they value segmentation may give greater priority to their family or private life and hence guard this domain from intrusion. But because they cherish their private lives, they do not screen out nonwork demands when they occur on working time.

Although not expected, we also found that segmentation was negatively associated with work ICT use on work days. Simply put, segmentation seems to be a luxury afforded to those whose work demands are less than our respondents who became integrators. This argument gains support from the relatively high negative correlation between work demands and segmentation $( r = - . 4 4 )$ . It could be that heavy work demands are driving work-related ICT use and at the same time driving the need for the use of ICT devices to cross the work/nonwork border leading to more border permeability and less segmentation (Ashforth et al., 2000). People with higher work demands are forced by their circumstances to become boundary-straddlers in order to manage the frantic-paced lives. Hence, it is not simply an af<sup>fi</sup>nity for technology, or a perceived utility of technology that drives boundary-blurring among managers, but there also is an element of necessity. We expected nonwork demands to be a significant contributor towards nonwork-related ICT use on both nonwork days and work days. However, nonwork demands did not appear as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributor for any of the contexts. It could be that some of nonwork demands captured in our data (e.g., elder care) may not be resolved using ICT means. Alternatively, it could be that these days there is much less variability in our respondents' use of technology to manage nonwork matters, so there is a rather steady amount of nonwork utilization across all domains. Perhaps people are inured to the impact of using ICT for nonwork matters regardless of domains, whereas they are more sensitive to the impact of work demands.

## 7.1. Limitations

The study adds to the literature by presenting a context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c view of ICT use and predictors of ICT use across different contexts. However, the study is not without limitations. First, rather than exploring an exhaustive list of predictors, we tested a parsimonious model driven by work/nonwork theory. The model focuses on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of external (work demands, work <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and nonwork demands) and interna (segmentation and perceived usefulness of ICT) factors predicting ICT use. These constructs appeared to be more successful in predicting cross-domain ICT use compared to within-domain ICT use. Therefore, future studies may need to specify and test the role of alternative predictors.

Second, we examine the respondents' aggregate estimates of ICT use during typical work and nonworking “days” rather than monitoring their speci<sup>fi</sup>c use throughout the day. Ideally the respondents could have provided daily diaries, however, few research subjects would have agreed to such an arduous method. Using a questionnaire, we had to limit respondent fatigue in order to have a higher reliability of responses and completion of the survey (Galesic & Bosnjak, 2009; Holbrook, Krosnick, Moore, & Tourangeau, 2007). By using our approach we could not distinguish if ICT use, for example, for nonwork in workdays referred to nonwork usage during regular work hours or outside work hours (those hours were considered as part of a ‘working day’). However, in a sample of managers, “hours of work” would lack precision regardless, as there is considerable evidence that managers' working days are long and their salaried positions lead to substantial unpaid overtime. In our pre-tests, it was clear that managers understood the concept of a work day and a nonwork day, but they would have been hard-pressed to describe hours of work in a typical day without the onerous step of keeping a daily diary. Thus, our results may be imprecise due to our inability to accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the respondents' actual schedules and ICT use on a more micro-basis.

The respondents reported estimated times for use of speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICTs (i.e., email, Internet, and portable communication devices), where the primary focus was communication and being connected to the external world. Therefore, our data did not capture the full spectrum of “technology assisted supplemental work” identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Fenner and Renn (2004, 2010), for example using a computer at home for work purposes by word processing or number crunching. Such work is no different to “old days,” when managers might be sitting at their kitchen tables pouring over documents and charts and making revisions by pen and pencil. While such tasks could amount to substantial amount of work performed beyond work hours, the focus of this paper is to ascertain whether speci<sup>fi</sup>c communication technologies (i.e., email, Internet, and portable communication devices) create border permeations and micro transitions across the work/nonwork border.

Data were collected in 2008. Then, except for BlackBerry® there were not many products with email in a hand-held device. More recent devices have changed the way individuals use ICT, especially in the amalgamation of email, Internet, and text/voice functionality of cellular phones almost seamlessly into a single hand-held device. Therefore, caution should be applied when generalizing the results into today's context.

The survey was very speci<sup>fi</sup>c about the reporting of Internet use, where it requested “non email access use.” However, such speci<sup>fi</sup>cations were not given for portable communication devices and emails. Since email could be accessed via some portable communication devices (e.g., Blackberry)<sup>3</sup> there is a chance of the respondents misreporting hours of use, for example by duplicating use time leading to double counting.<sup>4</sup> To assess that risk, we ran frequency distribution analysis and correlation analysis for the usage patterns reported for email and portable communication devices. The frequency distribution pattern was very similar for all types of technologies. Correlations between email use and cell phone use in each of the contexts was positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but not extremely high [e.g., Wk\_WD: r (419) = .28, p b .001; Wk\_NWD: r (417) = .45, p b .001; NWk\_WD: r (413) = .28, p b .001; and NWk\_NWD: r (410) = .17, p b .001]. Therefore, it was safe to assume that even if few respondents had reported the ICT usage in both email and cell phone use, the effect on the analysis would have been minimal.

Our segmentation scale had only two items as two other items (SEG3 and SEG4) didn't stand the reliability testing. To further examine impact of the elimination of SEG3 and SEG4 from the analysis, we correlated each item in the original segmentation scale with the dependent variables. None of the items showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlation with nonwork-use on nonwork days (NWk\_NWD). SEG4 was not significantly correlated with any of the dependent variables. SEG1. 2 and 3 showed a significant negative correlation with ICT use on work days for both work and nonwork purposes. SEG3 was the only item that showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative correlation with nonwork ICT use on work days. Therefore, while the elimination of SEG4 would have had a minimal impact, the elimination of SEG3 could have had some effect on the <sup>fi</sup>nal results. We ran a post-hoc analysis to see if there is a considerable impact of the exclusion of SEG3 from the composite scale. The results were very similar to that with the composite segmentation scale without SEG3 as an item, even in predicting nonwork ICT use on workdays. Therefore, while eliminating SEG 3 and 4 did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcomes, this would have led to more reliable and valid results with the use of a more robust scale.

We measured nonwork demands using an ordinal scale based on the sum of nonwork demands on an individual which included elder care, education/training, volunteering/community, sports/<sup>fi</sup>tness, relationship status, and presence of children. However, this failed to recognize the intensities of certain nonwork demands in comparison to others (e.g., a working single mother with children could face a higher intensity of nonwork demands compared to a single individual who is doing an MBA, who volunteers and engaged in sports or <sup>fi</sup>tness program<sup>5</sup>). While we could have adjusted for the intensity of nonwork demands through a proxy variable, for example recognizing pressures faced by single parents or divorced/widowed individuals with children, we chose not to make assumptions about the respondents' subjective experiences with intensity of effort. For example, a single parent might be noncustodial, while another respondent might have extraordinarily high eldercare duties. Secondly, our item does not capture the intensity of an individual's social interactions. The intensity of social interactions could manifest in the form of a high volume of personal emails, phone conversations, and most importantly use of social media sites (e.g., Facebook). This could have an implication on, for example, nonwork ICT use on work days and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally leading to underestimating the strengths of relationships for Hypothesis 5. While recognizing this limitation, it is important to emphasize that the prevalence of social media use is far greater today than when we surveyed our respondents in 2008.

We asked participants to report their actual use of ICT use for work and nonwork purposes during work and nonwork days. There is a possibility that social desirability (Podsakoff et al., 2003) could have impacted this reporting, especially for nonwork ICT use on work days, and thus be underestimated. Our sample of managers and professionals tend to have discretion in performing their tasks and generally are not entitled to overtime payments for their long work hours. Therefore, compared to a person on the clock, these individuals may have minimal reasons to hide the actual way they use their time. Thus, while we acknowledge that social desirability could have impacted self-reported data, the impact may not be dramatic.

Finally, data were obtained from survey responses by Canadian managers and professionals in 2008. As Johns (2006) indicated, the research context may often in<sup>fl</sup>uence study <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which includes the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and shape of associations between exogenous and endogenous variables in the research model. Therefore, caution should be used when generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other populations. Also, the cross-sectional data prevented us from testing hypotheses of causality on a longitudinal basis.

## 7.2. Implications for practice

Our results can have implications for organizations where employees operate beyond normal work hours and locations. First, we found that work demand and work <sup>fl</sup>exibility were positively associated with ICT use for work on nonwork days, which indicates that work design changes may in<sup>fl</sup>uence not only the intensity but also the location of work. For example, increasing an individual's work load and <sup>fl</sup>exibility might potentially lead up to “24/7” standards of operation with minimal differentiation between work and personal duties (Patel, 2002). To the extent that this is not a desirable outcome, the organization might look at redesigning processes and rebalancing work load to provide a clearer separation between work and nonwork.

On a similar note, segmentation was negatively related to work ICT use on nonwork days. While some individuals may prefer the integration of work and nonwork, others may want to keep a separation between domains to maintain some form of work–life balance (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996). Individuals who value segmentation would prefer in<sup>fl</sup>exible and impermeable role boundaries. They tend to draw “mental fences” around work and nonwork roles to keep them separate (Ashforth et al., 2000, p.476), and especially. we found. to prevent work from intruding on private lives. When there is an external imposition contrary to individuals' preference and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t (e.g., work intrusions via ICT means on nonwork domain), this could lead to reduced work–life balance (Greenhaus & Allen, 2011) and lower employee morale. This again suggests that organizations need to be aware of the consequences of the ICT-driven, constantly-open communication channels for work purposes.

The <sup>fi</sup>nding that perceived utility of ICT might increase nonwork activity at work might at <sup>fi</sup>rst be perceived as a double-edged sword. The more organizations improve technology connectivity, speed, and functionality to increase productivity, the more staff might engage in nonwork-related ICT use during work days which could lead to a situation of cyberslacking (Johnson & Indvik, 2004) and cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (Lim, 2002). This may be more prominent if the individuals enjoy relatively high work <sup>fl</sup>exibility, especially in terms of <sup>fl</sup>ex time (Garrett & Danziger, 2008). It also appears to be more of an issue for people who erect segmentation strategies to fend off work demands at home, but allow non-work matters to enter working days. However, the general observation we make based on our sample is that managers work long hours, rely greatly on ICT to manage their lives, and that the border that separates work and home has eroded to the point that for most busy managers – except those who consciously practice (unidirectional) segmentation – the boundaries have blurred to the point that there is little purpose to close supervision and monitoring of managerial hours.

## 7.3. Implications for theory

This paper presented a theoretical framework to assess the predictors of the context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT use incorporating theory from the work/nonwork literature (i.e., border theory and boundary theory) together with insights from the human agency perspective. On the one hand, this framework adds to the work/nonwork literature by providing empirical support to the role of ICT in blurring work/ nonwork boundary and creating border permeability. On the other hand, this paper adds to the IS literature by a suggesting a framework beyond the traditional TAM-based models to assess the predictors of ICT.

There are many studies that focus on work-related use of ICT and also the nonwork use of ICT (Brown & Venkatesh, 2005; Venkatesh & Brown, 2001). Recent studies examined the cross-domain use of ICT, with some studies focused on work-related use on nonwork locations (Cousins & Robey, 2005; Fenner & Renn, 2004, 2010; Richardson & Benbunan-Fich, 2011) and nonwork-related ICT use on work settings (Garrett & Danziger, 2008; Hislop & Axtell, 2011; Mastrangelo, Everton, & Jolton, 2006; Weatherbee;

Young & Case, 2004). Our study combines all these contexts of ICT use to investigate how a group of predictors affect each of these contexts. The results indicate that different factors have differing in<sup>fl</sup>uences based on the context of use. This contributes to the IS literature by establishing the need to distinguish the context of use, in examining user adoption and continued use of ICTs. This <sup>fi</sup>nding may be even more relevant when studying the usage patterns of some of the latest ICT devices (e.g., the iPhone and other latest smart portable devices) which are capable of seamlessly integrating all four contexts of use.

## 8. Conclusion

This study investigated predictors of ICT usage for work and nonwork applications on working and nonworking days. While there is a general consensus that modern ICT seems to integrate work and nonwork domains, very few studies have examined ICT use across the work/nonwork boundary to understand what drives such cross-domain ICT use. Therefore, this study adds value to both work/ nonwork literature and ICT literature by examining ICT use based on work/nonwork boundary theory perspective. The study advances the established ICT literature by providing empirical evidence that perceived usefulness of ICT is related, not only to within domain work and nonwork ICT use, but also to across-domain ICT use.

Differential permeability of the work/nonwork border is an established concept in the work/ nonwork literature (Frone et al., 1992: Gutek et al., 1991: Hall & Richter. 1988). We found the same was true in identifying context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c predictors of ICT use across the work/nonwork border. Work-to-nonwork in<sup>fl</sup>uences were more prominent than nonwork-to-work in<sup>fl</sup>uences even in the prediction of across-domain ICT use. Also, work characteristics such as work <sup>fl</sup>exibility and work demands seemed to play a key role in driving micro transitions from work to nonwork domain via ICT means.

The study has limitations that are common to empirical management studies as discussed earlier. Perhaps its main limitation relates to technology. ICTs change continuously by incorporating new applications and capabilities. For example, when data were collected in 2008, social networking was not as popular as it is today, and hand-held portable devices were not as versatile. Use of these new devices could have had substantial impact on ICT usage patterns. The novelty of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and the dynamics of information technologies suggest the need for continued research on the antecedents of ICT use across different contexts of application.
