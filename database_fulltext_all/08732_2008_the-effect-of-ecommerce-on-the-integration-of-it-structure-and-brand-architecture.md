# The effect of e-commerce on the integration of IT structure and brand architecture

Dr Horst Treiblmaier\* & Dr Andreas Strebinger<sup>†</sup>

\*Institute for Management Information Systems, Vienna University of Economics and Business Administration, Augasse 2-6, A-1090 Vienna, Austria, email:

horst.treiblmaier@wu-wien.ac.at, and <sup>†</sup>School of Administrative Studies, Atkinson School of Liberal and Professional Studies, York University, 4700 Keele Street, Toronto, Ontario M3 J 1P3, Canada, email: strebing@yorku.ca

Abstract. A company’s information technology (IT) structure and its brand architecture are intended to minimize transaction costs both within the organiza tion and between the organization and its customers. Business-to-Consumer (B2C) e-commerce fundamentally alters the structure of those transaction costs relevant to the IT structure and the brand architecture. We conducted a survey among 102 chief information officers and chief marketing officers in 67 of the 100 most important B2C enterprises in Austria. The results show that companies typically implement a certain set of changes in the IT structure and the brand architecture if B2C e-commerce is highly important to them and that these changes result in a stronger integration within and between the IT structure and the brand architecture. B2C e-commerce projects thus require closely aligned conceptual, organizational and financial measures in both areas.

Keywords: IT structure, brand architecture, e-commerce, transaction costs

## INTRODUCTION

In the past few years, manifold experiences have shown that information technology (IT) plays a pivotal role in e-commerce but can by no means be considered its sole success factor (Grover & Saeed, 2004; Reinartz et al., 2004; Sedera & Gable, 2004). Rather, e-commerce has repercussions for next to all corporate functions, which calls for new metrics for netenabled organizations (Straub et al., 2002). Previous research has paid particular attention to the interdependencies between e-commerce and a company’s IT structure (Rodgers et al., 2002) as well as the impact of e-commerce on branding (Varadarajan & Yadav, 2002). These studies are confined to the disciplines they have emerged from and focus solely on individual measures. However, a common reason why e-commerce projects fail is not mistakes that occur in individual functional units of the organization but a lack of alignment of these functions or a lack of customer orientation (Rotondaro, 2002). Few interdisciplinary studies have investigated the ways in which e-commerce impacts two or more corporate functions simultaneously (Strebinger & Treiblmaier, 2004). In view of the relevance of these simultaneous impacts for corporate practice, this research gap seems worth closing. Only if companies are aware of the changes e-commerce triggers in their entire organization can they align the measures taken by individual functional units beforehand.

With the growth of e-commerce, the functions of IT and branding have become particularly important. In this paper, we therefore investigate how business-to-consumer (B2C) ecommerce simultaneously impacts the structural aspects of IT and branding. Our research focuses on large, multi-divisional companies that address multiple target groups with various products and services. Based on transaction cost theory, we propose that two effects become visible when B2C e-commerce becomes more important to a company:

First, the company is faced with the need for typical changes in both the IT structure and the brand architecture, which only in combination can ensure the success of e-commerce measures. Second, these changes trigger a strong integration of a company’s IT structure (enterprise-wide integration of customer databases and IT integration of different parts of the company) and its brand architecture (by supplementing formerly independent brands by a shared corporate brand).

We illustrate these effects by using a case study from the hotel industry and a quantitative study among 102 chief information Officers (CIOs) and chief marketing officers (CMOs) in 67 of the 100 most important B2C companies in Austria. We confine our analysis to e-commerce transactions involving consumers, as brand names play a more significant role for consumer goods and are often the sole vehicles of a company’s external communication. For capita goods, meanwhile, information is primarily provided in face-to-face interactions (Reinartz et al., 2004).

## DEFINITION OF THE MAIN CONCEPTS

In the late 1980s, the various formats that IT structures had adopted became the focus of academic research (Fiedler et al., 1996). The move from centralized systems to distributed systems (Ahituv et al., 1989) and later to decentralized systems, which facilitate direct communication among individual units, has been classified according to the systems’ (de)centralization of computing power and their level of interconnectedness (Leifer, 1988). This approach has been extended to include the possibility of sharing applications and resources (Lee & Leifer, 1992). Nonetheless, it has to be mentioned that the issue of centralization versus decentralization also depends on the desired decision-making process, which itself is influenced by factors such as the value of remote information and the cost of communicating remote information (Malone, 1997). This paper uses the term ‘IT structure’ in a broad sense, to denote the sum total of (a) IT components, (b) human IT infrastructure, (c) shared IT services; and (d) shared and standard applications (Weill & Vitale, 2002). The issue of how to design IT structures is especially important in multi-divisional companies that target a multitude of customer groups with a wide variety of products and services. Previous research has shown that the need for coordination triggers innovations in the IT infrastructure (Mustonen-Ollila & Lyytinen, 2003).

While the technical implementation of in-house processes is for the most part invisible to consumers, it is brands that represent companies to the outside world. In companies with many products and target groups, the question of how many brands are needed to adequately address those diverse customer groups arises. Should only one brand be used for all products and target groups or rather different brands for different products and target groups? This problem is addressed by the brand architecture strategy (Kotler, 2003). Formally, brand architecture is defined as the assignment of the brands of the company to its products, target groups and geographical markets (Aaker & Joachimsthaler, 2000, p. 8). A common classification distinguishes three basic types of brand architectures (Keller, 2003). First, companies adopting a pure corporate-brand strategy use solely one brand for all their product categories and target groups (e.g. General Electric, Yamaha, IBM). Second, companies pursuing a pure product-brand strategy maintain separate brand names for each offering, disguising or at least not making it obvious to consumers that the brands belong to the same company (e.g. Procter & Gamble with product brands such as Pampers, Tide, Mr. Clean, Wella and Crest). Ultimately, a dual-brand strategy lies between these two extremes, with each offering having its own brand name (’sub-brand’) in addition to the name or logo of the corporate brand (e.g. Ford plus Focus, Mustang, Taurus, etc.).

Based on transaction cost theory, we argue that a high level of importance of B2C e-commerce necessitates a simultaneous optimization of IT structure and brand architecture. Previous research has used transaction cost theory to explain IT phenomena, such as outsourcing success (Wang, 2002) and the relationships between players in electronic markets (Bunduchi, 2005). According to transaction cost theory, the most efficient form of the interorganizational and the intra-organizational structure minimizes the sum total of transaction costs and production costs (Coase, 1937) and maximizes the accumulation of strategic resources (e.g. knowledge and inimitable transaction networks, which are costly and timeconsuming to build), which subsequently may lead to supra-normal returns (Knott et al., 2003). Williamson (1985, p. 21) argues that transaction costs include the ex ante costs of drafting, negotiating and safeguarding an agreement and ex post costs such as (a) the maladaptation costs incurred when transactions drift out of alignment with requirements; (b) the haggling costs incurred if bilateral efforts are made to correct ex post misalignments; (c) the setup and running costs associated with the governance structures (often not the courts) to which the disputes are referred; and (d) the bonding costs of effecting secure commitments (e.g. legal agreements). Both ex ante and ex post costs depend on the nature and frequency of transactions and the specificity of the contribution both parties make to the transaction (i.e. the non-transferability of their investments) and environmental uncertainties. In order to optimize the IT structure and the brand architecture in large multi-divisional companies, three different types of transaction costs are of importance. First, companies incur internal transaction costs, e.g. for data analysis, data exchange among different divisions and data management. Second, they incur external transaction costs, including communication costs of building and maintaining brand awareness and brand image, costs of giving detailed product information and advice, and costs for transaction settlement and contract conclusion. Third, customers incur transaction costs, including costs of information and costs of risk (Strebinger & Treiblmaier, 2006). Information costs include financial, time and cognitive costs for the search and processing of the information necessary to choose and order a certain brand. Particularly, risk costs arise when the chosen brand does not comply with the customers’ expectations.

E-commerce has significant repercussions on the structure of a company’s external and internal transaction costs as well as on the transaction costs of the customer (Strebinger & Treiblmaier, 2006). Following the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2000), we adopt a broad notion of e-commerce and take into account not only the internet as a transaction and communication channel but also activities undertaken as part ot an e-Customer Relationship System. Electronic customer relationship management (e-CRM) activities can be defined as ‘computerized CRM systems’ (Gummesson et al., 2002, p. 14) and are aimed at ‘attracting and keeping economically valuable customers and repelling and eliminating economically invaluable ones’ (Romano & Fjermestad, 2003, p. 233).

In order to demonstrate that the interplay of IT structure and brand architecture depends on the importance of e-commerce to the company, we investigate three different cases (see Figure 1):

Case A: A situation representing the ‘pre e-commerce era’ or ‘no e-commerce era’, which illustrates how an optimal brand architecture can be derived from the customer’s and the company’s transaction costs.

Case B: E-commerce is highly important and the company has an enterprise-wide integrated IT structure.

Case C: E-commerce is highly important and IT systems are isolated (divisional)

In the context of e-commerce, we understand integration as the activities undertaken by an effective central unit that coordinates the technical and organizational aspects of the planning, implementation and further development of the system and helps to avoid redundancies, incompatible interfaces and proprietary solutions. Total costs (i.e. from the consumer and the organization) have a dark cross on a light-coloured ground.

## Case A: transaction costs and the optimal brand architecture without e-commerce

With the help of the brand architecture, brand management structures the communication flows between companies and consumers and determines the distribution of transaction costs. If one looks at a given portfolio of products and services consisting of, for example, 10 products or product lines and if one understands, for simplicity’s sake, brand architecture as a con tinuum stretching from a pure corporate-brand strategy to a pure product-brand strategy, the ex ante information costs incurred by the consumer typically fall as the degree of brand differentiation rises. The fewer products or services are offered under one brand, the more specific is the information conveyed by the brand. Conversely, consumers’ perceived risk of being tricked by companies increases with growing brand differentiation, since broad brands are more vulnerable to consumer sanctions than brands that are only marketed in one product segment (Rao et al., 1999).

The company’s external transaction costs rise with the number of brands, i.e. costs for communication (e.g. advertising) go up (Aaker & Joachimsthaler, 2000). Meanwhile, internal costs arising from coordinating brand positioning in different product categories and on different markets decrease at first, as the number of products sold under one brand goes down. The complexity of very large brand portfolios, in turn, causes internal coordination costs to rise again. Containing an appropriate number of brands with sensible relationships among them, efficient brand architectures minimize the overall transaction costs for companies and consumers, depending on the heterogeneity and the complexity of the company’s offerings, the volume and structure of the communication costs as well as consumers’ involvement and product knowledge (e.g. Smith & Park, 1992; Maoz & Tybout, 2002). Case A in Figure 1 depicts these typical relationships between the degree of differentiation in the brand architec ture and the types of transaction costs in a situation without e-commerce. The optimum level of brand differentiation $( \mathsf { S } _ { \mathsf { A } } { } ^ { \star } )$ is at the minimum overall transaction costs of consumers and companies $( T _ { \mathsf { A } } { } ^ { \star } )$

In a next step, we analyse how the emergence of e-commerce influences transaction costs. We choose two different settings, which will be discussed subsequently, and argue that the influence of e-commerce on the brand architecture depends on whether the IT structure is integrated across product lines and free of redundancies (case B) or whether it is made up of several isolated IT systems (case C).

## Case B: e-commerce supported by an integrated it structure

When using the internet as a transaction medium, consumers incur high fixed information costs initially, as they need to familiarize themselves with booking or ordering systems or the rules of a customer loyalty programme. At the same time, they reap the benefits offered by these programmes. These greater cognitive efforts coupled with lower variable transaction costs bring about not only stronger lock-in effects in favour of familiar systems but also result in lower information costs for consumers, if brand differentiation increases (Johnson et al., 2003). Once consumers use a certain number of loyalty programmes and booking systems, the efforts needed to learn and manage an additional system may even exceed the benefit of obtaining more useful product information thanks to the existence of separate brands. In this case, consumers’ information and acquisition costs rise again as the number of brands grows. In addition, large or broad brands are in a more favourable position than smaller brands when i comes to risk costs because trust is of paramount importance in online transactions and customers grant ‘credit’ to a brand when they collect points in customer loyalty programmes. This effect is more pronounced online than in the offline world (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Salam et al., 2005).

If the costs for establishing the technical and organizational infrastructure necessary for online communication, online transactions, and e-CRM are added to the external transaction costs of the company, two opposite effects of e-commerce can be observed: On the one hand, costs for the acquisition, maintenance and continuous upgrading of infrastructure as well as employee training raise the company’s fixed external transaction costs (Chen et al., 2000). On the other hand, variable external transaction costs per customer may be reduced (Tapscott, 1996) and synergies may arise, for example by cross-selling among product categories, by bundling products (e.g. insurance products) (Pula et al., 2003), or by identifying individua customer needs across a number of offerings (e.g. hotel chains) more rapidly. Which of these opposite effects prevails depends upon a variety of factors, including, for example, the potential to digitize products (Khan & Motiwalla, 2002), their complexity, the confidentiality required for the transaction (Udo, 2001), the need for tactile information, or industry characteristics such as the narrowness of the market and the geographic dispersion of the customer base (Weill & Vitale, 2002). In Figure 1, we exemplify this by assuming that the positive and negative impact of e-commerce on the company’s external transaction costs are more or less balanced for a pure corporate-brand strategy. However, more central to the interplay of IT structure and brand architecture is the assumption that the company’s external transaction costs rise more significantly as brand differentiation grows than in a situation without the use of e-commerce. Separate loyalty programmes, booking systems or information portals on the World Wide Web should be separately promoted and explained for each brand. Furthermore, synergies resulting from cross-selling, product bundling or higher customer loyalty for different offerings (e.g. reward points redeemable at all sales outlets of the company) are more difficul to communicate to customers when companies maintain separate brands.

Implementing an integrated e-commerce solution is rather complex and requires technical and organizational alignment, thereby raising fixed internal transaction costs. At the same time, however, the company’s variable internal transaction costs fall for a number of reasons. These include diminishing variable costs for analysing data and extracting information (Rayport & Sviokla, 1995) as well as for exchanging data among divisions and country units (Torre & Moxon, 2001). Furthermore, internal transaction costs fall as more efficient and effective ways of data management become available (Pula et al., 2003). This, in turn, lessens the negative impact of a corporate-brand strategy, which relies heavily on internal coordination compared to a multi-brand strategy. Overall, the slope of the internal transaction cost curve flattens out as a function of brand differentiation.

Based on this premise, we hypothesize that e-commerce reduces the sum total of transaction costs incurred by companies and consumers (compare ${ \mathsf { T } _ { \mathsf { A } } } ^ { \star }$ and $T _ { \mathsf { B } } { } ^ { * } )$ ), if the company has implemented an integrated IT structure rather than a pure offline structure, but does so typically together with a stronger integration of the brand architecture (see ${ \mathsf { S } _ { \mathsf { A } } } ^ { \star }$ and $S _ { 8 } { } ^ { * } )$

## Case C: e-commerce supported by isolated IT systems

Case C in Figure 1 depicts the case of isolated and redundant e-commerce solutions for a company’s offerings. Despite little need for internal coordination, this solution is often not a viable option in corporate reality, because of inflated costs associated with establishing the technical and organizational infrastructure and supporting and maintaining multiple systems. Furthermore, the increased difficulties encountered when exchanging and cross-analysing customer data may elevate internal transaction costs for a corporate-brand strategy, thereby shifting the minimum of transaction costs arising from a brand architecture somewhat back towards stronger brand differentiation (compare ${ \mathsf { S } } _ { \mathsf { B } } { } ^ { \star }$ and $\mathsf { S } _ { \mathsf { C } } { } ^ { \star } )$ . However, even at this point, the transaction costs incurred are typically higher than those arising from an integrated e-commerce solution (see ${ \sf T } _ { \sf B } { } ^ { \star }$ and ${ \sf T } _ { \mathbb { C } } { } ^ { * } )$ . We therefore conclude that B2C e-commerce can reduce the total transaction costs of companies and customers, but only if the company moves towards both a more highly integrated IT structure and a better integrated brand architecture

## Core assumptions

Drawing on these theoretical considerations, we assume that e-commerce triggers a set of simultaneous integration efforts in a company’s IT structure and its brand architecture. As for the IT structure, we posit that different parts of the company are harmonized and enterprisewide customer databases are implemented as the importance of B2C e-commerce for the company rises. Furthermore, as integrated IT systems and standardized interfaces facilitate the analysis of complex customer data from multiple divisions, we expect an increase in data mining activities in companies with a high importance of e-commerce. As regards brand architecture, we assume that the corporate brand is used to make it obvious to consumers that formerly separate brands for products or product lines now actually belong to one company, resulting in a dual-branding strategy. In order to test our propositions we pursue a twofold strategy. We first present a case study of a large hotel chain that is well suited to illustrate our central assumptions. Subsequently, we present the results of a quantitative survey in order to demonstrate their generalizability.

## The case of the Accor Group

With more than 4000 hotels in 90 countries, the Accor Group is the market leader in Europe and one of the largest hotel chains in the world. The group operates under the names Sofite (premium segment), Mercure, Novotel and Suitehotel (medium-priced segment), Ibis, Red Roof Inn (economy segment) and Etap, Motel6 and Formule 1 (budget segment). E-commerce has become the cornerstone of its success in recent years. In some countries and marke segments, the internet is used for more than 50% of all bookings. The ‘Accor Favorite Guest Card’ was introduced as an e-CRM measure, with membership growing from 500 000 to 1 400 000 in the period from 2001 to 2003. The online revenue totalled 431 million Euros in 2004 and in June 2005, this programme actually generated 9.6% of total revenue. Members not only receive a discount and are guaranteed room availability in all hotel brands belonging to the group, but they also receive a special welcome and earn reward points, which can be redeemed at all hotels belonging to the group (24/7 Real Media, 2006; Accor, 2006).

As can be expected from our theoretical analysis, this strong emphasis on B2C e-commerce was accompanied by integration efforts in both the IT structure and the brand architecture of the Accor Group. Integrated IT systems have been developed for this broad range of e-services. A single reservation system, standardized and open computer systems at hotels and a global information system for sales ensure that bookings and information are directly transferred among all entities belonging to the Accor Group. Accortel, an integrated system, connects all sales channels (internet, call centres, direct bookings, travel agencies) and almost all hotels belonging to the group. Customers can use either the booking sites of individual hotel chains or the central booking site operated by the Accor brand (http://www.accorhotels.com). The e-CRM programme attaches even more importance to the umbrella brand ‘Accor’ by signalling to regular customers that the hotel chains belong to one group and by letting them redeem their reward points at any outlet of the group. The company considers joint customer relationship management, the reservation system, the internet, and consumers’ familiarity with the umbrella brand to be the major synergies within the group. The umbrella brand is managed by a central management team in Paris and is already used in all hotels, on all websites and in all offline communication channels of the group. In 2004, a global campaign was launched to familiarize people with the group, its core values and its umbrella brand (Accor, 2006).

## METHODOLOGY

The sample chosen for the analysis consists of the 100 most important brand companies in Austria. Their importance was measured in terms of their cumulative advertising expenditure in the period from 2000 to 2002 as reported by FOCUS Media Research. Both the CMO and the CIO of each company were contacted. A total of 51 CMOs and 51 CIOs belonging to 67 of the 100 companies approached were willing to participate in the telephone survey conducted between November 2003 and February 2004. The companies belong to the segments of fast moving consumer goods (17), retail (12), durable consumer goods (11), financial services (11) and other services (16). In 35 companies, both CMOs and CIOs were interviewed. In these cases, their answers were averaged for those questions that were posed to both of them with identical wordings, which raises the objectivity and reliability of the results (Bruggen et al., 2002).

The sample of Austrian companies used for the analysis has two inherent advantages. First, it gives an up-to-date reflection of the impact of B2C e-commerce, given that international studies have found Austria to be among the leading countries in Europe in terms of B2C e-commerce (European Commission, 2002). Second, a varied sample including companies from different industries, of different sizes and in different positions within their groups international organizational structures increases the extent to which the results can be gen eralized to various types of companies. Also, Austria is frequently used by many companies as a test market for (continental) Western Europe, suggesting that consumer behaviour in Austria is comparatively representative.

To measure the importance of B2C e-commerce, three indicators were used, including ‘importance of the internet as a communication channel’, ‘importance of the internet as a transaction channel’ and ‘importance of e-CRM’. CIOs and CMOs were asked how important they consider these three indicators to the success of the company. For example, to measure the importance attributed to the internet as a transaction channel, the following question was asked: ‘Please think of the importance of the internet for transactions with consumers and compare it to other transaction channels. Do you think that the Internet as a transaction channel is very important, rather important, rather unimportant or very unimportant to the success of your company?

Data on changes in IT structures and brand architectures were collected by presenting the CIOs and CMOs with a list of potential changes, which will be discussed in the following section. The items were selected based on a thorough literature review and on the findings from nine qualitative interviews conducted with senior managers from large brand companies in the course of a pilot study. In view of the broad definition of IT structure and the exploratory nature of the study, changes in the IT structure not directly related to e-commerce were also analysed. Afte defining IT structure and brand architecture, the respondents were asked to indicate whether or not these measures had been implemented in their organizations in the past three years or whether they were ‘very likely’, ‘rather likely’, ‘rather unlikely’ or ‘very unlikely’ to be implemented in the coming three years. Questions on IT also took into account measures implemented several years ago to allow for the dynamics of change present in the late 1990s.

## RESULTS

The following sections report the findings on (a) the importance of B2C e-commerce perceived by CIOs and CMOs; (b) the changes implemented in IT structures and brand structures in the period from 2001 to 2006; and (c) the link between the importance of B2C e-commerce and structural changes.

## The importance of e-commerce as perceived by CIOs and CMOs

Overall, 20% of respondents (24% of CIOs and 16% of CMOs) indicate that the internet as a transaction channel is very important' to the success of their companies, and another 26% (29%, 22%) consider it to be ‘rather important’. In addition, 27% of all respondents (29%, 24%) view the internet as a ‘very important’ communication channel, and 36% (33%, 39%) deem it ‘rather important’. e-CRM is considered slightly more important: 35% (31%, 39%) think it is ‘very important’ and 41% (45%, 37%) deem it ‘rather important’. Meanwhile, 18% (12%, 24%) consider the internet ‘not important at all’ as a transaction channel; 6% consider it ‘not important at all’ as a communication channel (0%, 12%); and 10% (8%, 12%) consider e-CRM ‘not important at all’ to the success of the company. For those 35 companies in which both the CIO and the CMO participated in the survey, the perceptions of these two executives were directly compared. A Wilcoxon test revealed that CIOs typically attribute more importance to the internet as a transaction channel than CMOs do $( Z = - 2 . 1 8 3 ; p < 0 . 0 3 )$ . However, differences in their perceptions of the internet as a communication channel $( Z = - 0 . 7 1 3 ; p > 0 . 4 7 )$ and of the importance of e-CRM $( Z = - 0 . 4 4 8 ; p > 0 . 6 5 )$ were not significant.

## Changes in it structures and brand architectures in the period from 2001 to 2006

Figure 2 shows the various changes studied for IT structure. Changes that were considered ‘rather likely’ or ‘very likely’ to be implemented in the future were combined into one category. As individual measures may be implemented multiple times, respondents were able to state a measure for all three periods under investigation.

The results highlight the enormous technological progress that has been made in recent years, with 67% of CIOs indicating that they have launched e-commerce applications in the B2C segment from 2001 until 2003. Almost the same number of managers report that they have merged IT systems of different companies or divisions (65%). For period 2 (2004 to 2006), the Austrian companies used for the analysis planned to focus their efforts on expanding and capitalizing on existing systems, as is evident from the low frequencies – relative to the previous years – for the introduction of e-procurement (18%), supply chain managemen (22%), B2C e-commerce applications (10%), B2C CRM systems (37%) and B2B CRM systems (24%). The proportion of companies dedicating resources to the introduction of e-procurement $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supply chain management $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and e-commerce $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) as well as to the integration of customer databases $( p < 0 . 0 2 )$ significantly declined from period 1 (2001 to 2003) to period 2 (2004 to 2006), as was revealed by McNemar’s tests. Meanwhile, the proportion of companies that planned to use data mining techniques grew significantly $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Slightly more changes were expected for period 2 regarding the integration of IT systems from different companies or divisions (67%) and changes in the structure of data models for customer data (63%).

As shown in Figure 3, the three most frequently cited changes in the brand architecture planned for period 2 include the extension of existing brands to new products – either without sub-brands (69%) or with sub-brands (49%) – and co-branding either involving brands of other companies or involving two in-house brands (37%). ## The link between B2C e-commerce and changes in IT structure and brand architecture

To analyse the links between strategy and structure, the companies had to be examined over a sufficiently long period of time. Therefore, the structural changes implemented in the period from 2001 to 2003 and those considered ‘rather likely’ or ‘very likely’ to be implemented in the period from 2004 to 2006 were combined. The managers’ perceptions of the importance of B2C e-commerce were assessed as the sum total of the three indicators studied (Cronbach’s a = 0.78). Based on this figure, the companies were categorized into three groups of roughly the same size, reflecting high, medium and low importance of B2C e-commerce.

We assume that B2C e-commerce simultaneously triggers certain changes in the IT structure and brand architecture of a company. Such typical sets of changes do not manifes themselves in a bivariate analysis. Hence, the structural changes implemented by the three groups of companies (high vs. medium vs. low importance of B2C e-commerce) were examined by applying multivariate correspondence analysis (Clausen, 1998). For the current study, this statistical analysis offers two inherent advantages. First, it looks at all structural changes in IT and brand architecture simultaneously rather than at one measure at a time, which is in line with the theoretical analysis and the targets of the study. Second, the orthogonal dimensions extracted by correspondence analysis can be conceived of as latent underlying continua (cf. van der Heijden et al., 1994) along which the positions of the three groups of companies can be tested for significance. The results of the correspondence analysis with symmetric normalization are depicted in Table 1. The first dimension explains 76.6% of the differences in structural changes implemented by the three groups of companies in a period of six years and the second dimension accounts for 23.4% of the variance.

If our core assumptions are to be supported by the data, two conditions would have to be met. First, the analysis must yield a latent continuum (dimension) where the group of companies with low importance of B2C e-commerce is located on the one end, the group of companies with high importance of B2C e-commerce on the other end, and the group of companies with medium importance of B2C e-commerce in between. Also, their positions on this continuum must be significantly different from each other. This condition is satisfied with the dominant first dimension detected by correspondence analysis. This dimension spans a continuum from low to medium to high importance of B2C e-commerce where the position of the ‘low-importance of B2C e-commerce’ group (coordinate: -0.497, 95% confidence interval: -0.517 to -0.477) significantly differs from the ‘medium-importance’ group (0.199, 95% confidence interval: 0.142 to 0.257), which in turn differs significantly from the ‘high-importance group (0.443, 95% confidence interval: 0.401 to 0.486).

Second, this continuum must be characterized by integration efforts in both the IT structure and the brand architecture undertaken by the group of companies with high importance of B2C e-commerce. Statistically, this condition is equivalent to a situation in which the measures, which we hypothesized to characterize the case of a high importance of B2C e-commerce, (a) are explained by this dimension and (b) are located significantly on the same side as the ‘high-importance’ group. A cut-off value of 70% of point variance explained is used for a point to be predominantly attributed to a dimension. This requirement is met, too, by the first dimension extracted by correspondence analysis. The end corresponding to a low importance of e-commerce can be named the pole of ‘brand flexibility’, as it is characterized by the following measures: elimination of a brand (variance explained: 98.2%; value: -1.228; 95% confidence interval: -1.278 to -1.178), launch of a new brand (99.8%; -1.014; -1.047 to -0.982), and sale or purchase of a brand (91.9%; -0.696; -0.739 to -0.653). More importantly, however, the opposite pole of this continuum, corresponding to companies with a high importance of B2C e-commerce, can be interpreted as ‘integration efforts involving IT structure and brand architecture’, i.e. integrating the information systems from different companies or divisions (99.9%; 0.135; 0.129 to 0.140), integrating customer databases (99.1%; 0.374; 0.361 to 0.387), implementing B2C CRM systems (96.5%; 0.324; 0.277 to 0.371), data mining (54,1%; 0.220; 0.182 to 0.257) and supplementing independent brands with the corporate brand (85.6%: 0.421: 0.387 to 0.454).

The correspondence analysis thus proves that e-commerce – depending on its importance to the organization – goes hand in hand with a particular set of structural changes, affecting both IT structure and brand architecture. Companies to which e-commerce is highly important tend to undertake more efforts to integrate IT structures and brand architectures.

Figure 4 depicts the crucial first dimension extracted by the correspondence analysis. It can be thought of as a ‘ladder of B2C e-commerce’. For each of the measures, a dotted line points to the exact position (score) of the measure on this ‘ladder of B2C e-commerce’. Figure 4 also shows the mean positions of the three groups of companies (low, medium and high importance of B2C e-commerce) and the confidence intervals of these mean positions (grey-shaded areas). The figure demonstrates that, as discussed above, the confidence intervals of the three groups of companies do not intersect. Hence, each of them occupies a unique space on the ladder of e-commerce. Generally, the closer a group of companies is to a particular measure of change in IT structure or brand architecture, the more characteristic this measure is for this group of companies. The distance between a group of companies and each of the changes is merely proportional to a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ distance. Some caution is therefore advised when interpreting this distance. As predicted, the upper end of the ladder, which is characteristic for companies with high importance of B2C e-commerce, is characterized by ‘integration efforts involving IT structure and brand architecture’, i.e. integrating the information systems of different organizations, integrating customer databases, implementing B2C CRM systems, applying data mining and adding the corporate brand to hitherto independent brands.

## CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

The internet is a medium that facilitates both direct communication and transactions with fina consumers while at the same time strengthening customer relationships. These three functions, referred to collectively as e-commerce, alter internal and external processes of customer service. thereby changing the structure of transaction costs. The empirical study has corroborated the assumption that e-commerce drives integration efforts in both the IT structure and the brand architecture of a company. If e-commerce is highly important to a company, it triggers a typical, cross-functional set of changes in the company’s IT structure and brand architecture. The data have confirmed the expected tendency towards stronger integration efforts in both areas. As for IT, companies to which e-commerce is highly important are most likely to merge information systems across different parts of the organization, integrate customer databases and intensify data mining activities. In brand management, the high importance of e-commerce makes it most likely that the company supplements hitherto independent brands with a shared common corporate brand.

These findings from the Austrian sample are in line with the findings of international case studies in which e-commerce played a crucial role. Hotel giants such as Accor or Starwood (Westin, Sheraton, Four Points, St. Regis) have established e-CRM systems (Accor Favorite Guest Card, Starwood Preferred Guest) and booking portals across different brands. At the same time, they have increased the visibility of their corporate brands (Accor and Starwood) to customers. Sometimes e-commerce systems tie several independent companies together in the areas of technology and branding. For example, the customer loyalty programme of the Star Alliance of airlines, ‘Miles & More’, allows its current 11 million participating customers to collect loyalty points (‘miles’) with 17 airlines (e.g. Lufthansa, Croatian Airlines, Austrian Airlines, LOT Polish Airlines, Adria Airways, SWISS), 25 hotel and car rental partners, and numerous financial service companies. It is based on an integrated IT system shared by all participating airlines and centrally managed customer databases. Both the CRM brand ‘Miles & More’ and the alliance umbrella brand Star Alliance are part of the regular communication o all member airlines (e.g. websites, stationary, parts of the aircraft design).

Some limitations of this study are to be taken into account when interpreting its results. Although the findings are in line with the predictions made based on transaction cost theory, the study is correlational in nature and offers only limited insights into the underlying changes of the transaction costs incurred by the company and its customers as a result of B2C e-commerce transactions. Additional data collected among the CIOs point towards a stronger interaction of B2C e-commerce, IT structure and brand architecture in their effect on consumer information costs rather than on internal transaction costs of the company. We used a Likert-type scale with answers ranging from 1 (very important advantage) to 4 (unimportant advantage). When CIOs were asked about their assessment of cost advantages arising from a corporate brand, no significant differences were found between CIOs from companies that differ in the importance of e-commerce (mean = 2.74). However, CIOs in companies with high (mean = 2.51) and medium (mean = 2.31) importance of e-commerce rate the advantage of a corporate brand for customer support significantly higher than those CIOs who work in companies with a low importance of e-commerce (mean = 2.94). The impact of the importance of e-commerce is statistically significant between those companies with a high and medium importance of e-commerce on the one hand and those with a low importance of e-commerce on the other hand (F(1,46) = 4.250, p < 0.05).

Future research is therefore needed to thoroughly investigate these changes in transaction costs which are supposed to mediate the effects found by this study. Furthermore. our study draws solely on a sample of companies that operate in Austria. Although this sample contains many well-known companies with global headquarters located in North America, Japan and Western Europe, future research should replicate the findings with a geographically diverse sample.

With these limitations, our findings are of high relevance particularly for the top management of large companies with multiple divisions. The more critical e-commerce is to the success of the company, the more central is the ex ante coordination of changes in IT structure and brand architecture in the course of e-commerce projects (cf. Xia & Lee, 2004). The cross-functional and cross-divisional impact of e-commerce uncovered by our study calls for a stronger coordination of marketing and IT across different product divisions. This may entail the need to install a new hierarchical layer of project teams that operate in a cross-functional and cross-divisional manner. Based on our theoretical considerations and empirical findings, we recommend three steps to be carried out in the design of an e-commerce project. The first step should identify the potential of the planned e-commerce initiative to reduce the internal and external transaction costs of the company and the transactions costs of the customers, depending on the joint design of the IT structure and the brand architecture of the company. The different types of transaction costs which we identified in the theoretical part of this paper and their interplay with different IT structures and brand architectures may serve as a general guideline and a practical checklist. In this analy sis, special emphasis has to be given to synergies that the e-commerce initiative can create between different divisions of the company. Also, technical, organizational and marketing aspects of e-commerce solutions need to be aligned in a manner that enables consumers to understand them easily and perceive them as value-adding elements (cf. Lee & Leifer,

1992). The optimal combination of IT structure and brand architecture identified in step one then becomes the input to step two, i.e. the planning of the changes to be implemented in the IT structure and the brand architecture of the company in the course of the e-commerce project. Their implementation, too, has to be designed jointly. In the third and final step, realistic budgets and time schedules are to be stipulated that ensure an appropriate order of the structural changes and the financial means necessary. As for the time frame, differen reaction times of IT structure and brand architecture have to be taken into account. As we found in our study, the time needed for changes in the IT structure to take effect will – although often underestimated – frequently be shorter than the time needed to psychologically build a new umbrella brand in consumers’ minds. Starting too late with the implementation of the changes necessary in the brand architecture might therefore leave the company with a technically working IT structure which does not deliver the full value to consumers.
