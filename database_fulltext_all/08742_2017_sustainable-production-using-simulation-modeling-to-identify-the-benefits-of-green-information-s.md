## ABSTRACT

Researchers and practitioners highlight the potential for information systems to promote sustainability in agricultural production, but little is known about the private and social benefits of specific agricultural decision support tools. In this study, we utilize the resourcebased view to assess a specific green technology using an agricultural-economics simulation to estimate the quantitative benefits of this technology expressed as dollars saved and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. In particular, we employ a five-step simulation modeling approach within a micro-economic model of crop production to assess the ability of yield monitors to promote liquefied petroleum (LP) gas savings and subsequently reduce production costs, reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with LP gas burning, and generate additional revenue at a market for GHG mitigation credits. We estimate that the total benefits of using the green IS to improve the harvesting decision would have been \$82 million in post-harvest cost savings and a significant reduction in greenhouse gas emissions. We present this simulation modeling approach, a common methodology in environmental sciences and economics, as a viable methodology for IS researchers interested in modeling intricate decision-making processes that are impacted by technology.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Molla et al. (2014) refer to environmental sustainability as “a crucial global challenge (p. 129).” Preliminary studies highlight the potential for information systems (IS) to promote sustainable production (Watson et al. 2010; Mattheis et al. 2007). The notion of “green IS”, i.e., IS promoting and supporting environmental sustainability, is growing in importance among researchers and practitioners (Gholami et al., 2013; Molla et al., 2014). According to Gholami et al. (2013), green IS “involves the positive impact of using IS to improve the eco-sustainability of businesses and society (p. 431).”

Numerous studies have explored the benefits of IS as decision support systems r et al., 2009; Molla et al., 2014). Extent literature is replete with studies that highlight the cost savings and improved Kauffman & Walden, 2001; Kulatilaka & Venkatraman, 2001; Rivard et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). However, the economic and environmental benefits of green IS are still being explored (Nanath & Pillai, 2017).

In this study, we propose the use of simulation modeling for estimation of the benefits of a specific green IS as DSS. We utilize the Resource Based View (RBV) to assess farmers’ and greater society’s environmental benefits from using mobile technologies that promote sustainable farming practices. Aubert et al. (2012) highlight the growing significance of information technology (IT) for the contemporary agricultural production. Bochtis et al. (2012) state “there is a need for innovation and technological development, which can contribute to an efficient utilization of applied resources while at the same maintain the overall sustainability of agriculture (p. 66).” Aubert et al. (2012) highlight the growing significance of information technology (IT) for the contemporary agricultural production:

IT is of increasing importance in the agricultural sector with innovations being more and more IT based. The agricultural industry creates a very distinct environment for technology adoption and use which can be expected to challenge and improve established theories. Furthermore, today's urgency related to food security and environmental protection creates an opportunity for IS research to provide substantial contributions reaching beyond a specific industry (p. 517).

The focus of our study is on yield monitors (YMs), which enable farmers to instantly observe hydroscopic crop’s moisture content and to make informed decisions on the optimal time to harvest the crop. We evaluate the ability of this DSS to promote Liquefied Petroleum gas (LPG) savings and subsequently reduce production costs, reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with LPG burning, and generate additional revenue at a market for GHG mitigation credits. W utilize a simulation approach within a micro-economic model of crop production to evaluate these savings. To gain the insight on the benefits of YMs our study evaluates: 1) the annual cost savings that end-users realize if they use this technology, 2) the distribution of the cost savings, 3) the reduction of the GHG emissions due to the use of YMs, and 4) the additional revenue that the use of YMs could generate if the credits for GHG emission reductions could be sold at a market.

Several studies have investigated the role of RBV on strategic decision making (Mahmood and Soon 1991; Nemati et al. 2010). However, few studies have utilized RBV to explore DSS. Carlsson (1997) presents the RBV as a valuable tool for assessing the role of DSS on organizational transformation. Respício and Burstein (2012) use RBV to discuss the role of dynamic capabilities and DSS. Power (2012) calls for more research on DSS and RBV, and on the role of DSS in increasing competitive advantage in particular.

This study contributes to the DSS literature in three key ways. First, we evaluate the economic and environmental benefits of YM as a tool that supports the decision on the timing of crop harvesting. Aubert et al. (2012) state “Precision agriculture (PA) describes a suite of IT based tools which allow farmers to electronically monitor soil and crop conditions and analyze treatment options (p. 510),” and posit that PA is “a sector of IT applications that is too rarely studied (p. 518).” Second, we utilize the RBV as a guiding framework to illustrate the potential for green IS to reduce GHG emissions. Finally, we respond to an existing gap in the literature by empirically assessing the role of IS in achieving environmental sustainability (Meacham et al. 2013). Jenkin et al. (2011) highlight the need for “integrating diverse areas of research to develop a comprehensive framework [that will] bring value to both researchers and practitioners by placing IT/S in conversation with the environmental management, environmental psychology, learning, and social marketing literatures (p. 34).” This study addresses that existing gap in the literature by utilizing an S and agricultural economics literature - to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the role of a decision support tool on sustainability.

In addition to our contributions to the literature, we also present a simulation modeling technique, frequently employed by economists, as a viable mechanism for exploring complex technology adoption decisions. We use a five-step process to create, test and apply a comprehensive model that assesses the impact of mobile technology utilization on energy savings and GHG emission reduction. We posit that this simulation modeling approach can be employed in future studies that evaluate other DSS, both in agriculture and in other sectors of the economy.

## 2. BACKGROUND LITERATURE

## 2.1 Resource-Based View

Penrose (1959) establishes the foundation for the RBV by describing a firm as a bundle of resources. According to the RBV, firms possess various resources, some of these resources enable them to achieve competitive advantage, and others lead to superior longterm performance (Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991; Barney, 2001; Clemons & Row, 1991; Grant, 1991; Mata et al., 1995; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Studies of firm performance using the RBV have found differences between firms in the same industry (Hansen & Wernerfelt, 1989), and within the tighter boundaries of groups within industries (Cool & Schendel, 1988). These findings suggest that the effects of individual, firm-specific resources on performance can be significant (Mahoney & Pandian, 1992).

The RBV has emerged as one of the prominent theories used to interpret the relationship between IT and firm performance (Crook et al., 2008; Almarri & Gardiner, owns. Organizations with numerous, profitable resources that are limited in availability to others are more likely to generate sustainable competitive advantages (Carter et al., 2012). In this view, “IT is considered a valuable organizational resource that can enhance organizational capabilities and eventually lead to higher performance (Ting-Peng, 2011).”

The RBV offers a valuable approach to explore how IS relate to firm strategy and performance (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Jenkin et al. (2011) present the RBV as a valuable framework for exploring green IS. Hart (1995) uses RBV to present environmental management in an organization as a strategic resource that results in better performance. Kirchoff (2011) posits organizations with an environmental orientation may be more likely to implement green practices, and realize an improvement in firm performance, compared to the competition. We apply this view to farmers, who are responsible for diverse human and non-human resources, such as land of varying quality.

In this paper, we define resources as assets and capabilities that are obtainable and beneficial in detecting and responding to opportunities or threats in the market (Christensen & Overdorf, 2000). Assets are tangible or intangible tools that an organization can use in its processes, and capabilities are iterative patterns of activities that utilize ass make, or provide products to a market (Sanchez et al., 1996). YM adopti infancy, hence we focus solely on this IS asset. To-date, few farmers have developed advanced capabilities for the use of YMs (Batte & Arnholt, 2003; Griffin & Erickson, 2009).

According to Ray et al. (2005) “resources are valuable when they enable firms to increase the efficiency or effectiveness of processes compared to what would be the case if these resources were not exploited in these processes.” YMs provide value by reducing the cost associated with drying a crop. Their use is rare, and the features and functions of a YM are currently inimitable and non-substitutable. The following sections provide the description of this technology and the potential benefits it could provide.

## 2.2 Yield Monitors and their Potential Benefits

A YM is a portable, GPS-linked technology, that farmers could use to determine if the crops on specific fields are ready to be harvested and stored without high temperature drying (Griffin & Erickson, 2009). For optimal long-term storage, hydroponic crops such as corn need to be dried to specific moisture content. The farmer has two options: allow the crop to dry naturally while in the field, or harvest the crop as it is and dry it using hightemperature drying equipment. The in-field drying doesn’t incur any explicit costs and can be very effective if the weather is warm and dry. However, this option is risky: if the weather becomes wet, the cost savings associated with in-field drying could be easily lost (Monsanto, 2011; Wilcke, 2004).

Option two is popular yet pricey. Typical Iowa corn production budgets show that the variable cost of drying constitutes approximately 70% of all post-harvest variable costs and between 7% and 8% of the total variable costs of production (Duffy & Smith, 2009), on par with the costs of fuel used to plant and harvest the crop (Wilcke & Wyatt, 2002). Depending on corn yield, tillage, and the crop grown in previous year, the costs vary between \$5 to \$7 per acre (ac) for fixed costs, which in addition to labor include the depreciation, interest, insurance and housing of the equipment, and between \$28 to \$32 per ac for variable costs, which include the costs of fuel, oil, and repairs. YM enables the farmer to track the amount of moisture in a crop and reduce the costly high-temperature drying.

Empirical assessments of the use of YMs in the U.S. agriculture are scarce. The U.S. Department of Agriculture has begun collecting data on YM use as a part of the ongoing survey of farmers in 1996 (Griffin & Erickson, 2009). Schimmelpfennig and Ebel (2016) used a 2010 national survey of 1,507 U.S. corn producers to estimate the production cost savings of YMs at an average \$14.50 per ac. However, the study did not analyze the specific sources of these cost savings. To our knowledge, no estimates have been developed on the monetary or environmental value of this decision support tool for avoiding unnecessary crop drying. The complex relationships between the choices that farmers make about technology use and the environmental outcomes have been recognized in the literature (Bergstrom et al., 1985; Lichtenberg et al., 2010; Kragt and Robertson 2014), but not in the context of farmers’ use of IS.

In this study, we evaluate two types of YM benefits: private, which accrue to the farmers that use YMs, and environmental, that accrue to the society as a whole, for a major U.S. corn production region, Iowa. We account for both major determinants of hightemperature drying costs – the spatial distribution of crop moisture, and the spatial distribution of realized yields, which in turn depends on the spatial distribution of soil quality and economic choice of rotation and tillage. Hence, we are able to assess the private benefits of cost savings and the social benefits - the GHG emission reductions that result from the use of this green IS.

The RBV posits that an investment in technological resources will improve organizational performance. Hence, we test the following hypothesis:

H1: Farmers that use YMs for monitoring crop moisture content at the time of harvest will have substantially lower crop drying costs than farmers who do not use this technology.

Avoiding unnecessary drying not only reduces post-harvest costs, but also avoids the emissions of potent greenhouse gasses such as ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } ,$ $\mathsf { C H } _ { 4 } ,$ and ${ \sf N } _ { 2 } { \sf O }$ associated with burning $\mathsf { L P G }$ . Dedrick (2010) states "in the case of climate change, a critical issue is the relationship of economic output to greenhouse gas emissions, which has been labeled carbon productivity. Increasing carbon productivity means that economic growth can be sustained while emissions are reduced. Informati technology has great potential to enhance carbon productivity." Jain et al. (2011) take this argument further by stating that sustainability measures for green IS tend to emphasize the triple bottom-line: economic, social, and environmental performance. To test how large the enhancements enabled by the YMs could be for increasing revenue (economic performance), and reducing GHG emissions (social and environmental performance), we test two additional hypotheses:

H2: Farmers that use YMs for monitoring crop moisture content at the time of harvest will have substantially lower GHG emissions while drying crops than farmers who do not use this technology.

H3: If markets for GHG mitigation develop, then farmers that use YMs for monitoring crop moisture content at the time of harvest could generate substantial revenue.

Finally, Wade and Hulland (2004) suggest that the RBV dependent variable should include “some sense of comparativeness, assessing performance relative to that enjoyed by key competitors. Taken in isolation, a firm's performance, whether strong or weak, contains only limited meaning. For example, a firm may enjoy strong share growth, return on

investment, and profit but actually lag key competitors on those measures. Conversely, traditional performance metrics may seem disappointing until compared to an industry average that is significantly worse. Unfortunately, to date this aspect of firm performance is the one that has been least emphasized by IS researchers using the RBV.” Following this argument, we posit that the benefits of YM will vary in different regions: H4: Distribution of the YM use benefits is uneven across Iowa.

## 3. METHODOLOGY

Wade and Hulland (2004) “encourage researchers to take fuller advantage of competitive assessment tools when measuring firm performance so as to provide a richer and more complete account of how the firm's resources influence its competitive position.” Mithas et al. (2012) state “limitations of data sets and modeling techniques may have led to the failure to detect a statistically significant relationship between IT investments and profitability (Dedrick et al. 2003).” This explanation has also been suggested by metaanalysis results that document the importance of sample size and modeling techniques (Kohli & Devaraj, 2003). Therefore, “richer datasets and improved modeling may have a better chance at detecting the effect of IT on profitability.” This study responds to that call by utilizing a comprehensive simulation economic model of Iowa crop production.

Out of the two crops, corn and soybeans, growing on the overwhelming majority of Iowa cropland, only corn is hydroponic and needs drying before storage. Most commonly used drying equipment runs on LPG. The LPG requirements for drying corn are well documented: Wilcke and Wyatt (2002) report that on average 0.02 gal of LPG is needed per bushel (bu) of corn per percentage point of moisture removed.

Moisture content of hydroscopic crops comes into equilibrium with the temperature and relative humidity of surrounding air. In Iowa, harvested corn needs to be at the 17% moisture content for optimal long-term storage (Monsanto, 2011). To capture the spatial variability of drying needs, we overlay the spatial distribution of corn yields over the spatial distribution of corn moisture content at the time of harvest. The latter depends on hybrid maturity and increases in the east-west and south-north direction within Iowa, varying between 18% and 24% (Shouse et al., 2011).

## 3.1 Modeling Iowa crop production

Corn yields depend on innate soil productivity, climatic factors, and crop producers’ economic decisions – choices of crop rotation, tillage system, and fertilizer use. The two major crop rotations (sequences) practiced in Iowa are continuous corn (CC) (corn after year of corn) and corn after soybeans (CS) (Secchi et al., 2011; Stern et al., 2008). The choice of rotation is extremely important for evaluation of the corn drying costs, since they are incurred on every field every year under CC and only every other year on the fields in CS rotation (or, equivalently, every year on half the area under ). Crop rotation impacts corn yields since previous year soybeans provide a yield bust via soil nitrogen benefit. Additionally, crop yields are affected by tillage systems by up to 20%. Details on these relationships are provided in Appendix A.

The majority of Iowa land is in CS yearly rotation, but the share of monoculture corn, prices (Plourde et al., 2013; Hendricks et al., 2014). Stern et al. (2008) estimates the growth of the share of monoculture corn from 15% to 21% from 2001 to 2007. These findings point to the need to use an economic model of Iowa crop production that is capable of predicting farmers’ crop rotation choices under alternative prices.

Two major approaches to developing economic models of cropping choices are econometric estimation and programming models calibrated to data on observed land uses (Chavas et al., 2010). The econometric approach relies on estimating the functions of interest statistically and allows for construction of confidence intervals. However, in the

context of crop choices, the econometric approach requires statistically representative survey samples with observations on crops chosen, crop prices, all major production inputs and/or prices – the data that are rarely available to researchers due to cost of collection or confidentiality concerns (Howitt, 1995; Chavas et al., 2010). Therefore, most econometric modeling for Iowa or for the regions that include Iowa limited considerations to single year crop choices rather than the choice of rotation (Orazem and Miranowski, 1994; Miller and Plantinga, 1999; Wu et al., 2004; Arnade and Kelch, 2007).

In contrast, positive programming could be used to predict farmers’ profit-maximizing choices with much less data (Howitt, 1995; Hennessy, 2006; Secchi et al. 2009; 2011; Livingston et al., 2015). On one hand, the advantage of needing less data for development of such a model comes at a price of not being able to test hypotheses statistically as no standard errors of parameters are customarily produced during model construction. On another hand, agronomic process-based crop growth models could be incorporated directly for simulating crop yields for alternative cropping sequences, soils, farming practices, and production inputs, thus enriching the model’s ability to capture the complexity of the real-life economic choices (Secchi et al. 2009; 2011; Robertson et al., 2013; Troost and Berger, 2015). The economic studies of Iowa crop rotations have mostly used programming frameworks. Hennessy (2006) and Livingston et al. (2014) analyzed the rotation choices for a representative acre in Iowa.

To capture the variability of growing conditions across Iowa, we opted for Secchi et al. (2009; 2011) model, which allows for prediction of rotation choices on the soils of alternative quality. Orta et al. (2014) posit that simulation modeling is broadly used to support decision-making in diverse business tasks. Matthies et al. (2007) highlight the importance of simulation models for environmental DSS. The simulation modeling approach has become an important quantitative tool in applied production analysis in agricultural

economics, especially, when used in conjunction with biophysical processes models to access the environmental outcomes of production over large, heterogeneous regions (Stoorvogel et al., 2004; Chavas et al., 2010; Doole and Pannell, 2013; Secchi, 2013; Donoghue et al. 2014).

Step 1: Identify a Group of Decision Makers

The study region is Iowa, the major corn-producing state of the U.S. The decision makers of interest are Iowa crop producers who are potential adopters of YMs.

## Step 2: Obtain a Data Set that Describes the Group of Decision Makers

Data on decision makers need to capture the within-group heterogeneity, i.e. there should be variance within the group that is meaningful for decision-making. Present study’s data reflects the land of differing quality, which in turn impacts the potential benefit of YMs.

Farmers’ choices are simulated on a 30-square-meter square grid covering all of the land in Iowa that has been cropped in 2009. Secchi et al. (2011) overlaid the grid, which is based on remote-sensing cropland data layer developed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture National Agricultural Statistical Service (USDA/NASS), with GIS-based soil maps. In the resulting dataset, each grid unit is associated with a measure of soil productivity – Corn Suitability Rating (CSR). CSR is an index from 0 to 100 with the higher CSR values corresponding to the higher land’s productivity.

Although the data utilized in this study is from 2009, it is a strong sample. Only 9,866 ac or 0.04% of the GIS-based crop-cover data have been excluded because of the missing CSR indicators. Overall, our simulations are carried out for some 22.0 million ac. In comparison, USDA/NASS (2014) reports that corn and soybeans were harvested on some 22.8 million ac in Iowa in 2009, implying that the study data covers approximately 96% of the state’s cropped land.

## Step 3: Construct a Decision Making Model

A decision-making model is constructed by a) clarifying the assumptions and b) developing a formal representation of the decision-making process such as a decision-tree or equation(s). Four assumptions are maintained in the study. First, we make an assumption about the decision-making goal; we assume profit maximization. Second, we make an assumption about the choices available to the decision-makers. We assume that given crop and production input prices, they choose among two crop rotations and three tillage systems. Third, we assume there is no ncertainty about crop production and the technology that supports this process. For instance, farmers know the costs (which have been minimized to the extent possible), they know how technology would perform, and they know the expected yields of all crops that can be potentially grown on their land. Fourth, we assume the drying-technology is available. These assumptions are commonly imposed in the analyses of crop production and are generally supported by empirical evidence (Chavas et al., 2010).

## Step 4: Calibrate the Model

Simulations for a scenario that has been observed allow researchers to check whether the model adequately predicts decision-making and to modify the model if needed. This step is commonly referred to as calibration of the model. The model we use has been successfully tested against historical data in multiple studies (Secchi et al., 2009; 2011; Kurkalova et al., 2009; 2010). For the sake of brevity, and because of the most specific details reported, we focus on the calibration results reported by Secchi et al. (2011).

Secchi et al. (2011) verified rotation predictions in two ways. First, the authors compared two 2006 data, and the other - based on the model predictions. The comparison showed that the model correctly predicts the location of the major crop rotations in the state, with the CC acreage concentrated in the most productive, north-central part of the state, and the CS occurring mostly on the less productive soils. Second, the authors reported that the statetotal corn acreage predicted by the model was within 4% and 6% of that reported by

USDA/NASS (2014), for 2006 and 2007, respectively. The difference between the modelpredicted and the historical data has several possible explanations. While the model predictions are constructed for the average prices, individual expectations are likely to differ from farmer to farmer based on individual experiences. Also, the differences could be attributed to the sampling error of the USDA/NASS (2014) data.

Validation of tillage predictions was precluded by the lack of statistically reliable estimates of farmers’ tillage choices by crop and previous crop. For example, no tillage data for CC are reported because the estimates are “statistically unreliable due to a combination of a low sample size and high sampling error” (USDA/NASS, 2014).

## Step 5: Predict the Behavior of Decision-makers in Various Scenarios

The final step in the simulation modeling approach is to use the data set and calibrated model to predict the behavior of the decision-makers under various scenarios. In this study, we varied the efficiency of the YMs and the availability of a market for GHG mitigation credits. The following sub-section presents the details on the scenarios.

## 3.2 Scenarios

To capture the economic, social and environmental performance of YMs, we consider a total of four scenarios. The decision-making facilitated by YM is not assumed to differ between scenarios: farmers use the information provided by the YMs to identify the optimal timing of harvest of their hydroponic crop, i.e., when crop moisture reaches the level recommended for long-term storage. Rather, the scenarios vary by the efficiency of YM use, and by the availability of a market for GHG mitigation credits. Scenarios 1 and 2 reflect the economic value of the use of YM, i.e., the reduction in farmers’ costs because of the use of the YMs. Scenarios 3 and 4 reflect both the social and the environmental value of the use of YM, i.e., assess not only the reductions in the costs of farmers but also assign monetary

values to social outcomes of the use of YMs, as represented by the GHG mitigation credits. Figure 1 provides an illustration of the simulation process used in the study.

Since no data are available on how much less moisture the users of YMs remove from the harvested corn when compared to the YM non-users, we bracket the estimates between the low and the high efficiency of YM use. The low efficiency estimates (Scenarios 1 and 3) are derived under the assumption that YM reduces the harvest time crop moisture by 1% (a conservative estimate), while the high efficiency estimates (Scenarios 2 and 4) assume that the use of YM completely eliminates the variable costs of high temperature drying. Under both low and high YM efficiency use assumption the value of YMs varies by land quality, which affects the choice of crop rotation and tillage, and hence, the realized corn acreage and yield.

The value of completely eliminating the need to dry corn (Scenarios 2 and 4) depends on one additional factor – crop moisture content at the time of harvest – which varies spatially across the state. The data on corn moisture content at harvest for six Iowa corn moisture content regions (CMCRs), Northwest (NW), Northeast (NE), Central-west (CW), Central-east (CE), Southwest (SW), and Southeast (SE) come from Shouse et al. (2011). The corn drying needs in Iowa increase from east to west and from south to north, ranging from less than 2 percentage points in SE to over 7 percentage points in NW. We overlay the spatial distribution of the average harvest time crop moisture content over the distribution of land by soil quality in the evaluation of Scenarios 2 and 4 (Appendix B provides additional details).

GHG mitigation credits are tradable claims on the reduction in carbon (and, potentially, other GHG) emissions (Gonzalez-Ramirez et al., 2012; Williams et al., 2009). Under a cap-and-trade program, a market for GHG mitigation credits is created when an overall limit (cap) on GHG emissions is set by a regulating agency, and emissions credits are issued to all GHG emitters equal in number to the total amount of the cap. Regulated entities (businesses) must hold enough credits to cover their total GHG emissions over a given time period or be penalized for any deficient credits. Regulated entities can undertake activities to reduce their own emissions, but it may be less costly for them to purchase credits from other businesses.

Depending on regulations, sources of emissions reductions outside of the capped sector of the economy can or cannot participate in a cap-and-trade program. To date, agricultural GHG mitigation credit trading possibilities are available in select countries in Europe and select U.S. states and regions only (Gonzalez-Ramirez et al., 2012; Williams et al., 2009). However, the potential participation of agricultural sector in cap-and-trade GHG emission reduction programs is being actively discussed and researched in both policy arena and in the academia. In present analysis, under Scenarios 3 and 4, we assume the simplest form of GHG mitigation credit trading available – that under which farmers could sell the claims on the reductions in greenhouse gases (GHGs) attributable to avoiding the need to burn LPG associated with the use of crop drying equipment.

## 4. RESULTS

Conditional on the 2009 crop and energy prices, we predict farmers’ choices on crop rotation and tillage, which in turn determine corn yields and acreage, for each land quality class. Simulations under the 2009 crop and energy prices predict that the land of lower quality, up to CSR =85, or some 18.1 million ac, are in CS, conservation tillage, and the remaining highest quality land, 3.9 million ac, or 17.9% of the total cropland, are in CC under conventional tillage. The split of the acreage between corn and so consistent with the 2009 observed corn and soybean acreage, 13.3 million ac and 9.5 million ac, respectively (USDA/NASS, 2014). Earlier applications of the model to different years have likewise shown that Iowa land of the higher quality gets allocated to CC while the lower quality land is mostly in the CS rotation (Kurkalova et al., 2009; Secchi et al., 2011; Elobeid et al., 2013;).

## 4.1 Scenarios 1 and 2: Economic Value of YMs

## 5. DISCUSSION

Dedrick (2010) states $\mathsf { \Omega } ^ { \mathsf { \Pi } mathfrak { \mathfrak { u } } } \mathsf { O } \mathsf { n } \mathsf { e }$ role of academics is to provide a scientific basis for others to make better decisions. The need for good academic research to guide

policymakers and other decision makers with regard to the environment and climate change is enormous.” Malhotra et al. (2013) highlights the need for additional research on green IS impacts. This paper presents one of the first studies to use RBV for DSS to explore the impact of green IS. It also responds to Dedrick (2010)’s call for more research on the potential of technology to enable environmental gains in the broader economy.

## 5.1 Implications for Research

All of the proposed hypotheses were supported. This study contributes to the extent body of knowledge by illustrating how IT decision support tool, YM, can be utilized to provide timely information that enables users to reduce environmental damage. With the RBV as a guiding framework, we use an economic simulation model to estimate the potential for green IS to reduce drying costs and GHG emissions. This study highlights the utility of interdisciplinary research for identifying the potential benefits of technology on sustainable production.

Wade and Hulland (2004) “suggest that any dependent variable used in an RBVbased study needs to exhibit three key attributes: (1) it should provide an assessment of performance, (2) it should incorpor e a competitive assessment element, and (3) it should address the notion of performanc over time.” Our study accounts for the first two attributes by 1) indicating the cost and energy savings associated with the adoption of mobile technology for sustainable adoption and 2) indicating the relative benefits of mobile technology in different geographic areas.

This study also responds to Florian (2014)’s call for more simulation research in the green IS domain. This study provides a complimentary perspective by examining agricultural data, which captures actual farming practices, to project the benefits of green IS. In contrast with the other methodologies frequently employed in IS research (e.g. conceptual modeling, surveys, interviews, case-studies), simulation modeling has several benefits. It can be used to quickly evaluate the effects of a change in a real-life situation that takes place over time (several months, years or decades). It can be used to study complex systems that would otherwise be difficult or costly to investigate. Finally, as the volume and variety of data continues to evolve, existing simulation models can be amended to take advantage of the additional data sets.

## 5.2 Implications for Practice

Lucas Jr. et al. (2013) highlight the need for more studies on transformational technologies that present results that can inform policy makers, managers and decision makers. Our study addresses this call by highlighting the economic and environmental benefits of an under-utilized green IS. YMs have the potential to significantly reduce costs and harmful GHG emissions. It could be in the society’s best interest to design and implement policies that provide informational and financial incentives for investments in the technology. Currently, two-thirds of the farmers in the United States do not use YMs. Future research is needed to identify barriers to adoption and explore the IT capability of the farms in addition to the financial quantity measurements used in this study. Such information could help design and implement programs to promote adoption of this cost-saving, green IS.

## 5.3 Limitations

As with any empirical assessment, results presented are conditional on the data available and model assumptions. Because of a relatively small sample size, only stateaverage fertilizer application rates are available through ARMS data (USDA/NASS, 2014) or in the typical crop production budgets (Duffy & Smith, 2009). A more spatially-explicit fertilizer application data would improve the prediction of expected yield in the economic model and would allow furthering the analysis of the within-state variability in YM benefits.

Due to data limitations our study did not estimate the costs of YM use; we focused on the benefits only. Future research is needed to explore the economic and social return on investment that results from the use of YMs.

## 5.4 Suggestions for Future Research

Some researchers suggest that IS assets, such as YMs, are the easiest resources for competitors to copy and, therefore, represent an insubstantial source of sustainable competitive advantage for a firm (Leonard-Barton, 1992; Teece et al., 1997). However, there is growing evidence that competitive advantage often depends on an organization’s superior utilization of capabilities (Christensen & Overdorf, 2000; Day, 1994) and intangible assets (Hall, 1997; Itami & Roehl, 1987; Srivistava et al., 1998). From an RBV perspective, Wade and Hulland (2004) indicate that “this advantage may result from development of capabilities over an extended period of time that become embedded in a company and are difficult to trade. Alternatively, the firm may possess a capability that is idiosyncratic to the firm (i.e., an IS expert with specialized knowledge who is loyal to the firm) or difficult to imitate due to path dependencies (Dierickx & Cool, 1989) or embeddedness in a firm's culture (Barney, 1991).” Research indicates that capabilities are often essential drivers of organizational performance (Eise hardt & Martin, 2000; Makadok, 2001; Teece et al., 1997). This study explores an IS asset (a YM). Future studies should explore the capabilities of farmers that utilize YMs to identify opportunities for sustained competitive advantage. Additional insights could also be obtained by amending the modeling system to capture the heterogeneity of decision makers in other important dimensions, such as the size of enterprises, or corporate organizational structure.

To improve the precision of the estimates that we bracketed between low and high efficiency of YM use further research is needed on how much less moisture the users of YMs remove from the harvested corn when compared to the YM non-users. Surveys that elicit such information together with the socio-economic data on farms and farmers could provide important insights on the famers’ decision-making (McCown, 2002). Specifically, interviews could help identify current challenges and opportunities associated with YMs in particular and green IS artifacts in general, and the ways to promote the adoption of YMs among more farmers. The literature on agricultural technology adoption has shown that both the level of adoption of environmentally benign farming practices and the perception of challenges and opportunities vary significantly with farm size, environmental attitudes, education, gender, age, and/or with other variables reflecting farmer’s socio-economic status (Pannell et al., 2006; Prokopy et al., 2008).

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

This interdisciplinary study utilizes literature and methods from IS and agricultural economics to illustrate the potential benefits of green IS. Our results suggest that technology can result in major savings for farmers. We estimate potential state-total LPG the high efficiency of YM use. Some farmers will realize greater benefits from this technology than others, as YMs are more beneficial in regions with better land quality.

In addition to highlighting the potential financial and environmental benefits of a specific green technology, this paper also makes a methodological contribution to the IS literature. The economic simulation model employed demonstrates quantifiable benefits of a green IS. We utilized an existing data set to simulate the advantages of YMs. We used economic scenarios to capture the economic, social and environmental performance of a green application. We posit that the simulation modeling approach, a common methodology in environmental sciences and economics, is a viable methodology for IS researchers interested in modeling intricate decision-making processes that are impacted by technology.

A combination of three features of our problem and data made the simulation methodology suitable for the analysis. First, the main driver of decision-making in farming – profit motif - is well known (Chavas et al., 2010). Therefore, decision-making about which crops to grow could be relatively easily simulated with the knowledge about resource constraints, such as land productivity. Second, obtaining YM use data directly, e.g., via a survey that would sample the major types of decision-makers in a statistically representative way, is impractical. Confidentiality concerns and high cost of collecting data directly from farmers commonly result in relatively small survey samples that are rarely available to researchers outside of the agency collecting the data (Woodard, 2016). However, we do know the distribution of the decision-makers across our study region - by land quality and by drying needs - and make use of this important information in the study. We encourage other researchers to apply the simulation approach presented when facing similar data availability issues.

Previous studies have highlighted the benefits of IS for organizations and society, with only limited attention to the “triple bottom line” of achieving socio-economic and environmental sustainability (Matthies et al. 2013). We evaluate the advantages of a specific green IS using an agricultural-economics simulation to estimate the quantitative benefits of this technology expressed as dollars saved and reduced GHG emissions. We suggest that yield monitors are an IS solution for environmental sustainability.

## Appendix A – Detailed Description of the Farmers’ Choices Modeled

To account for the crop rotation in the estimation of net returns, an acre of land in CS rotation is thought to comprise of a ½ of the acre that is in corn in the current year but was soybeans in the current year but was in corn in the previous year (soybeans after corn). Every acre of the land in CC is in corn after corn.

The model allows for each of the two rotations to be practiced using one of the three tillage systems: conventional tillage, mulch till, and no-till. Standard definitions of tillage systems are used: any tillage system that leaves less than 30% residue on soil surface after planting is conventional tillage (CTIC, 2012). Mulch till disturbs the entire soil surface and involves up to three tillage passes from the harvest of previous crop to the planting of the current crop. No-till is an umbrella term used for the tillage systems such as strip-till, vertical tillage, and fluffing harrows, which disturb only the minimal amount of soil (CTIC, 2012). The farming operations assumed under specific tillage systems and cropping sequences are based on the typical practices documented by the Iowa State University’s Extension (Duffy and Smith, 2009).

Fertilizer rates, the crop previously grown on the field, and the tillage affect yields, but the exact magnitude of the effect depends on a multitude of factors such as soils specifics and climatic factors. The fertilizer application rates assumed in the study are based on Duffy and Smith (2009). Based on the long-term agronomic studies conducted in Iowa (see Kurkalova et al. (2009) for a summary and discussion), we assume that corn after soybean yields are equal the maximum potential corn yields, under both conventional and mulch till. No-till corn after soybeans is assumed to achieve the 95% of the maximum corn yields. Corn after corn yields are equal 95%, 90%, and 80% of the maximum potential yields under conventional, mulch, and no-till, respectively. Soybean yields are equal the maximum potential yields under both conventional and mulch till, and the 80% of those under no-till.

## Appendix B – Moisture Content Data

We assign each grid unit to a CMCR based on the county in which the unit is located. Since Shouse et al. (2011) did not detail the exact boundaries of the CMCRs, in allocating counties to the CMCRs, we begin with the 9 standard, multiple county units within a state, known as Crop Reporting Districts (CRDs). The boundaries of CRDs are determined by the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA/ERS 2000). To reduce the number of regions from 9 CRDs to 6 CMCRs, we split the 3 middle (in the east-west direc CRDs, each in half in the west-east direction, and assign the western portions of the split CRDs to the corresponding western CMCRs, NW, CW, or SW, depending on whether the CRD was located in the northern, central, or southern part of the state. Similarly, the eastern portions of the split CRDs are assigned to the corresponding eastern CMCRs (NE, CE, or SE).
