# Technology-as-text in the communicative constitution of organization

Senem Güney ⁎, Anthony M. Cresswell

College of Computing and Information, Faculty Fellow, Center for Technology in Government, University at Albany, SUNY, 187 Wolf Road Suite 301, Albany, NY 12205, United States

## a b s t r a c t

In this paper, we introduce the concept of technology-as-text, based on a distinct variant within the perspective of the communicative constitution of organization (CCO). We apply this concept in a case study on the emergence of a new organizational form for IT governance within a state government. Our analysis illustrates the insights to be gained from the technology-as-text concept for the study of constitutive entanglement, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with respect to technology's role in the materialization of authority relationships that constitute organization.

## 1. Introduction

The understanding of technology as more than a collection of inert artifacts (Woolgar, 1991) formed the foundation of research on how the meaning and use of technology get formed and validated in practice (Barley, 1986, 1990; Orlikowski, 1992, 2000). In this study, we intend to build on the structurational foundation (Giddens, 1984) of this previous research to explore the constitutive entanglement of technology and organization (Orlikowski, 2007) in the day-to-day acts of organizational communication.

For this exploration, we use a theory perspective, commonly known as the communicative constitution of organization (CCO) (Ashcraft, Kuhn, & Cooren, 2009; Cooren, Taylor, & Van Every, 2006; Putnam & Nicotera, 2009; Taylor & Cooren, 1997) to study empirical phenomena from a case on IT governance in a state government. CCO-oriented research explores how organization and technology are constituted in communication— where communication is theorized as negotiated representations of dynamic interrelationships between obiects and agencies. Our application of this theory perspective is based on a particular stream within this perspective—the “Montréal School” (Ashcraft et al., 2009; Cooren et al., 2006; Taylor & Van Every, 2000, 2011). Research of the Montréal School distinguishes itself from other perspectives on the discursive construction of organizational phenomena (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2000) and from other approaches in CCO (Putnam & McPhee, 2009). According to this particular school of CCO-oriented research, the constitutive function of communication is grounded in the interdependent relationship between conversation (ongoing <sup>fl</sup>ow of interactions) and text (the script that transforms conversation into a focused response).

The structuring of organization on the text embedded in and mobilized through technology is most specif ically developed in Taylor, Groleau, Heaton, and Van Every's (2001) work. According to Taylor et al. (2001) previous research had a conceptual blind spot on the communicative mechanisms by which the local (inter/action) becomes global (structure)—both in the stream of adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Poole & DeSanctis, 1990, 1992; Poole, Holmes, & DeSanctis, 1991) and in <sup>fi</sup>eldwork-based structurational investigations of the technology–organization interplay (Barley, 1986, 1990, 1996; Orlikowski 1992, 1996, 2000). Taylor et al. (2001) argue that investigations into the communicative constitution of organization are necessary to understand how technology becomes the (infra)structure (text) that stitches to gether dispersed local actions (conversation) for organization to form and function.

We argue that the Montréal School CCO perspective on the emergence of organization out of the conversation-text dynamic presents a useful approach to examine the constitutive entanglement of technology and organization. We use this perspective to pursue the research question, “How does organization form, sociomaterially, in the practice of IT governance?” in our case study. An important concept originating from Montréal School CCO is technology-as-text. This concept is based on the understanding that technology becomes the text that “writes organization into being” (Taylor & Van Every, 2000; Taylor et al., 2001), stabilizing emergent conversations into a focused response on which future conversations can be built. The negotiated (re)construction of authority is a fundamental component of writing the (text of) precedence relationships that sustain organization—who follows whom in the pursuit of organizational goals. The case we analyze here is particularly appropriate to study the role of technology in the negotiated (re)construction of organizational authority, because the case is about IT governance—a domain where major questions of authority and technology come together.

Our purpose in this paper is to explore new conceptualizations of what technology is and what people do with it from an emergent theory perspective. While the perspective of communicative constitution has been making its way into organization studies (Ashcraft et al., 2009), it is still fairly unknown among the IS research community. We believe that new ways of thinking about the technology–organization interplay are critical for IS research to build on previous structurational research (Jones & Karsten, 2008). Investigations into how actors build organization on technology-as-text can particularly extend research on the mobilization of agency, embedded in and enacted through technology (Boudreau & Robey, 2005), in the structuring of organization.

The case we analyze here contributes to the existing literature in IT governance (Brown & Grant, 2005) by examining the communicative–constitutive processes whereby IT governance emerges and plays out in practice. We do not intend to ignore this opportunity for new knowledge about how IT governance actually happens. However, our major concern here is to show how authority relationships in IT governance get established, sociomaterially, in the mobilization of technology for organizing. In the next section, we provide a brief review of the study of IT governance. This review is followed by an explanation of fundamental concepts in Montréal School CCO. Then we present our methods and case analysis. The paper concludes with a discussion on what our analysis suggests for the usefulness of the Montréal School CCO perspective in IS research on the technology–organization interplay.

## 2. A brief review of the literature on IT governance

This review is aimed at pulling together different views about what IT governance is and how it operates. Using such a typology, previous work can be roughly assembled into three streams of research. We will call these the utilitarian-regulatory, public administrative, and public policy streams.

With the exception of a few studies in the public policy stream, research that is most relevant to our discussion here is predominately in the utilitarian/regulatory stream. This work is structural in approach and treats IT governance in terms of a linear rational logic. The underlying assumption in this stream is that certain combinations of structures and authoritative procedures for IT-related decision making are preferable to others based on certain value premises. The utilitarian value preferences are for greater organizational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness. The regulatory value preferences are based in legal or ideological principles (e.g., accountability, transparency, equity, democratic participation, <sup>fi</sup>duciary responsibility, etc.). The utilitarian approach is most frequently expressed in prescriptive management models of IT governance (Beimborn, Schlosser, & Weitzel, 2009; Brown & Grant, 2005; Weill & Ross, 2005). Similar views are also expressed in the private sector guides and frameworks for IT governance (Institute, 2009) and those for government by NASCIO or the GAO's ITIM model (GAO, 2004; NASCIO, 2009). These models sometimes include assessment frameworks and criteria for the quality or effectiveness of IT governance, for example, in terms of a maturity scale, as in ITIM, or value to the organization, as in the Val IT framework (IT Governance Institute, 2009). The regulatory part of this stream consists primarily of more general work vis-à-vis corporate governance, premised on conformance with legal and public policy frameworks such as the Federal Sarbanes–Oxley legislation (Baird & Henderson, 2008; Cearns & Ferran, 2008; Lamoreaux, 2009). Research in this stream also includes attention to the effectiveness of these regulatory regimes (Bhagat, Bolton, & Romano, 2008). Relatively little is known, however, about how these regimes play out in practice or interact with the organizational structure in which they are implemented. Most analyses of the impacts of various governance regimes is based on statistical modeling and do not examine the underlying social processes. The recent record of rapid and diverse modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in the frameworks for IT governance in state government suggests that this lack of emphasis on social processes also carry problems for professional practice (Canestraro, Hrdinová, & Raup-Kounovsky, 2009).

The public administration stream in IT governance research focuses on the exercise of authority and control across levels of government (Robichau & Lynn, 2009). The bulk of material in this literature is structural in focus and includes a mix of normative and empirical content. Governance as a factor in performance is a central concern. Many of these performance-related studies are based on a hierarchical view of the public organization (Lynn, Heinrich, & Hill, 2000; Robichau & Lynn, 2009). While the structurational study of technology (Barley & Tolbert, 1997; Orlikowski, 2000; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004) has been applied to some degree in the study of governance and technology in public organizational settings (Cresswell & Hassan, 2007; Cresswell, Pardo, Thompson, & Zhang, 2006), this approach is not characteristic of this stream, where process is explicitly separated from structure. This is illustrated in studies that de-<sup>fi</sup>ne the problem for public management research as the separation of “how the deck is stacked from how managers <sup>fi</sup>gure things out and exercise discretion within a governance arrangement” (Lynn et al., 2000: 240). The idea of separating structure from process is not restricted to hierarchy-based de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of governance and extends to recent work that increasingly recognizes network-based structures of governance (Moore & Hartley, 2008; Provan & Kenis, 2008). The public administration stream includes an eclectic mix of recommended practices with empirical studies of links between performance and governance, with less emphasis on prescriptive models of good governance than in the utilitarian/regulatory stream.

There is speci<sup>fi</sup>c attention to social process in a distinct line of IT governance research in the public policy stream. This line of research extends a conception of governance that includes citizen participation in elections, policy making, and policy implementation. Studies of co-production of services by providers and clients (Bovaird, 2007) as well as studies of public participation in IT development and use fall in this line. The case study by Girard and Stark (2007), for example, illustrates how a government organization's information decisions and policies can affect the public and can in turn be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the public's response. Another group of studies in the public administration stream also examine governance as an emergent social process, but from a more political perspective and without a consistent theory framework. Brown (1997)'s case study treats governance in structural terms and examines the emergence of hybrid forms. The comparative cases studied by Bekkers (2007) trace the political processes of back of<sup>fi</sup>ce integration in terms of trust formation and the importance of informal relationships. The importance of relationships is also examined in Schwartz and Hirschheim (2003)'s study of how IT alignment with business develops and in Boudreau and Robey (2005)'s study of IT integration from a human agency perspective. These studies follow a path established in earlier studies of IT systems development from a social process perspective (Hirschheim, Klein, & Lyytinen, 1995; Hirschheim & Newman, 1991; Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Robey, 1994).

While different streams in IT governance research put different levels of theoretical emphasis on social process, the existing work in this area does not quite embrace the assertion that communication is constitutive of the structures that support as well as the processes that realize governance. The next section describes a particular variant in CCO that explains how governance happens as an emergent process of organizing in and through communication.

## 3. The constitution of organization in communication

While CCO has recently become an umbrella term for the thinking that communication generates, not simply expresses, organizational reality (Putnam & McPhee, 2009), its roots can be traced back to Barnard (1938)'s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of three fundamental elements of organization: (1) persons who are able to communicate with each other and (2) who are willing to contribute to action (3) to accomplish a common purpose (Ashcraft et al., 2009: 7). The current theorizing of CCO has been developing in the study of organizationa communication within the interpretivist paradigm (Putnam, 1983) as a position against the ontological separation of organization and communication. CCO theorists explore how organization is constituted as “a con<sup>fi</sup>guring of diverse agencies, human, artifactual, and technical, held together in spite of their differences, by communication” (Taylor & Van Every, 2011: 242).

The constitution of organization as a con<sup>fi</sup>guring of diverse agencies has been investigated from a structurational view (for example in the work of DeSanctis and Poole, 1994). This research has shown how interacting agents create, justify, or resist collective rules and resources, and how communication enables organization across time and space (McPhee, 1985, 2004). These studies fall under a variant of CCO that focuses on how communication (agency) and structure in<sup>fl</sup>uence each other in a recursive duality. This focus Taylor et al. (2001) argue, makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to answer the question of how one set of local (inter)actions get the authority to shape multiple sets of local interactions and to know “when organization is quite organized.” The role of technology in linking the communicative actions “here-and-now” to actions “there-and-then” in the constitution of organization is the focus of a distinct variant of CCO (Taylor et al., 2001), known as the Montréal School (Ashcraft et al., 2009; Brummans, 2006).

Montréal School CCO-oriented research aims to build a “precise theory of communication that has as its objective to trace the genesis and grounding of organizational form and process in the communication event” (Taylor, 2009: 154–155). In this approach, a critical topic of inquiry is how authority is constructed sociomaterially in communication for organization to emerge and be sustained. Researchers taking the Montréal School CCO perspective point to the etymological link between authority and authorship (which share the Latin root auctor). In this distinct CCO perspective, authority is conceptualized as the right to author the text that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the system of agency relationships in processes of organizing. Conversation, as a CCO term, refers to the universe of interactions among humans and nonhumans who relate to each other around a shared objective. Text comprises the communicative acts and the products of these acts that emerge out of and form scripts for the constantly evolving conversation. For example. if the conversation of the organization involves project-related storytelling around the water cooler, going to the water cooler to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the latest news about a project would be part of the text of the organization. CCO posits that organization is constituted in this conversation-text cycle—ongoing conversations become the site for emergent patterns of relationships and meanings, which are stabilized into texts that move through time and space and become the basis of future conversations.

Organization is constituted (authored into being) in this dynamic relationship between conversation and text. This communicative constitution takes place in the collective acknowledgment of a particular text as representing the recognizable (that is, legitimate) system of relationships linking the members to what they do and with whom they do it (Taylor & Van Every, 2000: 36). An important question in this process is determining who and what has precedence over what and whom; that is, how hierarchy gets established in the constitution of organization as a super-structure of agencies. In this study, we focus on the sociomaterial role of technology-as-text in the “writing of the organization” (Taylor et al., 2001) and in the constitution of precedence relationships that are author(iz)ed for the accomplishment of organizational objectives.

We should note that our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of technology-as-text is slightly different from previous uses of this concept in the literature (Hutchby, 2001). Our de<sup>fi</sup>nition is based on the CCO view that technology sociomaterially participates in the writing of precedence relationships by shaping the text that legitimates these relationships between the members and between their activities. This conceptualization is different from the use of the text metaphor to argue for the interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility of technology and to describe technologies as fundamentally open forms (Grint & Woolgar, 1997). In the CCO view, text “stitches” dispersed local actions. The concept of technology-as-text from this view focuses on the material ability of technology to stabilize the ongoing <sup>fl</sup>ux of conversations into a pattern of meanings and relationships, which represents precedence and thereby constitutes the organization. This understanding of what people do with and through technologyas-text is not the same as the argument that technology's material properties can be reinterpreted through human agency (Boudreau & Robey, 2005).

We should also underline the two important distinctions between Montréal School CCO-oriented research and structurational studies (Barley, 1986; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 2000), and even other CCO-oriented research (McPhee, 2004; McPhee & Zaug, 2009), on the constitutive entanglement of technology and organization. First, according to Montréal School CCO, organization emerges as a process of writing, where agency is authorship; that is, the ability to (re)construct the text of the organization. Precedence relationships that constitute the organization are de<sup>fi</sup>ned, challenged, negotiated, and rede<sup>fi</sup>ned as agents interact to author the text that is extracted from the conversations here-and-now and that becomes the script for conversations there-and-then. When technology enters into negotiations over precedence relationships, its built-in text, which represents a certain way of linking the members to what they do and with whom they do it, becomes part of this writing process.

Another important assertion of the Montréal School CCO perspective is that agency is not simply to have the capacity to act but also to have the ability to act for another. This aspect of agency is usually formulated in a triadic communicative unit of A–B–X (Taylor & Van Every, 2000), also known as a co-orientation unit. Coorientation refers to “a simultaneous relationship to something to be done, and to others with whom one is doing it” (Taylor, 2009). A and B are human or nonhuman agents that relate to each other for the accomplishment of a shared (organizational) goal X. For simplicity of description, we will assume that both A and B are human agents (or clusters of human agents) who share X as the object of their interest. If A and B belong to different communities of practice or epistemic domains (Brown & Duguid, 2001) (and most contemporary organizations operate on the collaborative action among units belonging to distinct epistemic domains), it is rather unlikely that they will instantly align with each other to achieve X. More realistically, in order to “organize." A and B will interact to determine who will follow whom: in other words, they will need to settle their precedence relationships. Taylor and Van Every (2011) also call these “<sup>fi</sup>rst-to-second relationships”—which agent is <sup>fi</sup>rst (acting to mobilize) and which agent is second (being mobilized to act) to achieve X.

Then the question becomes why any (human) agent would accept the position of secondness. Agents take on secondness (A) when they recognize that the other agent (B) is acting for the organization and its purpose. This recognition needs to be (re)instantiated in interaction between A and B, even when the precedence of B over A is a given (as in a bureaucracy) in order for B to claim legitimate authority—rooted in the text that legitimates B's agency to mobilize A's agency. Authority that is not rooted in such a text and is not (re)instantiated in conversation is coercive, not legitimate, and cannot sustain collective action, at least not very effectively. This is how organization is communicatively constituted as a con<sup>fi</sup>guring of diverse agencies in the dynamic relationship between text and conversation. The authorship of the text that legiti mates precedence relationships gets (re)instantiated in conversation over space and time for “organization to organize” as a super-structure of agencies. In contemporary organizations, these negotiations about how to author the organization often take place around issues of technology adoption, where technology-as-text is mobilized to settle <sup>fi</sup>rst-to-second relationships in an A–B–X unit in a particular way over another.

In the following section, we provide examples from a case study on the creation of an IT governance framework for the business of a state government. This case describes the emergence of an ad hoc clustering that turned into an institutionally recognized governance body among the Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cers (CIOs) of social service-related departments in this state government. Evidence from this case illustrates the authoring of the text that settled precedence relationships, which paved the way for the emergence of a new organizational form in the practice of IT governance in this state government.

The emergence of SESAC is a critical case, because it provides an opportunity to con-<sup>fi</sup>rm, challenge or extend the propositions of an existing theory on the emergence of organization. ## 5. Case study: IT governance as emergent organization

## 5.1. The institutional landscape before SESAC

The institutional landscape of the state government in 2007, which marked the beginning of SESAC, involved multiple positions of authority for the governance of IT. The State Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (SCIO), newly appointed in 2007, directed the largely centralized IT service functions of the Technology Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (email, state data center, etc.) and the policy functions of the Of<sup>fi</sup>ce of the Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (OCIO). The latter included approval of IT procurement plans for the executive branch agencies. State departments had their own Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cers (CIOs), who were responsible for their departments' IT functions and reported to the department Commissioners or other department heads appointed by the Governor. The Governor's budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce reviewed and approved all departmental budgets and participated in policy deliberations. In addition, the state General Services Department was the executive branch responsible for overseeing all procurement for the business of the state. The State Comptroller, a separate constitutional department not under the Governor, had some authority over procurement and contracting as well as operations of the state accounting system.

## 5.2. The Cúram requirement

The events that brought a group of CIOs together as an ad hoc governance body began with a con<sup>fl</sup>ict over a proposal to implement Cúram, an enterprise administration application based on an objectoriented architecture (OOA), as the statewide standard for social services administration. The state Department of Labor (DoL) had been chosen for a test Cúram implementation and had been working in it for several months prior to the appointment of the new SCIO. DoL staff had evaluated the test results and made a presentation on their assessment of Cúram, which was not favorable. DoL decided that the object-oriented architecture for Cúram was not a good development path for their department or for the several partners with whom they shared data and other applications; they preferred a more open, service-oriented architecture (SOA). However, the SCIO announced plans to adopt Cúram as a requirement for use in state social services departments. In response, DoL joined with four other departments—the Departments of Health, Children and Family Services, Social Services, and Taxation & Finance. CIOs from these <sup>fi</sup>ve departments proposed SOA as an alternative architecture to build applications for their data sharing needs. An impasse resulted in which the CIOs refused to adopt Cúram and the SCIO refused to approve their budget proposals for high priority IT development projects. The impasse was resolved when the Governor's staff approved the procurements, and the <sup>fi</sup>ve CIOs met with the SCIO to resolve the Cúram matter.

## 5.3. The emergence of a new governance body

Resolving the Cúram matter resulted in both a short-term answer for the immediate issue and a longer-term practice for IT governance. The immediate discussions between the SCIO and the <sup>fi</sup>ve CIOs focused on the value of SOA as a development path for these <sup>fi</sup>ve departments, given their current IT con<sup>fi</sup>guration and data sharing needs. The SCIO asked the CIOs to prepare a white paper making the case for their position, which they did. That paper formed the basis for these departments to pursue the SOA path rather than to adopt Cúram. This process also prompted the CIOs to begin informal meetings to share ideas and discuss common problems and project plans. They agreed to a regular Friday meeting schedule and added three other departments' CIOs with common interests and service objectives. The new CIO members were from the Governor's Of<sup>fi</sup>ce of Employee Relations, The Department of Motor Vehicles, and the Department of Mental Health. These CiOs chose a formal name for their group that reflected their common interests and service objectives: SESAC—Social and Economic Services Advisory Council (our pseudonym, which preserves the social services focus of the real name).

## 6. Analysis: the sociomateriality of IT governance

Before we analyze the case events. it is important to briefly mention the fundamental material differences between the platforms of OOA and SOA. The older technology, OOA, was born in the early 1960s, the pre-Web era, when distributed processing and network operations or services were very new and limited. Software objects contain data that are part of the operation of the code. These objects can be reused in different applications on the same system, but they are not readily used across systems in distributed processing. The more contemporary technology, SOA, emerged in 2002. In this architecture, sections of computer code that execute actions are referred to as “services.” A service is an open component that supports rapid composition of distributed applications. “Web Services” are commonly known implementations of the service concept. The distinguishing capability of SOA is to provide “the necessary support for the consolidation of multiple services into a single composite service corresponding to the overall process” (D'Ambrogio & Bocciorelli, 2007: 78).

A simple example would be a section of code that checks the validity of a credit card number as part of an online shopping transaction. That service would be just one part of the overall shopping process, but it would be a common component in many different kinds of shopping applications. A server (typically an online computing system) is the provider of a service, which makes SOA-based applications well suited to the Web environment. These applications are also well suited to distributed processing and integration of business processes across systems.

The fundamental architectural differences between these two design platforms were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the case we studied to understand the sociomateriality of IT governance. Next, we elaborate on certain events from this case to illustrate how technology-as-text was mobilized in authoring the text that materialized SESAC during the episode of the Cúram requirement.

## 6.1. Precedence relationships before and after the emergence of SESAC

At the beginning of the Cúram episode, there was not a clear precedence relationship for all issues or courses of action involving the SCIO and the various department CIOs. While there was a common understanding of the SCiO's precedence in some aspects of procurement-related decisions. no such understanding had been established about statewide standards for department management applications. The SCIO and the <sup>fi</sup>ve CIOs who formed SESAC had different positions on who should act and who should be acted on in making decisions generally about standards and particularly about establishing which management applications the SESAC members' departments would be allowed to use. Thus the question of implementing Cúram presented an opportune moment, for both sides, to de<sup>fi</sup>ne an ambiguous situation with respect to their A–B–X relationships.

The two technologies, OOA and SOA, represented markedly different design methods and requirements. These material differences played an important part in writing the text that legitimated precedence relationships between the SCIO and the CIOs and, thereby, in constituting the organization of SESAC. The SCIO, making a move to act for the state government's goal to consolidate IT and reduce costs, seized the moment to push the CIOs to accept OOA-based Cúram as a common architecture for their operations. The imposition of OOA, as a highly centralized and monolithic design platform, re<sup>fl</sup>ected the organizational objectives of the SCIO. The CIOs' counter-move to say, “We want SOA” showed their desire to operate in a design environment that allowed the use of diverse systems and a mix of applications, enabling collaboration and selective shared processes. The CIOs' preference for SOA over OOA also indicated that they would not readily accept a centralized approach to IT governance within the state government.

When the <sup>fi</sup>ve CIOs resisted the imposition of Cúram, SCIO refused to approve those CIOs' procurement requests for several high-priority new IT projects: no Cúram, no procurements. This was the SCIO's attempt to extend precedence in a settled area—IT procurement—to the as yet unsettled area of application standards. In response to this impasse, the CIOs appealed through their department heads to the Governor's of<sup>fi</sup>ce. They made a case for their interpretation of the state government's interests and how adopting SOA <sup>fi</sup>t those interests. They also made a case for approving the new projects, which were aligned with the Governor's priorities. The Governor's budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce then approved the new procurements over the SCIO's objections and asked the parties to discuss and resolve the Cúram issue, which they did. At the end, the Cúram requirement was dropped and these departments were allowed to pursue development based on SOA. More importantly, precedence with respect to standardization and the authority that came with it for IT governance stayed with these CIOs as members of a “governance cluster.” The mobilization of SOA as-text, sociomaterially, was fundamental to the institutional recognition and legitimation of this governance cluster. ## 6.2. The “writing” of SESAC

The <sup>fi</sup>ve CIOs, who initially opposed the SCIO's promotion of Cúram, agreed that OOA was incompatible with much of their installed software base and staff capabilities. Neither did they see Cúram as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient platform for their cross-department information sharing needs. Their clustering as an ad hoc governance body gave them a collective agency to act and speak for the business needs of their social service-related state departments. The SCIO promoted Cúram in pursuit of state-level IT goals: Consolidation, cost savings greater standardization, integration, and resource sharing, which were linked to the new Governor's campaign promises for rapid progress in reform and improvements across the state government. The Cúram proposal <sup>fi</sup>t easily into this agenda and could have resulted in some application cost savings through volume procurement. To claim “<sup>fi</sup>rstness” (acting to mobilize) as an agent of the statewide agenda, the SCIO attempted to use precedence in one decision area to establish it in another area. However, the political culture of the state and the long history of relatively high autonomy among government departments made it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve wide consensus on such an issue as Cúram or to unilaterally impose a statewide decision.

Under the executive branch policy at the time, departments' IT procurement plans required approval by the SCIO before they could be included in the Governor's executive budget. When the Cúram issue got to the Governor's budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce, it enacted a different set of A–B–X relationships: As an agent of the Governor, the budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce had legitimate authority over both the CIOs and the SCIO. Faced with the impasse over the CIOs' procurement plans, the Governor's budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce had to determine the precedence relationships between the CIOs, acting collectively for their individual departments, and the SCIO. By allowing the procurement plans to proceed, the budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce legitimated the CIOs' collective position with respect to Cúram and initiated an additional dialog between the parties, extending the shifts in governance.

In this case, the term “enterprise”—with its implication for the sweeping consolidation of different technology frameworks across multiple units of an organization—was at the basis of many tensions. The phrase “consolidation for consolidation's sake” came up several times in the SESAC members' descriptions of what tipped the balance for them between enterprise solutions that they judged to be appropriate for their businesses and those that were not, such as the OOA-based Cúram. The requirement to implement Cúram would have enabled consolidation for consolidation's sake but it would have been detrimental to the individual business needs (and the evolving code development for those needs) in each government department. The alliance among the CIOs happened as a counter-move to author a different text for the same objective of consolidation for enterprise-level IT governance. SOA, with all of its material distinctions from OOA, became the basis of the text for IT governance that the CIOs acted collectively to author.

SESAC emerged as, what the members called, a “Little e”, which became a new text for enterprise-level IT governance in the state government. The term “Little e” was coined by a SESAC member to describe the practice of IT governance at the enterprise level of a particular domain of business within the state government, rather than for the government as a whole. There was a precedent, an existing formal coalition of CIOs from criminal justice units, to this practice of forming enterprise clusters at a level below the state. SESAC was the <sup>fi</sup>rst institutionally recognized governance body, however, to be formed on an alliance around a design platform. Compared to OOA, SOA was better suited to the shared services and integrated processing across systems in the technical infrastructure of the services delivered by the SESAC members departments. If there were no more to the Cúram issue than the budget of<sup>fi</sup>ce's decision, it would represent at best a small adjustment in IT governance. However, the CIOs' ad hoc alliance against Cúram was a statement of their longer-term IT governance objectives.

The “writing” of SESAC into being was built on SOA-as-text. This design platform provided the material support for the rapid composition of distributed applications. It also helped form the organizational infrastructure necessary for the conversations among an informal group of CIOs to be stabilized into the text of “Little e.” The following account by a member CIO describes how SESAC emerged from ongoing conversations, which carried over space and time and stabilized in a new text for IT governance. This text was authored on a collective opposition to OOA and a collective preference for SOA:

“We don't really take a step technically without a conversation. It's important that we have that conversation, because […] there is experiences that we've had. There are vendor relationships that we've had and we have an opportunity to put those on the table, drill down and learn from one another. Initially, it was survival […]. It was a necessary collaboration to survive, because we were being forced down back […] to be positioned now where we would not be bringing products to market. We would be in a position where the framework [Cúram] would have been extremely limiting to us, would not have provided us with the agility that we all needed.” (from the focus interview with SESAC members).

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

As we mentioned earlier, existing research, especially within the public policy stream, investigates the interplay between the formal/structural and informal aspects of IT governance. For example, the cases reported by Bekkers (2007) and Brown (1997) highlight the importance of trust and informal relationships in the emergence of hybrid forms of IT governance. Our case study contributes to the work in this area that takes the perspectives of human agency (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) and social process (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Robey, 1994) by examining how informal relationships around IT systems development and integration lead to interactions out of which hybrid governance forms emerge.

Our study differs from previous research on the interplay between technology and organization by grounding the analysis of this interplay in Montréal School CCO. This study contributes to the current thinking on this interplay by revealing, through CCO concepts, the “micro-mechanisms of sociomateriality” in the interactions whereby governance is realized as an emergent process of organizing. While previous research has shown how human agency and social process are integral to IT-related decision making, only by taking a CCO approach could we demonstrate the sociomaterial agency of technology in the struggle over precedence for IT governance. CCO conceptualizations of how organization is authored into being are essential to understand and examine how the constitutive entanglement of technology and organization unfolds in practice. Only through the CCO view can we account for the symbolic and material agency of technology-as-text in the writing of a super-structure of agencies into patterns that stabilize the ongoing conversation, establish precedence between the stakeholders in the conversation, and constitute organization.

The CCO-oriented conceptualization of technology-as-text is based on an understanding of technology as a “hybrid” (à la Latour. 1994) that is a complex and historical entanglement of material and subiective elements (Taylor et al., 2001). Design architectures, such as OOA and SOA, are quite literally texts that represent different grammars for code development. These texts are written by a community of designers located in a certain time and place, in a certain culture of work. The evocation of “reading” and “writing” in the CCO view of technology-as-text differs from the previous use of these metaphors in the study of technology, because in CCO, text-ness is not so much about the interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility that comes from the situatedness of technology design and implementation (Grint & Woolgar, 1997). Text, in CCO, is a symbolic and material mediator of communication that transforms conversations into a focused response. In Taylor et al.'s (2001) words, text “mediates the structuring activities of interaction, whatever their location” and text artifacts, such as information and communication technologies, make organization possible by materializing and carrying its authority from the local to the global conversation.

In our case study, we applied this understanding of technology-as-text to an analysis of events around the adoption of one design platform over another. This application showed how the communicative constitution of organization for IT governance was mediated by technology as a hybrid text artifact of symbolic meaning and material inscription. The case events illustrated the constitutive entanglement of authority relationships between different agents and the choice between OOA and SOA. The case also showed how the material properties of technology provided the grounds for symbolic acts—extending certain modes of ordering and dissipating others—to (re)settle precedence and constitute organization.

Like any other text artifact, technology-as-text never represents a neutral mode of ordering among operations and the agents who carry out these operations. The case events presented vivid empirical evidence on how organizational actors mobilized the text embedded in technology to (re)write their agency relationships in order to keep the upper hand, or “<sup>fi</sup>rstness” (Taylor & Van Every, 2011). This is how, we argue based on CCO, technology and organization are entangled in inseparable ways—in the writing of the text that connects A to B and de<sup>fi</sup>nes their positions with respect to each other in the accomplishment of X. A and B need a text to give global meaning to their local actions (that is, to make these actions “organizational”) and to legitimate their agency relationships with each other. Technology becomes part of this text by enabling a particular mode of ordering for the precedence between A and B. This is also why technology adoption is never simply a technical issue but also always a political question of how authority gets distributed and enacted in organizations.

We do not intend to claim that authority is up for grabs in every organizational-communicative encounter or that technology provides the only text for its enactment. The role of technology-as-text in the enactment of authority becomes clear in situations that are most explicitly ripe for what Goffman (1959) called a “frame game”—when interactants do not have an agreed-upon de<sup>fi</sup>nition (text) of the situation in which they <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves and they make moves to de<sup>fi</sup>ne it to their own advantage. The situ ation in which SCIO and the CIOs found themselves put them in a frame game. We can argue that especially because this frame game was taking place within a bureaucracy, the CIOs built their move on SOA-as-text—a design platform that enabled collaboration, sharing applications, and “agility” as one SESAC member said. This text matched the text that would establish their <sup>fi</sup>rstness and give them collective authority over the SCIO, who held legitimate authority over them individually. While this instance illustrates a very explicit rendition of the frame game, every communicative instance implicitly involves this game, especially in organizational encounters (see Benoit-Barné & Cooren, 2009 for an exemplary analysis of how organizational actors re-instantiate their authority relationships in routine interactions and mobilize objects in this process). The communicative constitution of organization is in this game of (re)establishing the text of precedence between agents in their ongoing conversation so that they can con tinue to engage with each other as agents of (acting for) the organization. In instances where there is no recognizable text of precedence, agents can readily mobilize the text embedded in technology to gain advantage in the frame game.

Potential for new insights into the sociomateriality of organizing is in this understanding of communi cative constitution. This understanding gives us new conceptual tools to investigate how technology is mobilized in the conversations where moves and counter-moves of authorship are plaved out to write the text of the organization. We believe that these tools will prove useful to IS researchers, who are interested in new ways of thinking about and examining the question of constitutive entanglement and extending existing work on communicative practices in processes of structuration (Boczkowski, 1999; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Yates & Orlikowski, 1992). We hope that future empirical work will put these conceptual tools to test in the ongoing effort to explain the fundamental insepa rability of technology and organization.
