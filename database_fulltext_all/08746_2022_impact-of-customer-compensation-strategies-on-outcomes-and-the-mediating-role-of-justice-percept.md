# IMPACT OF CUSTOMER COMPENSATION STRATEGIES ON OUTCOMES AND THE MEDIATING ROLE OF JUSTICE PERCEPTIONS: A LONGITUDINAL STUDY OF TARGET’S DATA BREACH<sup>1</sup>

Hartmut Hoehle Mannheim Business School, University of Mannheim, 1-6 68161 Mannheim, GERMANY {hoehle@uni-mannheim.de}

Viswanath Venkatesh Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA 24061 U.S.A. {vvenkatesh@vvenkatesh.us}

Susan A. Brown Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, McClelland Hall 430Q, Tucson, AZ 85721 U.S.A. {suebrown@eller.arizona.edu}

Bennett J. Tepper Fisher College of Business, Ohio State University, 2008B Fisher Hall, Columbus, OH 43210 U.S.A. {tepper.15@osu.edu}

Thomas Kude ESSEC Business School, 3 Avenue Bernard Hirsch, 95000 Cergy-Pontoise, FRANCE {kude@essec.edu}

Data breaches are a major threat to organizations from both financial and customer relations perspectives. We developed a nomological network linking post-breach compensation strategies to key outcomes, namely continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining, with the effects being mediated by customers’ justice perceptions. We conducted a longitudinal field study investigating Target’s data breach in 2013 that affected more than 110 million customers. We examined customers’ expectations toward compensation immediately after the breach was confirmed (survey 1) and their experiences after reparations were made (survey 2). Evidence from polynomial regression and response surface analyses of data collected from 388 affected customers showed that customers’ justice perceptions were influenced by the actual compensation provided as well as the type and extent of compensation an organization could and should have provided (i.e., customers’ compensation expectations). Interestingly, both positive and negative expectation disconfirmation led to less favorable justice perceptions compared to when expectations were met. Justice perceptions were, in turn, associated with key outcomes. We discuss implications for research on data security, information systems, and justice theory.

Keywords: Data security breach, compensation, justice, Target

## Introduction

Target’s then-CEO Gregg Steinhafel described his initial reaction to the attack in late 2013 that involved personal information of more than 110 million customers: “My heart sunk … [our customers] have a lot of confidence and faith in our brand and they hold us to a very high standard. And we didn’t live up to that standard. So, we’re going to have to figure out how we can elevate our game” (CNBC 2014). On May 5, 2014, Gregg Steinhafel resigned.

Data breaches, in which individuals gain access to the personal data of millions of customers, have affected several organizations including Adobe, Anthem, Equifax, Home Depot, Sony, and Target (Wall Street Journal 2015). A data breach differs from a traditional service failure.<sup>2</sup> Whereas it is possible to fix traditional service failures (e.g., provide a new meal), fully fixing the problem is almost always impossible in the context of a data breach: the lost data can never be recaptured and remains a lifelong inconvenience in some cases (e.g., stolen social security number). Thus, organizations need to develop unique strategies for responding to this problem. Given that it is not possible to prevent all data breaches (Goode et al. 2017; PWC 2015), organizations must implement interventions that help to mitigate the negative impacts of data breaches on customers (Kim and Kwon 2019). The success of the interventions will be reflected in customer sentiment indicators such as customers’ shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaints about the breached organization (Forrester Research 2011; Hu et al. 2012; Posey et al. 2011; PWC 2015). Data breaches, such as those at Equifax, Home Depot, Sony, and Target, show that, to repair the business-to-customer relationship, breached organizations often throw money at the problem by providing customers with various compensation packages—free products or services, discounts, or free credit monitoring. Such customer compensation strategies are expensive and getting the level of compensation right to repair customer sentiment is crucial.

A review of the research in information systems (IS) security shows that there has been little systematic study of the effectiveness of these intervention strategies.<sup>3</sup> In particular, it is unclear whether or not costly interventions successfully restore targeted customers’ perceptions of fairness and favorably influence critical downstream outcomes. We extend research on IS security, especially related to customer compensation to data breaches (e.g., Choi et al. 2016; Goode et al. 2017), and build on justice theory<sup>4</sup> and congruence research to develop a nomological network that links customers expectations and experiences regarding type and level of com pensation to customers’ justice perceptions that, in turn, are theorized to affect customer outcomes in the form of continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining. To test our model, we collected longi tudinal data related to Target’s 2013 data breach. Consistent with expectation confirmation research (e.g., Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Brown et al. 2008), we collected expectations regarding data breach compensation and experiences with the compensation separately in order to minimize potential biases (e.g., Festinger 1962; Irving and Meyer 1994).

Our key contribution thus relates to research on IS security, as we leverage a well-established theory base—justice theory— to examine the effect of customer compensation as a mechanism to restore customers’ justice perceptions. Our work is one of the few customer-centric investigations of how organizations can use compensation as a response to mitigate the negative effects of data breaches on customers’ justice perceptions. Hui et al. (2016) reviewed the theoretical developments in IS security research and concluded that the progression of research in MIS Quarterly has primarily focused on (1) behavioral compliance, (2) risk management, (3) investments in IT security, and (4) market effects of IT security. Their review illustrates the rich problems surrounding IT security and provides a foundation for future research in this area. Specifically, with a few recent exceptions (e.g., Goode et al. 2017; Syed 2019), research has overlooked customer reactions in the context of data breaches (Bansal and Zahedi 2015; Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Chakraborty et al. 2016). We advance this important area by identifying mediating mechanisms that are crucial to developing a scientific domain (see MacKinnon and Fairchild 2009)—for instance, Judge and Colquitt (2004) identified the mediators that carried the effect of justice on stress. Mathieu et al. (2008) note that “developing an understanding of the underlying mechanisms or mediators (i.e., M), through which X predicts Y, or XöMöY relationships, is what moves organizational research beyond dust-bowl empiricism and toward a true science” (p. 203). Thus, we contribute a rich nomological network in a critical IS-related managerial context, namely data breaches and IS security.

## Conceptual Framework and Hypotheses Development

The conceptual framework guiding our research is shown in Figure 1. The model builds on literature on service failures and justice perceptions arguing that customers expect compensation following negative service experiences (Smith et al. 1999) such as data breaches (Crossler et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2019). Further, customers expect organizations to keep their data safe (Malhotra and Malhotra 2011) and when this does not happen, it results in a service failure.

Customers will cognitively match their compensation expectations with the compensation provided and their expectations will be either met or unmet. Some customers will likely have no experience with a data breach. Regardless, they will form expectations regarding compensation based on their own experiences. Zeithaml et al. (1993) presented 11 items that represent the nature and determinants of service expectations. Among the items influencing expectations are past experience, implicit and explicit promises made by the company, personal needs, and “enduring service intensifiers” that represent influence stemming from an individual’s personal service philo sophy as well as expectations of others who are important to the person. In the absence of direct personal experience, an individual will draw on related experiences as well as experiences of others (e.g., as shared in the news) to form their expectations regarding an acceptable level of response from the organization.

Met expectations (i.e., expectation confirmation), is the scenario in which the breached organization provides the exact compensation that customers expected from the organization. Unmet expectations (i.e., expectation disconfirmation), is the scenario in which the provided compensation is either too high (i.e., positive disconfirmation) or too low (i.e., negative disconfirmation). In other words, to determine if they feel justly treated, customers compare the compensation they received with what the breached organizations could and should have done in response to the breach, consistent with justice conceptualizations that are based on counterfactual thinking (Brockner et al. 2015; Folger and Cropanzano 2001).

To conceptualize expectation disconfirmation, we build on Goode et al. (2017) and research on congruence that has often leveraged polynomial modeling and response surface analyses to examine expectation disconfirmation levels in IS (Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Nishant et al. 2019; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) and management (Edwards 1994, 1996; Edwards and Cable 2009; Lambert et al. 2012; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). As explained in greater detail later, Target provided a discount and free credit monitoring to affected customers in response to its data breach. In developing our hypotheses, we follow Smith et al. (1999) and argue that a discount—a compensation with a monetary focus for customers—affects distributive justice perceptions, whereas credit monitoring—a compensation with a focus on managing customers’ inconveniences—affects interactional and procedural justice perceptions.

## Effects of Expectation Disconfirmation on Justice Perceptions

Justice theory focuses on antecedents and consequences of individuals’ perceptions of being fairly treated (Greenberg 1990). Prior work has relied on justice theory to explain dissatisfied customers’ complaining behavior (Blodgett et al. 1993; Feng et al. 2019), customer’s satisfaction with efforts at service recovery (Smith et al. 1999), or customer satisfaction in general (Barclay and Kiefer 2014; Bitner and Hubbert 1994;

Martínez Tur et al. 2006). Research on organizational justice has traditionally focused on three justice perceptions, namely distributive, procedural, and interactional justice (Greenberg 2009).<sup>5</sup> Distributive justice perceptions refer to the perceived fairness of the outcome of a decision or exchange in terms of the allocation of resources (Colquitt 2001). In the context of our work, distributive justice perceptions relate to the subjective benefit that customers receive to offset the inconveniences or costs that were caused by the data breach (Smith et al. 1999). Based on the notion of counterfactual thinking (Folger and Cropanzano 2001), we propose that when a provided discount compensation falls short of customers’ expected compensation (i.e., negative disconfirmation), customers’ distributive justice perceptions are likely to be low. A discount represents a monetary benefit for cus tomers, and if they feel undercompensated, the costs asso ciated with the breach are likely to outweigh the perceived benefits associated with the discount. Such negative expectation disconfirmation levels can have negative effects on distributive justice perceptions (for a discussion, see Smith et al. 1999). Drawing from research on congruence (Edwards and Cable 2009; Lambert et al. 2003; Lambert et al. 2012) and a counterfactual conceptualization of justice perceptions (Folger and Cropanzano 2001), we propose that positive expectation disconfirmation will also decrease customers distributive justice perceptions. Customers who feel that the provided discount is too high (i.e., perceived overcompensation) may believe that the breached organization could have provided less compensation because, based on the available information about the data breach, there was no obligation to extensively compensate customers. Further, overcompensa tion might lead to the impression that the breached organization could generally offer its products at lower prices at the expense of current profit margins, thus negatively affecting distributive justice perceptions. Consequently, when perceived overcompensation related to discounts occurs, distribu tive justice perceptions will also be low. These arguments are also in line with empirical research on customer reactions toward data breaches (for a discussion, see Goode et al. 2017). Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1a: As negative disconfirmation decreases to fit between expected and experienced compensation perceptions related to a discount as a response to a data breach, distributive justice perceptions increase; as positive disconfirmation increases, distributive justice perceptions decrease.

The perceived fairness of procedures used to accomplish a certain outcome is referred to as procedural justice perceptions (Barsky and Kaplan 2007). When a data breach has occurred, procedural justice perceptions relate to the perceived fairness of the organizational process and procedures in resolving the data breach (Chen et al. 2018; Patterson et al. 2006). The concept of interactional justice perceptions refers to the perceptions of fair interpersonal treatment (Barsky and Kaplan 2007; Bernerth 2020; Bies and Moag 1986). Interactional justice perceptions are perceived to be high if individuals believe they have been treated with respect, dignity, truthfulness, and propriety (Colquitt 2001; Matta et al. 2020), and if they feel that they are given explanations for the decision that led to certain outcomes (Folger and Konovsky 1989; Zhao et al. 2022).

We propose that free credit monitoring affects customers procedural and interactional justice perceptions. Although credit monitoring has a fee, it is a nonmonetary form of com pensation that focuses on supporting customers in monitoring their personal finances and credit card transactions. Credit monitoring can be seen as a procedure that helps customers to overcome the effort associated with managing the personal consequences of a data breach. If customers feel undercompensated and if they expected more extensive free credit monitoring (e.g., for extended periods of time), their perceived procedural justice perceptions will be low because they will believe that they would need to personally put procedures in place for monitoring their credit card transactions. This will also be the case for interactional justice perceptions because negative disconfirmation regarding credit monitoring will cause affected customers to feel disrespected and left alone to manage their personal finances. Building on these arguments, we propose that positive expectation disconfirmation related to credit monitoring will also be associated with low procedural and interactional justice perceptions (see Edwards and Cable 2009; Lambert et al. 2003; Lambert et al. 2012). A key reason for this is that customers who feel overcompensated in terms of credit monitoring will have doubts about the breached organization’s motives and concerns about the severity of the problem. Thus, customers who receive more credit monitoring compensation than expected from the breached organization may become suspicious that some information and procedures about the breach were hidden and not communicated truthfully (Gillespie and Dietz 2009; Schweitzer et al. 2006; Tomlinson et al. 2004). Generally, the higher positive expectation disconfirmation regarding credit monitoring becomes, the more customers will view the organization as irresponsible leading to lower levels of procedural and interactional justice perceptions (Nakayachi and Watabe 2005; Schweitzer et al. 2006). Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1b: As negative disconfirmation decreases to fit between expected and experienced compensation perceptions related to credit monitoring as a response to a data breach, procedural justice perceptions increase; as positive disconfirmation increases, interactional justice perceptions decrease.

Hypothesis 1c: As negative disconfirmation decreases to fit between expected and experienced compensation perceptions related to credit monitoring as a response to a data breach, interactional justice perceptions increase; as positive disconfirmation increases, procedural justice perceptions decrease.

## Effects of Absolute Level of Expectation Confirmation on Justice Perceptions

Our second set of hypotheses focuses on the situation when customers’ compensation expectations are met. Met expectations (or fit) is a continuum where delivered and expected compensation ranges from low to high levels (see Edwards 1994). Low expected compensation levels will be met if customers perceive the actual compensation as low and in alignment with their expectations. In contrast, customers who expect high compensation levels and view the provided compensation as high will also have confirmed expectations, but at a higher level compared to those customers with met expectations at low levels.

Research on expectation disconfirmation suggests that desired outcomes are lowest in the low-expectations/low-experiences scenario and highest in the high-expectations/highexperiences scenario (Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Olshavsky and Miller 1972). For example, to study the effect of expectations regarding the quality of a certain product created through advertisement, Olshavsky and Miller (1972) conducted an experiment in which they created intentionally high- and lowquality expectations and manipulated participants’ experiences. The results suggested that customers whose expectations were high and received a product with high quality were more satisfied with the product performance compared to the low-expectations/low-experiences scenario. Based on this, we expect that higher levels of met compensation expectations will lead to higher justice perceptions compared to lower levels of met compensation expectations. Customers who expected high discounts and received the expected amount will feel that the organization allocated the resources appropriately. Thus, these customers will form high distributive justice perceptions. This is due to the fact that fulfilling higher expectations takes higher investments from the breached organization that speaks to the esteem with which the organization holds the customers who are affected by the breach. In contrast, customers in the low-expectations/lowexperiences scenario related to discounts will believe that the organization did not have to go to great lengths to repair the customer relationship. In this situation, customers expected a low discount and received a discount in line with those expectations. Likewise, customers who expected and experi enced an extended period of free credit monitoring will have higher procedural and interactional justice perceptions compared to customers in the low-expectations/low-experiences scenario related to free credit monitoring. This is due to the fact that customers with high met expectations will be more likely to perceive that the breached organization respected affected customers and they will view that the breached organization treated them with dignity (i.e., a fair interpersonal treatment). Lower levels of met expectations of free credit monitoring will have lower procedural and interactional justice perceptions because, although expectations have been met, the esteem with which the organization held the customers will be perceived to be lower compared to the high-expectation/highexperiences scenario. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2a: Compared with when expected and experienced compensation related to a discount as a response to a data breach are both low, when expected and experienced compensation related to a discount are both high, distributive justice perceptions are higher.

Hypothesis 2b: Compared with when expected and experienced compensation related to credit monitoring as a response to a data breach are both low, when expected and experienced compensation related to credit monitoring are both high, procedural justice perceptions are higher.

Hypothesis 2c: Compared with when expected and experienced compensation related to credit monitoring as a response to a data breach are both low, when expected and experienced compensation related to credit monitoring are both high, interactional justice perceptions are higher.

## Justice Perceptions and Outcomes

Our next set of hypotheses focuses on the effect of justice perceptions on outcomes of interest. Retailers are typically focused on customer service and they have to address multiple issues when facing a data breach. First, every retailer’s ultimate goal is to ensure that customers will continue to shop for products in their store so as to avoid detrimental effects on profit (Groth et al. 2009; Priem 2007; Sorescu and Sorescu 2016). Here, we define continued shopping intentions as the extent to which a customer intends to continue to shop for products from a breached organization (see Roggeveen et al. 2011). Second, marketing literature suggests that positive word-of-mouth is a useful proxy for customers’ satisfaction with a given store experience or service incident recovery (Allard et al. 2020; Brown et al. 2005; Noone 2012; Van Hoye and Lievens 2009). Here, we define positive word-ofmouth as the extent to which an individual spreads favorable information about a breached organization (see Xu et al. 2014). Third, given the network effects of online media (e.g., discussion forums) and social media platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter, examining customers’ online complaining behavior following a data breach provides critical information for breached organizations (Ivaturi and Bhagwatwar 2020; Kau and Loh 2006; Orsingher et al. 2010). Here, we define online complaining as the extent to which a customer intends to make it public that his/her personal data got hacked using online channels such as customer forums, social media platforms, and corporate blogs (Gregoire et al. 2010; Gregoire et al. 2009). Taken together, continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining are key outcomes of interest in the context of data breaches.

Although these outcomes have been studied in other contexts, they have not been examined together in the context of data breaches and IT security.<sup>6</sup> For instance, studies on service failures have found that high justice perceptions have positive effects on customers’ outcome evaluations in service incident contexts including shopping behavior and word-of-mouth (e.g., Maxham and Netemeyer 2002). Specifically, customers who feel treated fairly in the context of service incidents will also continue to shop and engage in positive word-of-mouth (Blodgett et al. 1993). Clemmer and Schneider (1996) suggest that customers evaluate service transactions based on three major dimensions, namely benefits distributed, organizational procedures and policies, and the quality of interpersonal communication received during the service encounter. If customers feel treated fairly in terms of the outcome, the procedure, and the interaction related to a transaction with an organization, this will likely lead to positive attitudes toward the organization and associated outcomes (Ambrose et al. 2007). One key mechanism through which justice perceptions influence customer outcomes is reciprocity, i.e., a feeling of indebtedness to return good for good (Gouldner 1960; Pervan et al. 2009). Reciprocity has been discussed as a key concept in the context of relationship marketing and investments into customer relationships (Bagozzi 1995; De Wulf et al. 2001). In particular, research suggests that customers reward organizations for extra effort through reciprocal behavior, for example, through continuing to shop at the same organization (Morales 2005; Wu et al. 2008). Therefore, we expect that all three justice perceptions, namely distributive, procedural, and interactional, will influence outcome evaluations of the data breach. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a: Distributive justice perceptions, procedural justice perceptions, and interactional justice perceptions have positive effects on continued shopping intentions in the context of a data breach.

Hypothesis 3b: Distributive justice perceptions, procedural justice perceptions, and interactional justice perceptions have positive effects on positive word-of-mouth in the context of a data breach.

Hypothesis 3c: Distributive justice perceptions, procedural justice perceptions, and interactional justice perceptions have negative effects on online complaining in the context of a data breach.

As discussed earlier, met or unmet compensation expectations in the context of data breaches affect customers’ justice perceptions. The level of perceived justice perceptions resulting from met or unmet expectations is likely to translate into outcome evaluations, such as continued shopping intentions and positive word-of-mouth, and to also influence customers propensity to complain online about the breached organization. Thus, we expect outcome evaluations to be affected by met or unmet compensation expectations because of changes in customers’ justice perceptions. For example, if received compensation in response to a data breach falls short of customers’ expectations, customers are less likely to continue shopping for products from the breached organization because they will have lower justice perceptions regarding the organization’s resource distributions. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4: Distributive justice perceptions, procedural justice perceptions, and interactional justice perceptions mediate the joint effect of expected and experienced compensation perceptions on customer outcomes in the context of a data breach.

## Method

## Setting: Target’s Data Breach

During November and December 2013, hackers attacked Target, one of the largest retailers in the U.S. The hackers gained access to Target’s computer networks and sensitive customer information, which was then stored on a server outside the U.S. Initially, personal and financial data of more than 40 million Target customers were stolen (CNBC 2014; Poremba 2013). Subsequent reports suggest that Target missed several opportunities to prevent the data breach and manage it more effectively (CNBC 2014). On December 15, 2013, Target confirmed that criminals hacked its systems and installed malware on its point-of-sale network. In response, Target removed the malware from registers in its retail stores, but customers were still unaware that their personal data were breached. On December 19, 2013, Target publicly acknowledged the data breach and confirmed that it would still investigate the breach, and that the data accessed included credit and debit card numbers, as well as card expiration dates. In response to Target’s announcement, customers jammed Target’s website and service hotlines. Target also announced that it would compensate affected customers with a 10% discount, which was available in stores on December 21 and December 22, 2013. In addition, Target offered its customers a free one-year subscription to a credit monitoring service. On January 1, 2014, Target communicated the results of a report provided by a security firm and announced that personal data of an additional 70 million customers were stolen during the breach, including email addresses and credit card information. On May 5, 2014, Target’s CEO resigned as a consequence of the data breach. This data breach at Target was the setting for our study. One point worth noting is that, although the Target data breach occurred a few years back, the lessons we can learn from this enduring and exploding phenomenon of data breaches are and will continue to be relevant, as organizations seek to plan for data breaches.

## Participants

We used a professional market research firm to identify and collect data from customers who were affected by Target’s data breach. Potential respondents were contacted via telephone and asked if they were willing to participate in our study. An important criterion for inclusion was that the potential respondents knew about Target’s breach. The survey was then conducted via telephone by trained interviewers. All respondents were advised that their personal data (e.g., name and phone number) would not be revealed to anyone. All participants were offered a small financial incentive (\$5) that was paid through the market research firm. The data collection started immediately after Target had publicly confirmed the data breach and it was reported on Krebsonsecurity.com on December 18, 2013 (Clark 2014). The first round was completed within two business days. In order to collect unbiased compensation expectations from affected customers, the organization stopped the data collection when Target announced actual compensation in the form of one year of free credit monitoring services and a 10% discount for instore purchases for the last weekend before Christmas in 2013. The second data collection round started on January 6, 2014 and was completed within one week.

In total, two rounds of survey responses were collected from 388 individuals who were affected by the data breach. The sample, 60% of whom were men, covered various income levels with a reasonable spread across salary levels. The average salary was USD 56,500 (SD: 31,384). The average age of the respondents was 39 years (SD: 13.40), which represents a typical sample of shoppers (Businessinsider 2014). The market research firm confirmed that the profile of our sample was consistent with the profile of the sampling frame used. Because we did not find significant differences between our sample and the sampling frame, we felt that nonresponse bias was not an issue. Given that all responses were collected in a short period of time and the organization did not employ reminders, we felt that comparing early versus late responses was not necessary (Churchill 1979).

## Measurement

We developed two surveys (1) to gather data about customers expectations toward compensation in response to Target’s data breach and (2) to gather data about customers’ experiences regarding compensation in response to Target’s data breach, customers’ justice perceptions, and customer outcomes. In the first survey, all compensation items were worded in future tense and they aimed to capture respondents’ expectations toward compensation in the context of Target’s data breach. In the second survey, all items were worded in past tense to reflect experienced compensation. We received responses from the same set of respondents in both surveys.

Research on expectation confirmation has highlighted the importance of measuring expectations and experiences separately in order to assess expectation (dis)confirmation (for a detailed discussion, see Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Brown et al. 2008; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) and we followed this approach. The first survey started before Target announced any compensation in response to the data breach. The second survey took place after Target announced a 10% shopping discount valid for all shoppers and a one-year credit monitoring service for all customers who registered for the service. Although Target offered both compensation types to all interested customers, we expected the answers to the compensation items during the second survey to vary. For example, Target’s 10% shopping discount was limited to December 21 and December 22, 2013, and the organization announced the offer only a few days prior to providing the in-store discounts. Affected customers who were traveling (e.g., overseas) or those who had no interest in buying products from Target on these days (e.g., because they already completed their holiday shopping for that year) would forgo the 10% discount compensation opportunity. Also, credit monitoring services are popular and some affected customers may already have subscribed to credit monitoring services prior to Target’s announcement. Thus, Target’s offers may not necessarily have been similarly appealing to all customers, likely resulting in different customer perceptions about the same compensations.

All items were measured on 7-point Likert scales, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The survey instrument, including all items and sources, is shown in Appendix D. In the two surveys, compensation expectations and experiences were measured with similarly worded items. We adapted validated scales for capturing perceptions of distributive, procedural, and interactional justice in the context of our study (Maxham and Netemeyer 2002). The measures for our outcomes—continued shopping intentions (Baker et al. 2008; Grewal et al. 2008; Roggeveen et al. 2011), positive word-of-mouth (Xu et al. 2014), and online complaining (Gregoire et al. 2010; Gregoire et al. 2009)— were adapted from prior work.

Similar to research in the context of justice theory, we controlled for age and gender (Smith et al. 1999). We also controlled for customers’ continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining before Target announced the compensation package (i.e., in our first survey), using the same set of items as in the second survey. Further, in order to rule out some alternative explanations for customer reactions that were not related to Target’s compensation strategy, we controlled for habit (Limayem et al. 2007; Venkatesh et al. 2012), perceived enjoyment in shopping (Van der Heijden 2004), as well as the hedonic value in shopping at Target (Sweeney and Soutar 2001).

## Pre-test

Given our prior work on this topic (see Goode et al. 2017), we were ready with a generic survey instrument to study any data breach. Before surveying customers who were affected by Target’s data breach, we conducted a pre-test of our generic instrument. The pre-test took place before the data breach actually happened at Target. In order to evaluate the effectiveness of potential compensation strategies as response mechanisms to data breaches, we added several compensation types, such as discounts, credit monitoring, product replacement and cash compensation, to our survey instrument. We derived these compensation types from existing reports on data breaches (USA Today 2013) as well as service failures (Forbes 2013). These compensation strategies were chosen independent of a given data breach and we felt that this would increase the likelihood of aligning our instrument with an actual compensation provided in a real-world data breach. Developing the instrument prior to a reported data breach enabled us to conduct our study of affected customers as soon as the data breach was announced. The instrument used for the pre-test did not refer to a specific company (e.g., Target) and the instructions did not describe a particular data breach scenario. Instead, we used placeholders indicating that a company’s name would be included in the actual study/survey. We asked five scholars at a U.S. university to complete a paperbased version of the survey. They were asked to mark items or sections of the instrument that were ambiguous. There were few comments and those were then discussed among the researchers and minor modifications were made to a few survey items in accordance with the feedback received. Once we learned of Target’s data breach, all instructions and items were modified to reflect Target’s data breach and the instrument was administered at two points in time, as already discussed.

Following existing research (e.g., Brown et al. 2012, 2014; Edwards and Parry 1993), we used response surface methodology to gain a richer understanding of the polynomial regression models. Given that polynomial equations are often difficult to interpret, researchers use response surface methodology to better interpret the results (Edwards and Parry 1993). Response surface methodology suggests the examination of three key features of the surfaces that result from the polynomial regression model: stationary points (i.e., the point at which the slope of the surface is zero in all directions), principal axes (i.e., lines running perpendicular to each other and intersecting at the stationary point, describing the orientation of the response surface with respect to the X and Y planes), and slopes along the lines of interest (i.e., the congruence line where expectations equal experiences, and the incongruence line that runs perpendicular to the congruence line and where expectations and experiences differ). Based on these key features, several tests have been suggested to further examine characteristics of surfaces and thus validate hypotheses that are based on a higher-order logic (Edwards and Parry 1993). These key features can be used to assess whether empirical results support a congruence effect (Edwards and Cable 2009). In particular, our first hypothesis suggested that justice perceptions increase as negative disconfirmation decreases and decrease as positive disconfirmation increases. A response surface supporting this hypothesis should have two features. First, the surface should be curved downward along the incongruence line, such that justice perceptions decrease as compensation expectations and experiences differ from each other—in terms of both positive and negative disconfirmation. Second, the ridge that describes the peak of the surface when moving along or parallel to the incongruence line should run along the congruence line, such that justice perceptions are highest when expectations equal experiences compared to when they differ. Our second hypothesis stated that justice perceptions are higher when expectations and experiences are high compared to when expectations and experiences are low. A response surface that supports hypothesis 2 should therefore have a positive slope along the congruence line, such that justice perceptions increase when moving along the congruence line from lower values of expectations and experiences to higher ones.

These three features can be represented using the following tests (Edwards and Cable 2009). To support hypothesis 1, two conditions need to be fulfilled. First, the quadratic slope of the incongruence line should be negative, and the first principal axis should have a slope of 1 and an intercept of 0. To support hypothesis 2, the congruence line should have a positive linear slope and a quadratic slope of 0. The interested reader is referred to Edwards (2002) for details on the formal representations of the incongruence line, congruence line, and principal axes. We tested the conditions that relate to the slopes of the congruence and incongruence line using standard procedures for testing linear combinations of regression coefficients (Cohen 1988). We used bootstrapping with

1,000 bootstrap resamples to test if the first principal axis had a slope of 1 and an intercept of 0 because these tests involve nonlinear combinations of regression coefficients (Edwards and Cable 2009).

In addition to the polynomial regression analysis used to test hypotheses 1 and 2, we applied standard multiple regression analysis for testing the linear effects of justice perceptions on continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining, as specified in hypothesis 3.

In order to test whether the effects of compensation on continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, as well as online complaining are transmitted through justice perceptions (hypothesis 4), we followed the block variable approach suggested by Edwards and Cable (2009). To obtain a weighted linear composite (i.e., the block variable), we combined the estimated coefficients (see equations 1 and 2) that yields the expressions $\mathrm { a _ { 1 } D _ { 1 } + a _ { 2 } D _ { 2 } + a _ { 3 } D _ { 1 } ^ { ~ 2 } + a _ { 4 } D _ { 1 } D _ { 2 } + }$ $\mathrm { a } _ { 5 } \mathrm { D } _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ and $\mathrm { a _ { 1 } C _ { 1 } } + \mathrm { a _ { 2 } C _ { 2 } } + \mathrm { a _ { 3 } C _ { 1 } } ^ { 2 } + \mathrm { a _ { 4 } C _ { 1 } } \mathrm { C _ { 2 } } + \mathrm { a _ { 5 } C _ { 2 } } ^ { 2 }$ . The five terms from each expression are then used as a block variable and the mediators (justice perceptions) are regressed on this block variable to obtain a regression coefficient that reflects the first-stage link of the mediation model (see Edwards and Lambert 2007). We followed the procedure of Cole et al. (2013) and regressed the three outcome variables on justice perceptions and the block variables. The second-stage link is reflected by the coefficients for the effects of justice perceptions on the three outcomes (Cole et al. 2013). The coefficients obtained from these analyses were then used to compute an estimate of the indirect effect for each justice dimension and the three outcomes. To test the significance levels of the indirect effects, we used bootstrapping with 10,000 bootstrap resamples (Edwards and Cable 2009). We note that we also split the sample into positive and negative confirmation to examine if the mediation effects exist in both groups. We did not find significant differences across the groups. Thus, we report the results for the entire sample here.

Although not shown here, as a post hoc analysis to understand the relative importance of each justice dimension on the outcomes, we performed a dominance analysis. The results showed that interactional justice was most influential for continued shopping intentions followed by distributive justice and procedural justice. Interactional justice was also most important for positive word-of-mouth followed by procedural justice and distributive justice. Finally, procedural justice was most influential for online complaining followed by interactional justice and distributive justice.

## Discussion

Our longitudinal study of Target’s data breach, during which more than 110 million customer records were stolen, provides some evidence of the effect of expectation disconfirmation regarding customer compensation after data breaches on customer outcomes. Our findings suggest that any positive or negative expectation disconfirmation related to discounts will lead to lower distributive justice perceptions and customers will believe that the subjective benefits they received did not appropriately compensate for their inconveniences associated with the data breach. We found similar results for free credit monitoring related to procedural justice and interactional justice. Firms will benefit if they cater compensation efforts in alignment with customers’ expectations. Any positive or negative deviations will trigger negative procedural and interactional justice perceptions that in turn impact critical customer outcomes. Specifically, our results supported the positive effects of justice on critical outcomes, such as continued shopping intentions and positive word-of-mouth, as well as a negative relationship between justice perceptions and online complaining.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our work makes three key theoretical contributions. First, we advance research on IS security because we empirically study a security threat for organizations (i.e., data breaches). Given the explosion of data breaches (Krebsonsecurity 2020) and the inability to fully protect organizations from data breaches, managerial reactions to data breaches are an issue of everincreasing relevance (Goode et al. 2017). Research on data breaches and IS security has primarily focused on organizational implications of data security incidents (e.g., Culnan and Williams 2009; Herath and Rao 2009; Kwon and Johnson 2018; Rakes et al. 2012). Our work builds upon and extends IS security research (see Hui et al. 2016) by drawing on a rich literature base, namely justice theory. We develop and test a model that helps organizations to restore justice perceptions and positive customer sentiment through appropriate compensation interventions. Our findings shed light on the complexities associated with the effect of compensation on customer reactions in a data breach context.

Second, the mediating mechanisms linking compensation to outcomes are a key contribution (see MacKinnon and Fairchild 2009; Mathieu et al. 2008). By integrating literature on expectations and justice theory, we are able to explain the nuanced relationship between expectations and outcomes in the context of compensation following data breaches—and particularly by highlighting something that is not intuitive: greater compensation leads to more negative outcomes. Reference research disciplines, such as management, can benefit from our work and study related yet distinct phenomena. For instance, Volkswagen compensated customers as part of the emissions scandal in order to mitigate the negative effects for harmed customers. Management scholars could use our model to investigate the effectiveness of compensation in such a context and examine the impact of context (e.g., Hong et al. 2014) on compensation models. Specifically, does higher compensation lead to negative outcomes in other settings or is it a function of the context (e.g., data breach vs. car safety) and nature (e.g., stolen credit card information vs. life-threatening air bag issue) of the potential harm experienced by customers?

Third, we contribute to the literature on service failures and organizations’ associated recovery efforts. These studies commonly theorize how customers’ justice perceptions change based on responses to service incidents (e.g., Smith and Bolton 1998). Our work leverages this theory base and applies it to the context of data breaches (Hong et al. 2014; Lankton et al. 2016; Whetten 2009), that is, a phenomenon that is not yet well understood but is characterized by its breadth of impact and uncertainty regarding the ultimate outcomes. Thus, we make a contribution by using service failure theory and polynomial modeling to study a less understood research domain, namely using compensation as a response in the context of data breaches. Applying polynomial modeling helped us to shed new light on the broadly accepted service failure literature and we show that the polynomial model results coupled with the response surface graphs reveal insights that linear service failure models do not.

## Theoretical Implications

Our work can be used as a spring board for theoretically motivated studies on organizational justice, service incidents, and data security. First, our work has contributions to and implications for the justice literature by explaining how justice perceptions connect customers’ disconfirmation and outcomes in a data breach context. This provides an opportunity for future research to further examine our established mediation chain involving justice as mediator in conjunction with alternative variables. For example, research proposes that justice and trust are related yet distinguishable constructs (see Colquitt et al. 2013). In the past, researchers have found support for models that position trust as mediator of the effects of justice on outcomes (Konovsky and Pugh 1994). Here, we modeled justice as a proximal consequence of disconfirmation to explain the outcomes studied here. Future work could model trust as a mediator between justice and critical outcomes in the context of data breaches. Such studies could complete the causal chain explaining important outcomes and could provide a more holistic view of customers’ reactions to data breaches. Although justice theory and the conceptualization of justice suggested similar effects for the three justice perceptions, we chose to conceptualize and measure the justice constructs separately (rather than one combined justice measure), and treat them separately in our analysis. By finding differential effects, especially in terms of the magnitude and significance of effects on different outcomes, related to each of the perceptions, we uncovered a nuanced understanding for the role of these different justice perceptions in this context. Such an approach of retaining the dimensionality of organizational justice may also help in other, emerging contexts.

Second, we focused on the effectiveness of organizational response strategies after data breaches in terms of restoring customers’ justice perceptions that leads to increased shopping intentions and positive word-of-mouth as well as decreased online complaining. This is a key step toward developing organizational response strategies. Compensation in the context of large-scale data breaches is a multi-million dollar expense. Thus, it is important that the costs associated with compensation outweigh the benefits in terms of restored customer sentiment. Although our model provides theoretically and empirically grounded recommendations to organi zations that face or need to prepare for a data breach, future research should develop more comprehensive models that consider the costs of different types and levels of compensation in a data breach context as well as actual organizational strategies for managing situations in which customers have heterogeneous expectations. The healthcare industry is an example due to the fact that the implications of lost data from a customer perspective could be very different and it seems difficult to put a price on the compensation. Instead of offering compensation that provides customers financial benefits (e.g., discounts), healthcare providers may need to identify alternative compensation types to satisfy patients.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our study has some limitations that should be remedied in future research. First, our work leveraged expectation confirmation and justice theory. Future research could examine challenges related to data breaches using other theoretical lenses such as privacy calculus. For instance, instead of focusing on the event of a data breach, future research could focus on the relationship between customers and a breached organization. Customers usually engage in a longer relationship with an organization by sharing personal data and thus understanding impacts over time will be important (Venkatesh et al. 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2021). Recent research suggests that customers follow a privacy calculus (Hong and Thong 2013) in that they agree to share personal and sensitive data if they feel that the benefits—for example, financial benefits and personalized or more convenient services—outweigh the costs of sharing data. In other words, customers expect future benefits in return for sharing personal data. A potentially interesting avenue for research would be to study how data breaches, such as the recent Equifax data breach (CNBC 2018), influence an individual’s privacy calculus (Norberg et al. 2007).

Second, although our literature review supported theoretical arguments for the direction from expectation disconfirmation regarding compensation to justice perceptions (e.g., Folger and Cropanzano 2001), future work using controlled experiments would be valuable for pinpointing directions of causality between the constructs. This would particularly help understand if justice perceptions are influenced by expectation disconfirmation regarding compensation or vice versa. Beyond lab/controlled experiments, field experiments would be necessary to balance both internal and external validity.

Third, we studied Target’s data breach and customer reactions related to the compensation effort of the breached organization. This unique setting relates to the context of IS security and we were able to study a high impact data breach, which occurred near the Christmas holiday. As noted earlier, we note that, despite the breach now being a few years old, the issue of customer compensation and strategies for the same continue to be relevant but future research efforts should test the boundaries of our work by testing the model in other domains as well as similar circumstances. For instance, airlines often compensate customers if they overbook flights. A replication of our study in such a domain could test the boundaries of our work and demonstrate if our model can be applied to alternative contexts (e.g., healthcare). Likewise, it would be interesting to identify a similar data breach in terms of size and its timing (in close proximity to a major event such as tax filing season) and investigate the generalizability of our model to such settings. More broadly, developing a typology of data breaches may be particularly useful, as the nature of the data breached, the extent to which breached data can be changed to protect long-term harm (e.g., credit card number vs. social security number), and possible other dimensions are identified. Such a typology will aid a systematic study of customer reactions and organizational responses.

Finally, we examined a limited set of compensation approaches. Future work should extend our research and measure additional forms of compensation such as identity theft coverage or use of additional discounts (e.g., 10%, 15%, or 30%). This would provide further clarity on whether affected individuals actually expected a lesser or greater discount. Such information would be beneficial in narrowing down the percentage customers deem appropriate. It would also be beneficial to measure and study compensation combinations (e.g., 10% discount, apology, identity theft coverage) coupled with the breach severity. These studies could leverage qualitative interviews to gain insights that inform subsequent quantitative studies (Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016). For example, such work could develop different measures for expectation confirmation in the context of large-scale data breaches by developing items that capture compensation combinations. These measures could then be used following the data collection procedure reported here. Those studies should also consider measuring actual customer behavior (see Choi et al. 2016; Homburg and Fürst 2005) instead of measuring intentions as a proxy for behavior.

## Practical Implications

Our findings are critical for organizations that must find ways to satisfy a large number of affected customers in case of a data breach using appropriate one-off compensation initiatives. To put it into perspective, a few months after its breach, Target announced that the associated costs of the breach were up to \$148 million dollars (Poremba 2013). This suggests that compensation in the context of data breaches clearly involves complex and unprecedented questions and decisions managers must make—including beneficial and negative outcomes. Our work illustrates how different types and levels of compensation can be leveraged to restore customers’ justice perceptions that, in turn, leads to reestablishing positive customer sentiment. Given the large number of customers affected by data breaches and the entailing threat of severely jeopardizing a breached organization’s business model, knowledge on the effectiveness of compensation strategies is highly valuable for practitioners. Also, given that data breaches cannot be entirely avoided through technical and managerial interventions, and because service incidents seem inevitable for most service companies (see Baker et al. 2008), our research is relevant to a large practitioner base.

Our findings stress the importance of having an organizational response framework available that specifies how customers should be compensated after a data breach. Our results show that if organizations under- or over-compensate customers, justice perceptions may be negatively affected that, in turn, is important because justice perceptions affect continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining. An important finding of our work is that the compensation level is key to positively influence customers’ justice perceptions. Our findings also provide guidance for managers who face the trade-off to engage in costly compensation. Given that data breaches usually involve millions of customers, providing compensation in this context—as opposed to more traditional one-off service failure incidents— is usually extremely expensive. Despite these high costs of compensating all customers, managers may be tempted to solve the problem by throwing money at the problem due to pressure from disappointed customers, widespread media attention, and competitors’ reactions to previous data breaches. Our findings point to the fact that such a strategy may in fact be problematic. In particular, positive expectation disconfirmation in terms of providing discounts or credit monitoring services may even have adverse effects because customers may have preferred other types of compensation or because they may associate such positive expectation disconfirmation with guilt and become suspicious. These findings urge organizations to carefully prepare for the possibility of a data breach. One way to do so may be to assess customers expectations regarding compensation up front (i.e., even before a data breach has happened). In order to do so, studies using our survey instrument may provide helpful guidance for managers. However, although we believe that our instrument would be helpful for developing such a response framework, managers should also take into account external factors such as industry norms. For instance, if the norm in a given industry is to provide credit monitoring for affected customers, managers might be better off conforming to this norm regardless of customer expectations regarding compensation.

## Conclusions

Our work leveraged research on justice theory and congruence to model the effect of organizational response strategies of customer compensation in the context of data breaches. We theorized expectations and experiences associated with compensation (i.e., discount and credit monitoring) as antecedents of justice perceptions that, in turn, were linked to key customer outcomes, namely continued shopping intentions, positive word-of-mouth, and online complaining. We found that compensation related to a discount contributes to restoring key outcomes by reestablishing distributive justice perceptions, whereas free credit monitoring services affect outcomes by positively influencing procedural and interactional justice perceptions. Our findings also suggest that providing too much compensation, both related to a discount and to free credit monitoring, can backfire and negatively affect justice perceptions and outcomes. We add to the body of work on service failures in general and data breaches and security research in particular. Our work also provides managerial insights for developing organizational response strategies to data breaches, with specific insights into the optimum level of compensation.

## About the Authors

Hartmut Hoehle is a Professor and Chair of Enterprise Systems at the University of Mannheim, Germany. He received a Ph.D. in Information Systems from Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand. His research interests include the design, implementation, and use of enterprise systems. Stemming from his professional experiences gained while working at Deutsche Bank, he is particularly interested in how services and products can be distributed through electronically mediated channels. He is a founding director of the Management Analytics Center at the Mannheim Business School. ## Appendix A

## Literature on Service Failures

Malhotra and Malhotra (2011) suggest that customers view data sharing as a social contract with the organization that collects and stores their personal data. During a data breach, customer data are compromised, violating the social contract and jeopardizing private data. This represents a violation of trust from the customer’s perspective (Bolton 1998). Therefore, when viewing the business-to-customer relationship from a service perspective, a data breach can be seen as an electronically mediated service failure because the violations of trust and privacy intrusions are key drivers of customers’ service perceptions (Malhotra and Malhotra 2011). Service failures have been studied in the marketing literature (Bitner et al. 1990) and various researchers have investigated customers’ reactions in a multitude of scenarios such as undercooked steaks in restaurants (Del Río-Lanza et al. 2009; Hess et al. 2003), overbooked flights (Von Wangenheim and Bayón 2007), and hotel rooms that have not been properly cleaned (Smith et al. 1999).

In reviewing the literature in this area, we found two key issues that are relevant to our work. First, previous studies have shown that organizations can use three distinct compensation types as recovery actions. The first compensation type is product and service compensation, which is free access to products or services and it can be fairly inexpensive for organizations because they grant customers access to internal resources (see Morrisson and Huppertz 2010). The second compensation type is privacy and security compensation, which are extra services aiming to restore customers’ privacy and data security. This compensation type has been typically studied in the context of electronically mediated service failures, such as electronic banking service outages (see Claessens et al. 2002). The third compensation type is financia compensation, which are monetary incentives for customers, e.g., giving discounts or shopping vouchers (see Vazquez-Casielles et al. 2012). Second, as noted by Goode et al. (2017), few studies have investigated how customer sentiment can be reestablished through compensation strategies on a large scale after service failures that involve millions of customers, such as data breaches.

## Appendix B

## Empirical Work on Customer Reactions to Data Breaches

Our review of the academic literature on data breaches resulted in about 40 empirical studies (for a review of the academic literature on data breaches, see Goode et al. 2017). We note two issues related to the existing literature on data breaches. First, with the exception of Goode et al. (2017), we were unable to identify work that studied actual customers’ reactions in the context of data breaches. Second, and related to the first issue, we were unable to identify work that has examined the effect of compensation packages on customers’ reactions (e.g., if compensation helps reestablish affected customers’ justice perceptions).

The current paper extends Goode et al. in two important ways. First, by incorporating justice in our framework, we explicitly model the mechanism that explains how expectation (dis)confirmation relates to customer outcomes. It is important to underscore that specifying the mediator associated with the link between expectation (dis)confirmation and customer outcomes will be key to enhancing the richness of our understanding. Modeling mechanisms is critical for the advancement of particular fields of study and has been noted to be a defining feature of thriving literatures in the applied organization sciences (e.g., Mathieu et al. 2008). Also, by modeling justice as the proximal outcome of expectation (dis)confirmation, we address the need for research that examines justice as a dependent variable (Brockner et al. 2015). Although much is known about the consequences of justice, far less research has been undertaken that is designed to predict justice perceptions. Data breaches provide a unique context to examine environmental factors that influence customers’ outcome evaluations when individuals experience varying levels of justice.

A second difference from Goode et al. pertains to the outcomes examined. Similar to Goode et al., the current paper examines continued shopping intentions as an outcome of expectation (dis)confirmation. However, the current paper also extends Goode et al. by examining additional outcomes (i.e., positive word-of-mouth and online complaining) that have the potential to impact the shopping behavior of other prospective customers. Evidence suggests that customers’ public comments affect an organization’s brand value and the behavior of other prospective patrons (Kozinets et al. 2010). It is therefore important to know whether the justice perceptions evoked by expectation (dis)confirmation influence customers’ inclination to engage in behaviors that influence other individuals’ consumption behaviors.

## Appendix C

## Customers’ Justice Perceptions in the Context of a Data Breach

Justice theory focuses on antecedents and consequences of individuals’ perceptions of being treated in a fair manner (Greenberg 1990). As shown in Table C1, research on organizational justice has traditionally focused on three justice dimensions, namely distributive, procedural, and interactional justice (Greenberg 2009). Distributive justice refers to the perceived fairness of the outcome of a decision or exchange in terms of the allocation of resources (Colquitt 2001). In the context of our work, distributive justice relates to the subjective benefit that customers receive to outweigh the inconveniences or costs that were caused by the data breach (Smith et al. 1999). The perceived fairness of procedures used to accomplish a certain outcome is referred to as procedural justice (Barsky and Kaplan 2007). Thus, when a data breach has occurred, procedural justice relates to the perceived fairness of the organizational process and procedures in resolving the data breach (Patterson et al. 2006). The concept of interactional justice refers to the perceptions of fair interpersonal treatment (Barsky and Kaplan 2007; Bies and Moag 1986). In particular, interactional justice is perceived to be high if individuals believe they have been treated with respect, dignity, truthfulness, and propriety (Colquitt 2001), and if they feel that they are given explanations for the decision that led to certain outcomes (Folger and Konovsky 1989). Much of the earlier work on justice theory has treated these dimensions separately and even found differential antecedents and consequences for each dimension (see Colquitt et al. 2013). Here, we followed this line of research and developed hypotheses for each justice dimension. Table C1 summarizes the extant work on justice theory.

## Survey Instrument, Measurement Model, and Preliminary Analysis

Table D1 shows the survey instrument that was used in our study. The items measuring compensation, i.e., 10% discount and free credit monitoring, were adapted from Goode et al. (2017) and contextualized based on Target’s retailing context. Goode et al. loosely adapted items from Bolton (1998) and Smith et al. (1999) to measure customers’ expectation disconfirmation levels regarding compensation in the context of Sony’s data breach. For instance, Goode et al. developed and validated the following compensation item for round 1 in their survey: “I expect that Sony gives customers free downloadable content if the network is breached.” Given that free downloadable content would be meaningless to ask in the context of our study, we replaced the compensation, i.e., downloadable content, with alternative compensation types. For instance, we replaced downloadable content with a 10% discount and free credit monitoring. Next, in order to reliably measure the compensation constructs, we developed several items that differentiated in wording but still represented the definition of the construct. Given that Goode et al. used only one item per compensation construct, that is, for free network membership, free downloadable content, and a new console, we felt creating additional measures would improve the validity with which the construct can be measured. This is also in line with research using expectation disconfirmation theory (e.g., Brown et al. 2012, 2014). To assess the face validity of the items and ensure the quality of the measurement items, we asked five researchers who earned Ph.D. degrees from U.S. business schools and who were unfamiliar with our study to provide feedback regarding the items. As a result, minor modifications to the wording of the items were made. We also felt that having five items per construct would be in line with guidelines for measuring constructs accurately in information systems research (see Hoehle and Venkatesh 2015 for a discussion).

In our preliminary analysis, we initially screened our data for outliers using Cook’s D and standardized residuals that we obtained from the regression equations (see Edwards 2002). Sixteen cases did not meet the minimum criteria suggested by Bollen and Jackman (1990) and were therefore excluded from our sample. We note that the excluded observations only represented a small part of our sample. Therefore, we concluded that the results did not suffer from statistical model bias. Next, following Edwards (2002), we estimated customers’ expectations and experiences by computing the average of the scale-centered compensation items. To reduce multicollinearity, we subtracted the scale midpoints from the actual scores (see Edwards 2002). This was also helpful for meaningfully interpreting the polynomial regression coefficients (Edwards and Harrison 1993). Because we used seven-point Likert scales for all items, the calculated scores after scale-centering ranged from -3 to +3. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) indicated that multicollinearity was not an issue and the VIFs were low (< 3) for all variables The Durbin-Watson test was used to check for correlated measurement errors among the expectation and experiences measures. The results (p > 0.05) suggested that this was not a problem.

We initially evaluated the distribution of the residuals to assess normality. To do this, we plotted histograms and checked for skewness and kurtosis. A key issue for polynomial regression is that enough observations can be found in each quadrant of the scatterplot so that the significance tests are valid (see Edwards 2002).
