# Conflicts and complements between eastern cultures and agile methods: an empirical investigation

## Abstract

Agile methods have been widely adopted around the world – particularly in the Eastern world, where cultural scripts differ from that of the West from where agile methods originated. However, there is a lack of understanding of the interplay between the basic tenets of agile methods and the Eastern cultures. This understanding is crucial for the effective use of the methods in these cultures. To examine how Eastern cultural phenomena interplay with agile methods, we followed an emic approach in a multisite case study. We examined the adaptation of agile practices in organizations in China, India, and South Korea. We developed a framework describing how Eastern cultural scripts often express the collectivist response to cultural theoretical concepts and how the cultural response interacts in complementary and conflicting ways with agile methods. To explore how conflicts are mitigated, we examined the ways organizations develop ambidextrous practices. European Journal of Information Systems (2017). doi:10.1057/s41303-016-0023-0

Keywords: agile methods; culture; theory of individualism and collectivism; ambidexterity; complementarity

## Introduction

The impact of culture on the design and implementation of information systems (IS) is well recognized (Couger, 1986; Daily et al, 1996; Gefen et al, 2005; Hoehle et al, 2015; Kumar & Bjorn-Andersen, 1990; Straub, 1994; Tan et al, 1995; Tuunanen & Kuo, 2015). The stream of literature concerning the cultural impacts on global software development argues that cultural factors, such as propensity to agree, level of expected documentation, attitudes toward bureaucracy, authorities, and the role of values and norms, are sources of problems in outsourced projects (Krishna et al, 2004). Several studies have shown that national cultural differences are not the dominant influential factors (Kriz & Fang, 2003; Nicholson & Sahay, 2001, 2004; Weisinger & Trauth, 2002). The need for further research to develop a nuanced and richer understanding of the role of culture in organizational practices has been acknowledged (Cater-Steel & Toleman, 2008; Myers & Tan, 2002; Preston et al, 2006; Straub et al, 2002; Walsham, 2002; Warkentin et al, 2015). This need is even more pronounced when we consider the adoption of agile software development methods. Agile methods have been used by many organizations to address the challenges resulting from a rapidly changing environment, ambiguous user requirements, and schedule pressure (Boehm, 2002). While agile methods were pioneered in the United States, they have been increasingly adopted throughout the world. Recent research has examined the suitability of agile development for various contexts, including large-scale projects and distributed development; however, much of this work is based on data collected in the Western world. Thus, findings from such studies may not necessarily apply to Eastern cultures because the general values, norms, and beliefs of individuals influenced by one culture can affect behaviors crucial to executing a process developed for another (Robichaux & Cooper, 1998; Tricker, 1988).

Prior research has highlighted the significant role of culture in software development teams in general. Since agile methods have gained widespread adoption across the world, it is important to examine how culture impacts the implementation of these methods. Specifically, we examine the impact of the cultural script, which refers to ‘‘a pattern of social interaction that is characteristic of a particular group’’ (Triandis et al, 1984, p. 1363), on the implementation of agile methods. In our countries of interest, the cultural scripts generally express Eastern (i.e., typically collectivistic) cultural attributes that are often different from those in the Western world that pioneered agile methods. These motivating factors directly respond to the call for ‘‘contrasting or converging Eastern and Western experiences and viewpoints’’ (Warkentin et al, 2015) and lead us to the following research question: How do Eastern cultural scripts shape the realization of agile methods in software development teams?

As a first step, we selected three Eastern countries (China, India, and South Korea), wherein we could reasonably expect some distinctive cultural scripts. We choose these three countries for several reasons. First, these three countries are important players in global software development and information technology (IT) services. India and China are rated as the top two most competitive and popular IT outsourcing destinations in the world (Minevich & Richter, 2005). South Korea is a major consumer of IT-enabled services. Second, while each country has their unique cultural scripts, they also share some common aspects in their cultural backgrounds. They are considered Eastern and are traditionally classified as vertical collectivists (Whitley, 1992). More importantly, the cultural scripts that many people in Eastern cultures follow are distinctly different from those in Western cultures, which are typically classified as horizontal or vertical individualists.

Cultural scripts may express cultural attributes that either complement and/or conflict with the tenets of agile methods. Therefore, we draw from two streams of literature that focus on these two impacts – the theory of complementarity and the theory of organizational ambidexterity. The theory of complementarity posits that organizations are likely to benefit from complementary activities where increasing one activity will increase the benefits of performing the other. The literature on organizational ambidexterity focuses on how organizations can benefit from addressing conflicting demands in trade-off situations. We conducted a multisite case study to develop a framework describing the cultural influence on the realization of agile practices. Specifically, we examined the conflicts and complements between Eastern cultural scripts and agile practices, as well as how organizations address the conflicts.

The remainder of the article proceeds as follows. First, we introduce the cultural theory used to frame the study and detail the cultural scripts from China, India, and South Korea that are relevant in the context of agile development. We then present details about our cases and the research methodology. The next section reports the findings from our study. Then, we discuss the contributions to theory and practice. The article concludes with a discussion of limitations and directions for future research.

## Theoretical background

## Cultural theory

There are two main approaches to studying culture: etic and emic (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012; Triandis, 1994). Etic approaches are culture-general and include the wellknown work of Hofstede (Hofstede, 1984, 1992). In evaluating culture using an etic approach, the emphasis is on identifying universal cultural dimensions. Emic approaches, on the other hand, propose that ‘‘attitudes and behaviors are expressed in a unique way in each culture’’ (Chan & Rossiter, 2003, p. 1586). In contrast to the case studies typically employed for the emic approach, studies using the etic approach tend to be survey based (Fletcher & Fang, 2006). It has been suggested that studying Eastern cultures using an etic approach is problematic (Fletcher & Fang, 2006). Specifically, Fletcher and Fang argued that applying Hofstede’s dimensions to Eastern cultures may be inappropriate because ‘‘the reliance on Western based cultural dimensions ignores the existence of indigenous cultural traits that are unique to Asian markets’’ (Fletcher & Fang, 2006, p. 431). Hofstede conceded this to some extent by adding another dimension, called Confucian dynamism, to his original model as a result of work in China. The model was later referred to as long-term versus short-term orienta tion (Hofstede, 1992; Hofstede & Bond, 1988). Our focus is on examining the role of Eastern cultures in the realization of agile methods in software development teams. Therefore, we followed an emic approach and used a case study methodology. This approach is suitable for our study, as it highlights the relevance of indigenous cultural phenomena unique to Eastern cultures that may have been underexplored in studies following an etic approach.

There is no shortage of scholarly work suggesting that culture impacts how business is conducted. There has been a trend in the IS literature away from universal cultural dimensions toward more nuanced examinations of cultural values, traits, and phenomena (Rowe & Struck,

1999). For example, Srite and Karahanna (2006) argued that culture can be studied at an individual level as ‘‘espoused national cultural values’’ and that individuals may embrace cultural values to different degrees within the same culture. Their study used Hofstede’s dimensions and constructs to determine the influence of espoused national culture at the individual level. Another recent study went further by using the named Chinese cultural phenomenon (script) of guanxi to study electronic marketplaces (Ou et al, 2014). Rather than using dimensions that examined differences across cultures, Ou et al (2014) studied a cultural script that allowed for a nuanced look within a specific culture. This trend can also be seen in the marketing literature where Fletcher and Fang discuss emic approaches in relation to network formation and relationships (Fang, 1999; Fletcher & Fang, 2006) and in the management literature where Triandis’ theory of individualism and collectivism (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012) is used to propose an alternative method to develop assimilators for crosscultural training (Bhawuk, 2001).

The current study provides an interesting cultural amalgamation in that, in Eastern cultures, we explore the adoption of agile software development that was pioneered in the West. We delve deeper to explore specific cultural phenomena within different Eastern societies and provide a fine-grained view of how behavior is impacted by culture. That is, we go beyond a high-level view of Eastern cultures through an examination of multiple cultural phenomena of Eastern societies at the individual level. In addition, we map the behaviors to a cultural theory of individualism and collectivism (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012) that is flexible enough to acknowledge the distinctions between and within cultures.

## Theory of individualism and collectivism

Triandis proposes a theory for differentiating culture, which identifies both etic universal cultural dimensions and emic culture-specific dimensions (Triandis et al, 1993), based on individualistic/collectivistic characteristics (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012; Triandis, 1972). While Triandis complements Hofstede (1980) and was influenced by his work, the theory of individualism and collectivism greatly expands the depth of the individualist/collectivist distinction and its influence on behavior. Furthermore, Triandis often advocates an individuallevel approach, stating that ‘‘tendencies towards individualism and collectivism exist within every individual and in every society’’ (1995, p. 13). This is a view echoed in Fang’s work, which promotes the concept of Yin Yang in analyzing cultures (Fang, 1999; Fletcher & Fang, 2006). They conceptualize culture ‘‘as possessing inherently paradoxical value orientations, thereby enabling it to embrace opposite traits of any given cultural dimension’ (Fang, 2012, p. 26) and suggest ‘‘all cultures share the same potential in value orientations, but at the same time they are also different from each other because each culture is a unique dynamic portfolio of self-selected globally available value orientations’’ (Fang, 2012, p. 45). Fang suggests ‘‘culture is ‘both/and’ instead of ‘either/or’’’ (Fang, 2005, p. 77), pointing in particular to paradoxes in Chinese culture with examples such as guanxi versus professionalism (Faure & Fang, 2008). Thus, it has been said that ‘‘individualism-collectivism is an ostensible universal or etic worldview dimension that always has culture-specific or emic specification of considerable interest for assessors’’ (Dana, 2005, p. 46).

In particular, Triandis (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012; Triandis, 1995) suggests that the individualistic–collectivistic spectrum is more complicated than either valuing individual achievement (individualistic) or emphasizing the importance of the group (collectivistic). To further differentiate, the idea of vertical versus horizontal was added, creating four dimensions: horizontal collectivistic, vertical collectivistic, horizontal individualistic, and vertical individualistic. This is especially helpful in distinguishing within Eastern and Western culture. For example, the US is classified as vertical individualistic where individual achievement is valued, and there is a desire to stand out (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). In contrast, horizontal individualistic cultures (e.g., Australia) value individual achievement but prefer blending in (i.e., avoiding being a stand out). This added dimension also distinguishes Eastern cultures. Whereas China, India, and Korea are most often classified as collectivistic, India and Korea are considered vertical collectivistic (Sivadas et al, 2008). That is, India and Korea emphasize the value of the group but they have structural hierarchies and place importance on social status and rank (Sivadas et al, 2008; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). China is also placed in the vertical collectivistic quadrant (Bhawuk, 2001) but has been acknowledged as an ‘‘interesting and complicated case of collectivism’’ in that ‘‘traditional Chinese culture may be inclined toward verticality, but the State advocates horizontal themes’’ (Sivadas et al, 2008, p. 202). Horizontal collectivism emphasizes cooperation, the need for affiliation, and modesty (Kurman, 2001, 2003; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012).

Another important contribution from Triandis’ work is the determination of attributes of individualism and collectivism (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012; Triandis, 1995). Triandis settles on four attributes (Bhawuk, 2001; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012), which are as follows:

• Concept of self – whether the self is defined as independent of the group or interdependent on it

• Prioritization of goals – whether the connection to the group is determined by self-interest or interest in the group outcome and whether the goals of the group take priority over individual interests

• Motivation for behavior – whether norms and obligations or individual needs guide behavior or, more generally, how the self interacts with the group

• Orientation to relationships (rational and relational orientations) – whether relationships are conducted for the benefit of the self or the long-term relationships of the group are themselves viewed as a benefit

The first attribute (concept of self) distinguishes between those individuals that clearly delineate boundaries between themselves and others, and those individuals whose socially constructed views of themselves incorporate an innate inclusion of other people. More concretely, an individualist defines his or herself as socially distant and independent from others, whereas a collectivist defines his or herself as interdependent on others and has difficulty separating his or herself from other people (Bhawuk, 2001; Markus & Kitayama, 2001; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). People in collectivistic cultures, such as the Eastern cultures in this study, tend to have an interdependent sense of self (Bhawuk, 2001; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). This attribute can be operationalized by individuals defining themselves through their social connection to others (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012; Triandis et al, 1990). Bhawuk (2001) describes how the interdependent sense of self that is common among collectivists can influence social behaviors. As an example, he discussed the social behavior of sharing resources that is considered necessary among collectivists but unnecessary among individualists (Bhawuk, 2001; Hui & Triandis, 1986).

The second attribute (prioritization of goals) is a byproduct of defining the self as interdependent on a group of others and refers to the prioritization of the group’s needs or goals over those of the individual. In collectivistic cultures, the goals of the group are often the same as those of the individual, whereas in individualistic cultures the individual and group goals may conflict (Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). For the individualistic person, the individual goals will most often win out if there is a conflict. Bhawuk (2001, p. 149) states that ‘‘when a certain group of people is accepted as trustworthy, collectivists cooperate with these people, are even willing to make self-sacrifices to be a part of this group, and are less likely to indulge in social loafing.’’ The differentiation between the individualistic and collectivistic under this rationale is the disparity between the definition of the in-group and how this group is regarded.

The third attribute (motivation for behavior) suggests that while collectivistic behavior is guided by norms, obligations, and duties, individualistic behavior is guided by individual attitudes, personal needs, individual rights, and contracts with others (Davidson et al, 1976; Triandis & Gelfand, 2012). Bhawuk (2001) describes collectivists as having tendencies toward conformity. Furthermore, he suggests that collectivists’ tendencies toward conformity are driven by a sense of duty and a desire to consider the opinions of others regarding their behaviors.

The fourth attribute is the orientation to relationships. Triandis and Gelfand (2012) state that in collectivist cultures, communal relationships are typical and people are more likely to maintain unpleasant relationships. Bhawuk (2001, p. 151) provides the following discussion:

When the self is viewed as independent, interpersonal relationships are developed to meet the need of the self to maximize the benefits to the self. Thus social exchange is based on the principle of equal exchange, and people form new relationships to meet their changing needs based on cost benefit analysis. Thus, individualists are rational in their social exchange. On the other hand, those with an interdependent concept of self and relationships that are inherited, are likely to view their relationships as long-term in nature and, therefore, unlikely to break a relationship even if it is not cost effective.

This suggests that collectivists value relationships over whatever is exchanged within them and are therefore more accepting of unequal exchanges and more likely to participate in these exchanges to build and maintain long-term relationships (Bhawuk, 2001).

Bhawuk (2001) uses Triandis’ theory of individualism and collectivism to suggest a template for developing cultural assimilators (i.e., training tools for individuals planning to work in other cultures). For each of Triandis attributes and the vertical–horizontal typology, Bhawuk provides examples of critical incidents in work-related and social contexts. For each work-related and social critical incident, Bhawuk presents the typical collectivistic and individualistic response. We use Triandis’ theory and Bhawuk’s template to interpret our data and organize our findings.

Identified cultural scripts: China, India, and Korea Triandis also describes culture at a more detailed level by examining cultural scripts. An example of a cultural script is the Japanese concept of amae, which is described as occurring ‘‘when a person wants to be dependent on another, to be passively loved’’ (Triandis et al, 1984, p. 1363). Another is the South African concept of ubuntu that prescribes ‘‘giving of sympathy and help to those who need it and showing respect for the dignity of others – people must not be manipulated; old people must be treated with respect; a caring sharing relationship is emphasized’’ (Triandis et al, 1984, p. 1363). As another example, simpatico is described as having ‘‘no equivalent in English but refers to a permanent personal quality where an individual is perceived as likeable, attractive, fun to be with, and easy-going’’ (Triandis et al, 1984, p. 1363). Bhawuk states ‘‘some culture-specific, or emic, aspects of concept of self like being simpatico among Hispanics and Pakikisama among Filipinos offer meaningful explanation for various work-related and social behaviors’’ (Bhawuk, 2001, p. 153).

Cultural scripts are named cultural expressions that are accepted in a culture as influencing behavior of the people enough to be assigned a word in the indigenous language and discussed among the people of that culture. While these scripts do not need to be associated with every individual or even constrained within the boundaries of a single country, they are important enough for individuals to possibly value them or to have realistic expectations that others may value or expect such behavior. Thus, it is reasonable for us to expect Eastern cultural scripts (i.e., behaviors) to have an impact on the implementation of a Western-developed software development approach that encourages particular behaviors to satisfy its goals. We used cultural scripts for the three focal countries in our study and mapped the behaviors related to these scripts to Bhawuk’s (2001) template, which follows Triandis’ attributes and typology from the theory of individualism and collectivism. We examined the cultural scripts that are most influential to software development while recognizing the fast-changing cultural environment in each country.

The cultural scripts of guanxi and mianzi have significant influence on social and business practices in China (Park & Luo, 2001). The Confucian ‘‘doctrine of the mean and harmony’’ (DOM), which emphasizes societal rules, balance, and harmony, is still followed by Chinese society (Tjosvold & Sun, 2002). Even though globalization has a dramatic impact on culture, and many traditional beliefs and norms have changed, these cultural scripts are still considered significant. For example, guanxi is still at the center of China’s social and economic structure. It is a key component of business relationships that may influence behaviors ultimately resulting in both positive and negative outcomes, such as business success or corruption (Ou et al, 2014). However, in this study we only focus on the aspects of the cultural scripts (guanxi, mianzi, and the DOM) that may be relevant to the implementation of agile practices.

The concept of social hierarchy is well entrenched in Indian culture (Ardichvili et al, 2012) and affects all facets of personal and professional life. The concept of apane log (loosely translated as ‘‘one of us’’) represents a dominant notion of collectivism in Indian culture (Varma et al, 2005). Finally, jugaad, the unique aspect of Indian problem solving that involves doing more with less, has received much attention in the recent literature (Gaur et al, 2014; Radjou et al, 2012).

Palli palli and jeong permeate the Korean culture (Bae & Lawler, 2000; Lim, 1993). Palli palli (meaning quickly quickly) is one of the most frequently pronounced adverbs in personal and professional life in Korea (Vegdahl & Hur, 2005). Jeong, as a bond of affection between people based on informal social ties, is expected in all relationships in Korea (Yang, 2006). ## Role of culture in agile software development: complements and conflicts

Agile software development comprises a collection of methods that adhere to several basic tenets for managing software projects. Specifically, agile development methods follow an incremental and iterative process and value interaction, collaboration, and adaptability (Conboy, 2009). The social aspect of the agile development process is a critical factor in its successful application (Nerur et al, 2005) and this aspect is shaped by the cultural context. The agile methods, exemplified by Extreme Programming (Beck & Andres, 2005) and Scrum (Schwaber, 2004) share some of the same values even though they differ in practice. In what follows, we discuss some of the core agile tenets (i.e., principles or practices) of agile methods:

1. Flexibility: Agile development recognizes that changes are inevitable and embracing continuous change is necessary throughout the entire development process (Conboy, 2009). Agile teams need to respond to the emerging and changing requirements in a timely manner (Lee & Xia, 2010). In addition, the agile approach emphasizes flexibility in solving problems and continuous adaptation of the process to the work at hand (Highsmith, 2009, p. 277).

2. Early value delivery: Agile methods use short, iterative, incremental, time-box development cycles, and frequent release for early and continuous delivery of working software (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2004). For example, in Scrum, software is delivered in increments called sprints (usually lasting 2–4 weeks) (Schwaber & Beedle, 2002).

3. Close customer collaboration: Customers are intensely involved in the development process and are expected to provide frequent feedback (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). In Scrum, the product owner representing the customer works very closely with the software development team. Product owners determine the product backlog and accept the sprints.

4. Open communication and continuous review: Agile methods emphasize constant and open communication among team members and between the team and customers. With each cycle, the team candidly reflects on the process in order to continuously improve (Williams, 2012). In Scrum, the team uses a daily Scrum meeting to communicate the status of the project to all team members and identify impediments to achieving project goals. A retrospective meeting is held at the end of each sprint to assess behaviors and identify opportunities to be more effective. These practices are largely social in nature and require open and forthright communication.

5. Self-organizing teams: Agile methods emphasize people over processes, encourage leadership and collaboration instead of a command and control orientation to project management, and promote self-organizing teams (Cockburn & Highsmith, 2001). The team is empowered with the authority and control to make necessary decisions to carry out the project (Lee & Xia, 2010). In Scrum, teams are self-organized (Adkins, 2010) and granted the resources and authority to make decisions such as what features to implement and how to implement them for each sprint (Schwaber & Beedle, 2002).

6. Cohesive teams: An agile team is a ‘‘whole team’’ (Beck & Andres, 2005) where all members communicate and collaboratively work together throughout the project (Williams, 2012). Agile requires a cohesive team in which team members share knowledge and information about the project, as well as their commitment to project goals. They help each other and respect each other (McHugh et al, 2012).

It is expected that the cultural response (i.e., behavior) tied to a particular cultural script may reinforce or complement certain tenets of agile methods while conflicting with others. For example, the expected collectivistic response when choosing between individual gain and group responsibility is to prioritize the goals of the group. However, these practices may conflict with the need for open communication in agile teams. Furthermore, as Fang’s paradoxical view of culture (i.e., Yin Yang principle) suggests, it may be possible for contradictory value orientations to coexist (Faure & Fang, 2008). For example, Faure and Fang (2008) contrast the reciprocal, network-based functioning of guanxi with professional development and suggest that both coexist in contemporary Chinese culture. We suggest this coexistence can be assisted through ambidextrous practices. We use the theories of complementarity and organizational ambidexterity as suitable theoretical lenses to examine this interplay between culture and agile development.

## Complementarity

The theory of complementarity suggests that organizations can benefit from complementary activities if ‘‘benefits from doing more of one activity increase if the firm is also doing more of the other activity’’ (Levina & Ross, 2003, p. 348; Milgrom & Roberts, 1990). Following Milgrom and Roberts’ (1995) definition of complementarity, Stieglitz and Heine (2007, p. 3) explains the concept as ‘‘assets or activities as mutually complementary if marginal return of an activity increases in the leve of the other activity.’’ Complementarity is often argued as providing synergy (Stieglitz & Heine, 2007), in that the value provided by a complementary set of activities exceeds that provided by these activities in isolation (Choi & Lee, 2012). Brynjolfsson et al (1997, 1997) highlights the benefit of coordinated changes as opposed to isolated changes in improving an organizational process. Complementary effects are also juxtaposed with substitutive effects that refer to reduced benefit of one activity that results from increase in another activity. Levina and Ross (2003) presents a good overview of the theory, which explains its origins as an economic concept and discusses its use in manufacturing, organizational design, and IT investments. The overview also clarifies the distinction between complementarity and fit by suggesting that the former depends on endogenous factors (those of which organizations have control), whereas the latter is associated with exogenous factors (those of which organizations have no control).

The literature examining the complementarity of IT investments and organizational practices (Aral et al, 2012), such as decision authority, incentive structures, and human capital (Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1997), presents interesting implications that are relevant for our study. Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1997) argue that the positive impact of certain types of IT investments increase when certain organizational processes are also changed. Levina and Ross’ (2003) soccer example presents a clear and compelling rationale for the suitability of this theory for our purpose. A soccer player’s individual skill and the skill as applied in his/her playing position are considered complementary in that one reinforces the other in improving team performance. However, an overemphasis of just one of these could adversely impact team performance. Similarly, we argue that culture and certain tenets of agile methods can be considered complementary in that certain cultural responses may reinforce the realization of specific tenets of agile methods. For example, the people-oriented focus of agile methods might benefit from cultural scripts that express the cultivation of longterm relationships. This also implies that over-reliance on process-oriented approaches might adversely affect performance when used in cultures that value relationships, interactions, and consensus. This presents a suitable context for examination via organizational ambidexterity. We discuss this theory below.

## Organizational ambidexterity

Organizational ambidexterity is defined as an organization’s capacity to pursue conflicting demands (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Organizational ambidexterity is well recognized as a critical capability that impacts business performance (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Prior research has confirmed the positive performance effects of ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Schwaber & Beedle, 2002; Lubatkin et al, 2006). For example, He and Wong (2004, p. 481) test the ambidexterity hypothesis by investigating ‘‘how exploration and exploitation can jointly influence firm performance.’’ Organizational ambidexterity gains its foundation from the extensive literature on exploitation and exploration (Nerur et al, 2005). Tushman and O’Reilly (1996) posit that ambidextrous firms that are able to balance exploitative and explorative activities achieve superior performance. This theory is considered relevant in contexts where organizations need the ‘‘ability to simultaneously balance different activities in a trade-off situation’’ (Rothaermel & Alexandre, 2009). The literature on organizational ambidexterity provides insights into different types of conflicting demands imposed on organizations, antecedents that shape ambidexterity, strategies for addressing the conflicts, and their consequences. It discusses a variety of conflicting demands, such as exploitation versus exploration (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Gupta et al, 2006), efficiency versus flexibility (O’Connor & DeMartino, 2006), alignment versus adaptability (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996), incremental versus radical innovation (Burgelman. 1991: Vinekar et al. 2006). traditional versus agile development (Burgelman, 2002), variationreducing versus variation-inducing processes (Gupta et al, 2006; Rothaermel & Alexandre, 2009), and so on. Ambidexterity is closely related to complementarity. While ambidexterity focuses on the simultaneous pursuit of conflicting demands, such as exploration and exploitation, investments in one of these (say exploration) are often expected to increase the benefit of the other (exploitation). Two types of ambidexterity, structura and contextual, have received much attention in the literature (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). Structural ambidexterity involves the creation of dual structures (e.g., separating business units or divisions that focus on exploitation from those involved in exploration). Contextual ambidexterity helps balance conflicts within the same business unit by ‘‘building a set of processes or systems that enable and encourage individuals to make their own judgments about how to divide their time between conflicting demands for alignment and adaptability’’ (Benner & Tushman, 2003).

Past literature has identified several antecedents and contextual variables that shape ambidexterity. Organizational context has been identified as a key antecedent of contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). It describes ‘‘the systems, processes, and beliefs that shape individual-level behavior in an organization’ (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). This is relevant in our context wherein the organizational context could help team members make decisions on how to balance the role of culture and tenets of agile methods in ways that allow for conflict resolution. Organizations also need to manage the balance between disciplined performance management and a social context that fosters collaborative work (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009). Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) identified discipline, stretch, support, and trust as the key elements of organizational context that foster contextual ambidexterity. These four variables balance the performance-oriented and social– relational priorities of organizations (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). With a suitable organizational context, team members could adapt the practices suggested by agile methods to reach a balance between the competing demands presented by culture and agile methods. Also, organizational ambidexterity has been used in conjunction with culture to theorize about multinational enterprises’ abilities to integrate internal resources with external learning (Luo & Rui, 2009). Ambidexterity has been considered an important capability in systems development as well. For example, Tiwana (2010, p. 93) referred to systems development ambidexterity as ‘‘the capacity to simultaneously exhibit alignment and adaptiveness in project activities.’’ Ambidexterity has also been considered an important success factor in agile projects (Vinekar et al, 2006) and in globally distributed projects that incorporate agile methods (Radjou et al, 2011; Preston et al, 2006).

In summary, the theories of complementarity and organizational ambidexterity together present a suitable lens to examine the role of culture in agile software development teams, how agile teams use the synergy between culture and tenets of agile methods, and how they balance conflicts between the two.

## Research methodology

We conducted a multisite case study of eight software development projects in India, China, and South Korea that had adopted agile development processes. The use of the case study method is recommended to study contexts that involve interactions among IT innovations and organizational contexts (Seaman, 1999; Yin, 1989). Case study methodologies are also commonly used in emic cultural studies (Fletcher & Fang, 2006). The realization of agile development in practice is the result of the interaction between cultural responses and agile processes. Therefore, we argue that the case study approach is suitable for our study, which investigates the occurrence of agile software development in its natural settings (Benbasat et al, 1987). The use of a multisite case study helps improve generalizability and provides the possibility of extending theory through cross-case analyses (Miles & Huberman, 1994).

## Overview of study sites

We chose the case study sites based on our research question. Eight projects (three in India, two in China, and three in South Korea) were selected using theoretical sampling (Patton, 1990). All the projects were involved in developing software to meet the dynamic needs of their respective markets. Specifically, they were characterized by evolving requirements and the need to develop and deploy solutions rapidly. Therefore, each project was considered an excellent candidate for the adoption of agile practices by their respective project teams. We studied both complementarities and conflicts that emerged as a result of using agile methods in the cultural contexts included in our study. The generalizability of our findings is strengthened by the use of eight different cases. Appendix 1 presents a summary of the projects, the informants, and the agile methods that were employed. The study sites are briefly described below.

India ISofttech is a small IT service provider based in India. It provides software development services for small-to-medium-sized organizations in the region. It attempts to engage intensely with its clients and therefore decided to adopt agile development methods for this purpose. The company had used agile methods (with a combination of Scrum and XP) in several projects. The focal project involved the development of a custommade application for a customer relationship management system for a medium-sized organization.

IManco is a small IT subsidiary of an organization with operations in the manufacturing sector. The IT organization primarily catered to the IT needs of the parent organization. While it had significant expertise and experience in using traditional development methods, its experience with agile development was limited. However, IManco had completed several pilot projects using select practices from XP and Scrum. The focal project involved the enhancement of capabilities of a small inventory management system that had been developed internally.

IEntertain is a small software development organization focused on the development of applications for the entertainment industry. Due to the fast-changing needs of the industry it serves, the company used agile development methods in its projects. Specifically, most of its employees were trained in the use of Scrum and had completed several projects using this method. The focal project involved the development of an innovative mobile application for a client in the entertainment industry.

China CMart is among the largest IT service providers in China. With over 20,000 employees, CMart has development centers and offices in more than 40 cities in China. The focal project involved the development of an enterprise system that was integrated with a commercial healthcare system. This project served internal customers. The development team was newly formed with members possessing different backgrounds and experience levels. CMart decided to experiment with agile methods in this project because the requirements for the project were unclear at the beginning.

SaleNet provides mobile business solutions, such as mobile business consulting services, mobile customer relationship management, and mobile supply chain management. Founded in 2000, SaleNet has offices in several major cities and has more than 300 employees. Agile methods were introduced in 2009 and adopted in all company projects. The focal project involved the development of a web-based supply chain management system developed for a large manufacturing company.

Korea KStar, a major IS service provider, is a subsidiary of a Korean conglomerate. KStar provides several IS services, including mobile commerce and intelligent transportation systems. With more than 3,500 employees, KStar has several offices in Korea and overseas. The company has been applying agile development practices for the past several years. The focal KStar project focused on the development of a system to deliver new financial services to its business, other subsidiaries, and individual customers.

KGame is an online game development company in Korea. About 3000 employees work at global offices located in several countries, including the US, China, Japan, and the UK. Its gaming products have been very successful on a global scale. The focal project was executed in a Korean development center and involved the development of a mobile game platform for the iPhone. Due to the rapidly changing environment of the gaming industry, developers were pressured to deliver quickly, prompting them to follow agile methods.

KAuto is a startup company that provides an online car repair management service. It was established in the late 2000s. KAuto received funding from a Korean government program that supported promising small startup companies. The focal project involved the development of a system for auto body repair professionals to purchase car parts through online auctions.

## Data collection

Qualitative data were collected through 36 semi-structured interviews with IT professionals engaged in the focal projects. Appendix 2 provides an excerpt of the interview guide. All of the participants had experience in agile software development methods, such as XP and Scrum. The roles of the participants included senior manager, project lead or manager, project owner, Scrum master, developer, agile coach, and customer representative. The semi-structured interviews focused on the interplay between Eastern cultural scripts and agile development practices.

We conducted the data collection and analysis iteratively. The interviews were conducted by the authors in three different languages (English, Chinese, and Korean) during a 14-month period in 2011–2012. Following the translation techniques suggested for crosscultural research (Rowe & Struck, 1999; Ravishankar, 2015), we backtranslated the interview guide. The guide (Appendix 2) was translated into Chinese and Korean by the two authors who are bilingual. Then two bilingual translators translated the Chinese and Korean versions of the interview guide back into English. The translators were graduate students in IS who were knowledgeable about the research area. The source and translated versions were compared to ensure that the Chinese and Korean versions were equivalent semantically and conceptually to the English version. The average duration of each interview was 2 h. We also examined different types of documentation (e.g., technical reports, planning documents, and meeting minutes) from the focal projects and triangulated our findings. We conducted follow-up interviews with informants, and carefully reviewed secondary sources to corroborate observations and triangulate the findings (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994).

During the interviews, we asked each subject about the cultural scripts, their importance, and examples of how they follow the scripts. We also explored each subject’s view of the various pertinent cultural scripts to ensure that the scripts were consistent with the descriptions in the culture literature and played an important role in shaping their work. For example, for subjects in India, we examined whether jugaad, social hierarchy, and apane log were considered important elements of their culture and whether these played an important role in and outside of their work. Similarly, we collected data on palli palli and jeong in projects from South Korea. We examined the roles of guanxi, mianzi, and the DOM in projects in China. All of the cultural scripts were consistent with what the subjects considered important and with what they followed both in and outside of work. We also observed the importance of these cultural scripts as consistently recurring themes in our data from these focal projects.

## Data analysis

We followed the coding procedures suggested by Strauss and Corbin (1990) to analyze the data. The interviews with informants from China and South Korea were conducted in Chinese and Korean, and cultural issues are very sensitive to languages; therefore, we carefully evaluated the approaches to translation used in qualitative studies (Hsu et al, 2015; Chang et al, 2015; Zhou et al, 2015). We followed a delayed translation approach (Zhou et al, 2015) in translating the qualitative data collected in Chinese and Korean. The purpose of the delayed translation was to keep the ties between the language and culture, as many of the subtle meanings of original language can be lost in translation (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Zhou et al, 2015). In this study, finding equivalent English words capable of capturing the exact meaning of some cultural phenomena was challenging. The process described below mitigated this challenge.

We first transcribed verbatim all recorded interviews in their original languages (Chinese, English, and Korean). We then conducted within-case analysis for each of the eight cases based on data in the original languages. Each of the three researchers separately analyzed a subset of the qualitative data and labeled the seed concepts in the original languages. Ambidexterity and complementarity theories were used in developing the initial coding scheme. In the first phase, events and practices related to the following seed concepts drawn from literature were analyzed: a) conflicts between the cultural scripts and agile practices, b) complements enabled by the cultural scripts, and c) the ambidextrous practices that addressed the conflicts. New concepts emerging from our data analysis were then related to the seed concepts.

After the within-case analysis, the two bilingual researchers translated the concepts and categories into English. A random sample of the analyzed data was backtranslated by two other translators who were knowledgeable about agile software development into Chinese or Korean. We compared the two versions to ensure the internal consistency in both languages.

We then compared the cases through a cross-case analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994). The resulting concepts were discussed and debated among the researchers. Modifications were made until the researchers reached agreement. The concepts were then validated in followup interviews. We also used secondary data, such as meeting minutes, when available, to help validate the concepts. In the follow-up interviews, we sought to clarify any ambiguous concepts. After several iterations of coding the data, we grouped the resulting set of concepts into appropriate categories guided by cultural, ambidexterity, and complementarity theories (Warkentin et al, 2009). Finally, we established the relationships among the theoretical concepts and used them to organize our findings.

The data analysis process described above helped us establish the plausibility and consistency of the elements of our conceptual framework. Furthermore, the criteria for validity and credibility of the conceptual framework, required in qualitative research, were met through the application of this structured process (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Pawlowski & Robey, 2004). We established the theoretical relevance of the concepts by examining their recurrence or prominent absence in the eight cases rather than through statistical analysis (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). This method is customary in qualitative studies.

## Findings

In our study, we explored the behaviors of individuals implementing agile practices in Eastern countries. In each of the three countries, we identified cultural scripts (Triandis et al, 1984, p. 1363) that may be relevant to the implementation of agile practices. Specifically, we referred to the cultural scripts by name in interviews during data collection. Using the cultural scripts to reference a set of culturally driven behaviors in interviews allows us to query the individuals, through the use of a word that has mutually understood meaning, about behaviors that are commonly accepted as being part of the cultural landscape. Thus, by using the specific scripts within a qualitative study, we are able to study culture at an emic level (the individual or group level expression of the cultural script in business). We develop a framework – presented in Figure 1 – that describes conflicts and complements between cultural responses and agile tenets and how ambidextrous practices are shaped by these conflicts.

The behaviors driven by the cultural scripts can be mapped to behaviors associated with individualistic or collectivistic cultures. The mapping of the behaviors driven by the cultural scripts to an individualistic or collectivistic ‘‘response’’ to a ‘‘critical incident’’ allows us to determine whether the behavior resulting from adherence to the cultural script is typically collectivistic or individualistic. To clarify, we adopt the terminology used in Bhawuk’s (2001) work and use the term ‘‘response’’ to refer to the two behavioral reactions to a particular occurrence or ‘‘critical incident’’. While we expect the collectivistic mapping in our settings, the framework is flexible enough to allow a behavior associated with a cultural script to be mapped to an individualistic behavior. The cultural response (i.e., the behavior driven by the cultural script mapped to the individualistic or collectivistic response) is represented in Figure 1 by the box labeled ‘‘Cultural Response (behavior driven by cultural script)’’.

Because our data is collected in locations with awareness of particular cultural scripts, we can examine the data for situations (i.e., critical incidents) where the cultural scripts are salient. The behavior (i.e., response) driven by the cultural script and the behavior most consistent with the agile tenet may be the same or different, as shown in the matrix in Figure 2. If the responses are the same, the cultural behavior complements the realization of the agile tenet. If the responses differ, a conflict exists. In situations where a conflict exists, the consideration of cultural scripts may be observed to shape ambidextrous practices to allow the cultural behavior to comingle with the company’s agile implementation.

To further clarify, let us consider an example. Suppose we found employees at a software development firm in China followed (or were respectful of others’ adherence to) the cultural script of Mianzi, which places importance on ‘‘saving face’’. Let us assume that we discovered employees adhering to or acknowledging Mianzi expressed the behavior of avoiding being direct and forthright (i.e., they avoided direct confrontation with other employees or customers). Under our framework, we would interpret this observation by noting that the employees adhered to or acknowledged the cultural script of mianzi, resulting in them expressing the collectivistic behavior of ‘‘avoiding’’ being direct and forthright. In this example, ‘‘being direct and forthright’’ is the critical incident and one can either ‘‘avoid’’ (collectivistic response) or ‘‘prefer’’ (individualistic response) to be direct and forthright. Now, let us consider the agile tenet of open communication. For this agile tenet, the individualistic response of ‘‘preferring’’ to be direct and forthright would be the most suitable. Thus, since the behavior of the employees was collectivistic and the behavior most consistent with the agile tenet is individualistic, we see a conflict. This example is one that would be located in the bottom-left corner of the matrix seen in Figure 2. Therefore, in order to accommodate the cultural script of Mianzi in the implementation of agile methods at this firm, we may observe ambidextrous practices that attempt to resolve this conflict (i.e., practices that attempt to accommodate the cultural behavior in the implementation of agile methods).

As the previous example illustrates, to structure our findings, we relied heavily on the theories described in the preceding sections. The expression of behaviors resulting from adherence to or consideration of a particular cultural script results in what we refer to as a ‘‘cultural response’’. Bhawuk (2001) presented both the individualist and collectivist response to critical incidents that arise in work-related and social situations for each attribute and the vertical–horizontal typology from Triandis’ theory of individualism and collectivism. We used this structure to present our results in the tables in each subsection below. We adopted the naming convention for Triandis’ cultural attributes/typology, as suggested by Bhawuk (2001), which correspond to each of our subsections: concept of self, prioritization of goals, motivation for behavior, orientation to relationships, and vertical–horizontal topology. Our theory and emic approach suggest culture is dynamic enough to encompass both individualistic and collectivistic responses within a country (Chan & Rossiter, 2003; Fang, 2012; Fletcher & Fang, 2006; Srite & Karahanna, 2006; Triandis, 1995). Our framework incorporates this theoretical approach because it is flexible enough to allow for either cultural response to be driven by the cultural script (i.e., either cultural response could be observed in any country), as well as allowing for the possibility that either response may be the one that is more consistent with the agile tenet. This flexibility is illustrated in Figure 2, which shows that any combination of responses may be possible within the framework. We focused on determining where behaviors most consistent with the agile tenets and behaviors driven by the cultural scripts are conflicting or complementary and then determining how the conflict was addressed by the organization to result in satisfactory software development practice (i.e., is respectful of the cultural scripts without sacrificing the agile practice). These answers provide insight into how practitioners can implement structured methodologies in organizations where culturally driven behaviors may affect the actual practice.

In the following subsections, we provide a detailed examination of the critical incidents observed in our data for each of the attributes and the vertical–horizontal typology from the theory of individualism and collectivism. We determine which response is driven by the adherence to the cultural script and which response is most consistent with the agile tenet. Following Figure 1, this mapping results in either a complement or a conflict that we observed in our data. In the last section of our findings, we summarize and discuss the ambidextrous practices that were developed to integrate adherence or recognition of cultural scripts into the organization’s agile software development practice. Our results illustrate that it is possible to meld unique cultural behaviors into agile software development and shed light on the considerations that may arise when attempting to be inclusive of culture while adapting a process developed by another culture into the workplace.

Collectivists perceive themselves as interdependent on a group. That is, their concept of self is more integrated with others than an individualist. Our focal countries are traditionally suggested to have collectivistic leanings, and indeed, our data revealed many of the behaviors resulting from adherence to or consideration of the cultural scripts were collectivistic cultural responses with regard to the concept of self. We have shown only the critical incidents identified by Bhawuk that were salient in our data. The presentation of our results follows the template outlined by Bhawuk and the primary heading of each group in Table 1 restates Bhawuk’s critical incident and the theory of individualism and collectivism responses for each critical incident.

The first critical incident in Table 2 is ‘‘being direct and forthright’’ and the theory suggests that the individualist ‘‘prefers’’ to be direct and forthright, whereas the collectivist ‘‘avoids’’ being direct and forthright. The collectivist’s inclination is to place great importance on smooth interpersonal relations and remain agreeable even in demanding situations (Bhawuk, 2001). Behaviors resulting from following the cultural script of mianzi, where it is important to preserve face (i.e., to avoid causing others to look bad) illustrate the collectivist tendency to avoid being direct and forthright in our data.

Agile teams are highly collaborative. Team members must work cohesively with a shared understanding of the goals of the project and commitment to these goals. Team cohesion is built through effective communication, trust, and conflict management. Therefore, the inclination to preserve face complements the agile tenet of building a cohesive team because adhering to the collectivistic response (i.e., avoiding being direct and forthright) means team members will make efforts to maintain harmony and the sense of working closely with one another. For example, the need to preserve face (mianzi) in Chinese society often results in people behaving according to the expectations of in-group peers. As one SaleNet developer said, ‘‘Mianzi is about the way of keeping others’ feelings in mind when you interact with them, as we do not live in a vacuum.’’ Behaviors that promote team cohesion are best served by the collectivistic response. As a result, the behaviors driven by mianzi and the behaviors consistent with building team cohesion required in agile development complement each other (row 1 in Table 2).

However, agile methods also require open communication among team members, keeping the development process transparent and project information available to all members. The team members should share information about their task status to synchronize work and align decisions, which requires the team members to be open and honest in communicating project information (the individualistic response of being direct and forthright). However, driven by the cultural script mianzi, team members reported being reluctant to share information they believed might damage their own reputation or the reputation of others. For example, at CMart, some members felt ‘‘threatened’’ or ‘‘uncomfortable’’ about the transparency of the process required by agile methods. They worried that it would hurt the mianzi if they said or did something inappropriate: ‘‘It would be embarrassing if my opinion were totally wrong, and showed that I don’t know what I’m doing’’ (CMart developer). The importance of open communication is evident in sprint reviews and retrospective meetings. All team members should reflect on the past sprint with the goal of identifying things that can be improved. Team members need to be open and direct with each other so that problems can be quickly identified and rectified, and this requires the individualistic response of being direct and forthright. Mianzi could make code review and retrospective meetings difficult because developers might take negative reviews personally, and might feel that they ‘‘lost face’’ because their work was criticized. A CMart Scrum master said, ‘‘Developers usually have strong egos and do not tolerate criticism of their work. So in the process of code review and identification of bug responsibilities, it is very common to encounter conflicts.’’ (row 2 in Table 2).

The strong relationships manifested by the notion of apane log, which encourages behaviors that favor the ingroup, were also found to lead to team members covering for others’ weaknesses. The strong personal relationships sometimes hindered teams’ abilities to meet project goals. For example, team members tended to cover up the mistakes and weaknesses of others, which could affect project outcomes. Such behavior conflicts with the need for open communication in agile development, which is better served by the individualistic response where individuals that prefer to be direct and forthright are able to critique each other’s work and identify others’ mistakes. Along these lines, a project manager at IEntertain said,

When the team members are so tightly connected, it is hard for me to get an accurate account of problems facing the project. The team members had the tendency to hide each other’s mistakes and, in the end, I had to face the consequences in front of the customer.

As a result, the collectivistic cultural response of ‘‘avoiding being direct and forthright’’ associated with mianzi and apane log was seen to conflict with the need for open communication in agile development that would most benefit from the individualistic response of ‘‘preferring to be direct and forthright’’ (row 2 and 3 in Table 2). This conflict was addressed by an ambidextrous practice ‘‘make decisions collaboratively but with respect for relationships’’ described in the section on ‘‘ambidextrous practices’’.

We observed a similar pattern in the data from South Korea relating to the cultural script of jeong. The cultural script of jeong reportedly helped team members of KGame and KAuto build psychological bonds through recurring informal gatherings. This is complementary to the agile tenet that encourages cohesive teams. The informal gatherings facilitated team building. Team members built trust that helped develop cohesion in teams. As a result, behaviors driven by jeong complement the behaviors consistent with building team cohesion in agile development (row 4 in Table 2).

However, those adhering to the cultural script of jeong expressed hesitation to openly talk about others’ weaknesses. When jeong is built up between team members, they tended to commit to their relationships and accept one another, including individual weaknesses. This meant that team members were required to patiently maintain relationships even in the face of others’ mistakes. While open communication between members is critical in agile development, this practice conflicted with the behaviors driven by jeong. As shared by a KGame consultant, this prevented members from sharing honest opinions: ‘‘In my opinion, jeong plays as a moderator. It can affect the relationship among members […]. Members (afraid of hurting others’ feelings) don’t want to talk about others’ weaknesses.’’ This conflict was addressed by an ambidextrous practice ‘‘foster relationships but with professionalism’’ described in the section on ‘‘ambidextrous practices’’ (row 5 in Table 2).

Another critical incident observed in our study and represented in Table 2 is ‘‘sharing material resources’’. The collectivistic response to sharing material resources is that it is necessary, often due to the scarcity of resources typical in collectivistic developing countries. Adherence to the jugaad cultural script of doing more with less and taking necessary shortcuts results in the expression of the collectivistic cultural response. Creativity, sharing, and reliance on others must increase to overcome lack of resources and enable project completion. The interdependence of the self with others in collectivistic cultures enables resource sharing among those in the social network (family, locality, etc.).

The agile tenet of flexibility is consistent with sharing material resources because sharing material resources is one of many avenues that result in innovative, flexible solutions to problems. With agile development, changes in all aspects of the project such as scope, features, priorities of features and procedures at any stage of the project are ‘‘embraced’’ in order to accommodate the client’s needs. A common way to achieve this level of flexibility is through sharing material resources. Given the tight schedules of time-boxed development, we observed teams that did not have the luxury of acquiring all the required resources before commencing development. Thus, those already comfortable working in a resourcelimited environment were adept at handling such situations. In this context, taking shortcuts sometimes sped up development and helped avoid waste, as minimal resources were expended on developing features that might not be valuable to the customer. The collective ability of workers to operate with flexibility was evident at IEntertain. For example, one developer who was asked to explore ways to access new mobile devices for the development and testing of the application improvised a very unique approach to obtaining these devices quickly:

He kept very late hours, and by the time most of us got to work each morning, he had completed some development and testing on some new mobile devices. When I enquired how he got these devices, he explained an arrangement he had made with a manager of a store that sells these [mobile devices], where he could get them ‘on loan’ when the store was closed. (Project Manager at IEntertain)

As a result, the behaviors driven by jugaad and the behaviors consistent flexibility in agile development complement each other (row 6 in Table 2).

## Prioritization of goals

The expected collectivistic response that pertains to this cultural attribute is to prioritize the goals of the group over those of the individual. Our findings that pertain to the cultural scripts of apane log and jeong illustrate this subjugation of individual needs for the good of the ingroup and the desire to prioritize group goals. A summary of the findings discussed below is provided in Table 3.

Individuals following or respecting the cultural script of apane log favor the in-group. Thus, we observed teams that shared the notion of apane log were comprised of people with shared backgrounds and experiences. These teams were able to pursue a project with a common purpose (i.e., express the collectivistic response of favoring group responsibility over individual gain). The ability to relate to each other’s backgrounds helped the team members take advantage of individual strengths and compensate for weaknesses. When team members had ties that extended beyond the workplace, it helped them rely on each other and contribute to shared goals. The collectivistic response of favoring the group goals over individual gain is well suited towards the realization of the agile tenet of cohesive teams. Therefore, the group responsibility focus consistent with apane log and need for a group responsibility focus to realize the agile tenet of building cohesive teams are complementary (row 1 in Table 3). The benefits of apane log were very evident in ISofttech, which had recruited many of team members from the same university. A team leader at IManco said,

[There is] an amazing amount of bond among the team members. In fact, I didn’t have to spend a lot of effort to create a team; they selected each other because they all had a common background and knew each other so well. No amount of interviewing could have helped me understand each person, but their abilities were quite obvious to other members of my team.

The project manager at IEntertain said,

A major advantage of having teams made up of people who come from the same background is that we don’t have to work hard to make them trust each other and work as a cohesive team. This is essential in an agile project.

An ISofttech project lead also shared along the same lines:

Most of the developers in our team relate very well with each other and take care of each other. They can also expect the others to do the same if they need any help. This has been very helpful for the project on several occasions when the team pulled together to deliver exceptionally good output. This made the customer and, of course, our own senior management very happy. This [value of apane log] can explain why people often go out of their way to help someone who is facing a difficult situation, even sacrificing their own work. This kind of collaboration is very helpful in agile projects.

The cultural script of jeong had a complementary relationship with the agile practice of building a cohesive team. To build jeong among team members, the Korean organizations tended to encourage team members to have unofficial social meetings in and outside the workplace. Such relationships helped enable cohesion among team members because jeong is typically developed through time spent together in meetings that occur in special gatherings outside normal work hours. According to one KAuto consultant, ‘‘Dinner gatherings [one activity to build jeong] worked well to build a family-like culture. Also going to first-year birthdate celebrations for members’ children was an important activity [to build jeong].’’ Sometimes the organizations provide financial support for social gatherings, such as dinner meetings, outside work. Although these gatherings are not official, team members are expected to attend. The willingness of the employees to spend much time for work purposes off work hours demonstrates the collectivistic response of feeling responsible for the group and favoring that group responsibility over individual desires. During these meetings, project members reported informally briefing their managers and customers about the difficulties in adopting agile practices. A KStar team leader said, ‘‘Our team had dinner together after work. Sometimes with beer or other times just dinner. We talked about the project, including the difficulty of meeting customers right away.’’ The extra socialization outside of work is meant to strengthen social ties, which would be beneficial to the agile tenet of cohesive teams. The employees’ sacrifice of personal time for the good of the group promotes team cohesion, and thus, the behavior consistent with jeong and the behavior best suited for the agile tenet of cohesive teams are complementary (row 2 in Table 3).

This means that social ties may be more important to the hiring process than skill set. This was seen to be detrimental to team success because the team resulting from the collectivistic favoring of the in-group in hiring also tended to be similarly trained, and thus, homogeneous in skill set. While team members often had strong personal relationships, the team was not always efficient in achieving the common goals of the project as agile development relies on cohesive, crossfunctional teams. ## Motivation for behavior

The behavior of a collectivist is motivated by his or her obligation to formal norms and to others. A summary of the findings related to this attribute is shown in Table 4.

The first critical incident in Table 4 is ‘‘workplace behavior’’ and the theory suggests that the individualistic response is less normative while the collectivistic response is to follow formal norms. In agile development, communication among team members should be open with less formality. Agile team members should communicate frequently with minimal hierarchy and not strictly adhere to formal norms that encumber open communication. Therefore, the collective response focusing on formal norms, adhered to by those recognizing DOM in China or social hierarchy in India, conflicts with the agile tenet of open communication that benefits from less normative behavior (row 1 and 2 in Table 4).

Our findings regarding DOM in China illustrated the collectivist response of following formal norms and hierarchical structure when interacting with superiors. Individuals adhering to the script of DOM would be obligated to formal communication with others and expect the same in return. Thus, the behaviors driven by DOM are in conflict with the agile practice of open communication. For example, open communication with the customer, which is necessary for agile development, was often missing because of the emphasis on DOM. At both CMart and SaleNet, only the product owners, no team members, talked directly to the customers. The lack of open communication with the customer sometimes created unrealistic expectations and significant rework. The effect of DOM was observed at SaleNet while addressing issues in the development process. For example, developers usually kept silent even when they disagreed with the project manager, one stated: ‘‘It was actually not my business. I got paid to develop the system. I am not the boss; I am not in the position to make decisions’’ (SaleNet developer).

We observed a similar situation when considering the cultural script of social hierarchy in India. Social hierarchy significantly influenced the way team members communicated with each other and customers. In the presence of authority figures, such as project managers, communication was often formal and less open. Similarly, to respect official hierarchies, communication occurred mainly between members at the same levels of the organizational hierarchy. Such channeled communication sometimes hindered free exchange of ideas and the development of a deep understanding of the needs of the customers among lower-level employees in the organizational hierarchy. An ISofttech project manager said,

Our developers don’t want to directly interact with our customers if the manager is present. They feel it is out of place for them to speak up in front of their supervisors. They are the ones who are intimately involved in the project, but out of respect for their boss, they would not share their thoughts. Sometimes, we find that what I told my customer was not accurate, but no one would correct me.

The second critical incident we observed in our data for the motivation for behavior is ‘‘work orientation’’. The individualistic response is ‘‘task oriented’’ and the collectivistic response is ‘‘more social’’. In Korea, the cultural script of jeong encourages individuals to interact with others and develop intimate relationships. In Korean businesses, individuals often nurture jeong through informal interaction such as social gathering after work. These after work social gatherings are intended to be social occasions where work can be discussed informally. Therefore, the behaviors driven by jeong would map to the collectivistic response of a more social work orientation, which may also best serve the agile tenet of open communication that requires open and candid communication. Close interaction and open communication were key factors to the successful implementation of agile practices in our data from Korean software development projects. As a social bond among members, jeong acted as a background factor supporting agile practices such as customer participation, pair programming, and stand-up meetings. Another Korean cultural script, palli palli, encourages individuals to deliver products and services fast, which would be a behavior ideally suited towards realizing the agile tenet of early value delivery. Thus, palli pall encourages individuals to complete tasks quickly, diligently, and accurately. The emphasis on quick and accurately performed tasks is more consistent with the individualistic response of a task-focused work orientation rather than the collectivistic social work orientation. In this case, the cultural script maps more accurately to the individualistic response, illustrating the flexibility of our framework. The agile tenet of early value delivery is best supported by a task-oriented approach. Thus, in this instance, the quick and accurate task approach of pall palli is complementary to the quick and accurate taskorientation that behooves early value delivery in agile software development (row 4 in Table 4). In fact, some Korean professionals referred to agile practices as palli palli practices. Among the studied companies, the focus was on delivering working software quickly to clients. In that sense, palli palli culture fit well with agile practices. Given uncertain economic conditions, clients tended to be anxious to see initial results early. A KAuto consultant shared that

[Client] companies feel insecure about their financial condition these days. They want to see the effects quickly [palli]. They adopt agile practices without knowing much about them. They would drop projects if no visible results come out quickly [palli].

## Orientation to relationships

Collectivists are relational-oriented. That is, they view relationships as long-term and will tolerate relationships where the benefits are greater to the other party (unequal exchange). The findings related to this attribute are provided in Table 5.

The impact of unequal exchange can be seen in the findings relating to guanxi. The two focal Chinese project teams invested great effort in developing and sustaining guanxi with different stakeholders in their client organizations. They carefully managed the reciprocity expected to maintain guanxi between the development team and customers. ‘‘Satisfying the customers’’ was the top priority of the teams to sustain guanxi with customers. A SaleNet project manager commented:

We always think from the customers’ perspectives, what’s best for them, and figure out a better solution for them. Sometimes we have to change the system several times – that means more work for us, but we are hoping that customers are happy.

This asymmetry of benefits between the two parties enabled commitment and support from the customer. Therefore, those individuals adhering to the guanxi cultural script were expressing the collectivistic response of ‘‘earn credit (unequal exchange)’’ to the critical incident of ‘‘providing service’’ by going above and beyond for the customer to earn the customer’s gratitude. For example, the customer at SaleNet was very supportive by ‘‘paying more attention to the project.’’ He cooperated with the team by quickly providing information on the new workflow and practices needed for the development. He also accepted a change to the business process, which dramatically reduced the complexity of the system:

[…] the project is the collaboration [between two parties] but we [the team] take the responsibility and risk. We want the customers to be happy with us because customers’ support is critical. For example, the system required that the orders in the branches be closed within three days. If the customer didn’t accept this change, then the complexity of the system would shoot up really high. He accepted it [the change] and that made the development a lot easier. (SaleNet customer representative)

The agile tenet of close customer collaboration is well served by the collectivistic response of earning credit (unequal exchange). Thus, the asymmetry of benefits between the customer and development team to nurture guanxi complements the close customer collaboration tenet of agile that benefits from a happy customer (row 1 in Table 5).

The guanxi cultural script also emphasizes nurturing long-term relationships, often through favors. The teams frequently communicated with the customers to address their requirements and inform them of project progress. The teams also tended to do favors for customers, going well beyond satisfying the initial requirements. These favors included incorporating changes and addressing issues important to the customer quickly, even though those issues might not be specified in the contract. ‘‘Software is different from other things, sometimes there is ambiguity – in the acceptance tests, the issues might be considered major or minor, depends on the customer, so it is important to maintain a good relationship with the customers,’’ said a CMart developer.

With guanxi, the team helped me when I needed help, such as incorporating requirements and changes without following the formal process […] also they were more open to us, telling us things that were important and could not be observed on the surface.

Close customer collaboration may be fostered through long-term relationships that encourage goodwill and familiarity. Therefore, the agile tenet of close customer collaboration is best supported by the collectivistic response of long-term orientations to relationships. In this situation, the behaviors best suited to the agile tenet of close customer relationships and the behaviors expressed as a result of adherence to guanxi are complementary (row 2 in Table 5).

## Vertical–horizontal typology

The focal countries for our study are typically classified as vertical collectivist. Thus, we could expect a vertical response; that is, an emphasis on the group but with structural hierarchies. The findings related to the vertical–horizontal typology are provided in Table 6.

The first critical incident in Table 6 is ‘‘decision making’’ and the theory suggests that the horizontal response is for decision making to be ‘‘independent’’, while the vertical response is to ‘‘check with a superior’’. In agile development, fluid teams must be quickly organized with less formality and rigidity than may be easily accomplished in a vertical collectivist culture. In other words, it is preferable in agile development for teams to be able to organize and react without directive from a superior. Thus, behaviors that promote self-organizing teams are best served by the horizontal response.

Our findings illustrate that behaviors resulting from following the cultural script of DOM encourage a hierarchical structure. Individuals adhering to the DOM cultural script would expect a superior to make the decisions (i.e., the vertical response). Thus, the behaviors driven by DOM are in conflict with the agile practice of selforganizing teams (row 1 in Table 6). For example, following DOM principles makes it challenging to build self-organizing teams. Many of the respondents from SaleNet and CMart were used to the ‘‘command and control’’ style; that is, the team members were accustomed to following instructions from the project manager. As a result, when switching to agile methods, the team members were reluctant to sign up for tasks or estimate the sizes of the tasks independently because they were concerned about the potential risk of being asked to take responsibility if something went wrong. A CMart Scrum master said,

They [the developers] were used to being told what to do and the process of doing things. They used to follow the instructions from the managers. They felt comfortable that someone in a higher position made the decision and took the responsibility. The concept of the self-organizing was new to them and some were lost initially.

As a result, the developers at CMart were reluctant to sign up for tasks in the sprint planning meetings and some tasks were left unclaimed. The Scrum master, described as a servant–leader, is accountable for the process but has no formal authority. However, the lack of authority sometimes makes the work of a Scrum master difficult because of the social order embedded in DOM:

I know for sure that I am a servant – I organize the daily meeting and update the daily report – but about the ‘leader’ part, I am in no position to say no to the PO [Product Owner], even if I know that what he wants to do would break the rhythm [of development]. (CMart Scrum master)

The second critical incident for the vertical–horizontal topology that we encountered in our data was ‘‘interaction with superior’’. The horizontal response is ‘‘as equal’ and the vertical response is ‘‘as unequal’’. In India, the social hierarchy cultural script emphasizes structural hierarchies. Individuals adhering to the social hierarchy cultural script would be apt to view themselves as unequal in interactions with a superior (i.e., the vertica response), which would make action in self-organized teams and recognizing delegated authority more challenging. Therefore, the behaviors driven by the social hierarchy cultural script would conflict with the agile tenant of self-organizing teams (row 2 in Table 6) that is better served by the horizontal response (i.e., as equal). The reluctance of the team members to create an entirely self-managed team was observed at IManco:

Our process requires a new mindset and a completely different way of thinking. While our developers are quick learners and are able to handle whatever I ask them to do, this new way of thinking about development with very little guidance on process, priorities, was somewhat unsettling for most of the team members. (IManco project lead)

The need to provide constructive criticism of teammates’ work is essential for ensuring quality and meeting the delivery goals of the project in agile development. However, developers did not feel comfortable questioning the authority of senior members of their teams. ‘‘Even when I know the project manager is wrong about estimates [of the number of tasks] that can be completed, I don’t speak up directly. I need to give him due respect,’ explained a developer at IManco.

At ISofttech, project managers still managed some aspects of development, as they would in traditional plan-driven development. Developers felt that this contradiction with agile values was impeding their work: ‘‘Our project manager didn’t quite relate to the role of a Scrum master. He wanted to have control over all the critical aspects of the work, and this is not how a Scrum master is supposed to act’’ (ISofttech developer). However, the project manager explained, ‘‘The boss is supposed to be in control. The concept of servant-leader that is used in Scrum has very negative connotations in our culture.’’

The vertical cultural response of ‘‘check with superior in decision making’’ associated with DOM and social hierarchy was seen to conflict with the need for selforganizing teams in agile development that would most benefit from the horizontal response of ‘‘independent in decision making’’ (row 1 and row 2 in Table 6). This conflict was addressed by the ambidextrous practices ‘‘establish self-organizing teams but with structure’’ and ‘‘make decisions collaboratively but with respect for relationships’’.

## Ambidextrous practices

In the sections above, we detail the interplay between the cultural responses and the agile tenets, which resulted in both positive and negative outcomes. In addition, we explore how the organizations handled the conflicts and what, if any, impact that had on the effectiveness of the agile implementation. We identify three crucial groups of ambidextrous practices that were leveraged to address many of the conflict. In what follows, we discuss each of these groups, how they mitigated the conflict.

Foster relationships but with professionalism We observed a practice that mitigated the conflicts that emerged due to the collectivist tendency in apane log to favor the ingroup and value relationships. This tendency leads to the protection of others, even at the expense of a better functioning project. To address this issue, the organizations developed a balance between fostering relationships among team members and ensuring that the team had the necessary competencies and skill sets. For example, to avoid creating teams comprising only members with close ties (which could impede the healthy exchange of ideas that often emerges among people with different competencies), the focal organizations paid close attention to the professional competencies of the members in addition to their backgrounds. Should we hire someone from the same college or find a database expert from somewhere else? The answer was obvious to me because the team had very limited expertise in the area. Even though I had to bring someone with a very different background, the team recognized the need for me to have him in the project.

Establish self-organizing teams but with structure Another observed practice that aided in mitigating the conflict between the agile need for self-organizing teams and the vertical collectivism in China and India (DOM and social hierarchy) involved the creation of an environment for self-organizing that accommodated the need to respect well-established authority structures. Several structures were added to the self-organizing team to provide the guidance the team members desired. For example, sensing the reluctance of project team members to take ownership of tasks, managers divided the system into several modules, and each member was designated as a ‘‘leader’’ for one or more modules. The leaders could then recruit other team members to perform tasks for the module. One Isoftsys project lead said,

Each person had responsibility for some aspect of the system, and they took the lead in managing that aspect. All of them reported to the project manager. This way, we created a structure such that each person had some leadership role, but this did not challenge the traditional structure of the unit.

In both CMart and SaleNet projects, the POs also took the role of project managers, even though there are no official project managers in a Scrum team. The Scrum master worked with the product owner to resolve issues that could be addressed by him directly: ‘‘He [one PO] could help me solve the problems, as he is actually the project manager.’’

CMart adopted a structure in which the PO was not a single person but a group of stakeholders. As CMart’s focal project was an enterprise-level commercial product, one of the company’s top managers was directly involved in shaping the product. In addition, the customer representatives, tech lead, and leader of the client team served together as POs. The entire team, which included the senior managers, was given the authority to create the product backlog from the product roadmap. This structure allowed even junior members of the project team the opportunity to shape the direction of development, while it also provided senior managers clear visibility and limited control over the process.

Make decisions collaboratively but with respect for relationships A careful balance was required to incorporate the cultural tendency to withhold opinions to protect relationships (mianzi, DOM, social hierarchy, apane log, jeong) with the open communication required by agile practices. A project manager realized that mianzi was important for conflict management and could cause problems if not carefully managed. This SaleNet PO said, ‘‘Mianzi is important, but not for the purpose of giving mianzi itself; when we have conflict, we don’t hurt other’s mianzi because this causes them to become very emotional and fight back irrationally.’’

Often, the development teams worked to ‘‘save the face’’ of the customer, even when the team did not agree with the customer: ‘‘The attitude is the key. We never reject the requests of the customer directly. It is about mianzi. If the requests cannot be satisfied, we just say that we will think about them, hoping that over time [the customer] will give up’’ (SaleNet PO).

Within the project team, the project manager had to carefully balance the need for working collaboratively with the need to assert his authority:

My team members may have different opinions, but they should follow my instructions. My job is to make sure that the project is going smoothly according to the plan; it is a balance – the developers and me, our goals are different, so we reached a balance at different points. The fundamental nature of DOM is to say, ‘to achieve our goals we have to compromise, give up something, we must choose the balanced points that connect all stakeholders’. Since they are the balanced points, then they won’t cause conflicts. Connecting all the points, you get the lines that lead to the goal. (SaleNet project manager)

This implies that developers approached each decision with a balanced view without swinging to extremes that could disturb harmony. Such balanced decisions that focused on avoiding conflicts and ensuring harmony cumulatively helped the team achieve their goal.

Another approach to collaborative decision making while respecting authority was novel in its use of social media (a wiki) to help developers communicate openly. The project team set up a wiki where any member could communicate openly and anonymously without appearing disrespectful. The project manager at IManco explained,

This has helped a great deal in identifying issues that never came up during meetings for two different reasons – respecting the senior members of the team and not exposing one of their own. We tried our best to convince the team members that we will take the comments posted at face value and use them as an additional way to assess project status without making any effort to identify who contributed to the wiki. Obviously, this is not ideal, but it at least opens up another channel for communication, which is good for an agile project.

The anonymous communication helped developers feel that they were preserving their respect for authority while providing a channel for openly sharing concerns.

## Discussion

Our study addresses the research question (How do Eastern cultural scripts shape the realization of agile methods in software development teams?) by developing a framework (presented in Figure 1) that represents a novel contribution in linking cultural scripts to ambidextrous practices in agile projects.

Our study presents the following key recommendations:

1. Devise practices that are sensitive to culture: Rather than adopting methods as prescribed, project managers should perform fine-grained adaptation of development methods so that the methods are appropriate for the cultural contexts in which they are used. Since a method might comprise some practices that work well and others that are ineffective in a cultural context, project managers should evaluate each practice and its fit/misfit with the cultural scripts when developing adaptations. It is essential for all project stakeholders (such as customers, senior management, and project team members) to be sensitive to the demands of the cultural context in which agile methods are implemented. This involves devising alternate mechanisms to achieve the same set of goals advocated by agile methods while respecting the importance of cultural values. For example, the practice of self-organized teams conflicted with the cultural responses of DOM and social hierarchy because these responses focus on the need for structure and respect for authority. Project managers should adapt the practice to facilitate selforganization but with some structure.

2. Mitigate the negative impact of adapted practices: Stakeholders should also carefully consider any adverse impact that adapted practices might have on the agile practices that are seemingly consistent with specific cultural scripts. For example, hiring team members to meet the need for diverse skillsets in the team might conflict with the cultural script of apane log. While this might help facilitate the healthier exchange of ideas, because of the cultural context, forcing employee selection outside of the in-group to ensure diversity of skills might adversely impact the cohesiveness of the team. Project managers adapting such practices should be careful to ensure a delicate balance between the needs for specific skills, information exchange, effective communication, and cohesiveness.

3. Nurture the complementarities: Stakeholders should recognize the complementary relationship between certain cultural scripts and agile practices, and then develop mechanisms to foster such synergies in cases that result in positive outcomes. For example, the complementarity between response of jugaad and the notion of doing whatever it takes to deliver value to the customer can be recognized by project managers to legitimize otherwise isolated practices that exemplify this relationship.

Our framework synthesizes the links between cultural theory and the theories of complementarity and ambidexterity. Our findings shed light on how primarily collectivistic responses can complement or conflict with agile tenets and also reveal some ambidextrous responses that arose to mitigate conflicts. Our study’s contribution to three areas of the literature warrant further discussion: (i) agile methods and culture, (ii) complementarity, and (iii) organizational ambidexterity.

Agile methods and culture: This study extends the current literature on the adaptation of agile methods by presenting boundary conditions for the application of some agile tenets based on complementary and conflicting relationships with Eastern cultural responses. We show that the implementation of agile methods without consideration of the role of culture will likely prevent a software team from achieving the most significant benefits that agile methods can provide. Our analysis examines specific critical incidents identified by Bhawuk (2001) where conflicts or complementarities between the behavior mapped to the cultural script and the behavior best suited to the agile tenet were observed in the data. We observed primarily collectivistic responses to critical incidents and mapped many of the agile tenets to the individualistic response, as expected. However, we did find individualistic behaviors in the Eastern cultures that demonstrated the flexibility of our framework and also found that behaviors mapped to a cultural script could simultaneously conflict and complement agile tenets. Our study highlights that not all agile practices may work well in Eastern cultures. For example, we found that open communication conflicts with the needs to maintain harmonious relationships and to maintain social structures (for example, apane log). On the other hand, we saw that agile practices can fit naturally with some cultural responses. For example, close customer collaboration was enabled through the emphasis on long-term relationships (a key to guanxi). While prior research has examined the relationship between corporate culture and the success of agile methods (Misra et al, 2009), our study takes an emic approach to studying the impact of Eastern culture on agile projects. Our study presents the first step in considering the impact of specific cultural scripts (such as guanxi, jeong, jugaad, etc.) on how teams adapt agile methods. Recent research in IS advocates the study of culture at the individual level or through the use of cultural scripts (Ou et al, 2014; Srite & Karahanna, 2006). Our study contributes to the IS literature by expanding this type of examination across multiple cultural scripts from several Eastern countries in the context of agile software development practice. Emic cultural studies, especially those utilizing cultural scripts, are rare in the IS literature. Thus, our work helps broaden the lens through which culture is studied in IS.

Complementarity: Our findings present interesting and nuanced variations of the implications of complementarity. While the theory of complementarity has been widely used as an economic concept in manufacturing, organizational design, and in IT investments, the role of the theory in examining the interplay between culture and software methods is novel.

An interesting implication of our findings relates to establishing boundary conditions for complementarity. Complementary activities typically reinforce one another, leading to positive outcomes for organizations (Levina & Ross, 2003). However, our study highlights the limits on such a reinforcing effect. While some cultural responses may reinforce certain agile tenets, they may simultaneously conflict with the use of other tenets. For example, the practice of forming teams with members who share some common traits (the notion of apane log in India) might work well until the point where teams become overly homogeneous and, thus, unable to provide diversity in ideas and skillsets. This presents a limit where one factor might reinforce the other in leading to better team outcomes.

A second extension relates to the contrast between complements and substitutes (Stieglitz & Heine, 2007). Stieglitz and Heine argued that organizations must be cognizant of substitutes and complements. Relating the implications from our study to this argument suggests that software teams should be aware of substitute and complementary effects of culture on tenets of software development methods. For example, information exchange and decision making might occur outside the workplace and in social situations, as observed at IManco. Do such practices substitute the information exchange and decision making practices common in agile methods? Our findings suggest that while such cultural responses and agile tenets reinforce each other in spirit, they might have a substitutive effect in practice. Mitsuhashi and Greve (2009) presented a closely related argument suggesting that complementarity and compatibility are critical elements in establishing a match between two entities. They argue that while complementarity relies on differences between the entities, compatibility relies on similarities. Our findings suggest that while compatibility between cultural responses and agile tenets is important, there are important differences that can bring greater value to teams when combined. For example, the conflict between mianzi and the need for open communication in agile teams may be viewed as a conflict that arises as a result of incompatibility between the two. However, mianzi might actually motivate developers to meet their commitments to the team so that they do not end up in a situation where they might lose face. This logic might present mianzi and the need for open communication as complementary because more of one might improve the role of the other, thereby leading to a better team outcome.

Organizational ambidexterity: The literature on organizational ambidexterity has identified a variety of antecedents that might help organizations pursue conflicting demands. One such factor is the behavioral integration of top management teams (Lubatkin et al, 2006), which refers to the senior management team’s ‘‘wholeness and unity of effort’’ that creates synchronized social processes. Our study suggests that behavioral integration of senior managers is essential, especially in cultures that value social hierarchy. This integration can help create ambidextrous practices that adapt tenets from agile methods to the relevant cultural scripts. It can also enable all the stakeholders to have a shared understanding and recognition of the goal of agile methods and the need to adapt them to mitigate conflicts with cultural scripts. While the partitioning proposed by Adler et al (1999) was not observed in the organizational structure, it was observed at the level of organizational processes. Partitioning tasks to allow a hybrid approach could help manage some aspects of the development process, thereby allowing focal organizations to achieve ambidexterity.

The adaptations of agile processes may also be viewed from the perspective of residual fit, as suggested by Gilbert (2006). Software teams may find residual fit between former development practices and the changed environment, despite the need for significantly different practices. We argue that this can be counterproductive. Rather than reconfiguring (software development) capabilities to suit agile development, we found that the focal organizations tried to find residual fits with existing capabilities that were incongruent with the cultural context. This resulted in adapted processes that retained some of the elements of the existing (residual) processes while also embracing agile processes.

Our use of the theories of ambidexterity and complementarity also revealed noteworthy connections. While prior research on organizational ambidexterity has pitted exploration against exploitation, describing the two as conflicting demands on organizations, it also acknowledges the need for complementarity between the two (Taylor & Helfat, 2009). That is, if explorative initiatives are well thought out and executed, they could become complementary to exploitative initiatives because they might enhance the role of exploitative initiatives in improving outcomes. This is different from the observations from our study and our use of ambidexterity. Our findings suggest that the conflict–complement association is nuanced, based on the granularity of cultural responses and agile practices we considered. For example, the cultural script of jugaad might be considered a complement to the flexibility needed in agile projects because it helps teams to reach goals through improvisation despite resource limitations. However, improvised solutions can result in temporary flexibility that might not be reproducible or scalable. Organizations can attempt to restrict the use of agile methods to teams whose cultural scripts seem completely consistent with the tenets of the method; however, this will not always be possible. For example, our findings suggest that some tenets complement and some conflict with cultural scripts. This is likely true even in traditional development approaches. Also, when cultural scripts conflict with tenets of agile methods, dropping those conflicting agile tenets altogether might not be conducive either. For instance, consider the conflict between the need for social hierarchy in India and the need for open communication in agile methods. As hierarchical structures may conflict with open communication, a possible solution might be to discard the practice of open communication. This could not only muffle the potential positive impact of agile methods, but also have a more serious impact. Open communication is complementary to team cohesion. Thus, if the practice of open communication is discarded, team cohesion could suffer. The team might not reap the benefits of team cohesion in isolation from open communication. This creates an important connection between the theories of complementarity and ambidexterity.
