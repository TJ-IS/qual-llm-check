# Impact of a firm’s physical and knowledge capital intensities on its selection of a cloud computing deployment model

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cloud computing deployment Physical capital intensity Knowledge capital intensity Degree of legal protection

## A B S T R A C T

Cloud computing has become an increasingly common computing infrastructure for contemporary firms. An important decision for firms to make in adopting a cloud computing model is whether to build it in-house (a private cloud) or outsource it (a public cloud). Prior literature has focused on the impact of firms’ characteristics but generated inconsistent results regarding the selection of cloud computing models. To add to this line of inquiry, we consider the relative resource structure, which reflects the importance of physical and knowledge resources for individual firms, and examine their respective effects on the selection of cloud computing de ployment models (CCDMs). Using data from 520 companies deploying cloud computing in mainland China, we find that firms with higher physical capital intensity (PCI) tend to outsource cloud computing, whereas those with higher knowledge capital intensity (KCI) tend to use private clouds. Firms with higher codified knowledge capital intensity (CKCI) are found to be more susceptible to the negative relationship between KCI and public cloud selection than those with higher tacit knowledge capital intensity (TKCI). The direct positive influence of regional legal protection on a firm’s preferences for a public cloud is also confirmed, as well as its indirect moderating effect on alleviating the negative relationships between CKCI and deploving a public cloud

## 1. Introduction

Cloud computing is an innovative information service model [1] that reshapes the access to and application of a firm’s information resources.<sup>1</sup> It can lower costs and improve eficiency [4], and allow firms to focus on core competitiveness and improved performance [5].

Some researchers have discussed the main deployment models for cloud computing. Armbrust et al. [6] distinguished between private cloud and public cloud, and Mell and Grance [7] identified four types of cloud: private, public, hybrid, and community. The first three deployment models are more common.<sup>2</sup> The public cloud deployment model refers to cloud infrastructures that are owned, managed, and operated by external providers, and openly used by the general public. The private cloud deployment model usually refers to cases where the cloud infrastructure is mainly owned, managed, operated, and used exclusively by a single organization. The hybrid cloud deployment model refers to a cloud infrastructure composed of an internal private cloud computing infrastructure and an external public cloud computing in frastructure, where the data and applications are portable between private and public cloud computing [6–8].

The literature on organizations’ cloud adoption processes, involving evaluation, adoption decisions, implementation, and governance, is quite abundant [9,10]. Prior studies on the cloud deployment model selection have been primarily conceptual [6,9,11], with few studies further discussing how firms choose among diferent deployment models. Cloud Computing deployment model (CCDM) refers to the ar rangement of the organization’s cloud computing resources, that is, whether the organization builds or outsources the cloud computing center. Hence, the choice of a cloud deployment model is one of the important issues firms have to consider when adopting cloud computing. In this study, we examine the extent to which a firm’s physical and knowledge capital intensity (KCI) influence its selection behavior, with an aim toward identifying the kind of cloud deployment model most likely to be selected by a firm.

In theory and in practice, the study of a firm’s choice of a cloud deployment model is highly significant. Two important practical reasons, organizational and social, make it imperative to study cloud deployment model choices. First, from an organizational perspective, the focal firm’s choice of a cloud deployment model is a key decision when adopting cloud computing, because diferent models require drastically diferent considerations of information technology (IT) infrastructure, human resources, and service providers [10,12]. This decision in turn influences the focal firm’s cost structure [13] and information risks [14], and further afects the return on the firm’s investment in cloud computing. Hence, the organizational decision maker needs guidance to ensure that the choice of a CCDM can be adapted to the internal and external environmental characteristics of the firm, and will ensure the best return on the firm’s investment in cloud computing. In addition, a firm’s model choice can help cloud vendors build profiles of potential clients and recommend cloud solutions accordingly, to more efectively target clients with diferent cloud service needs. Second, from a social perspective, cloud computing can achieve economies of scale and reduce carbon emissions by centralizing computing resources in shared infrastructures through virtualization technology, especially in the public cloud [15]. Understanding a firm’s selection behavior regarding the cloud deployment model and its influencing factors, can help reduce risk concerns in choosing a public cloud. This can in turn promote the firm’s use of and the role of cloud computing in the firm’s operations, thus impacting economies of scale and the reduction of carbon.

There are also important theoretical reasons to examine cloud de ployment model choices. Cloud computing has been examined in the literature as a new form of IT outsourcing (ITO) [16–18]. The choice of a cloud deployment model is understood as a firm's ITO decision in the cloud context, that is, the trade-of between cloud outsourcing (choosing the public cloud deployment model) and cloud insourcing (choosing the private cloud deployment model). Transaction cost theory (TCT) is often used to explain a firm’s ITO behavior [16,17,19,20]. On the basis of principal attributes of transactions, those of asset specificity, frequency, and uncertainty [20,21], TCT focuses on comparing the costs of information resources operation<sup>3</sup> in diferent cloud deployment models. It argues that diferent information resources operations have diferent transactions, production, and management costs in diferent firms, which can lead to diverse property rights arrangements—that is, operating cloud computing inside or outside the organization. TCT has good explanatory power in the ITO field [20], yet some empirical results regarding the determinants of cloud deployment models are inconsistent, in not conforming to the logic of TCT. Schneider and Sunyaev [18] summarized these inconclusive influence factors as asset specificity, client firm characteristics, and external environment. TCT mainly addresses the transaction cost of outsourcing information resources operation from the aspects of asset specificity, frequency, and uncertainty, but does not thoroughly analyze the impact of relative resource structure on the transaction cost of information resources for firms, which is related to firms’ characteristics.

The resource-based view (RBV) posits that a firm should outsource a resource based on its importance and strategic value to the firm [24]. Barney [24] and Wernerfelt [25] believed that enterprise resources are the sum of various elements owned by enterprises, that can bring competitive advantages or disadvantages, and that include intangible resources and tangible resources, such as firm brand, technology, knowledge, employee skills, information, social capital, land, plant, equipment, process, and financial resources, and others. However, in the set of enterprise resources, not all resources play a consistent role. As firms prefer retaining important and valuable resources internally [24,26], the importance and value of the resource could afect the firm’s sourcing decisions. If a firm’s competitive advantage mainly comes from intangible knowledge resources, it is more likely to keep the operation of information resources internal. That’s because information resources [2], such as literature and data, are one of the carriers and embodiments of knowledge resources [27], and closely related to the firm’s value creation. In other words, the information resources operation might not be outsourced if the internal knowledge resources contained in information resources are important to a firm [28].

K. Foss and N.J. Foss et al. [29] and others [30,31] proposed that RBV and TCT should be combined in the study of organizational outsourcing behavior. Pitelis and Pseiridis [31] suggested that a firm’s resource value could influence transaction costs, because opportunism—that is, market transactions of high-value resources—brings greater uncertainty to firms and the consequent cost of supervision, which leads to increased transaction costs. Therefore, in a firm’s resource structure, the proportion and importance of knowledge resources directly afect the value of information resources to the firm, and thus, encourage it to choose diferent CCDMs. Prior literature has examined some important factors afecting cloud deployment selection based on a firm's characteristics, such as size and IT budget [18.32–34] but has not addressed the influence of the firm’s relative resource structure characteristics. RBV considers how firms tend to internalize and continuously strengthen their strategic resources to sustain a competitive advantage [3,24,26], which can push up the proportion of important resources they access. A firm’s relative resource structure will thus reflect the diferent degrees of importance it assigns to internal resources. It is, therefore, meaningful from a theoretical perspective to further study how the firm’s relative resource structure influences its cloud computing transaction costs, and further influences its cloud computing deployment selection. By opening the enterprise’s black box, such analysis can explain some inconsistent study results in ITO, greatly improve the research framework of TCT’s transaction costs, and promote research on the influencing factors of transaction costs.

A firm’s selection of a cloud deployment model is also impacted by the external environment, especially the institutional environment. The firm’s outsourcing behavior regarding information resources involves many participants, such as cloud service vendors, clients, subcontractors, and internet service providers, whose behaviors are mainly governed by contracts protected by the legal system. The risk of opportunistic behavior by these external partners is one of the main sources of information resources transaction costs. The size of the risk is restricted by the external institutional environment, which in turn can afect the willingness of a firm to outsource its cloud service. Hence, the degree of legal protection (DLP), especially in information security systems, is an important external environment factor for promoting ITO [35]. The DLP can reduce the risk of cloud outsourcing and further moderate cloud deployment model selection behaviors afected by the relative resource structure. DiMaggio et al. [36] argued that the institutional environment afects an organization’s structure through external coercive and normative pressures. Moreover, some scholars have confirmed the relationship between cloud computing outsourcing and governmental regulation [33,37,38]. Drawing on previous studies, we further discuss the direct influence and indirect moderating efects of the DLP on a firm’s selection of a cloud deployment model. This information could help the government understand the influencing factors and mechanisms of a firm’s CCDM selection behavior. The government could then benefit from being able to make adjustments at the institutional level and reduce the risks of information outsourcing.

Firm resources are diverse, and are thus classified into multiple categories. When studying outsourcing behavior [39–42], some researchers have divided firm resources into two types: knowledge capital and physical capital. The current study uses this classification to examine CCDM selections of firms with diferent relative knowledge and physical resource structures. Based on RBV, a firm’s relative knowledge and physical resource structure could reflect the varying degrees of importance of knowledge and physical resources. We introduce the firm’s physical capital intensity (PCI) and KCI into the transaction cost analysis of TCT, and focus on two questions: First, how do the firm’s knowledge capital intensity and physical capital intensity influence its cloud computing deployment model selection? Second, what are the direct influ ences and indirect moderating efects of the DLP as a factor of the external environment on the firm’s cloud computing deployment model selection?

Answering these questions is important for developing a holistic view of the influence of inner resource structure characteristics and outer legal protections on the choice of a cloud deployment model; it also further promotes the study of ITO by combining TCT and RBV. From a practical perspective, the potential findings will promote cloud computing implementation and ofer insights for cloud vendors and government policy makers.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the main related theories on ITO and cloud outsourcing, which provides the theoretical foundation for our study; we also develop the research model and propose the research hypotheses. Then, in Section 3, we discuss the research methodology used to test the hypotheses. In Section 4, we present the results of our empirical testing. Section 5 discusses the findings, implications for theory and practice, limitations, and future directions. The last section introduces the main conclusion of our study.

## 2. Theoretical background and research hypotheses

A firm’s decision to deploy cloud computing is related to the boundaries of the organization, where firm-level decisions are reflected in the chosen IT organizational model. Both TCT and RBV have been widely used to explain organizational decisions regarding IT [16,19,20,26].

## 2.1. Theoretical background

TCT posits that incomplete contracts create the potential for both parties of an operation to engage in opportunistic behavior. Because the transaction costs of contracts vary, the transaction should match the governance structure for each contract [43]. According to TCT, all of a firm’s production resources can be either self-produced or outsourced, and the choice depends on a comparison of internal production and management costs with outsourcing costs. The governance models for the same production resource difer among firms. In the context of cloud computing, firms can choose between private, public, or hybrid cloud deployment models.

Empirical work related to factors influencing the choice of cloud deployment models, however, has not been entirely consistent with TCT logic. There are three main aspects to this inconsistency: (i) asset specificity, (ii) client firm characteristics, and (iii) external environment. Regarding asset specificity, Watjatrakul [20] reported a negative re lationship between technical specificity and cloud outsourcing, but other scholars have come to the opposite conclusion [34,44]. Client firm characteristics include firm size and internal IT capabilities, where Kern et al. [32] found a negative relationship between the firm’s internal IT capabilities and cloud outsourcing, but Lian et al. [33] found a positive relationship between them. A similar paradox exists for the impact of client firm size [34,45]. External environment includes environmental uncertainty and institutional influences, where some scholars propose that uncertainty of demand and products could significantly promote cloud outsourcing [44], but the opposite view is also given [17]. Whereas Rieger et al. [37] considered that governmental regulation could negatively influence cloud outsourcing, others found no significant relationship between them [33].

Using TCT logic to study cloud deployment models may yield some inconsistent results, because it leads researchers to overlook some of the factors that impact cloud outsourcing decisions [46], such as the boundary conditions of cloud computing—the firm’s external context and distinctive internal context comprising the firm’s various resources [18]. That means it is necessary to pay attention to the firm’s internal resource characteristics and external environment, and to analyze how these factors generate and afect the transaction costs of cloud computing. Some prior literature has considered the impacts of a firm’s internal resource characteristics on its cloud deployment model choice, such as top management’s support, firm size, prior ITO experience, and employees’ IT skills [3,32–34]. However, there are few studies on the impact of the firm’s resource structure characteristics on cloud computing deployment selection.

Firms have many resources that can positively or negatively influence the cloud deployment model decision. A firm’s internal resource characteristics include not only the type and amount of resources (i.e., resource constitutive characteristics), but also the proportion and im portance of diferent resources (i.e., relative resource structure characteristics). In fact, a firm’s cloud deployment model decision is generally the result of the comprehensive efects of various resources. RBV considers that a subset of a firm’s resources can create a competitive advantage and become a key part of its resource structure [3,24]. The relative resource structure, which reflects the proportion and importance of diferent resources in the firm, is therefore, an integrated indicator that could influence a firm’s decision to build or outsource cloud computing. Scholars have noted the impact of relative resource structure on organizational decisions. For instance, Aubert et al. [47] examined the negative impact of firms’ knowledge intensity<sup>4</sup> on ITO, but did not discuss the reasons for or mechanism of this negative impact. Hence, the current study analyzes the impact of a firm’s relative resource structure on the transaction cost of a CCDM selection.

Knowledge capital and physical capital are the two most important components [39–42] of a firm’s resource structure. Knowledge capital refers to intangible assets, such as human resources, technological capability, and institutions [49,50]. Physical capital is related to tangible assets, such as facilities and stock [51]. On the basis of the pro portion and importance of knowledge capital and physical capital, a firm’s relative resource structure can be described by two terms: knowledge capital intensity (KCI) and physical capital intensity (PCI). KCI reflects the importance and proportion of knowledge resources to the firm, and PCI reflects the importance and proportion of physical resources to the firm [48,52]. RBV believes that firms prefer to increase their core resources to maintain competitive advantages [24,26]. Each firm’s unique resource structure characteristics are thus formed, wherein its relative resource structure reflects the varying importance of physical and knowledge resources. For firms with high KCI, the importance and proportion of knowledge resources is foremost among their resources [48,53]. Likewise, for firms with high PCI, compared with their other resources, the importance and proportion of physical resources are the highest.

The transaction costs of outsourcing information resource operations vary in firms with diferent relative PCI and KCI structures. Information resources are closely related to knowledge resources, where information can be created out of knowledge [54] and is the embodiment of knowledge [27]. For firms with high KCI, knowledge as a strategic resource is more likely to be maintained in-house because of its importance for the firm [24]. Information as the carrier of knowledge resources is thus more likely handled within the organization. On the contrary, for firms with high PCI, the proportion and importance of other resources are less than those of physical resources because physical capital is the key resource of the firms’ operations. As the im portance of information resources is relatively lower,<sup>5</sup> it is more economical to outsource information resource operations and choose the public cloud deployment model. This allows the firm to focus on the operation of its core physical resources, as it need not allocate resources to build software and hardware for cloud computing. A firm’s relative PCI and KCI structure could in this case influence its CCDM decision.

## 2.2. Research hypotheses

We consider that PCI and KCI reflect the characteristics of a firm’s relative resource structure. because they indicate the relative im portance of physical and knowledge capital. The production, management, and transaction costs of information resource operation are dif ferent in firms with high KCI or PCI. A firm’s PCI and KCI can thus have important efects on its choice of a cloud deployment model. The DLP also influences the CCDM decision and moderates the relationship be tween KCI and the selection of the public cloud deployment model. The research model is presented in Fig. 1.

## 2.2.1. Physical capital intensity

Firms with high PCI own more physical assets, such as plants and facilities, where these assets are more important to them than other assets [53,55]. The literature on strategy has reported that firms with relatively high PCI are more likely to choose outsourcing over direct investment [40,41,56]. Few scholars, however, have considered PCI when it comes to research on ITO or cloud computing deployment. As companies often strengthen their core resources, firms with high PCI prefer to maintain core physical capital in-house, and outsource other relatively less important resources [24]. With information being one embodiment or manifestation of knowledge [27], it does not belong to core value resources for firms with high PCI. Information operations are thus more likely to be outsourced, and firms with high PCI are more likely to choose the public CCDM.

PCI could afect the production and management cost of building or outsourcing cloud computing. Firms with high PCI often have high fixed costs because they maintain their core physical assets [39,41]. The self-built cloud computing data center, which belongs to noncore assets, is not significantly useful in terms of strengthening the core physical assets. On the contrary, building cloud computing infrastructure, such as an internet data center, increases the average internal costs of noncore resources’ production and management, especially if data usage is relatively low. Production and management costs are often the most robust factors in decisions regarding an IT governance model [47]. Prior research has proved the positive impact of production cost savings (e.g., hardware and software costs) on ITO and cloud computing outsourcing [57]. Hence, the need to reduce production and management costs could steer firms with high PCI toward outsourcing and to the public cloud deployment model.

In addition, PCI also afects the transaction costs of building or outsourcing cloud computing. Firms with high PCI regard core physical assets as their most valuable inputs [53,55] and as more important than noncore assets, such as knowledge resources. Hence, high PCI reduces the importance of information resources as one of the knowledge resource carriers, and the potential losses incurred by firms with high PCI in cloud outsourcing may be less than those incurred by firms with high KCI. This implies that the efects of specific assets and uncertainty in cloud outsourcing are relatively lower for firms with high PCI. Compared with firms having high KCI, firms with high PCI tend to select public cloud deployment because of lower transaction costs.

In conclusion, PCI indicates the relatively lower importance of knowledge resources, and could afect the production, management, and transaction costs of building or outsourcing cloud computing. PCI could, therefore, influence CCDM selection. Public cloud deployment is a more eficient arrangement for firms with high PCI than is nonpublic cloud (including private and hybrid cloud) deployment. These eficiencies and cost savings encourage firms with high PCI to choose public cloud deployment, leading to the following hypothesis:

H1. Firms with high physical capital intensity are more likely to choose the public cloud deployment model than the private or hybrid cloud deployment model.

## 2.2.2. Knowledge capital intensity

In firms with high KCI, knowledge assets are more important and play a more significant role in production, compared with other assets [18,47,55]. Firms with high KCI generally rely on knowledge resources to maintain competitiveness. Because information as the embodiment of knowledge [27] is one of the core resources of firms with high KCI, these firms are more likely to maintain cloud computing in-house. Aubert et al. [47] have investigated the impact factors of ITO and found that a firm-level characteristic—knowledge intensity—is negatively associated with the extent of outsourcing. But the mechanism of negative relationships should be further studied. In this research, we consider KCI to be a major factor afecting the firm’s choice of ITO, which influences the transaction, production, and management costs of building or outsourcing cloud computing operations.

High KCI can enhance the importance and proportion of information resources because it is one of the carriers and expressions of knowledge [27]. Hence, cloud computing is more likely to be built inhouse at the firm. Using the public CCDM could also incur relatively high external transaction costs for firms with high KCI, for the following reasons [58]: First, the public cloud provides on-demand and scalable computing services with shared resource pooling on a pay-per-use basis [7]. Firms with high KCI usually depend on knowledge assets and fre quently use knowledge information to maintain daily operations. As a measured service, pay-per-use cloud computing indicates a positive relationship between asset usage frequency and outsourcing costs [17]. It is, therefore, economical for firms with high KCI to build a private cloud and keep information resource operations within the organization. Second, in the public cloud, vendors provide measurable, on-demand services to clients through broad network access. This public cloud service model entails greater uncertainties than the private cloud model, including technical immaturity, network connection instability, hostile attack policies, and legal risks. There are other risks arising from unpredictable vendor behavior in terms of price, service quality, reliability, and lock-in [7,21,59]. Because of the high importance of information assets for firms with high KCI, they are more sensitive to any uncertainties of outsourcing information management. Firms with high KCI are thus more likely to choose a nonpublic cloud to reduce uncertainties. Third, vendors own the physical assets in a public cloud, whereas clients own the data assets running in the public cloud. Knowledge data, however, are a special asset that has the characteristics of nonrivalry and nonexcludability [56,60]. Nonrivalry means that knowledge data stored in a cloud can be applied in various facilities without reducing the value in any single facility. Cloud computing provides multiuser, real-time access and multiterminal sharing of knowledge data. In a public cloud, clients may not recognize data leakage in a timely manner, because knowledge data are nonrivaled. Nonexcludability means that the vendor cannot guarantee that knowledge data stored in the public cloud cannot be accessed by other organizations, including the vendor itself. As information is considered the embodiment of knowledge, the nonrivalry and nonexcludability properties of knowledge data make it risky for a firm with high KCI to outsource information-related assets, and adopt a public cloud. For example, it is dificult to discover a copy, leakage, or deletion of knowledge data in real time, making it also dificult to measure the value of the loss, apportion ex post liability, or confirm the loss and liability in law [61]. Therefore, it may also be dificult for firms with high KCI to trace loss and to receive compensation for it.

The internal costs of cloud computing facilities are relatively lower for firms with high KCI than for firms with high PCI. The assets of firms with high KCI are concentrated in knowledge assets that have relatively lower fixed costs. Compared with firms having high PCI, building cloud computing infrastructures by those with high KCI, as a result, would increase their fixed or average cost of production to a lesser extent. In addition, firms with high KCI have abundant human resources, especially a large number of technical staf who can support the construction and maintenance of cloud computing. It is, therefore, economical for firms with high KCI to build cloud computing centers, which discourages them from choosing public cloud deployment. The following hypothesis is thus proposed:

H2a. Firms with high knowledge capital intensity are more likely to choose the private or hybrid cloud deployment model than the public cloud deployment model.

We also consider diferent types of KCI. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) defines four diferent kinds of knowledge: know-what, know-why, know-how, and know-who [62]. These types can be categorized as codified knowledge (know-what and know-why) and tacit knowledge (know-how and know-who) [58] A firm’s codified knowledge is explicit and easy to express in a compact or standardized form, such as in patents and technical literature [63]. It can also be transformed into information data, stored in the cloud, transmitted among multiple terminals, and reproduced in another firm. A firm’s tacit knowledge is unstructured, implicit, and not easily recorded in language, charts, and formulas. Examples of tacit knowledge include skills, enterprise culture, and social capital [63]. Compared with codified knowledge, tacit knowledge is dificult to be transformed into information data stored in the cloud and transferred across organizational boundaries. Hence, the importance of information assets in firms with high TKCI is relatively lower than in firms with high CKCI.

The knowledge assets of firms with high CKCI are mainly found in explicit, compact, or standardized forms [64]. Codified knowledge is easy to transform into information and store in a cloud, where it is accessible to multiple users. Firms with high CKCI may thus face more risks in using a public cloud. Because it is very dificult to evaluate and compensate for the loss of knowledge or information leaked from a public cloud, firms with high CKCI may prefer a private cloud to a public cloud.

On the contrary, firms with high TKCI have more intangible tacit knowledge assets such as management, operational, and innovation competences [64]. Tacit knowledge is implicit and hard to transform into information that can be stored in a cloud. Also, when tacit knowledge is stored in a cloud, it is not easy for other users to learn or imitate it. Firms with high TKCI may thus face relatively lower information risks when using the public cloud. But because of the high importance of information, and high potential capabilities for information management in firms with high TKCI, these firms tend to keep cloud computing in-house rather than outsourcing it. Taking into account these relationships and influences, we believe that compared with firms with high CKCI, firms with high TKCI are not so sensitive to a CCDM selection. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2b. Compared with firms with high tacit knowledge capital intensity (TKCI), firms with high codified knowledge capital intensity (CKCI) are more susceptible to negative relationships between knowledge capital intensity (KCI) and the public cloud deployment model.

## 2.2.3. Degree of legal protection

Because the behavior of the many firm participants involved in cloud computing (e.g., service vendors, clients, subcontractors, and internet service providers) is governed mainly by contracts protected by the legal system, the DLP can influence the transaction costs of cloud computing outsourcing, and moderate the relationships between a firm’s relative structure model and its cloud deployment model decision. In essence, selecting a CCDM means choosing between building and outsourcing information resource operations. Because of diferent relative resource characteristics in diferent firms, the value of information assets is distinct. This causes diferent firms to have varying transaction costs, such as production and management costs, when outsourcing or building information resource operations. One of the transaction costs in choosing public cloud deployment is the risk of opportunistic behaviors by cloud service vendors. The size of this risk is restricted by the external institutional environment, which could sub sequently afect the willingness of a firm to outsource cloud services. DiMaggio et al. [36] proposed that the institutional environment afects an organization’s structure through external coercive and normative pressures, including laws. This implies that the DLP is an important external environmental impact factor.

The virtualization technology inherent in cloud computing ofers a revolutionary IT service model with the advantages of high eficiency and low cost. Because the various participants in a public cloud, in cluding vendors, clients, subcontractors, and internet service providers, are likely to be in many diferent regions and countries [65], the literature has discussed legal conflicts among countries and the negative influence of an immature legal environment on cloud adoption [18]. Similarly, the current study addresses the positive efect of improving regional legal protections and responsibilities—that is, a complete, relevant, and strictly enforced legal system—on driving firms’ adoptions of CCDMs, especially the public cloud deployment model. We consider that if the relevant law is refined, it will reduce the transaction costs of cloud outsourcing and afect firms’ decisions about deploying cloud computing. There are two main risks associated with cloud computing: (i) technical risk, where data are lost, intercepted, attacked, deleted, or embezzled in the process of data transmission, and (ii) legal risk, where relevant laws about outsourcing information resource operations are absent in some regions or are not compatible with laws of other nations in cross-border jurisdictions, making it dificult to prove or diferentiate the legal responsibilities of participants in the public cloud [16,61]. This means that if firms choose the public cloud, they will be more concerned with the quality and enforcement of relevant laws in different regions or nations.

A complete legal protection system can efectively reduce the risk of cloud computing, especially a public cloud. A perfect legal system can efectively prevent and control the risks of public cloud services by establishing rules of electronic evidence, protecting cloud users’ digital rights, clarifying the responsibilities of cloud computing providers services, and standardizing cloud service contract standards. On the one hand, the legal protection to cloud users forms external normative and coercive forces on cloud computing providers, prompting them to strengthen internal control and constantly improve the technology and management level of cloud computing risk prevention and control, so as to reduce the technical risks caused by the opportunistic behaviors of cloud computing providers. On the other hand, the legal protection can also efectively protect the rights and interests of cloud users in cloud security incidents, and avoid legal risks, which might damage the rights and interests of cloud users, caused by the ambiguity of legal provisions and unclear the division of tort liability, so as to encourage firms to choose the public cloud service model. Hence, the DLP has an impact on firms’ public cloud choices. We put forward the following hypothesis:

H3a. A high degree of legal protection is positively related to the selection of a public cloud deployment model.

The DLP could also moderate the relationships between relative resource structure and public cloud adoption. As mentioned above, the importance of information is distinct among firms with diferent relative structures of resources, but outsourcing information resource operations could expose firms that deploy the public cloud computing model to some forms of risk, such as technical or legal. Hence, the higher DLP helps to clarify the responsibilities of both parties, reduce the opportunistic behavior of cloud service vendors, and protect information security in the cloud, which could encourage firms with diferent relative resource structures to choose the public cloud. Specifically, the DLP could enhance the possibility of firms with high PCI choosing a public cloud, and could reduce the possibility of choosing private and hybrid clouds for firms with high KCI. The following hypotheses are proposed:

H3b. A high degree of legal protection positively moderates the positive relationships between PCI and adopting the public cloud deployment model.

H3c. A high degree of legal protection alleviates the negative relationships between KCI and adopting a public cloud deployment model.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research data and sample

This study uses data from Chinese-listed companies to examine our hypotheses. By 2013, there were nearly 2600 listed companies in mainland China. First, we browsed the websites and annual reports of those listed companies and searched for keywords such as cloud computing and cloud service. This produced a list of companies that had been involved in cloud computing as of December 2013. Second, we col lected telephone numbers or e-mail addresses of these companies’ IT departments (from websites or customer service departments). Third, we confirmed which cloud deployment models companies had chosen by contacting their IT personnel through phone or e-mail. The survey we used was designed so that only responses from IT departments were retained.<sup>6</sup> Fourth, we collected the financial data of companies from China Stock Market Accounting Research(CSMAR).<sup>7</sup> On the basis of data collected in the previous steps, we used multinomial logistic regression (MLR) to verify the proposed research hypotheses.

Following Blumberg et al. [66], we used several sample screening criteria, where the companies (i) had legally registered as listed companies in China’s Shanghai or Shenzhen stock exchange before December 2013; (ii) had published complete financial statements in CSMAR from 2010 to 2013; (iii) were nonfinancial companies, because the financial statements of nonfinancial firms difer from those of financial companies<sup>8</sup> and (iv) had adopted public, private, or hybrid cloud computing. Based on these criteria, our final research sample was 520 Chinese listed companies from a range of industries.<sup>9</sup> They are shown in Table 1.

## 3.2.1. Cloud computing deployment model

Armbrust et al. [6] classified the CCDM in the private cloud and the public cloud. Mell and Grance [7] further distinguished four types of clouds: private, public, hybrid, and community. Prior research has often used a dichotomous approach to study the discriminant power of in dependent variables for the CCDM [68]. However, the classification method of Armbrust et al. ignores the commonly chosen hybrid cloud deployment model [69]. Furthermore, as mentioned before, a com munity cloud is essentially a kind of private cloud or hybrid cloud deployment model [8], and is not widely adopted in practice. This study thus uses the categorical variable to represent a firm’s choice of private, public, or hybrid deployment model. Private cloud adopters are firms that completely build their own cloud computing facilities, without using any public cloud service; public cloud adopters are firms that outsource all their cloud computing; and hybrid cloud adopters are firms that use both the private and public clouds.

## 3.2.2. Physical capital intensity

PCI is often associated with the ratio or importance of tangible assets. Aubert et al. [47] considered physical capital that includes plant, machinery, and other assets with fixed, tangible, and durable features [51]. In financial statements, the tangible assets ratio can be used as a measurement of PCI, but it is highly correlated with the intangible capital ratio as an indicator of CKCI, which could cause multicollinearity in the regression analysis. Several ITO scholars have considered this factor [70]. López-Bazo and Moreno [71] used monetary stock to measure physical capital. Markusen and Xie [56], as well as Bergstrand and Egger [40] used fixed capital to approximately substitute for physical capital. Following these authors, this study uses the fixed assets ratio as the measure of PCI to replace the tangible assets ratio, defining it as the ratio of net fixed assets to total assets in com panies’ financial statements.

## 3.2.3. Knowledge capital intensity

Researchers have measured KCI as the share of research and de velopment (R&D) expenses and the proportion of highly skilled workers in the workforce [47]. Others have used IT capital share and advertising intensity to represent KCI [56,72]. We think that these indicators—R&D expenses, IT capital share, and advertising intensity—are more suitable for measuring the total amount of knowledge capital, and do not reflect the intensity of knowledge capital in a firm’s resource structure. In addition, following Foray and Lundvall [62], the current study divides KCI into CKCI and TKCI, and discusses the impact of each on the selection of a CCDM. It is, therefore, necessary to identify separate in dicators for these variables.

From a financial accounting perspective, the intangible assets ratio published in companies’ financial statements is an appropriate measure of CKCI [73]. It presents the ratio of a company’s net intangible assets to its total assets. Intangible assets are those that can be codified, estimated, and traded, such as patents and brand value, which are rela tively consistent with the connotation of codified knowledge [63]. Hence, the intangible assets ratio can better reflect a firm’s CKCI. It is dificult to measure TKCI, because tacit knowledge is basically unstructured, implicit, and unobservable. Markusen and Xie [56], as well as Jinji et al. [74] adopted the Tobin’s Q of public companies as the knowledge intensity variable. Jinji et al. [74] considered that a firm with a higher Tobin’s Q is more knowledge intensive. Similarly, we adopt Tobin’s Q as the representative variable of TKCI. In financial statements, Tobin’s Q is measured as the ratio of a company’s market value to its total assets, representing investors’ asset valuations. The premium part of the total assets can be seen as an evaluation of the company’s tacit knowledge. To more accurately measure a firm’s asset premium, which reflects the investors’ valuation of the firm’s TKCI, and to reduce the interference of coding knowledge, we subtract net in tangible assets and net goodwill from the total assets when calculating Tobin’s Q.

## 3.2.5. Control variables (CV)

Cloud computing is an innovation in information technology, and its application in a firm constitutes an information system upgrade by the firm. Selection of the CCDM may be influenced by the firm’s prior IT investment, which is regarded as a sunk cost. Hence, we investigate the firm’s prior IT investment, including IT assets (ITA), and IT department staf (ITS). Compared with the firm’s peers, the degree of its ITA and the number of ITS are categorized into five grades (Grade 1 = low IT investment, Grade 5 = high IT investment). Four CV are introduced in this study as indicators of a firm’s prior IT investment: industries, firm scale, ITA, and ITS.

## 4. Results

This study employs maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to estimate the parameters of the logistic model and evaluate the hypotheses. MLR is sensitive to sample size because MLE requires larger samples. Hosmer and Lemeshow [82] recommended that the sample size should be greater than 400. Because the empirical data of this study are drawn from 520 listed companies, it meets the MLR sample size requirement. MLR is also sensitive to data multicollinearity, which means a high correlation among independent variables. Data multicollinearity reduces predictability in the MLR model [81,83]. We test the tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) to examine whether multicollinearity exists for the independent variables of the study. The test results indicate no multicollinearity, in that VIF values are below the threshold of 10. The descriptive statistics of data and tests of multicollinearity are shown in Table 3. The enter method is used to estimate the MLR. Tables 4 and 5 present the model evaluations and results.

## 4.3. Robustness check for endogeneity

## 5. Discussion

This research considers the reasons why firms choose diferent CCDMs, and hypothesizes that the intensities of physical capital and knowledge capital could be important factors influencing this decision. The hypotheses are tested on a sample of 520 firms’ observations from mainland China. The results of the initial hypotheses tests are shown in Table 7. Generally, a firm’s relative resource structure, which reflect the importance and proportion of physical versus knowledge resources, is found to influence its choice of CCDM.

## 5.1. Implications for research

First, this study extends extant works on CCDMs. There has been considerable research on cloud adoption and choice of service models $\left[ 1 0 , 1 8 , 9 3 \right]$ , which drives the research into the behavior and approach of IT adoption. But few studies have focused on cloud deployment model selection. We consider that cloud computing deployment is one of the most important decisions firms make once they move down the path of cloud computing, where the choice of building a cloud within the organization or outsourcing it must be made. This study contributes to the literature by analyzing the influence mechanism of relative resource structure on cloud deployment model selection. Prior research on client firm characteristics has been rich in addressing impact factors on cloud outsourcing decisions, such as firm size and internal IT capabilities [18,32–34,45]. Consistent with research on client firm characteristics, we consider a relative resource structure to be the key firm characteristic. According to RBV, firms are committed to strengthening their core resources, where relative resource structure integrates various resources and reflects their importance in the firm. PCI and KCI, capturing firms’ relative resource structure, could influence their costs of building or outsourcing cloud computing. Our results indicate a significant relationship between relative resource structure and cloud deployment model selection. To some extent, relative resource structure can be used to interpret why firms with similar IT capabilities or of similar size choose diferent CCDMs. There is a fruitful avenue of future research to understand how the relative resource structure influences the selection of a cloud deployment model.

Second, this research contributes by revealing the influence of information resource attributes on the choice of a cloud deployment model. In previous studies on the selection of a cloud deployment model, the impact of information resource attributes is not analyzed in depth. However, we distinguish between the tangible and intangible attributes of outsourcing assets, and further analyze the impact of their tangible and intangible attributes on the outsourcing decision. Though tangible asset sourcing decisions are mainly related to observable production costs, intangible asset sourcing decisions are more sophisticated and involve the consideration of implicit costs. Firms that outsource the operations of intangible information resources face diferent risks, depending on their internal relative resource structure. This study promotes research on the attributes and importance of outsourcing assets in the field of $^ \mathrm { I T O , }$ and further distinguishes two types of intangible knowledge assets, and proposes that each has a different degree of impact on the cloud deployment model decision. Prior research on the influence of KCI on ITO has been relatively general [47] or focused on firm capability [94]. In fact, firm knowledge assets can be divided into diferent types, such as static intangible assets and dynamic capabilities, which could have diferent efects on CCDM selection. Firms with higher CKCI are more likely to select the private or hybrid cloud deployment model than those with higher TKCI. This finding suggests that there is a potentially fruitful avenue of future exploration of the impact of diferent knowledge asset types on CCDM selection.

Third, our study combines RBV and TCT to analyze firms’ CCDM selection behaviors, which further enriches the ITO research. Previous researchers have compared RBV and TCT and discussed, which is more suitable for the study of firms’ insourcing or outsourcing behaviors [20,95]. Some scholars have proposed that the two should be combined for analysis [29–31]. This study builds on this line of literature by providing a combined path to analyze the impact of the firm’s resource structure characteristics on its transaction costs for cloud computing outsourcing [31]. Based on RBV, our research further improves the study of TCT in the area of cloud computing outsourcing by introducing the relative resource structure, which reflects the relative importance and proportion of physical and knowledge resources. Our finding that most of the moderating efects are nonsignificant, however, implies that further work is required to understand the functional mechanism by which the DLP impacts public cloud selection. Only when firms choose between public and private cloud deployment can the DLP significantly alleviate the negative relationships between CKCI and public cloud adoption. This indicates that when firms with high CKCI are striking a balance between complete outsourcing and self-built cloud computing, the DLP can efectively alleviate the risk anxiety of firms with high CKCI, and increase the possibility of them choosing the public cloud. But the moderating efect is not significant in the context of firms' selections between the public cloud and hybrid cloud, nor is it important in the relationship between TKCI and public cloud adoption. Possible explanations for this are as follows: First, a hybrid cloud includes public and private clouds. A firm is free to choose which information resources to put in the public cloud and which to keep in the firm’s information data center. Generally speaking, a firm tends to outsource noncore business information processing to the public cloud while leaving core information processing inside the firm. With this arrangement of information processing, the firm could reduce anxiety over information security with the choice of a hybrid cloud. Therefore, the sensitivity of the external DLP is reduced. A company can reduce risk by adjusting the proportion of public cloud and private cloud or by outsourcing the noncore business information resource, which could cause nonsignificant moderating efects of the DLP on the relationship between relative resource structure (including CKCI, TKCI, and PCI) and public cloud selection. Second. the nonsignificant mod erating efect of the DLP between TKCI and public cloud selection may be related to the characteristics of tacit knowledge. Tacit knowledge is unstructured, implicit, and not easily recorded in language, charts, and formulas, so it is difficult to transform it into information data stored in the cloud and transferred across organizational boundaries. Hence, compared with codified knowledge, tacit knowledge information is les likely to be damaged. leaked. and imitated in the public cloud. Moreover, the sensitivity of firms with high TKCI to the DLP is lower than that of firms with high CKCI, which leads to the nonsignificant moderating efect of the DLP on the relationship between TKCI and public cloud selection. Third, the distribution of listed companies is mainly concentrated in Beijing, Shanghai, and the Guangdong province, which may result in limited DLP diferences. This finding suggests that further work is needed to understand the paths by which legal protection afects CCDMs.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The findings of this study ofer insights for cloud vendors and government policy makers. First, the study reveals that with the rapid growth of the cloud service market, a key issue is the selection of a deployment model. Understanding this selection mechanism could affect vendor decisions about the scale of their cloud data centers and help them forecast the entire demand for a public cloud. From an investor’s perspective, the CCDM is related to the eficiency and risk of the cloud computing investment [68]. From a social perspective, it also afects the scale of economic benefits and low-carbon efect of cloud computing [15,97].

Second, by analyzing the attributes and importance of the client’s resources, cloud vendors can use the insights ofered by this study to better guide their clients to choose an appropriate cloud deployment model. This will help enhance customer satisfaction and facilitate better cooperation between transactional parties. Our findings indicate that understanding the influence of a firm’s PCI and KCI can help public cloud vendors better target customers and improve the vendors’ op erational efectiveness.

Third, these research results reveal what firms with high KCI consider when choosing cloud computing, especially outsourced cloud services in a public cloud. Cloud vendors should thus pay more attention to the relative importance of a particular client’s physical and knowledge resources, which may influence its implicit transaction costs. Cloud vendors can reduce these costs with standardized services and contracts that clearly assign the division of responsibilities.

Fourth, for government policy makers, cloud computing is an in novative IT service model that can improve the operational efective ness of IT and save social resources. Public cloud computing can also contribute to a low-carbon economy by concentrating on data centers and improving the utilization rate of cloud servers [97]. Our findings have confirmed that the institutional environment is an important factor influencing the selection of a CCDM, especially the legal environment. Empirical results of this study show that although the DLP has a complicated moderating efect on the relationship between relative resource structure and public cloud selection, it has a significant and direct positive efect on public cloud selection. Because the DLP can decrease environmental uncertainty and restrain vendors from pursuing opportunistic behaviors, governments should attach great importance to constructing a legal protection system, particularly in regard to (i) strongly improving the legal system associated with the cloud, such as data rights, and (ii) building an efective system of legal enforcement and supervision.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations, which reveal opportunities for future research. Scholars could extend our research by studying the problems of cloud deployment model selection, such as the dynamic selection of enterprise cloud computing deployment patterns, and researching ITO in the context of cloud computing. Because our sample contains Chinese listed companies that tend to be large, future studies could examine the applicability of our findings to a sample broadened to include small firms and businesses from diferent countries. Findings of this study validate the influence of PCI and KCI on cloud computing deployment selection, but our statistical analysis reveals only the re lationships between them, not causal efects. Future research could use other methodologies, such as case studies [98], to draw more refined conclusions on how PCI and KCI afect cloud computing deployment selection. Also, the measurement of PCI and KCI is based mainly on the objective financial data of listed companies. Although the relative resource structure of firms can be reflected to a certain extent through such indicators, the influencing factors of these indicators are more complex. Future researchers are, therefore, advised to improve the scale for measuring PCI and KCI. Finally, although our findings validate the positive efect of legal protection on the selection of a public cloud, most of the moderating efects of legal protection are insignificant. Thi study mainly uses the IPR protection index as the proxy variable for the DLP. Besides the IPR protection index, there may be other relevant legal factors not fully considered, which could moderate the relationship between relative resource structure and cloud outsourcing, such as local regulations and law enforcement eforts. Future studies should include more comprehensive legal factors and discuss the mechanism through which external legal protection influences cloud deployment decisions.

## 6. Conclusions

This study proposes that a firm’s physical and knowledge capital intensities impact the selection of a CCDM. Empirical tests support most of our hypotheses. The selection of a CCDM reflects the property rights arrangements for the organization’s ITA—self-building or outsourcing. Choosing a deployment model is the key issue for firms that adopt cloud computing. Studying this issue could promote ITO research in the context of cloud computing and, to some extent, explain inconsistent results reported in extant research. These inconsistencies are based mainly on certain resource characteristics of the firm’s internal environment. We focus on the relative structure and importance of the diferent resources that inform a firm’s physical and knowledge capital intensities. The relative resource structure could influence the importance of information resources to firms, and further influence their information outsourcing behavior. Study findings reveal that physical and knowledge capital intensities significantly influence a firm’s CCDM decision. Although most of the moderating efects of the legal environment on the relationship between relative resource structure and cloud-sourcing decisions are insignificant, legal protection still has a significant and direct efect on public cloud selection, as well as a significant moderating efect between CKCI and public cloud selection. Meanwhile, our findings imply that to provide appropriate cloud service models, it is necessary to pay attention to both external environmental efects and the internal business environment, especially the characteristics of the relative resource structure.
