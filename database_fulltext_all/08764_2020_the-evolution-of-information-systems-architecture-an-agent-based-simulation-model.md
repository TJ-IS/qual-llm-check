# THE EVOLUTION OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS ARCHITECTURE: AN AGENT-BASED SIMULATION MODEL

Kazem Haki Institute for Information Management, University of St. Gallen, Müller-Friedberg-Strasse 8, 9000 St. Gallen, SWITZERLAND {kazem.haki@unisg.ch}

Jannis Beese SAP AG, Bahnstrasse 1, 8274 Tägwerwilen, SWITZERLAND {jannis.beese@sap.com}

Stephan Aier Institute for Information Management, University of St. Gallen, Müller-Friedberg-Strasse 8, 9000 St. Gallen, SWITZERLAND {stephan.aier@uisg.ch}

Robert Winter Institute for Information Management, University of St. Gallen, Müller-Friedberg-Strasse 8, 9000 St. Gallen, SWITZERLAND {robert.winter@unisg.ch}

Understanding how information systems (IS) architecture evolves and what outcomes can be expected from the evolution of IS architecture presents a considerable challenge for both research and practice. The evolution of IS architecture is marked by management’s efforts to keep local and short-term IS investments in line with enterprise-wide and long-term objectives, so they often employ coercive mechanisms to enforce enterprise-wide considerations on local actors. However, an organization is shaped by a multitude of heterogeneous local actors’ actions that pursue their own, sometimes conflicting, goals, norms, and values. This study offers a theory-informed simulation model that explores how IS architecture evolves and with what outcomes in various types of organizations. The simulation model is informed by institutional theory to capture various types of organizations that are characterized by different combinations of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures, and by complex adaptive systems theory to capture the emergent character of IS architecture’s evolution. First, we outline the insights from simulation experiments. Then, building on the simulation model and theoretical insights, we discuss implications for both research and practice.

Keywords: Information systems architecture, complex adaptive systems, institutional theory, agent-based modeling, simulation

## Introduction

Because of the enormous growth of investments in information systems (IS), almost all large organizations deal with hundreds or thousands of IS components that are typically introduced over several years to serve the diverse purposes of the organization’s local actors (Ciborra et al. 2000; Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013).

Various IS research streams have taken a perspective beyond that of an individual information system to consider the entire set of an organization’s IS components, which we refer to as the IS architecture perspective. IS architecture is the set of an organization’s IS components and their interdependencies, as well as management’s efforts to keep local and short-term IS investments in line with enterprise-wide and long-term objectives (Richardson et al. 1990; Zachman 1987).

While endeavors to design and develop IS must keep up with continual changes in organizational requirements, information technologies give rise to new organizational requirements because of their fusion into an organization’s fabric (Bharadwaj et al. 2013; Drnevich and Croson 2013; Zammuto et al. 2007). These organizational and technological mutual adaptations bring about the emergent evolution of IS components and their interdependencies and, therefore, the emergent evolution of IS architecture (Allen and McGlade 1987; Allen and Varga 2006).

Several IS research streams are concerned with capturing and explaining the evolution of IS architecture, the most prominent of which is enterprise architecture (EA) research (Boh and Yellin 2007; Lange et al. 2016; Richardson et al. 1990; Ross et al. 2006; Schmidt and Buxmann 2011; Zachman 1987). EA seeks to provide a holistic view of an organization’s IS components (Ross et al. 2006; Schmidt and Buxmann 2011) and to guide their design and evolution to meet enterprise-wide objectives (Boh and Yellin 2007; Richardson et al. 1990). EA is expected to contribute to an organization’s critical measures of efficiency and flexibility (Lange et al. 2016; Schmidt and Buxmann 2011).

To explain the evolution of IS architecture, Ross et al.’s (2006) maturity model has received considerable attention in EA research and practice. This model differentiates several stages of EA maturity through which organizations improve their expected outcomes (i.e., efficiency and flexibility) as their EA maturity rises. To help organizations evolve to the next maturity level, a classical EA research stream promotes the design and implementation of coercive mechanisms (Peristeras and Tarabanis 2000) such as architecture standards (e.g., Boh and Yellin 2007) and principles (e.g., Greefhorst and Proper 2011; Richardson et al. 1990). Coercive mechanisms seek to preserve and enforce enterprise-wide considerations by restricting local actors’ design freedom when they develop new or enhance existing IS components (Dietz 2008). Nonetheless, organizations are shaped by an intrinsic flux of local actors’ actions (Tsoukas and Chia 2002) with competing and sometimes conflicting goals, norms, values, and beliefs (Levina 2005; Tsoukas and Chia 2002). Acknowledging the bottom-up contributions of local actors, the EA literature has extended its perspective by considering local actors’ norms and values (Aier 2014; Haki and Legner 2013; Weiss et al. 2013) and their manifestation in management practices (Ross and Quaadgras 2012; Winter 2014) to account for the emergent character of IS architecture’s evolution.

By integrating these research streams, we explain how institutional pressures delineate local actors’ actions, which are driven not only by coercive pressure imposed by the organization but also by normative and mimetic pressures imposed by actors’ peers (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Scott 2014). While the existing research has investigated the roles of various types of institutional pressures (e.g., Brosius et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2007), it has not addressed how these pressures interact with one another over time or how their interactions bring about emergent outcomes. For instance, how would the coercive implementation of a system (e.g., a centralized ERP system as a large-scale enterprise system with many dependencies that considerably impacts IS architecture) work in the long run in an organizational culture with high (or low) levels of normative and mimetic pressures? Therefore, we seek to answer this research question: How do institutional pressures independently and jointly affect the outcomes of IS architecture?

We posit that anticipating the emergent outcomes of IS architecture’s evolution may be feasible with the assistance of a theory-informed simulation model. Therefore, our primary contribution is a theory-informed simulation model to explore how IS architecture emerges under various levels of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures, considering their interplays and the dynamics of their changes over time. To this end, we employed institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Scott 2014) to model the three institutional pressures. We also employed complex adaptive systems (CAS) theory (Anderson 1999; Axelrod and Cohen 1999; Holland 1995) to capture the emergent evolution of IS architecture and to model local actors, their adaptive interactions in organizational networks, and their environment.

We first present the simulation model and discuss insights from the experiments conducted on the simulation model to answer our research question. Then we discuss implications for both research and practice, building on the simulation model and the theoretical insights we derived.

## Theoretical Foundation

Complexity theory generally, and CAS in particular, conceptualizes evolution as resulting from interactions among the constituent actors of a system (Anderson 1999; Holland 1995). CAS represent systems with nonlinear properties at the global level that result from interactions among the system’s actors at the local level (Anderson 1999; Holland 1995). Such systems adapt and evolve through their dynamic interactions with an environment (Merali 2006), and their adaptive behaviors are characterized by self-organization and emergence (Merali 2006; Vessey and Ward 2013). Selforganization refers to the ability to generate new internal structures and forms of behavior owing to a system actors spontaneous restructuring of relationships with neighboring actors (Axelrod and Cohen 1999; Benbya and McKelvey 2006; Merali 2006). Self-organization brings about the emergent behavior of a system as a whole (Merali 2006). Emergence refers to the notion that a system’s overall state results from individual actors’ local actions, their complex interactions, and constant changes in their modes of interaction over time (Merali 2006; Vessey and Ward 2013). Emergence renders the outcomes of local actors’ interactions unpredictable (Anderson 1999; Dooley 1997; Holland 1995).

To understand self-organization and emergence behavior in CAS, we focus on their constituent components, namely agents, interactions, and environment (Drazin and Sandelands 1992; Holland 1995; Nan 2011) that underlie such behavior. Agents are people (individuals, groups, or coalitions of groups), organizations, objects, or concepts that share certain attributes and behavioral rules. Interactions reflect mutually adaptive behaviors by agents, realized through connections between and resource flows among agents. The environment mediates interactions among agents; that is, environmental properties provide the conditions for interactions among agents.

In an organizational context, institutional properties define the conditions for interactions among agents, so that these institutional properties affect and can be affected by the interactions among agents (Orlikowski 1992; Orlikowski and Robey 1991; Orlikowski et al. 1995), and that the behavior of CAS can be explained by these institutional properties (Anderson 1999; Holland 1995; Lyytinen and Newman 2008; McLeod and Doolin 2012). To conceptualize the institutional properties prevalent in the behavior of CAS, we employed institutional theory as a theoretical lens.

The key assumption of institutional theory is that organizations are social constructions that constantly seek to gain legitimacy in their social contexts. To survive, organizations must adhere to the rules and beliefs that prevail in their environment (DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Meyer and Rowan 1977). The main discourse in institutional theory is based on institutional pressures, as outlined by DiMaggio and Powell (1983) and later adapted by Scott (2014). As mechanisms of institutional isomorphism, institutional pressures include coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures. Coercive pressures are forces that constrain and regularize behavior. Normative pressures are forced by organizational counterparts that introduce an obligatory dimension into social life via values and norms, where values are the desired conceptions to which existing behaviors can be compared, and norms are the legiti mate means to pursue these values. Finally, mimetic pressures create the frames through which shared conceptions are made. Mimetic pressures are evident when actors encounter uncertain causes and solutions in which they unconsciously model themselves on other, successful actors.

Scholars have traditionally employed institutional theory to study a category or network of organizations (the interorganizational level). Thus, institutional pressures represented exogenous factors in an organization’s environment that dictate the rules and beliefs of an institutional arena to the organization.

Initially, Zucker (1987) distinguished between interorganizational and intra-organizational levels of theorization and discussed two theoretical approaches in institutional theory: the environment as an institution and the organization as an institution. The latter initiated a considerable discourse in institutional studies that argued for the necessity of considering endogenous institutional pressures (e.g., Greenwood and Hinings 1996; Pache and Santos 2010; Powell and Colyvas 2008; Souitaris et al. 2012). In effect, organizations are micro-institutions that involve formal structures and procedures to achieve organizational goals. Organizational actors (e.g., departments and units) operate and interact under intra-organizational coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures that align their behaviors to create enduring social structures and systems (Elsbach 2002; van Dijk et al. 2011). In line with recent institutional studies (e.g., Kondra and Hurst 2009; van Dijk et al. 2011; Vermeulen et al. 2007), we focus on endogenous institutional pressures since they represent the internal dynamics of an organization in response to environmental pressures. ## Research Method

Scholars have acknowledged the potential of simulation-based research in advancing organizational (Burton and Obel 2011; Davis et al. 2007; Fioretti 2013; Harrison et al. 2007) and IS research (Beese et al. 2019; Spagnoletti et al. 2018; Zhang and Gable 2014). Simulation experiments are particularly useful for investigating complex systems since they exhibit nonlinear behavior and their emergent properties unfold over time and in various levels of analysis (Davis et al. 2007; Harrison et al. 2007). Because of our theoretical assumptions, specifically CAS, this study lends itself to an agent-based simulation approach. Agent-based modeling allows us to describe interactions among agents, to observe the behaviors that arise from these interactions in their environment (Bonabeau 2002; Curºeu 2006; Fioretti 2013; Hedström and Manzo 2015), and to conduct the what-if analyses (Nan and Tanriverdi 2017) that are needed to answer our research question.

## Development of the Simulation Model

The simulation model is informed by our synthesized theoretical foundation (Table 2). Table 3 summarizes the simulation model’s constituent components in relation to its theoretical foundation.

Agents: The simulation model comprises two types of agents: actors (learning- and utility-based agents) and IT applications (reflex agents). In our simulation model, an actor represents a group of people (a local project or an operating unit) that is capable of undertaking autonomous actions to meet its design objectives (Jennings 2000). This type of agent is a utility-based agent, as it is rational and chooses actions to maximize its utility function (Russel and Norvig 2009). An IT application represents an IT artifact that actors build, use, and maintain to execute opportunities and to fulfill their business tasks (Orlikowski and Robey 1991). This type of agent is a reflex agent, as its behavior is defined by a set of condition–action rules, depending on its designer (here, actors) (Russel and Norvig 2009). Our simulation model represents actors by their attributes and behavioral rules (Holland 1995; Nan 2011).

Interactions: The simulation model includes interactions among actors and among actors and IT applications (Orlikowski 1992; Orlikowski and Robey 1991; Orlikowski et al. 1995). These interactions are specified via connections between and resource flows among agents, which explain agents’ adaptive behaviors in adjusting their attributes (Holland 1995; Nan 2011). The actor–actor connection is formed by organizational networks that influence resource flows among connected actors (Lancichinetti et al. 2008; Leonardi 2015). In the actor–IT connection, actors build or use IT applications to execute opportunities.

Environment: The simulation model includes both an actor’s environment and the organization’s environment. An actor’s environment comprises endogenous coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures that define the conditions for the actors actions and interactions. Coercive pressure refers to the same enterprise-wide rules that are enforced on all actors. Rules reflect the shared needs of all actors and prevent local actors from building IT applications that are similar to those already built by other (connected and non-connected) local actors. Normative pressure refers to the norms and values of the connected actors, which reflect the shared needs of the closely connected actors who share needs and are informed about available IT applications in their actor–actor network. Mimetic pressure refers to changes in actors’ attributes and actions that reflect the attributes of the closely connected successful actors.

The simulation model also includes dynamism in an organization’s environment. Building on Davis et al.’s (2009) model, we modeled environmental dynamism as a flow of heterogeneous opportunities. To capture heterogeneity, each opportunity includes four dimensions of environmental dynamism: velocity, complexity, ambiguity, and unpredictability (Davis et al. 2009). Technical details about the simulation model’s agents, interactions, and environment are provided in Appendix A.

In the simulation model, local actors are exposed to opportunities from the organization’s environment (Davis et al. 2009). To capture opportunities, local actors compare the features the opportunities require with existing IT applications’ available features. In seeking existing IT applications, how actors behave (the use decision) depends on the level of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures in their environment. While coercive pressure forces actors to use any existing IT application in the organization that matches the opportunity (instead of building a new one), normative pressure encourages them to use known existing IT applications in their network, and mimetic pressure encourages them to use the same IT applications closely connected successful actors have used. The same procedure holds when actors find no match between an opportunity’s features and those of existing IT applications, and make a build decision with an associated cost: Actors build IT applications in adherence with the shared needs of all actors in the organization (coercive pressure), the shared needs of closely connected actors (normative pressure), and similar to IT applications closely connected successful actors have built (mimetic pressure). Thus, local actors’ use or build decisions, made under coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures, determine how an IS architecture evolves in an organization. Once an opportunity is executed (by either using an existing IT application or building a new one), a randomly associated payoff value will be obtained (Davis et al. 2009).

The objective of the simulation model is to evaluate IS architecture’s evolution against its expected outcomes. We include efficiency and flexibility as two key outcomes (Lange et al.

2016; Schmidt and Buxmann 2011). Efficiency refers to an organization’s ability to provide its actors with the IT applications required to capture and execute opportunities while minimizing the redundancies of IT applications (Schmidt and Buxmann 2011). Flexibility concerns an organization’s ability to adapt its IT application landscape quickly to meet the new requirements that new opportunities demand (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011; Vessey and Ward 2013). Our simulation model captures efficiency as the sum of payoffs from all executed opportunities (Davis et al. 2009) divided by the sum of operations costs of all IT applications and captures flexibility as the ratio of executed opportunities against all opportunities to which the organization was exposed from its environment.

## Validation of the Simulation Model

Before conducting simulation experiments to answer the research question, we validated and verified the simulation model (Axtell et al. 1996; Boero and Squazzoni 2005; Burton and Obel 2011; Sargent 2005). Similar to existing agentbased simulation studies in IS (e.g., Nan and Tanriverdi 2017), our verification and validation efforts focused on two areas: First, we built our simulation model on simulation models that other studies have already validated. Specifically, our model built on Davis et al.’s (2009) model by capturing environmental dynamism and its constituent dimensions and by reflecting how an organization reacts to its dynamic environment. We also modeled the organizational network following Lancichinetti et al.’s (2008) so-called LFR benchmark. Second, we tested our simulation model’s reliability through internal validity tests to demonstrate that the simulation is consistent across multiple simulation runs. We also conducted degeneracy tests to evaluate the parameter ranges (boundaries) in which the simulation model is assumed to be valid. Finally, we carried out parameter variability–sensitivity analyses to show that the effects of variations in the simulation model’s input parameters (coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures) on its targeted outputs (efficiency and flexibility) are meaningful. These efforts to verify and validate the model are explained further in Appendix B.

We conducted seven simulation experiments to answer the research question (Table 4). In the first set of three experiments (one per institutional pressure), we incrementally changed the level of one institutional pressure while holding the other two pressures constant at a moderate level. In the second set of three experiments (one per two-way effect), we varied two pressures while holding the third pressure constant at a moderate level. In the final experiment, we varied all three pressure levels. The first set of experiments provided data on the individual effects of each institutional pressure, and the second set of experiments and the final experiment provided data on the pressures’ two-way and three-way interaction effects, respectively. In all experiments, we assigned random values to the dimensions of environmental dynamism—velocity, complexity, ambiguity, and unpredictability—in their specified boundaries to capture the diverse levels of dynamism in the organizations’ environment.

## Simulation Results

For the examined relationships in experiments, we conducted both linear and nonlinear regression analyses. Since nonlinear models (compared to linear models) explain a significantly greater proportion of the observed variance in efficiency and flexibility,<sup>2</sup> we present the results of the polynomial regression analyses (using quadratic or cubic terms). This section presents the results of the statistical analyses, and the next section puts the resulting insights into context and discusses their implications for both practice and research.

## Institutional Pressures’ Joint Effects

Figures 8 to 13 plot three-dimensional graphs for the two-way interactions of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures on efficiency and flexibility outcomes. The regression analyses (Table 8) show that the interaction between coercive and normative pressures is statistically significant in relation to both efficiency $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and flexibility (â = $0 . 9 9 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ outcomes. This indicates that coercive pressure’s impact on both efficiency and flexibility differs at different levels of normative pressure. As shown in Figure 8 and Figure 9, both efficiency and flexibility outcomes vary considerably differently to changes in coercive pressure when comparing organizations with low normative pressure to organizations with high normative pressure. In organizations that have high normative pressure, coercive pressure’s impact on efficiency and flexibility is less pronounced than its impact in organizations that have low normative pressure. High coercive pressure in organizations that have low normative pressure leads to the lowest efficiency and flexibility. Therefore, to ensure high levels of both efficiency and flexibility, coercive pressure should be imposed based on the level of normative pressure.

## Discussion

This study investigates the evolution of IS architecture as a function of institutional pressures’ levels. We have developed a theory-informed simulation model that examines how differently IS architecture evolves and what different outcomes are expected under different configurations of institutional pressures. The experiments conducted through this simulation model demonstrate the significance of coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures and their interplays in the evolution of IS architecture and in obtaining IS architecture’s outcomes (i.e., efficiency and flexibility). Building on the theoretical insights from the simulation experiments and because of the IS architecture discipline’s practical nature, we discuss the study’s implications for both practice and research. Before discussing the implications of our research, it is important to examine some of its limitations and the boundary conditions of its theoretical insights.

First, similar to other simulation models, our model focuses on selected aspects of a phenomenon, resulting in computational representations that simplify real-world instances (Davis et al. 2007). This simplification applies particularly in agent-based models that involve representations of human actors who, in the real-world, may undertake irrational behaviors, make subjective choices, and have complex psychology (Bonabeau 2002). Second, our theoretical insights should be interpreted with care since our simulation experiments cover limited ranges of the four dimensions of environmental dynamism (velocity, complexity, ambiguity, and unpredictability). In assigning random values within the defined boundaries to our model, we focused on only low levels of ambiguity, avoided very low or very high levels of unpredictability and velocity, and set complexity to a moderate level. These choices for the four dimensions of environmental dynamism are bounded to our experiments’ settings because of experiments’ primary focus on various ranges of institutional pressures (rather than on various ranges of environmental dynamism) and to ensure that the simulation does not degenerate in these specific settings. Third, our simulation model captures only endogenous institutional pressures and does not include exogenous institutional pressures (e.g., government regulations, professional ecosystem norms, rival firms’ forces). This choice is due to our study’s focus on organizations’ internal dynamics, but future research can extend our findings by examining the interplay between endogenous and exogenous institutional pressures (e.g., Bitektine and Haack 2015; Gary et al. 2015; Powell and Colyvas 2008) in the evolution of IS phenomena. Fourth, our model of IS architecture’s evolution contributes directly to the EA research field. However, while EA comprises business processes, data, IT applications, and technologies (Ross et al. 2006, pp. 48-49), our simulation model only captures IT applications in the execution of opportunities because of the centrality of IT applications that reside at the intersection of business processes, data, and technologies.

## Implications for Practice

Organizations must provide their operating units with the required IT applications to execute opportunities from the environment at justifiable costs (IS architecture outcome efficiency) and adapt their IT application landscapes to be able to capture new opportunities that emerge in the environment (IS architecture outcome flexibility). Efficiency and flexibility are non-substitutable functions, so that organizations as complex systems need both if they are to survive and thrive in their environment (Adler et al. 1999; Davis et al. 2009). As a result, organizations constantly seek to optimize their efficiency and flexibility by shifting the efficiency–flexibility tradeoff or moving toward an efficiency–flexibility sweet spot with regard to their institutional properties.

Among the endogenous institutional pressures, coercive pressure (e.g., procedures, principles, rules, standards) is somewhat under the direct control of managers because it is formally enforced and deviations are sanctioned in the organization. In contrast, normative and mimetic pressures (e.g., expectations, norms, values, beliefs) tend to be morally and culturally governed and may not be controlled directly by managers. Therefore, we discuss how managers can employ coercive pressure as a lever to achieve desirable efficiency and flexibility outcomes.

Our results draw practitioners’ attention to the cautious exploitation of coercive pressure. That is, while coercive pressure is necessary in obtaining efficiency and flexibility outcomes, it may also create considerable collateral damage. This study demonstrates that imposing high coercive pressure in IS architecture endeavors does not necessarily sustain efficiency and flexibility outcomes, while low to moderate levels of coercive pressure are useful in many situations and essential in some.

Our results demonstrate that a lack of coercive pressure leads to low efficiency in obtaining expected payoffs from executing opportunities and low flexibility in capturing new opportunities (Figure 2 and Figure 3). When they lack principles and standards, local operating units (e.g., project teams, departments, subsidiaries) spontaneously build new IT applications based on their local needs without considering enterprise-wide needs or existing IT applications, and they instantaneously address opportunities that occur in the environment. This leads to redundancies in IT applications (low efficiency) and that the continuous building of heterogeneous IT applications over time brings about an IT landscape that is not sufficiently flexible to meet new opportunities’ requirements (low flexibility). Similarly, when excessively strict procedures are in place that enforce specific principles and standards in every design decision (high coercive pressure), the organization forces local operating units to prioritize enterprise-wide needs over their own, which leads to the risk of building IT applications that are needed by the organization as a whole but less by local operating units to fulfill their specific needs (low efficiency; Figure 2). High coercive pressure also restricts local operating units’ autonomy, which decreases the likelihood of promptly capturing of new opportunities (low flexibility; Figure 3). Therefore, coercive pressure should be imposed carefully and purposefully, particularly with regard to the normative and mimetic pressure levels, as we discuss in the following.

In organizations that lack consensus and communication among local units, expectations and valued ends are not clear (low normative pressure). In such organizations, local units have the freedom to execute opportunities that are related to their own needs and competences, and thus obtaining the payoffs that are associated with these opportunities (high efficiency; Figure 4). However, these organizations cannot capture a wide range of opportunities because of the local units’ lack of awareness about existing IT applications in peer units that may be used to capture similar opportunities quickly (low flexibility; Figure 5). Our results indicate that, in this situation, imposing coercive pressure (e.g., setting up centrally enforced approval procedures for IT investment decisions) is not recommended, because coercive mechanisms are detrimental to both flexibility and efficiency in this organizational context (Figure 8 and Figure 9).

In organizations in which local units closely communicate their needs for new or existing IT applications, local units needs and expectations are harmonized (high normative pressure). However, such organizations may capture only similar opportunities and a limited range of opportunities, and thus suffer from low levels of both efficiency and flexibility outcomes (Figure 4 and Figure 5). As Figure 8 and Figure 9 show, imposing coercive pressure up to a moderate level brings about increasing both efficiency and flexibility in this situation. Such organizations should employ coercive mechanisms carefully since high levels of coercive pressure deteriorate efficiency and flexibility. For example, organizations can set up new principles and standards to co-evolve with emerging opportunities in the environment and to guide and encourage design decisions toward capturing new opportunities. Nonetheless, strict enforcement of new principles and standards may result in, for instance, unsuitable IT applications to local units’ needs.

In some organizations, best practices are not recognizable because successful operating units that obtain high payoffs from executing opportunities through building or using specific IT applications are not visible to their peer units (low mimetic pressure). In such organizations, local units execute diverse opportunities by building various fitting IT applications (high efficiency; Figure 6), but they do not learn from one another in building new or reusing existing IT applications, in capturing mainstream opportunities, or in predicting upcoming opportunities (relatively low flexibility; Figure 7). Conversely, in some organizations, there are dominant identities so that local units often tend to imitate the behavior of successful peer units (high mimetic pressure). As the environmental conditions change dynamically, organizational units struggle to meet their performance objectives. To coevolve with the changes in the environment and to increase their performance, local units adopt the same IT applications as successful peer units, assuming that successful peer units have already built or used the right IT applications to capture and execute opportunities. This imitation behavior seems to increase flexibility (Figure 7) in reusing existing IT applications, in capturing prevailing opportunities, and in reacting to environmental turbulence, but it decreases efficiency (Figure 6) by repeatedly using the same IT applications, thus preventing local units from widening the range of opportunities they can capture. In both of these types of organization, imposing coercive pressure up to a moderate level is recommended to increase efficiency or flexibility. However, coercive mechanisms should be employed carefully since high levels of coercive pressure can have a negative effect on the expected outcomes (Figure 10 and Figure 11).

To summarize, coercive pressure is much more under the direct control of managers than normative and mimetic pressures, so if managers are aware of the normative and mimetic pressures levels in their organizations, they can use our simulation results to anticipate how changes in coercive pressure will affect efficiency and flexibility. Still, manipulation of normative and mimetic pressures is of practical interest, although they seem to be beyond the direct control of managers and may take more time to put in place. Inspired by Scott (2014) and Vermeulen et al. (2007), normative pressure can be manipulated to an extent by, for instance, organizing architecture-related events and architecture training sessions and issuing architecture awards. Similarly, mimetic pressure can be manipulated to a degree by, for instance, highlighting architecture success stories and architecture role models. Thus, normative and mimetic pressures can also be employed to steer the evolution of IS architecture to achieve desired outcomes.

Consider, for example, organizations that have low normative pressure and suffer from low flexibility (Figure 5). As discussed earlier, imposing coercive pressure to improve flexibility is not recommended in this organizational context because it would be detrimental to both flexibility and efficiency (Figure 8 and Figure 9), but imposing mimetic pressure would increase flexibility (Figure 13). Therefore, in organizations that have low normative pressure, it is recommended to avoid coercive pressure and instead expose local units to mimetic pressure from their successful peer units. Conversely, in organizations that have high normative pressure and suffer from low efficiency and low flexibility (Figure 4 and Figure 5), although imposing coercive pressure up to a moderate level is recommended (Figure 8 and Figure 9), mimetic pressure does not seem to be impactful (Figure 12 and Figure 13).

## Implications for Research

Our study incorporates environmental dynamism and institutional pressures in the investigation of IS architecture’s evolution and its efficiency and flexibility outcomes. We adopted the dimensions of environmental dynamism from existing research (Davis et al. 2009) that predominantly theorizes a tension and tradeoff between efficiency and flexibility (e.g., Adler et al. 1999; Davis et al. 2009; Eisenhardt et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2012). Our study’s incorporation of institutional pressures extends current conceptualizations by demonstrating an efficiency–flexibility tradeoff, where organizations must trade one for the other, and an efficiency– flexibility sweet spot, where both efficiency and flexibility can be maximized. Our study also sheds light on the mechanisms (e.g., manipulations of the level of coercive pressure) to shift the efficiency–flexibility tradeoff or to move toward an efficiency–flexibility sweet spot. Such mechanisms are discussed for various organizational settings that are characterized by different levels of institutional pressures.

We randomized the level of environmental dynamism dimensions within a limited range in the simulation model. To gain a more complete picture, we call for investigating how IS phenomena (e.g., IS architecture) evolve in various configurations of environmental dynamism that are characterized by different levels and combinations of velocity, complexity, ambiguity, and unpredictability dimensions. In addition, finding the optimal structures for dealing with various levels of environmental dynamism is of both theoretical and practical value. Our simulation model can be used for this purpose and can be extended to include structures. IS research has captured structure in centralized, decentralized, and hybrid IT governance modes (Olson and Chervany 1980). Thus, we encourage researchers to extend our simulation model by investigating optimal IT governance structures for various configurations of environmental dynamism. The optimality of IT governance structures can be measured against these structures’ impacts on efficiency and flexibility and can be discussed in terms of efficiency–flexibility tradeoffs and sweet spots.

Our simulation model inspects IS architecture’s evolution in various types of organization. To take a step further, future research could investigate how to harness or guide the evolution of IS architecture toward a desired state. Such an inquiry would be of considerable value since organizations may follow specific architectural styles or principles (e.g., standardization, modularization, integration), for which they impose purposefully designed interventions to reach a desired state (e.g., modular architecture, standardized IT applications) (Boh and Yellin 2007; Richardson et al. 1990; Ross et al. 2006). The theoretical insights we derived from our simulation model inform these types of inquiries in two ways. First, we discussed and demonstrated how coercive pressure can be used to shift the efficiency–flexibility tradeoff or to move toward an efficiency–flexibility sweet spot, and consequently to guide the evolution of IS architecture to a desired state. Second, we demonstrated that coercive mechanisms effects on an organization’s efficiency and flexibility depend on the organization’s levels of normative and mimetic pressures.

Our study also demonstrated that, similar to coercive pressure, manipulation of normative and mimetic pressures can affect organizations’ efficiency and flexibility. Therefore, it is of both theoretical and practical interest for future research to investigate how organizations can employ all three institutional pressures to reach desired outcomes. Building on these insights, we call for researchers to explain and even design means through which these institutional pressures can be manipulated and applied in organizations. The institutional theory’s body of knowledge refers to these means as carriers, that is, a set of symbolic systems, relational systems, activities, and artifacts to exercise the various types of institutional pressure (Scott 2003; Scott 2014). IS architecture research offers a set of carriers for coercive pressure, such as architecture practices (COBIT 2012), standards (Boh and Yellin 2007), and principles (Richardson et al. 1990), so we encourage researchers to analyze, describe, and design carriers for normative and mimetic pressures. As we called for studying the “guided evolution” of IS architecture, normative and mimetic carriers along with coercive carriers can, therefore, be employed to purposefully guide the evolution of IS architecture. Studying practical IS architecture initiatives through case studies may provide rich insights into how organizations can employ normative and mimetic carriers to guide the evolution of their IS architecture toward desired states.

Because of the distinct theoretical premises of complexity theory, specifically in conceptualizing emergent and selforganizing systems, several calls have been made to employ complexity theory in developing new or advancing extant IS theories (e.g., Merali 2006; Tanriverdi et al. 2010). However, in line with Levy (2000), we argue that complexity theory cannot simply be imported from the natural sciences and applied off-the-shelf to the investigation of IS phenomena. When we examine IS phenomena, we need an organizational explanation for self-organizing systems and their emergent behavior. What we offer to prospective IS research that intends to exploit complexity theory as a theoretical lens is an institutional explanation of the self-organization process. Employing institutional theory, we elaborated on selforganization as a process in which local actors (as units of a complex system) seek to gain legitimacy in response to institutional pressures in their environment. Thus, institutional pressures and their joint effects can be considered as organizational agencies of self-organization and the resulting emergent behavior. This may also explain how institutional pressures are inherent in emergent systems, as they delineate a self-organization process that can render an IS phenomenon complex, yet not chaotic. Therefore, we call for future research to adopt this conceptualization of self-organization and to demonstrate how emergent properties of complex IS phenomena result from the local actors’ process of gaining legitimacy in response to the institutional pressures that prevail in their environment.

## Simulation Model

Agents: Table A1 describes actors’ behavioral rules. In maximizing their utility function and executing opportunities, actors communicate their needs for new IT applications and their knowledge about existing IT applications with other actors, use existing or new IT applications, and build new IT applications (Orlikowski 1992; Orlikowski and Robey 1991; Orlikowski et al. 1995).

Inspired by Davis et al.’s (2009) simulation model, each IT application is operationalized as a ten-elements vector of 1s, 0s, and “?”s (e.g., [011?100??0]) that represent a set of features that characterize the given IT application. Each “?” in this vector represents the potential of actors to improvise when using IT applications. Similar to Davis et al.’s model, each improvised feature (i.e., the “?”s) is realized in this process as either a 1 or 0 with equal (50/50) probability. Therefore, each IT application contains some fixed features (represented by 1s and 0s) and some features that are left open for improvisation (represented by “?”s). Thus, the combination of an IT application’s fixed and improvised features [011?100??0] could produce the vector [0111100010]. This representation of IT applications provides the possibility of adapting an IT application to support various opportunities. In addition, each IT application has an associated operations cost that is defined by the sum of the connected actors and the age of the applications in time steps, such that larger and older IT applications are likely to incur higher costs (Aleatrati Khosroshahi et al. 2016; Mocker 2009).

Interactions: The simulation model includes interactions among agents (reflecting their connections and the flows among them), as described in Table A2. These interactions are represented as graphs in our model—that is, agents are represented as vertices and interactions as edges (Agliari et al. 2006; Newman 2012; Porter et al. 2009).

Environment: Our modeling of the organization’s environment builds on Davis et al.’s model of environmental dynamism. Following Davis et al., we modeled the organization’s environment as a flow of heterogeneous opportunities, each of which is operationalized as a ten-elements vector of 1s and 0s (e.g., [0101101101]). Each element in this vector represents a feature, and the whole vector represents a feature set with which to obtain an opportunity’s associated payoff. Each opportunity has a random, normally distributed payoff value. Specifically, the model captures four dimensions of environmental dynamism:

• Velocity: The arrival rate of new opportunities in the organization’s environment, following a Poisson distribution.

Complexity: The number of an opportunity’s required features that must be matched to capture the given opportunity (i.e., to obtain the associated payoff value). The more complex the environment is, the more difficult it is to obtain the payoff. Since a vector has ten features, complexity may range from 0 to 10.

Ambiguity: The proportion of perceived opportunity features that differ from the required ones. Each opportunity has a required feature set (e.g., [0100100110]) and a misperceived feature set ([0110100110]). In this example, ambiguity = 0.1, as there is one (out of ten) difference between the required features and the perceived features (the bolded feature). Therefore, ambiguity ranges from 0 (everything is as perceived) to 1 (everything is different).

Unpredictability: The probability that any opportunity’s feature will be a 1 or a 0. A disorder computation from mathematical information theory is used for this calculation. Opportunities with required features that have a high probability of 1 or 0 are less unpredictable than opportunities with features that have an equal probability of 1 or 0. Unpredictability ranges from 0 (all 1s or all 0s) to 1 (0s and 1s appear with equal likelihood).

Actors compare the feature sets of existing IT applications with the feature sets of the opportunities to which they are exposed (considering improvisations for “?”s as well as misperceptions that are due to ambiguity). If the number of an IT application’s features that match an opportunity’s required features equals or exceeds the value of the complexity parameter, then the given IT application is used to execute the opportunity, and the payoff value is obtained. If not, actors may choose to build a new IT application to get the associated payoff, whose features will reflect the associated opportunity.

In making the use and build decisions, actors adhere to institutional pressures to gain legitimacy for their decisions. Coercive pressure enforces actors to use any existing IT application in the organization that matches the opportunity instead of building a new one. Normative pressure encourages actors to use known, existing IT applications in their network and to consider the needs of their peers when building a new IT application. Normative pressure differs for different actors as they belong to different networks, and is affected by actors’ interactions (Orlikowski 1992; Orlikowski and Robey 1991; Orlikowski et al. 1995). Actors are perceived to be successful when they accumulate higher payoff values (execute more opportunities) than their peers. Mimetic pressure encourages local actors to use the same IT applications that are built or used by closely connected, successful actors.

Use decision: To execute opportunities, actors match an opportunity’s feature set with those of existing IT applications. Consider the example in Figure A2: The actor is assigned to opportunity [0100010101]. The best known IT application for this opportunity (i.e., the IT application with the lowest number of expected mismatches) is [010?011??1], with only one mismatch (in the seventh feature) and three improvisations (“?”s). However, because of the environment’s ambiguity (= 0.1) parameter, one additional misperceived feature is switched during the realization. Consequently, there are four mismatches, which is still lower than the complexity parameter value (complexity = 6; maximum 4 mismatches allowed), so the opportunity is executed.

Build decision: An actor’s decision to build a new IT application is based on the actor’s perception of the opportunity’s requirements in comparison to available IT applications. Each actor makes these considerations for itself, for other connected actors (normative pressure), and for the overall organization (coercive pressure). Consider, for example, an actor that is connected to only two IT applications, [010?011??1] and [0?0?1010?1], and is currently assigned to the opportunity [1010010100] (Figure A3).

In this process, an actor first calculates how many of its previously assigned opportunities could have been completed by an IT application whose feature set completely matches the current opportunity. All of the actor’s connected actors make the same evaluation (supporting the decision, weighted by the level of normative pressure), and check the availability of similar IT applications throughout the organization (opposing the decision, weighted by the level of coercive pressure). Thus, normative pressure forces an actor to consider not only its own needs but also those of its connected peers, and coercive pressure hinders an actor from building IT applications that are already available elsewhere in the organization. If the overall evaluation exceeds the average expected result, depending on the level of institutional pressures in the given simulation run, the actor decides to build a new IT application; otherwise, the actor rejects the opportunity and takes no further action. Any built application is then connected to the actor and to all other actors that positively supported the build decision.

## Validation of the Simulation Model

We built the simulation model and baseline scenario on relevant simulation models that other studies have already validated. Then we established our simulation’s reliability by demonstrating consistency in the simulation’s internal operations and by testing the simulation model’s boundaries using degeneracy tests.

Development of the simulation model and the baseline scenario: As described in Appendix A, we adapted Davis et al.’s (2009) simulation model to our phenomenon of interest. Further, due to the centrality of agents’ interactions and our agent-based approach to simulation, we added an actor network component based on the recommended parameter settings for the LFR benchmarks (Lancichinetti et al. 2008) to model organizational networks.

The LFR benchmarks (Lancichinetti et al. 2008) are employed to generate realistic community structures in organizational networks.
