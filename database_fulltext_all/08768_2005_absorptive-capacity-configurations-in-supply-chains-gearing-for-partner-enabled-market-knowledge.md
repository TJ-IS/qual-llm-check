## Abstract

The need for continual value innovation is driving supply chains to evolve from a pure transactional focus to leveraging interorganizational partner ships for sharing information and, ultimately, market knowledge creation. Supply chain partners are (1) engaging in interlinked processes that enable rich (broad-ranging, high quality, and privileged) information sharing, and (2) building information technology infrastructures that allow them to process information obtained from their partners to create new knowledge. This study uncovers and examines the variety of supply chain partnership configurations that exist based on differences in capability platforms, reflecting varying processes and information systems. We use the absorptive capacity lens to build a conceptual framework that links these configurations with partner-enabled market knowledge creation. Absorptive capacity refers to the set of organizational routines and processes by which organizations acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit knowledge to produce dynamic organizational capabilities.

Through an exploratory field study conducted in the context of the RosettaNet consortium effort in the IT industry supply chain, we use cluster analysis to uncover and characterize five supply chain partnership configurations (collectors, connectors, crunchers, coercers, and collaborators). We compare their partner-enabled knowledge creation and operational efficiency, as well as the shortcomings in their capability platforms and the nature of information exchange. Through the characterization of each of the configurations, we are able to derive research propositions focused on enterprise absorptive capacity elements. These propositions provide insight into how partner-enabled market knowledge creation and operational efficiency can be affected, and highlight the interconnected roles of coordination information and rich information. The paper concludes by drawing implications for research and practice from the uncovering of these configurations and the resultant research propositions. It also highlights fertile opportunities for advances in research on knowledge management through the study of supply chain contexts and other inter-organizational partnering arrangements.

Keywords: Knowledge management, supply chain, absorptive capacity, interorganizational information systems, configuration approaches, rich information, process modularity

## Introduction

The above quote from an Information Technology industry executive provides a glimpse of the motivation for knowledge sharing efforts underway in supply chains. There is increased focus on the integration of knowledge resources and on knowledge creation in collaboration with partners for longer-term advantage (Majchrzak et al. 2000;

Malhotra et al. 2001). Supply chain relationships are going beyond operational efficiency and are being structured to pursue higher-order goals such as understanding new market dynamics, discovering new partnering arrangements to provide greater customer value, and learning from partners to achieve long-term competitiveness (Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven 1996). Knowledge management is poised to become a critical imperative in supply chain interactions.

Interorganizational relationships have been recognized to provide two distinct potential benefits: short-term operational efficiency and longer-term new knowledge creation (Clark 1989; Dyer 1997; Sobrero and Roberts 2001). Past research has examined interorganizational relationships as a source of long-term learning in the context of research and development joint ventures (Inkpen and Dinur 1998; Lane and Lubatkin 1998), in consortia (Tripsas et al. 1995), and in alliances (Hamel 1991; Roberts and Berry 1985). Research studies have tended to focus either on the supporting technology or the relational aspect in generating insights for improving the effectiveness of IT-supported interorganizational partnerships. The former stream has explored automated IT-based information exchange systems that are deployed between business partners (Bensaou 1997; Choudhury 1997) and studied inhibitors and promoters of adoption of technologies such as electronic data interchange (EDI) (Hart and Saunders 1998). The latter stream has explored relational and governance-related aspects of interorganizational partnerships (Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Subramani 2004). Both streams have largely focused on transactional improvements rather than more strategic market knowledge outcomes. Furthermore, our literature search has not shown studies where strategic knowledge creation is linked to patterns of inter-organizational activities. We seek to pursue this research opportunity by focusing on the understudied context of new knowledge creation among enterprises in a supply chain (i.e., manufacturers, distributors, and retailers) engaged in day-to-day operations around routine processes such as order management.

While earlier studies have studied interorganizational relationships being configured to make a trade-off, maximizing either operational efficiency or strategic knowledge creation (e.g., Sobrero and Roberts 2001), we seek to uncover partnership configurations that can achieve both objectives simultaneously. A supply chain partnership configuration refers to the specific patterns of IT usage, information processing, and process structuring characteristics of a focal enterprise related to its boundary activities interfacing with another enterprise in the supply chain. This study seeks to provide a foundation for theory building that links knowledge creation in supply chains to information systems and business process infrastructures that support effective interorganizational partnerships. We adopt a broader perspective that considers complementarities between internal information processing and externally directed information sharing systems, as well as complementarities between IT infrastructure and business process design.

This study is intended to be exploratory in nature. Our objectives are to understand evolving supply chain partnerships and to help generate theory to inform efforts to better support these partnerships. In the next section, we examine previous work on interorganizational systems and supply chain partnerships. We develop theoretical foundations and research constructs around the concepts of absorptive capacity to inform our exploration. This is followed by an explanation of our research methodology and field context. We then use cluster analysis to uncover and describe the key supply chain partnership configurations. We develop propositions around the nature of information exchange and process management infrastructures in each of these clusters and the consequent impact on operational efficiency and new knowledge creation potential for enterprises in each type of cluster. Finally, we discuss the implications for research and practice in the area of partner-enabled knowledge creation in supply chains.

## Theory Development

## Earlier Research on Inter-organizational Systems

The dramatic reduction in electronic communication costs and ease of computer-to-computer linkages has resulted in opportunities to create new channel structures, fueling interest in interorganizational systems (Clemons et al. 1992; Malone and Rockart 1993; Scott Morton 1991). Interorganizational systems (IOSs) are planned and managed ventures to develop and use IT-based and/or human-based information exchange systems to support collaboration and strategic alliances between otherwise independent actors (Kumar and van Dissel 1996). These systems are motivated by the desire to reduce supply chain uncertainty and transaction costs (Konsynski 1993; Kumar and Crook 1999), increase resource utilization (Clemons et al. 1992), and diffuse products and services into new markets (Kumar and van Dissel 1996). IOSs allow for the exchange of information between partners for the purpose of coordination, communication, and cooperation (Premkumar 2000). Thus, IOSs have the potential to increase operational efficiency through the reduction of production and coordination costs. In addition, IOSs can also increase information availability and processing capabilities (Premkumar 2000).

Studies of IOS have contributed to the identification of archetypical configurations for better understanding interorganizational systems and relationships. Table 1 presents a synopsis of three studies of IOS that have proposed such typologies. Each of these studies raised important issues, which, when combined with insights gained during our initial fieldwork, motivated this study.

The Kumar and van Dissel (1996) study raises the issue: Can enterprises in a relationship deploy hybrid IOSs that are a mix of elements of the value/supply chain IOS and networked IOS? If this is possible, then what are the different configurations that emerge from various combinations of these elements? Choudhury's (1997) study provides anecdotal evidence which suggests that electronic monopolies can result when partners exchange rich information. His study leads to the question: How is the knowledge-creating potential of firms involved in the electronic monopoly configuration affected by rich information exchange, and is such an exchange possible in other configurations? Bensaou and Venkatraman's (1995) study suggests that enterprises in industries faced with a highly uncertain and complex environment have high information processing needs. They found that two partnership configurations—electronic interdependence and mutual adjustment (Table 1)—helped enterprises cope with such environments. Hence, the majority of the supply chain enterprises in the IT industry (the context of this study) should also be expected to engage in either electronic interdependence or mutual adjustment partnership configurations. However, in our initial fieldwork, we observed a variety of partnership configurations with different characteristics. This raises the question: Are enterprises organizing their supply chain partnerships for higher order learning goals that are not reflected in the "information processing to reduce uncertainty" perspective?

When discussing various partnership configurations, Bensaou and Venkatraman (1995) suggest that rich information exchange between partners is influenced by the choice of mechanisms used to structure their relationship. Further, rich information exchange potentially impacts the operational performance of the partnership. Specifically, they focus on the breadth and frequency dimensions of rich information exchange. These two dimensions may be sufficient to understand operational performance impact, but in order to understand the corresponding impact on knowledge creation more dimensions of richness need to be explicitly addressed.

## Earlier Research on Structuring Supply Chain Processes

The IOS studies have also addressed issues pertaining to the structuring of supply chain processes. Kumar and van Dissel (1996) hint at the structuring of interorganizational relationships, such that work is divided between partnering organizations by assigning them specific roles, and coordination between them is embedded in standard and well-specified protocols. Bensaou and Venkatraman (1995) also refer to the structuring of interorganizational processes in a way that creates mutual interdependence. Thus, partners engaged in structural relationships break supply chain activities into manageable and well-understood steps and procedures. This raises the question: What is the impact of modular process design on richness of information exchange and consequently on knowledge creation?

Studies of process structuring standards and operational efficiency in supply chains also provide some groundwork for this study. Information sharing in supply chains is considered a major obstacle to achieving operational efficiency (Lee et al. 1997). Research on dynamic supply chains shows that enterprises in supply chains adapt to their environment and adopt innovations by exploiting the complementarities between process design and information technologies (Whang 1998). Adoption of new information exchange and process linkage standards based on the extended markup language (XML) is also expected to improve information flows between partners and reduce coordination misalignments across the supply chain (Gosain et al. 2003). Such standards have the potential to overcome the reach versus richness trade-off (Evans and Wurster 1999) by allowing enterprises to link with a larger number of business partners and exchange rich information that goes beyond day-to-day transaction-related information. Further, joint activities and process redesign surrounding adoption of these new standards increases the potential for inter-organizational collaboration (Gosain et al. 2004; Premkumar 2000).

These studies point to the need for exploring patterns in (1) how enterprises in supply chains redesign their existing relationships based on the adoption of new standards, (2) relationship configurations that emerge as enterprises adopt these new standards, redesign their interorganizational processes, and develop and use sophisticated internal IT-based systems, and (3) the ability of relationship configurations to achieve both efficiency of operations and create new market knowledge. We draw upon the absorptive capacity view to outline a framework that enables us to address these issues.

## The Absorptive Capacity Framework

Zahra and George (2002a) conceptualize absorptive capacity as a dynamic capability pertaining to knowledge creation and utilization that enhances a firm's ability to gain and sustain competitive advantage. According to them, absorptive capacity consists of acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation capabilities. However, in their conceptualization, the focus is on the individual firms' internal capabilities without consideration of the external sources (such as supply chain partners) from which information may be acquired. Hence, we complement the absorptive capacity perspective with the relational view of the firm (Dyer and Singh 1998) to derive a set of attributes that influence the absorptive capacity of an enterprise in supply chain partnership contexts. These attributes, taken together, form the basis of a capability platform that enhances the potential of an enterprise to share information with its supply chain partners and create new knowledge (Figure 1).

The relational view focuses on interorganizational “routines and processes as an important unit of analysis for understanding competitive advantage” (Dyer and Singh 1998, p. 661). This view especially emphasizes information-sharing routines, relation-specific assets (with appropriate safeguards), and effective governance (with informal and self-enforced mechanisms) for deriving advantage through partnerships. The configurational perspective that forms the basis of our methodological approach also suggests that information systems and organizational routines are important elements in determining the quality of organizational configurations (Miller 1993). In addition, the absorptive capacity view suggests that knowledge creating absorptive capacity is an IT-driven capability (Zahra and George 2002b). Accordingly, we focus on routines, processes, and information systems directed at interorganizational interfaces to uncover supply chain partnership configurations and understand differences between them in terms of their absorptive capacity capabilities. We argue that integrative inter-organizational process mechanisms between an enterprise and its supply chain partners can influence absorptive capacity by enabling better acquisition and assimilation of information. Further, the partner interface-directed information systems that an enterprise develops to complement its interorganizational process mechanisms can have a positive impact on the enterprise's absorptive capacity by enabling better assimilation and transformation of information.

In summary, the choice of specific attributes for assessing capability platforms in this study is based on theoretical as well as pragmatic considerations. These attributes focus on partner-related routines and processes as suggested by the relational view of the firm. The attributes also comprehensively cover the dimensions proposed in the recent conceptualization of absorptive capacity capabilities. From a pragmatic information systems perspective, the IT-related attributes have clear implications for information systems design. The attributes associated with the capability platform are examined in greater detail in the next section. We also use the absorptive capacity lens to establish their potential impact on operational efficiency and knowledge creation potential by enabling the acquisition, assimilation, and transformation of rich information between supply chain partners.

## Building Absorptive Capacity in Supply Chain Partnerships

Enterprises recognize that their competitive advantage is derived from knowledge resources embedded in social relationships with other enterprises (Koka and Prescott 2002; Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). To effectively leverage these resources, enterprises have to develop the ability to absorb information (Cohen and Levinthal 1990) and combine it with internal information to develop new knowledge (Henderson and Cockburn 1994).

![](/api/attachments/APCXB5AJ/fulltext/images/c70db32864a4f28ecd7f70c20b6e12facf793bc094f0762db0dea52f198611cd.jpg)

Several studies have examined absorptive capacity as a predictor of organizational learning outcomes (e.g., Mowery et al. 1996; Szulanski 1996). Research, initially focused on an organization's internal determinants of absorptive capacity (Pennings and Harianto 1992), has expanded to interorganizational settings (Lane and Lubatkin 1998). However, these studies have focused on issues such as R&D expenditures and innovation related to managerial and organizing practices. The process and IT infrastructure that influence absorptive capacity have not been examined.

This study explores how enterprises in supply chain partnerships configure their processes and IT infrastructures to build absorptive capacity to acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit information resources (Figure 1).

We have identified two distinct groups of constructs that represent the elements of an absorptive capacity enhancing capability platform for enterprises engaged in supply chain relationships: integrative interorganizational process mechanisms and partner interface-directed information systems. Another group of constructs mediates this influence: the nature of information exchanged between enterprises and their supply chain partners. We also present a group of constructs that represent the exploitation element of absorptive capacity: operational efficiency of business activities with partners and partner-enabled market knowledge creation. The groupings of constructs and their definitions are presented in Table 2.

## Construct Group #1: Integrative Inter-organizational Process Mechanisms: Enabling Acquisition and Assimilation

Integrative mechanisms play an important role in impacting the efficiency and effectiveness of knowledge exchange (Zahra and George 2002a). These mechanisms serve to reduce the structural, cognitive, and structural barriers that impede knowledge sharing (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). Interorganizational relationships manifested as social attachments tend to increase the absorptive capacity of enterprises by reducing cognitive costs and effort (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). Enterprises engaged in such interorganizational relationships understand each other's information requirements and attempt to provide information based on their partner's needs. This reduces the receiving enterprise's effort and costs required to adapt the acquired information in order to suit its requirements. Past studies suggest that integrative mechanisms between partners deserve attention when studying interorganizational learning (Cyert and March 1963; Levinthal and March 1991; Nelson and Winter 1982). Complex processes can be decomposed into subtasks and coordinated by the actors such that they can connect sufficient know-how to meet situational demands (Weick and Roberts 1993). Such structuring of tasks can increase the information processing capabilities of the whole system (Tushman and Nadler 1978). We focus on three integrative mechanisms that are relevant to interorganizational settings.

Joint Decision Making: Social interaction and collaborative mechanisms are instrumental in the free flow of information between parties (Sheremata 2000). Joint decision making is one such mechanism through which partner enterprises collaboratively make decisions related to the factors that influence their interlinked processes. This mechanism provides an avenue for the sharing of richer information through socialization and articulation (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). Joint decision making reduces the need for explicit exchange of coordination and synchronization information (Austin et al. 1997), and can extend to areas such as joint marketing programs, strategic planning, pricing, sharing of technical skills, demand development, and new market creation (Austin et al. 1997; Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995). Enterprises engaged in joint decision making are able to develop a deeper understanding of the needs of their partners, which results in richer communication between them. This in turn increases their awareness of the environment (Van de Ven 1976). In essence, joint decision making between enterprises enhances information acquisition and assimilation capacity.

Interorganizational Process Modularity: In order to achieve efficiencies when integrating information from external sources, the amount of coordination communication needs to be economized while richer information exchange needs to be maximized (Grant 1996). Modularity is a principle of structuring interorganizational processes to minimize the amount of coordination communication and to maximize rich information exchange. Table 3 shows the analogy between the concept of supply chain process modularity and the concept of product modularity, which is based on Simon's (1972) ideas of breaking complex systems into subsystems and Williamson's (1985) principle of hierarchical decomposition. The modularity of interorganizational processes can be defined as the breaking up of complex processes into subprocesses (activities) that are performed by different enterprises independently (such that subprocesses occur simultaneously) with specified outputs managed through embedded coordination enabled by standardized process interfaces and information exchange (Grant 1996; Sanchez and Mahoney 1996; von Hippel 1998).

Enterprises executing subprocesses in the modular supply chain process architecture have the freedom to experiment with their part of the process as long as they stay within the parameters of the output required from them (Sanchez 1996). This modular knowledge architecture helps enterprises focus on their subprocesses while maintaining an understanding of how the subprocess interrelates with the overall supply chain process. Thus, enterprises are better able to recognize the holes in their knowledge and identify external sources for obtaining information to improve their own capabilities.

Standard Electronic Business Interfaces: In our field study, we found the IT infrastructures of industry supply chains to be in a state of flux. While traditional electronic data interchange (EDI) was widely used, newer XML-based standard electronic business interfaces were being deployed between supply chain partners as well. These standards—labeled as partner interface process specifications—go beyond traditional EDI standards in that they allow real-time information exchange and application integration to suit modular process design (Gosain et al. 2003). Such standards reduce the need to reprocess information received from diverse partners. Prespecified formats make it easy to interpret and manipulate data, which enhances the acquisition and assimilation capabilities of the receiving enterprise. In addition, these standards specify how activities being performed by individual enterprises, as a part of a supply chain process, should be choreographed using the information exchanged. Thus, standardized interfaces govern both the scope and processing of the information exchanged.

Further, interorganizational process and information exchange standards that support modular processes are the foundation of embedded coordination. Deployment of these standards frees enterprises from managing the coordination process, and provides them latitude to focus on rich, higher value-adding information exchange (Koka and Prescott 2002) rather than coordination (“statusing”) information. This reduction in cognitive load effectively increases the assimilation capabilities of the enterprises engaged in these partnerships.

## Construct Group #2: Partner Interface-Directed Information Systems: Enabling Assimilation and Transformation

Dynamic capabilities required to achieve superior performance are increasingly derived through the use of information systems to leverage knowledge assets (Gold et al. 2001; Sambamurthy 2000). While past studies have examined how inter-organizational information systems are used to coordinate information flows between partners (Bensaou 1997; Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Choudhary 1997), we shift the focus to partner interface-directed information systems that enable an enterprise to process information collected from its supply chain partners so as to create new knowledge. Such systems compare incoming information with existing insights, merge information or replace existing insights, and help in the generation of new insights by integrating or synthesizing information (Alavi and Leidner 2001; El Sawy and Pauchant 1988; Pentland 1995). They also provide support for interpreting information based on task characteristics (Becerra-Fernandez and Sabherwal 2001) and individual cognitive styles (Markus 2001), resulting in an increase of the absorptive capacity of an enterprise. We propose two types of information systems: one that enhances the ability of an enterprise to absorb (through capture and retention) and another that enables an enterprise to digest (through processing) information received from supply chain partners to create new knowledge.

Memory Systems for Interorganizational Activities: Organizational memory is the means by which knowledge from the past can be utilized to understand and influence current activities (Stein and Zwass 1995). Information systems that support organizational memory store information from past activities and outcomes (El Sawy et al. 1986; Walsh and Ungson 1991). Research suggests that supporting organizational memory through information systems plays a significant role in the creation of new knowledge (Huber 1991; Walsh and Ungson 1991). Memory systems can manifest themselves as databases that store and enable retrieval of the history of events related to the formal interactions or informal information exchanges with business partners, both in terms of the processes that ensued as well as the outcomes (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1994). Such systems allow enterprises to bring previously stored information to bear on information received from external sources in order to create new knowledge (Henderson et al. 1998) as well as create operational efficiencies (Hult et al. 2004).

Interpretation Systems for Interorganizational Information: Employees and teams within enterprises are conduits for knowledge creation and sharing. Their diverse cognitive needs require the use of information systems that are able to represent information in multiple ways—from global to minute—and allow dynamic configuration of interpretation (Boland et al. 1994; Jarvenpaa and Ives 1994). Use of IT-based interpretation systems enables information obtained from supply chain partners to be organized, rearranged, and processed to create new knowledge. A manifestation of such a system is data analysis and mining software that can help uncover patterns in data and enable insights to be generated by processing large quantities of raw data. IT-based interpretation systems have been shown to enable more effective knowledge creation (Cooper et al. 2000) and generation of insights that lead to improved performance outcomes (Grecich 2000; Goodhue et al. 2002).

## Construct Group #3: Rich Information Exchange: Mediating Absorptive Capacity Outcomes

Understanding the impact of absorptive capacity-building interorganizational process mechanisms and partner interface-directed information systems requires a detailed analysis of the nature of information exchange which they enable. Learning based on the sharing of information with partner enterprises is critical to developing competitive advantage and often leads to significant innovations (Levinson and Asahi 1996; March and Simon 1958; Powell et al. 1996; von Hippel 1998). Making sense of the competitive space, knowing how to perform new roles, and developing new capabilities requires the flow of broad ranging and high quality information between partners. Such information exchange triggers knowledge that is not easily imitated and thus becomes a source of competitive advantage (Barney 1991; Zander and Kogut 1995). We propose that the following dimensions of information sharing are salient to absorptive capacity.

Extent of Coordination Information Exchange: Information meant for coordination in demand-side supply chains refers to synchronization information, such as inventory status, logistics status, and production status (Lee et al. 1997). This information does not necessarily help enterprises understand market dynamics or formulate strategic plans. Many enterprises believe that increased coordination information exchange will improve supply chain performance in terms of operational efficiency. This may be true to some extent, but beyond a certain point, exchange of coordination information can overwhelm enterprise cognitive capacity, leading to information overload for enterprises involved in exchange (Van de Ven 1976). This can hinder enterprises from using more strategic information for making sense of their environment. Hence, enterprises need to architect interorganizational processes to manage the need for coordination information exchange.

Breadth of Information Exchange: A major ingredient of knowledge creation is access to a wide range of information (Child and Faulkner 1998). Hypercompetitive business environments require enterprises to deliver offerings at a faster pace, gauge market trends in real-time, and be ready to reinvent their role in value creation networks. This requires enterprises to exchange and assimilate a broad range of information from external sources (Gries and Kasarda 1997). Studies have elaborated on supply chain efforts to share information related to process improvements, strategic directions, market conditions, and new capabilities (Anand et al. 1998; Austin et al. 1997; Fites 1996). Enterprises are making an effort to go beyond the exchange of day-to-day operational information with partners by sharing long-term information such as market trends, changes in customer preferences, new product introductions, and future product plans.

Quality of Information Exchange: Although it is beneficial for partners to exchange a broad range of information, the quality of information exchange is equally important. Changes in the environment create signals that enterprises may need to heed to in order to survive (Dill 1962). Unfortunately some signals are weak, many are confusing, and others are spurious (Choo 1998). Thus, from a cognitive perspective, enterprises are better off as the quality of information they receive increases. In order to ensure efficiency of the assimilation of information, exchange partners have to reduce the need to filter incoming information. The more they expect each other to provide quality information, the more they are able to focus on leveraging the information received from each other to create new knowledge.

Privileged Information Exchange: The value of information exchange depends on how specific the information is to the needs of the receiving partner involved in the exchange. Arm's length relationships may promote exploitative learning through the collection and reporting of standard information that enables enterprises to enhance their current competencies and processes. However, such relationships do not afford enterprises the capability to innovate through the restructuring of their competencies and processes (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). Innovations through interorganizational relationships occur when the commercial transactions are embedded in social attachment that allows for the transfer of idiosyncratic and privileged information (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). Such information can be related to an enterprise's strategy, distinctive competencies, undocumented product capabilities, critical customer or supplier dependencies, etc.

Most of the information conventionally exchanged in supply chains is very generic, point-of-sale information or inventory availability information that is tied to operational processes and provides little insight for strategic redirection. On the other hand, privileged information is specific to the receiving partner in terms of it being proprietary and confidential. It provides the partner a unique perspective not available otherwise. Privileged information is also easier to assimilate as it is tailored to the cognitive needs of the partner.

## Construct Group #4: Absorptive Capacity Outcomes

Operational Efficiency: The focus of developing processes and IT for information sharing and processing is to make supply chains more efficient: cutting down costs and increasing the speed with which the product moves from production to the customers' doorstep (Wilder 1999). Information sharing obstacles can lead to dysfunctional behavior in the supply chain and building up of inventory (Lee et al. 1997). The focus of a majority of supply chain initiatives has been the improvement of information flow design between supply chain partners to unlock the potential efficiency gains (Lee et al. 1997).

Partner-Enabled Market Knowledge Creation: A higher order impact of the acquisition, assimilation, and transformational elements of absorptive capacity is partner-enabled market knowledge creation, which is defined as the ability of business enterprises to increase their knowledge base in conjunction with business partners. Zahra and George (2002a) use the term realized absorptive capacity to refer to an enterprise's capacity to leverage knowledge that has been absorbed. The new knowledge that is created through assimilation and transformation has an impact only when it is incorporated into the enterprise's way of doing business and enables innovation and strategic flexibility (Barney 1991).

The impact of partner-enabled knowledge creation can be observed in the form of new products and services (products and services are embodiments of knowledge), new categories of products and services (which come from understanding the competition and market needs), new ways to make, assemble, or deliver products and services (process innovation), new ways to organize to make, assemble, or deliver products (architectural or organizational innovation), and, in extreme cases, creation of new industries—category killers, again a function of market understanding (Rogers 1995). Enterprises that have honed their absorptive capacity exploitation capabilities are likely to be more adept at continually revamping their knowledge stock by spotting trends in their external environment and responding to these trends (Zahra and George 2002a).

In this study, we seek to explore how enterprises engaged in supply chain partnerships configure the elements of absorptive capacity (acquisition, assimilation, and transformation) enhancing capability platform, i.e., interorganizational process mechanisms and partner interface-directed information systems. We also seek to understand how the various supply chain partnership configurations differ in knowledge creation and operational efficiency—the exploitation element of absorptive capacity. In the next section, we describe the research methodology and field context used to explore the configurations.

## Research Methodology and Field Context

## Field Context and Data Collection

This research study was conducted in the context of the RosettaNet B2B initiative (www.rosettanet.org). RosettaNet is a consortium of major IT, electronic components, semiconductor manufacturing, telecommunications, and logistics enterprises working to create and implement industry-wide, open electronic business process standards for supply chain collaboration. This study builds on the authors' two-year involvement with the RosettaNet consortium. In the preliminary stage, we conducted 1 to 1.5 hour in-person and telephone interviews with 35 key executives (purchasing managers, customer relationship managers, supply chain managers, IT managers, distribution managers) in 16 enterprises in the IT-industry demand-side supply chain (7 hardware manufacturers/software publishers, 1 logistics provider, 4 wholesale distributors, and 4 retailers). These interviews helped us operationalize key constructs and ground the findings of this study. The main data collection was based on a multiple-section survey questionnaire that was pretested and refined for clarity with supply chain managers at a major computer manufacturer (not part of our sample) and experts at a consulting enterprise.

Representatives of each enterprise on the RosettaNet board (executive champions) helped us identify their supply chain partners (restricted to three supply chain tiers: manufacturers, distributors, and retailers). This resulted in identifying 91 partnerships (focal enterprise with supply chain partner). The RosettaNet champions at each enterprise were mailed surveys for each of their partnerships. They were asked to drill down within their enterprise and find the executive (key informant) responsible for the day-to-day functioning of the partnership under investigation. The key informant was asked to assign various sections of the survey to be completed by managers that they felt were most likely to provide accurate responses for a line of questioning. For instance,

IT related questions were completed by the IT manager, marketing issues related questions were completed by the managers in that area, and so on. For questions that would require a convergent view from multiple areas in the enterprise, the key informant was asked to hold a meeting to complete in those questions. Finally, the section that measured the performance of the supply chain relationship was completed directly by the key informant. The completed surveys were collected and returned to us by the RosettaNet champion at each enterprise. We received responses from 13 enterprises that covered 41 out of 91 pairs identified in the earlier phase.

A concern with this approach was that one-sided responses for supply chain relationships could lead to some biases. A few checks and balances prevented this from being a severe limitation. We selected the specific relationships that a particular enterprise would report on, rather than leaving it to the respondents to choose. Our second concern was that a particular tier of the supply chain (manufacturer, distributor, or retailer) might wield greater power or market-making influence. Our initial interviews suggested that, in terms of day-to-day transactions, distributors in the IT supply chain had significant influence (since there were only five major players at this tier while there were several hundred major players in the manufacturer and retailer tiers). Hence, we ensured that we received a perspective on each of the distributors in our sample (five in total) from either a manufacturer or a retailer. Additionally, we also made sure that each of the distributors provided us a perspective on a relationship with either a manufacturer or a retailer. This was done so as to obtain complete coverage of the distributor tier in the supply chain. After the data collection, we conducted a preliminary analysis to see if the data on the relationships involving the distributors was different from that involving relationships in other tiers (manufacturer-distributor and retailer-distributor compared to manufacturer-retailer). No such biases were evident from this analysis, i.e., there were no significant differences in means of the two sample groups with respect to the variables used for cluster analysis.

## Cluster Analysis

Cluster analysis is a statistical technique for the grouping of entities (in this study, the focal enterprise in a supply chain partnership) into groups such that the variation within groups is similar relative to the variation across groups (Aldenderfer and Blashfield 1984; Lorr 1983). Researchers have used cluster analysis techniques to study phenomena of interest in the Information Systems arena (Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Jain et al. 1998). Experts recommend a two-stage procedure (Ketchen and Shook 1996; Merchant 2000). In the first stage, we followed Bensaou and Venkatraman's (1995) recommendations for cluster analyses: (1) We standardized the aggregate scores for each of the constructs used in the analysis, (2) squared Euclidean distances were used as similarity measure, and (3) Ward's minimum variance method was used for cluster formation. In order to determine the number of clusters, as suggested by Aldenderfer and Blashfield (1984), we inspected the dendogram produced by Ward's method using Euclidean distances and the amalgamation coefficient (numerical value at which various cases merge to form a cluster).

Since our research is exploratory in nature, we wanted to test the robustness of the five-cluster solution suggested by the hierarchical procedure. Therefore, in the second stage, we used the K-Means algorithm to generate two-, three-, four-, five-, and six-cluster solutions. For all of the constructs in our analysis the means for all clusters differed significantly across all clusters in the five-cluster solution. For the two-, three-, four-, and six-cluster solutions, the constructs' discriminatory power was relatively weaker. Based on the robustness of the five-cluster solution across two stages of analyses, we present the results based on the five-cluster solution obtained from the hierarchical procedure. Tables 6, 7, and 8 (in the following section) present a series of one-way analyses of variance across the cluster variates (the inter-organizational integrative process mechanisms and partner interface-directed information systems), differences in characteristics of information sharing, and absorptive capacity outcomes (operational efficiency and partner-enabled knowledge creation) between each of the five clusters.

As a final note, Gong and Richman (1995) have shown that the accuracy of cluster analysis is impacted by sample size. They suggest that Ward's hierarchical method as well as the k-means algorithm achieve higher accuracy with small sample sizes, compared to other clustering methods. Due to our sample size constraints, we used these two methods in our two-stage process. We were also encouraged by the fact that other researchers have also used cluster analysis with small sample sizes and found interesting results (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004). We have heeded to their caution against over-interpreting the membership of these clusters and have focused on the description of the clusters themselves. We also wanted to ensure that our empirical analysis represented the true "in-the-field" situation rather than being just data-driven. For this reason, we made several presentations to the RosettaNet board members as well as supply chain managers in the IT industry. The executives indicated that our findings were congruent with the type of configurations and related behaviors prevalent in the IT industry supply chains. In addition, the CEO of RosettaNet helped us establish the practical validity of our configurations (clusters) and understand them in detail over multiple discussion sessions.

## The Supply Chain Partnership Configurations

We describe each of the partnership archetypes we uncovered based on the differences in the manner in which the enterprises (engaged in supply chain partnerships) configure their integrative interorganizational processes and partner interface-directed information systems. We also explore the ability of each of these configurations to absorb information received from their partner to achieve operational efficiency and partner-enabled market knowledge creation. Complementing these results with the qualitative data collected during our interviews and our field observations, we present propositions regarding the likely contingencies driving these patterns and the absorptive capacity of each of the partnership configurations.

## Configuration #1: Collector Supply Chain Partnerships

Enterprises structuring their supply chain partnerships as collectors appear to focus their resources on deploying standard electronic business interfaces with partners for supporting interorganizational process linkages and information exchange. As a large OEM in the IT industry suggested,

There are 15,000 VARs in the industry and to truly understand the complete picture you need information from all of them. We feel that standards will help us get this information coverage.

The focus of these enterprises seems to be the amassing of information. They take advantage of IT-based memory systems to store information pertaining to exchanges and outcomes with their partners. The enterprises engaged in collector type partnerships also indicate that they share and store a broad range of information with their partner. However, this information is perceived to be of low quality and not of a privileged nature.

This type of supply chain partnership appears to have several drawbacks. The partners engaged in collector type partnerships seem to have a low level of joint decision making activities. Information exchanged in these partnerships can be viewed as rather mechanistic and satisficing. Collector partnerships use standard electronic interfaces to exchange coordination information in a more structured and regular manner. As an executive remarked, “because we can exchange information easily, so we do it as a routine.”

Based on our results, it appears that enterprises engaged in these partnerships do not leverage their adoption of interface standards by complementing them with modular interlinked processes. The downside of this may be the need to exchange large amounts of coordination information. Further, enterprises engaged in this partnership configuration also indicate a lack of development of IT-based systems to support information interpretation. Thus, the extensive coordination information exchange can potentially have a “swamping” effect on the human interpreters within enterprises engaged in collector type partnerships.

The overall outcome appears to be that enterprises involved in collector partnerships are not able to make strategic use of the broad range of information they have acquired to enhance their market understanding, nor can they use the coordination information optimally to increase the efficiency of their transactions with business partners.

Proposition 1: Enterprises engaged in collector type partnerships will

(a) Attain limited knowledge creation potential due to a lack of joint decision-making activities with their partner, which inhibits their ability to acquire and assimilate information that is of high quality and privileged in nature.

(b) Be able to acquire a broad range of information aided by standard electronic business interfaces, but lack the capability (information systems for information interpretation) to transform the information exchanged to create operational efficiency and new knowledge.

(c) Exchange higher amounts of coordination information due to a lack of modular organization of processes interlinked with their partner, potentially overwhelming their capability to assimilate and transform more value-added information.

## Configuration #2: Connector Supply Chain Partnerships

The focus of enterprises engaged in connector supply chain partnerships appears to be the structuring of interorganizational processes. These enterprises indicate a high degree of modular design of processes, a moderate level of adoption of standard electronic business interfaces, and a moderate amount of joint decision making with their supply chain partner. However, enterprises engaged in connector supply chain partnerships seem to indicate a low level of use of partner interface-directed information systems to complement their interorganizational integrative process mechanisms. As one retailer stated about their OEM partners, “we have the capability to deliver information about the markets to the manufacturers and have done so in the past, but the manufacturer does not have the systems capability to process this information.”

Connector enterprises indicate exchanging a moderate range of high quality information with their partners, but the information that is exchanged does not appear to be privileged in nature. Interestingly, these enterprises appear to be able to achieve higher operational efficiency through the exchange of a moderate level of coordination information with their partner. However, enterprises involved in connector supply chain partnerships seem to have a low level of market knowledge creation potential.

Proposition 2: Enterprises engaged in connector type partnerships will

(a) Have a low ability to create market knowledge, even though they have well-developed capabilities to acquire information, due to minimal capacity to assimilate and transform information resulting from the low level of development and utilization of partner interface-directed information systems.

(b) Achieve high operational efficiency through modular design of their interlinked processes, resulting in the reduced need for coordination information exchange.

## Configuration #3: Cruncher Supply Chain Partnerships

From an absorptive capacity perspective, cruncher supply chain partnerships appear to be a polar opposite of connector configurations. While connector enterprises are externally focused (structuring their interorganizational processes), cruncher enterprises appear to focus on partner interface-directed information systems for storage and processing of information. One possible explanation could be that the enterprises in cruncher configurations are technology-savvy enterprises (such as software publishers) themselves or have technological alliances with software vendors, and are internally rather than externally focused. While engaging in a moderate level of joint activities, enterprises in cruncher supply chain partnerships exhibit a low level of modular design of interlinked processes as well as a low level of adoption of standard electronic business interfaces with their partner. Cruncher enterprises indicate that they exchange a limited range of information that is of moderate quality. Further, the partners do not seem to engage in the exchange of privileged information. In terms of their exploiting information exchanged with partners, cruncher enterprises tend to achieve a low level of operational efficiency with partners and a moderate level of market knowledge creation.

Proposition 3: Enterprises engaged in cruncher type partnerships

(a) Exchange a narrow range of information that is not privileged in nature and exhibit moderate acquisition and assimilation capacities due to a lack of well-developed integrative process mechanisms.

(b) Are only able to create a moderate level of market knowledge from high quality information exchanged with partners due to moderate acquisition and assimilation capacities, even though their well-developed information systems capabilities enable high transformation capacity.

## Configuration #4: Coercive Supply Chain Partnerships

This is an interesting cluster of supply chain partnerships in terms of the relative size of partners. An examination of the partners indicates that they are asymmetrical pairs in terms of size: a very large enterprise (focal enterprise responding to the survey) with much power in the supply chain (a powerful proverbial "800 pound gorilla") and a much smaller enterprise ("weak monkey"). Our observations regarding this cluster are similar to Hart and Saunders (1998), i.e., relative dependence determines the power that can be exerted by enterprises specifically for adoption of inter-organizational information sharing systems. The larger enterprise (e.g., OEM providing new generation hardware or software products) appears to be throwing its weight into "coercing" the smaller one (mostly retailers wanting to sell these products). The coercion that takes place in these partnerships tends to be very subtle. One large manufacturer we spoke to promised higher product allocation (when new products are introduced there is not enough supply to satisfy everyone's demand) to partners who were willing to undertake standards initiatives proposed by the manufacturer even though these were not industry-wide standards. Another OEM suggested that

For new product introduction, we provide distributors and select retailers with marketing development funds. But in order to qualify for these funds, the business partners have to report sales-out to us in formats that we specify.

Enterprises engaged in coercive partnerships indicate a low level of joint decision making activities with partners. These enterprises seem to emphasize adoption of standard electronic business interfaces (albeit their prescribed standard) and exhibit a moderate level of modular design of processes interlinked with partners. The information exchanged between partners appears to be limited in range and not of high quality. However, these partners do seem to exchange privileged information with each other. Further, they exhibit reduced levels of coordination information exchange. They also have well-developed partner interface-directed information systems to assimilate and transform information received from partners. In terms of their ability to exploit the information exchanged with partners, enterprises in coercive partnerships seemingly achieve high levels of operational efficiency as well knowledge creation.

Proposition 4: Enterprises engaged in coercive type partnerships achieve operational efficiency and market-related knowledge creation through mandated information sharing dictates, based on substantial power differences between the two partners. However, in the long term, coercive type partnerships may be unstable due to potential deleterious effects.

(a) The coerced partners may refuse to adopt new interface formats or invest in new information sharing systems in the future, thereby impeding the development of coercing enterprises' assimilation capacity.

(b) The coercing enterprises may suffer from a progressive reduction in their assimilation capacity, because they do not collaborate intensively through joint decision making activities, engaging only in a limited range of information exchange with specific partners.

In examining the stability of the coercer configuration uncovered in this study, it is useful to look at the research on EDI adoption. This research has documented the use of coercive power by large enterprises to influence adoption by their supply chain partners (e.g., Chwelos et al. 2001). The long-term strategic payoff from EDI implementations has been found to depend upon whether the partnership is cooperative or adversarial, shaping the initiator's EDI investment actions and process design very differently (Chatfield and Yetton 2000). Coercion reflects a short-term approach with adverse consequences for long-term infrastructure growth. A coercive strategy potentially constricts the willingness of suppliers to implement evolving and new interface standards, which inhibits future information exchange (Hart and Saunders 1998). Whether coercer configurations are sustainable and effective in the long term for rich information-focused supply chain partnerships can only be empirically validated through longitudinal research studies under changing environmental conditions.

## Configuration #5: Collaborator Supply Chain Partnerships

Enterprises involved in collaborator partnership configurations appear to be the most progressive in the evolution of supply chain partnerships. Interestingly, they also represent the largest cluster (17 partnerships). These enterprises indicate advanced capabilities in the structuring of their interorganizational integrative processes. They seem to engage in a higher degree of joint decision making with their partner compared to enterprises in other clusters. An OEM commented to us,

We routinely engage in collaborative sessions with our largest component manufacturer and software manufacturer to collectively share information about the trends in hardware and software that would impact the industry on the whole. This is a source of competitive advantage for us.

The nature of information exchanged between partners reflects this strategic intent: it is of high quality and is broad ranging. As an example, a large hardware OEM in the United States has an initiative underway to engage in collaborative planning with its top 15 partners (including partners in Asia) so as to “exchange high quality information.” The executive at a distributor enterprise, which is one of the 15 partners, commented,

Besides inventory and sales information that every one wants, the [OEM] tells us when they are planning to discontinue their products, what is the shelf life of the products, and what are the new products plans underway. We feel that this range of information gives us an advantage over our competitors.

Collaborator enterprises appear to exchange more privileged information with their partners, compared to those involved in the partnership configurations described earlier. An example of this privileged information exchange is an OEM's effort to inform the channel about the time frame in which a new product is going to be introduced and the associated transition plan. The close collaborators (distributors and retailers) are given this information six months in advance, while the rest of the supply chain players are only given a month's notice.

The enterprises involved in collaborator supply chain partnerships also focus on building partner interface-directed information systems to assimilate and transform the information acquired from partners. Hence, these enterprises exhibit a high level of operational efficiency and market knowledge creation.

Proposition 5: Enterprises engaged in collaborator type partnerships will achieve high operational efficiency and knowledge creation by

(a) Creating high absorptive capacity (acquisition, assimilation, and transformation) through the development of interorganizational integrative process mechanisms as well as the complementary partner interface-directed information systems.

(b) Using joint decision making to exchange privileged information and signal collaborative intent, as well as using standard interfaces to reduce information processing needs.

These five sets of propositions derived from the characterization of each supply chain configuration provide a foundation for further theoretical development of an absorptive capacity model that helps to explain how partner-enabled knowledge creation and operational efficiency can be influenced through organizational capability platforms.

## Limitations of the Study

We recognize some inherent limitations of this exploratory study and the opportunity they present for future work. First, there are sampling limitations, as the target population was limited to 28

enterprises in the RosettaNet consortium. Even with high response rates, the limited set of sample data points precluded us from conducting analyses around additional factors that could potentially impact knowledge creation in supply chains. To overcome the sample size concerns, we used a number of different techniques for triangulation that increase confidence in our results and ensure that the number of clusters we derived was not "overdoing it." Future studies with larger sample sizes (perhaps in more fragmented industries) will be needed to empirically replicate our results and confirm the validity of the clusters. Second, although we diligently attempted to control for response biases due to a one-sided view of supply chain partnerships, we recognize that the effect of such a bias may still persist. Future studies should collect data from both enterprises involved in a partnership to ensure a balanced view. Third, we attempted to control for common method bias by asking multiple executives in each enterprise to fill out different parts of the survey or for executives within a functional area to collaborate when answering sections of the survey. The response for dependent variables was obtained from the most senior executive dealing with the supply chain partner (he/she was asked to only provide responses for dependent variables). However, we recognize that these steps do not make our study completely immune to common method biases, and more objective performance data would further increase confidence in the results. Fourth, as is the case with many empirical studies conducted in one industry, there is always the issue of generalizability. Although we have no reason to believe that our results are only specific to the IT industry demand-side supply chains, future studies are needed to replicate our results in other industry contexts. Fifth, this was a cross-sectional rather than a longitudinal study, which uncovered configurations of supply chain partnerships but did not investigate how enterprises switch (or drift) from one configuration to another or how the switching paths affect the elements of absorptive capacity and interorganizational knowledge management. Sixth, and finally, we did not explicitly take into account how power differences between partners affect an enterprise's ability to create knowledge related to the market environment in conjunction with its partner, especially over the long-term.

## Implications for Understanding Knowledge Management in Interorganizational Partnership Contexts

The uncovering and characterization of supply chain configurations have enabled us to better understand how partner-enabled knowledge creation and operational efficiency can both be affected by shortcomings in organizational capability platforms and the nature of information exchanged. Using the absorptive capacity framework, we were also able to derive research propositions that provide a solid foundation for understanding knowledge creation in supply chain partnership contexts. These results highlight a number of implications for research and practice, as well as suggest a number of fertile opportunities for research on knowledge management.

## Shifting the Focus: Knowledge Management in Interorganizational Contexts

With changing business environments, the locus of value creation is “no longer within the boundaries of a single firm, but occurs instead at the nexus of relationships between a variety of parties that contribute to the production function” (Schilling and Steensma 2001, p. 1149). Enterprises, especially those in industries similar to the rapidly evolving high-technology industry, have to rely on business partners to share knowledge and continually respond to change. Partners can help each other design better products, create new markets, and adapt to newly created markets. With the growing importance of the pooling of knowledge resources, knowledge management will have to transcend organizational boundaries to include supply chain partners. However, the focus of previous empirical research studies has mainly been on intraorganizational knowledge management. In this study, we have attempted to direct the attention of knowledge management researchers toward inter-organizational interfaces. Our study indicates that enterprises have to build requisite absorptive capacity to prepare for collaborative knowledge creation with their supply chain partners. Absorptive capacity in this context is the ability of enterprises to acquire and assimilate information from their supply chain partners and to transform and exploit this information to achieve superior operational and strategic outcomes.

## The Value of Absorptive Capacity-Based Configurational Approaches in Interorganizational Knowledge Management

We have also shown that the study of knowledge management in complex interorganizational contexts may benefit from using a configurational approach, which enables an understanding of organizational partnerships as a set of “rich gestalts.” The strength of this approach lies in discovering the complementarities and tensions among variables, rather than establishing the importance of one variable at a time (D. Miller 1996). In keeping with Miller’s (1981) suggestion of trying to find clusters of attributes rather than looking at a few variables or the linear association between such variables, we have attempted to discover the pattern of capability platform attributes and the consequent impact on absorptive capacity of an enterprise engaged in a supply chain partnership. The resultant gestalts have enabled us to develop research propositions around preferred configurations of capability platform attributes, and around configurations that are less than optimal, but still empirically observed. The characterization of configurations that outperform others has obvious implications for practice, but also sheds light on building theories for more effective knowledge creation in interorganizational partnership contexts.

The results of this study suggest that achieving meaningful absorptive capacity outcomes requires that diverse variables be managed in concert. This study contributes new insights to a rich stream of literature studying interorganizational relationships by synthesizing and building on past research. Three main perspectives have guided earlier studies. First, the transactions cost perspective has been used to derive typologies focusing on efficient governance choices under contingencies related to asset specificity, demand uncertainty, and market variability (e.g., Choudhury 1997). A second perspective focuses on information processing, coordination, and interdependence concerns. In this view, typologies for IT-supported interorganizational relationships have been developed based on the nature of interdependencies (e.g., Kumar and van Dissel 1996) or fit between information processing capabilities and needs (Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995). The last perspective focuses on the type of functionality of interorganizational information systems (e.g., Cash and Konsynski 1985). The absorptive capacity perspective adopted in this study focuses on higher-order capabilities that encompass the above three perspectives. The chosen antecedents reflect concerns for managing transactions costs (through standard interfaces), for managing dependencies (through modular processes and joint decision making), as well as the interface-focused IS functionality (through memory and interpretation systems). The uncovered clusters echo existing typologies, but at the same time are more holistic and capture a broader range of concerns. For instance, the largest cluster (collaborators) in this study corresponds to the largest cluster (electronic interdependence) uncovered by Bensaou and Venkatraman (1995) with similar patterns of information processing. However, our clustering approach also highlights the capability platforms required for achieving not only day-to-day operational efficiency, but also new knowledge creation.

The five configurations of supply chain partnerships (collector, connector, cruncher, coercer, and collaborator) uncovered by this study provide a parsimonious and memorable way to differentiate enterprises' supply chain partnership configurations based on their ability to absorb the information from their partner to achieve operational efficiency and partner-enabled market knowledge creation. Our exploratory findings suggest that integrative interorganizational process mechanisms may influence an enterprise's ability to acquire and assimilate rich information from its supply chain partners while minimizing the need for coordination information exchange. Simultaneously, the partner interface-directed information systems enable an enterprise to assimilate and transform the rich information exchanged with its supply chain partners. Consequently, partner-enabled market knowledge creation is enabled through the configuration of these capability platforms.

The clusters uncovered in this study are also helpful for practice in that they have predictive validity: distinctions among the configurations are useful in predicting differences in performance of the relationship. Enterprises having relationships supported by effective integrative process mechanisms and information systems will be able to outperform other comparable enterprises. Moving to higher-level configurations requires efforts to change interorganizational processes and investment in new IT-based systems. For instance, creating common partner interfaces in supply chains using the RosettaNet Partner Interface Process specifications is estimated to cost between \$5,000 and \$10,000 for each interface (Chabrow and Sullivan 2004). However, the results from such investments, in the form of absorptive capacity outcomes, may only be realized over a long period of time. Thus, it is unlikely that all organizations will be able to transition to higher-level configurations. Differences in power across supply chain partners may also result in coercer configurations being preferred, whereby costs for change efforts may be borne disproportionately by the weaker partners. Earlier research suggests that organizations may also utilize a mix of the various configurations across their portfolio of partners (Holland and Lockett 1997). Thus, an organization may choose to maintain connector relationships with suppliers when technological uncertainty and market variability is low, and collaborator relationships with others when the need for shared knowledge creation is more intense.

## Information Exchange Dimensions and Interorganizational Knowledge Management

Our exploration of coordination information exchange also yields some interesting insights for understanding the dynamics of interorganizational information overload and how it influences knowledge creation when interpreted through the configurations. The cruncher configuration indicates that extensive coordination information exchange may have a detrimental effect. The statusing type of coordination information may be swamping enterprises in such partnership configurations. Instead of focusing on and making sense of rich information exchange, enterprises involved in cruncher configurations may be preoccupied with the exchange of coordination information. Consequently, they fail to sense the changing market environment due to lack of rich information exchange. An alternative explanation could be that the excessive amount of coordination information exchanged between an enterprise and its supply chain partner in cruncher configurations is symptomatic of operational inefficiency. The worse the enterprise performs in partner-linked processes, the more the partners seek to overcome the situation by focusing on coordination information exchange. Surprisingly, in our field interviews, we found that many of the partners were still focused on deploying technologies to intensify exchange of coordination information. Our exploratory findings suggest that this could further perpetuate a vicious cycle caused by the overload of statusing information.

Prior research in the areas of IOS and supply chain management has tended to focus on the exchange of coordination information between supply chain partners, with the “information for inventory” paradigm (i.e., the exchange of coordination-related, inventory status, and location information) being a major theme (Austin et al. 1997; Lee et al. 1997). This study has gone further by examining how richer information exchange—broad, high quality, and privileged information—can be perpetuated between supply chain partners in order to sustain market knowledge creation. We observed three types of information being exchanged in supply chain partnerships: collaborative, coordination, and privileged information. Collaborative information (broad ranging and high quality) appears to be instrumental in the creation of new knowledge for the receiving organization, while coordination information is largely related to the achievement of operational efficiency between supply chain partners. In terms of privileged information exchange, interestingly, the two configurations that exhibited a high level of market knowledge creation (collaborator and coercer) also exhibited a high level of privileged information exchange. It is likely that different mechanisms may be at work in the two configurations. In the case of collaborator configurations, trust and reciprocity norms (indicating a high level of social capital) may be driving the sharing of this information. In the case of coercer configurations, power differential and asymmetric resource dependencies may be leading to coerced exchange of privileged information. The implication of this result is that privileged knowledge exchange may be a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for high levels of market knowledge creation. Overall, by developing a finer-grained view of rich information exchange, the study has provided a lens for Information Systems scholars to use in future research when studying the potency of interorganizational partnerships especially in terms of their knowledge creating potential.

## Organizational Capability Platform for Interorganizational Knowledge Management

The study has also attempted to shed some light on the nature and composition of organizational capability platform, and how that may influence enterprise absorptive capacity (and consequently market knowledge creation) in supply chain contexts. The study has conceptualized two distinct types of partner interface-directed information systems that enable assimilation and transformation: memory systems for interorganizational activities and interpretation systems for inter-organizational information. As Uzzi and Lancaster (2003) point out, the ability of an enterprise to create new market knowledge is not only dependent on the capabilities of its partnering relationships, but also its internal capabilities for leveraging these partnerships. Future research studies would benefit by adopting a more comprehensive view of interorganizational systems, especially considering the complementarity of information processing and information sharing systems, as indicated by this study.

The other critical component of the organizational capability platform considered in this study is integrative interorganizational process mechanisms. Within that component, the role of standard electronic business interfaces has been highlighted. In light of our results, it appears that that standard electronic business interfaces may have an impact on supply chain relationships in two salient ways. First, these interfaces may have a structural impact (i.e., the adoption of standardized interfaces has the potential to create network effects), which allows adopting enterprises to quickly and easily link with potential supply chain partners. This quick linkage can enable an enterprise to acquire and assimilate information from each new partner it links to in order to create new knowledge. In turn, this new knowledge can be of use to all other partners to which the enterprise is already connected. Second, these interfaces may have cognitive impact (i.e., standardized interfaces in conjunction with modular process architecture provide a platform for embedded coordination). This can reduce the cognitive load for supply chain managers thereby increasing their absorptive capacity and freeing them up to assimilate strategic information obtained from their partner enterprise.

Conventionally, enterprises are seen as needing to choose between short-term (operational efficiency) and longer-term (knowledge creation) goals for their supply chains. This is because of the inherent tradeoffs between the need to create deep relational bonds that support knowledge creation and the need to maintain low asset specificity, to guard against opportunistic behavior, and thus maintain operational efficiency. A significant challenge is that enterprises, over time, develop sticky socialization patterns with entrenched partners and these may be very resistant to change (Van den Bosch et al. 1999), making them vulnerable to opportunistic behavior. In this paper, we have shown how enterprises may meet objectives related to both operational efficiency and knowledge creation. The variables included in our analysis suggest directions for careful management of interdependencies between partners. Recent research suggests that interorganizational dependencies should be managed by structuring of interorganizational processes and information exchange such that partnering enterprises are loosely coupled—responsive to each other but able to change partners spontaneously (Gosain et al. 2004). These loose-coupling strategies underlie the high-performing configurations uncovered in this study. On one hand, joint decision making creates the coactivity needed to develop shared language, cognitive schema, and relational ties driving coupling. On the other, modular design of inter-linked processes and use of standardized interfaces counterbalance by ensuring looseness.

## Conclusion

This study has contributed to a better understanding of interorganizational relationship configurations that differ in their ability to achieve partner-enabled market knowledge creation capabilities. As Bensaou and Venkatraman (1995) point out, previous configurational studies have either focused on uncovering a taxonomy or testing a typology. This study does both, uncovering the taxonomy and also relating configurations to absorptive capacity outcomes, rather than assuming that different configurations are equally effective. We have controlled for contingency conditions by looking at similar supply chain relationships in one industry so that the environmental dynamics and complexity facing them are likely to be similar. Rather than emphasizing a fit to environmental contingencies, we emphasize that the organization achieve a mutually consistent set of capabilities that drives effective information exchange and processing in conjunction with partners, and consequently impacts knowledge creation. The study has provided a conceptual framework for linking operational and knowledge creation capability-building aspects of interorganizational supply chain partnerships, and has revealed the tensions that exist in both overcommitting and undercommitting to a partner relationship. It has also provided an understanding of both of the mechanisms needed to manage this tension.

We hope that this study will stimulate researchers to further explore the nature and characteristics of supply chain configurations through the absorptive capacity lens, and to better understand how to effectively create and share knowledge in supply chains while executing operational transactions. Our findings suggest that information exchanged between supply chain partners can lead to new knowledge creation in supply chains, even when learning from partners may not be an explicit goal. Supply chain partnerships are a type of inter-organizational partnership whose primary purpose is to coordinate business processes around the exchange of goods and services. Using the conceptual framework focused on absorptive capacity, we were able to show how both operational efficiencies and market knowledge creation could be achieved in this type of supply chain partnership. We believe that there are fertile research opportunities to apply the absorptive capacity conceptual framework used in this study to other variants of interorganizational partnering arrangements that are primarily knowledge-centered, such as research and development partnerships, product development joint ventures, and licensing networks.

## Construct Scales

## Operational Efficiency [1 = Unsatisfactory to 7 = Excellent]

How would you rate your transactions with <partner company> in terms of:

1. Meeting agreed upon costs per unit associated with <partner company> [OPEFF1]

2. Meeting productivity standards associated with <partner company> [OPEFF2]

3. Meeting on-time delivery requirements associated with <partner company> [OPEFF3]

3. Responding to each others requests [OPEFF4]

5. Ability to meet inventory requirements (finished goods) associated with <partner company> [OPEFF5]

## Partner-Enabled Market Knowledge Creation [1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Disagree]

Working with <partner company> has helped you...

1. ...better understand the market segments you serve. [PEMKC1]

2. ...better understand the needs of customers [PEMKC2]

3. ...better understand new or emerging markets [PEMKC3]

4. ...better understand intentions and capabilities of your competitors [PEMKC4]

5. ...find better ways of distributing/selling the products [PEMKC5]

6. ...improve service for the end customers [PEMKC6]

7. ...develop strategies to compete in the market, that would not have been possible otherwise. [PEMKC7]

## Joint Decision Making [1 = No Joint Decision Making to 7 = Extensive Joint Decision Making]

Extent to which you and <partner company> make joint decisions...

1. ...when planning marketing programs. [JDM1]

2. ...by analyzing market trends. [JDM2]

3. ...by analyzing response to marketing promotions. [JDM3]

4. ...when planning for new market creation. [JDM4]

5. ...when planning for demand development. [JDM5]

## Interorganizational Process Modularity [1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree]

1. The processes conducted in conjunction with the <partner company> are divided into clearly understood activities to be performed by you and <partner company>. [MOD1]

2. The output requirements of <partner company> from your company and your requirements from <partner company> are precisely specified and understood. [MOD2]

3. The activities performed by your company and <partner company> are performed simultaneously to a large extent. [MOD3]

## Standard Electronic Business Interfaces [1 = Not at All to 7 = to a Large Extent]

1. Extent to which the business processes interfaces with <partner company> are similar to the process interfaces linked with other channel partners—in terms of rules and procedures. [STD1]

2. Extent to which information exchanged (e.g., sales reporting, product information, product availability, inventory information, etc.) with <partner company> needs to be converted/translated to be interpreted by your company [STD2]

3. Extent of which content of information exchanged (e.g., sales reporting, product information, product availability, inventory information, etc.) with <partner company> can be interpreted similar to information exchanged with other partners [STD3]

## Memory Systems for Interorganizational Activities [1 = Not All to 7 = 100%]

1. What percentage of transactional activities that took place in your previous exchanges with <partner company> and other similar partners are stored using information systems. [ISMEM1]

2. What percentage of performance outcomes of previous exchanges with <partner company> and other similar partners are stored using information systems. [ISMEM2]

3. What is your company's level of utilization of information systems to search and retrieve a required piece of information about past experiences with <partner company> and other similar partners [ISMEM3]

## Interpretation Systems for Interorganizational Information [1 = Not at All to 7 = 100% of the Time]

What percentage of time do you use information systems...

1. ...to derive inferences from past events (e.g., process exceptions, patterns of demand shifts, impacts of different company responses, what worked and what did not work) [ISINT1]

2. ...to combine information obtained from different channel partners to create new interpretations and uncover trends and patterns. [ISINT2]

3. ...to slice the information obtained from the channel partners into parts depending upon the requirements of different units or individuals in your company. [ISINT3]

4. ...to interpret information obtained from channel partners in multiple ways, depending upon the requirements of various units within your company. [ISINT4]

5. ...to represent the information obtained from channel partners in multiple ways, depending upon the requirements of various units within your company. [ISINT5]

## Breadth of Information Exchange [1 = Not at All to 7 = Very Frequently]

1. Extent to which you exchange details of upcoming product or service related changes with <partner company>. [INFOBRD1]

2. Extent to which you exchange future plans such as promotion and marketing plans, long-term production plans, capital investments and capacity utilization with <partner company> [INFOBRD2]

3. Extent to which you exchange information related to market demand trends and forecasts with <partner company> [INFOBRD3]

4. Extent to which you exchange information on demand shifts and changes in customer preferences with <partner company> [INFOBRD4]

5. Extent to which you exchange information related to changes in supply chain structure—such as addition or dropping of partner companies, merger and alliances, with <partner company> [INFOBRD5]

6. Extent to which you exchange process information needed to support changes in product features or volumes, with <partner company> [INFOBRD56]

## Quality of Information Exchange [1 = Poor to 7 = Excellent]

How would you rate the information exchanged with <partner company> in terms of its...

1. ...relevancy to your business needs, compared to information exchanged with other similar partners? [INFOQUAL1]

2. ...value-added to your business needs, compared to information exchanged with other similar partners? [INFOQUAL2]

3. ...timeliness, compared to information exchanged with other similar partners? [INFOQUAL3]

4. ...its completeness, compared to information exchanged with other similar partners? [INFOQUAL4]

## Privileged Information Exchange [1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree]

1. In our relationship with <partner company>, we provide each other proprietary information if we feel it can help our business partner. [PRIVINF1]
