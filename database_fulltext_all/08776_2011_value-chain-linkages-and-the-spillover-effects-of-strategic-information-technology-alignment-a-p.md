# Value Chain Linkages and the Spillover Effects of Strategic Information Technology Alignment: A Process-Level View

Paul P. Tallon

Paul P. Tallon is an associate professor of information systems and executive director of the Lattanze Center for Information Value in the Sellinger School of Business and Management at Loyola University, Maryland. He previously worked as an IT auditor and chartered accountant with PricewaterhouseCoopers in Ireland and New York. His research interests include the economic and organizational effects of IT, strategic alignment, real options approaches to IT investment, portfolio analysis, and the economics of information management. His research has been published in the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Global IT Management, Information Technology and Management, Communications of the AIS, and Communications of the ACM. His paper on sensemaking-based perceptions of IT business value as a proxy for quantitative IT business value metrics published in JMIS received the 2007 Paper of the Year AIS award.

Abst ract : The alignment of information technology (IT) and business strategy is a perennial challenge for corporate executives. While earlier studies confirm the value of alignment, there is still some question as to how alignment creates value and the level at which value is created. In this research, we use a series of theoretical arguments based on the interconnected structure of the value chain to consider the extended effects of alignment at the process level. Since processes are often linked to create a complex chain of activities, the absence or presence of alignment in any process could have implications for business performance elsewhere in the value chain. Minimally aligned processes can not only disrupt performance within the focal process, but their effects may also be felt further downstream in the form of bottlenecks and a diminution in the business value of IT. Using a simplified form of the value chain and data from matched surveys of business and IT executives at 317 U.S. and EU firms, we examine how the effects of alignment on a given process spill over into processes further downstream, creating higher IT business value in those downstream processes. We also show that these spillover effects continue along the length of the value chain and do not diminish based on distance from the focal process. Our results reinforce the call for firms to improve the fit between business and IT strategy by showing how efforts to improve alignment in a given process can deliver a stream of benefits along the value chain. This research provides a fresh perspective on the value of alignment, facilitating a deeper understanding and appreciation of the link between strategic IT alignment and firm performance.

Key words and phrases: IT business value, process bottlenecks, profile deviation, spillover effects, strategic IT alignment, value chain, value disciplines, value flows.

Not wit hst anding t he rise of globalizat ion and, in more recent years, a marked increase in business risk due to volatility in global financial markets, shrinking product life cycles, and erratic consumer tastes, strategic alignment, reflecting the extent of fit between information technology (IT) and business strategy, has remained one of the most complex and enduring managerial challenges [17, 35]. Since the publication of the now-classical paper by Henderson and Venkatraman [27] noting the intricacies and implications or the what and why of alignment, information systems (IS) academics have examined alignment from many different but complementary angles. From the earliest studies that viewed alignment in binary terms as the presence or absence of cross-referencing between formal, written, IT and business plans [7, 52] to recent studies that place alignment within a complex nomological net or that go beyond generic strategy labels to portray alignment as a multidimensional process-level construct [51, 66], much has been learned and yet, as Chan and Reich [14] note, much is still unknown. What is clear from the IS literature is that alignment matters, whether in terms of its contribution to standard metrics such as net profit, sales growth, return on investment or productivity, or its contribution to a more focused set of metrics around the effects of IT on individual business processes [13, 44, 66]. Yet even with the knowledge of a key associative link between alignment and performance, important questions remain. For example, how exactly does alignment lead to higher firm performance; what are the first-order effects of alignment; can these effects be aggregated and interpreted at a higher level (such as at the firm level) or are they only interpretable as first-order effects at the process level?

Part of the challenge of understanding how alignment affects firm performance is deciphering the nomological network through which the effects of alignment proliferate throughout the organization. If an organization selects an IT investment on the basis of how it will support a critical business endeavor, the question is often asked as to where the effects of that investment will be felt. For example, are the effects confined to the area of the initial IT investment or are those effects likely to appear elsewhere in the firm, in processes that are unrelated to the initial investment? This has not been a factor in prior research where the primary level of analysis has been at the firm level but with studies showing that the first-order effects of IT materialize at the process level, there is growing interest in exploring alignment at the process level [66]. Our goal in this study is to develop an understanding of how alignment creates value at the process level by considering whether its effects are localized within a focal process (based on where a specific IT investment occurs) or whether these effects can trickle down to other areas of the firm. We use a series of arguments based on the design of the value chain to create an awareness of the extent of first-order effects of alignment on IT-led firm performance at the process level and to outline how, in an era of increasingly interconnected processes, these effects can spill over into other processes. As such, alignment in upstream processes can affect IT business value in downstream processes. Because processes are often serially linked, creating what Thompson [69] describes as long-linked technologies, the effects of alignment can be felt in processes that are not directly linked to each other. These spillover effects can be positive or negative. For example, a high degree of fit between IT and supplier activities could improve inventory management but it could also improve customer relations and market share if sales agents can better predict order delivery times. Similarly, misalignment in supplier relations can lead to inventory shortfalls and production delays with the possibility, further down the value chain, of damaged customer relations and lower market share.

One way to examine the question of how alignment affects performance is to identify the paths of influence that alignment has on performance at the process level. The design of the value chain provides a simple illustration of how these paths can be arranged within the firm. Each path contains a root or source process where IT and business activities are first aligned and a number of sequentially linked downstream processes that can benefit, either directly or indirectly, from alignment in the root process. In Figure 1, we present a stylized form of these spillover effects, showing how IT business value in a given process is affected not only by alignment within that same process but also by alignment in each upstream process and how alignment in each process can affect IT business value locally and in each downstream process. What this argument leads to is a many-to-many relationship between alignment and IT business value at the process level. The challenge with this approach, however, is determining the ordering of processes. As Stabell and Fjeldstad [63] reveal, processes may be configured as traditional value chains (where process linkages are predefined and invariant), value shops (where process linkages are partially flexible but still follow preset patterns), or value networks (where processes linkages are indeterminate, independent, and customizable). Consequently, the spillover effects of alignment may follow any number of paths through the value chain.

Using data from matched surveys of IT and business executives at 317 firms, we test for spillover effects in two antithetical models. The first model uses a predefined process structure that closely follows the generic value chain (process flows move in a rigid manner from left to right: from inbound logistics or supplier relations to outbound logistics or customer relations) while the second model is entirely free form so that any process can connect to any other process in the value chain. We use perceptual measures of IT business value as proxy measures of firm performance in each of five primary processes, recognizing that while these measures are imperfect, they nonetheless are useful indicators of business success [12, 13, 66, 68]. Regression analysis on both models confirms that spillover effects exist and that these effects are not confined to adjoining processes—spillover effects can span several processes. Recognizing that alignment can act in this manner, executives may be better able to focus their efforts to improve performance, not by pursuing alignment in some holistic, firm-level sense but by targeting alignment inside specific processes.

This research contributes to the growing literature on strategic IT alignment in two respects. First, building on the notion that efforts to pursue alignment and the first-order effects of alignment occur at the process level, we show that the effects of alignment in any area of the value chain spill over into processes further downstream. This helps to explain how alignment affects firm performance and how it behooves firms to consider not just how IT can satisfy particular process needs, but how the effects of process-level alignment can flow down the value chain. It also shows how the effects of misalignment can trickle down the value chain, creating a much wider swath of financial problems than previously imagined. Second, we show that spillover effects tend to move in one direction—downstream—consistent with the physical and logical layout of the value chain, beginning with suppliers and ending with customers. While theory does not prevent alignment in downstream processes affecting IT business value in upstream processes, there is little evidence of this. The implication for firms is to pay specific attention to alignment in upstream areas of the value chain, beginning with supplier relations/inbound logistics. As spillover effects take root and trickle down the value chain, managers can expect to see the effects of tighter fit between IT and supplier activities spilling over into a number of processes that extend right through to customer relations.

The remainder of the paper is as follows. In the next section, we offer a series of arguments based on the interconnected nature of the value chain to illustrate how the effects of alignment can trickle down the value chain from process to process. Our arguments culminate in two hypotheses. We then present our survey methodology, measures, and analysis. After discussing our results, we consider the limitations and contributions of our research to the literature, examine implications for future research and practice, and conclude.

## Theoretical Discussion

Spillover effect s have been considered in a variety of contexts in prior research. For example, in a review of the literature on the productivity effects of IT, Dedrick et al. [19] refer to the possible spillover effects on labor productivity attributable to nationwide changes in IT capital formation as new innovation in hardware, software, and networks leads to a radical shift in work practices. Using a game theory model, Gal-Or and Ghose [24] show how the effects of security IT investments and security information sharing by users and service providers can lead to demand-side spillover effects or positive externalities across an entire sector. Similarly, Cheng and Nault [15] find that

IT spending by suppliers within a specific industry creates positive and significant output spillover effects for firms elsewhere in the supply chain. Bresnahan [6] finds that the effects of IT investment by financial service firms can spill over to consumers in the form of higher consumer surplus. While banks may have initially gained an advantage from investing in ATMs or, more recently, in mobile banking, portions of their producer surplus may be transferred to consumers, particularly if the adoption of these new banking channels comes at no additional cost to bank customers.

Thus, while there has been some attempt to identify IT spillover effects at the economy, industry, and firm levels, there has been much less focus on how (or if) the effects of IT investment might spill over within the firm, from process to process. There is general consensus in the literature that processes can be, and are, interconnected, but this does not address the issue of how value can spill over from one process to another. In describing the composition of activities within the value chain, Porter declares that “value activities are related by linkages within the value chain [while] linkages are relationships between the way one value activity is performed and the cost or performance of another” [48, p. 48]. Linkages can affect firm performance through optimization or coordination [48]. Optimization refers to potential tradeoffs between activities. For example, the use of IT to enhance product variety may increase production costs but it may also boost sales by making it easier for firms to tailor their products and services to meet the unmet needs of isolated market segments. Coordination refers to the physical handoff of products or services between processes. For example, partially assembled products can be passed from suppliers to end-stage producers as, for example, when mortgages move from an application stage to an underwriting stage to an appraisal stage and finally to a loan securitization stage. As Porter reveals, “exploiting linkages usually requires information or information flows that allow optimization or coordination to take place” [48, p. 50]. Coordination can be information intensive as in the case of equity investment companies where information moves between clients, managers, and traders or in the airline industry where information moves between online reservation systems and pricing systems as a way to offer last-minute airfares to specific customer groups.

Past research shows that while strategic alignment is positively associated with specific firm-level performance measures, strategic alignment is also positively associated with IT business value when both constructs are evaluated at the process level [66, 68]. As such, alignment in any process of the value chain has a direct effect on IT business value in that same process. From a theoretical viewpoint, the question is whether the effects of alignment in a particular process can be felt outside that process (whether upstream or downstream within the value chain) and how firms might leverage and benefit from this type of effect. As outlined below, several theoretical arguments based on knowledge sharing, uncertainty reduction, and coordination of interconnected activities help to frame a theory of spillover effects within the value chain.

## Framing a Theory of Spillover Effects Within the Value Chain

One of the most important and recognizable features of the value chain is how, at each step in the value chain, the output of one process serves as input for another process.

Work moves through the entire system through a series of connected input-processoutput actions. Coordination efforts help to streamline the handoff between processes, helping to eliminate delays between steps and the potential for bottlenecks that can cause work to stall. Research shows that IT can reduce coordination costs both between firms and within the firm itself [61]. One of the keys to improving coordination is information sharing that ensures a greater degree of visibility into activities across process boundaries. The reengineering literature indicates that multiple steps can be removed from an activity, leading to higher productivity if information is openly shared or where IT-enabled access to critical data allows the locus of decision making to be moved closer to the point where the data is generated [18, 26, 64]. Information sharing facilitates uncertainty reduction, which can, in the case of firms with integrated systems, improve coordination of activities across the firm. In general, increased alignment between IT and business strategy at the process level will likely mean that more meaningful information is being generated within each process, information that can then be shared across process boundaries [52, 66]. If IT is providing adequate levels of support for the business strategy, individual business processes are unlikely to be starved of critical information in a way that might prevent the firm from achieving its goals. Besides the provisioning of information, alignment could also mean that activities within a process are automated, a measure that can help to move work through the process faster and with fewer errors. If alignment leads to greater coordination, greater information sharing, and reduced uncertainty, and if these same factors can lead to spillover effects elsewhere in the value chain, alignment could be a key enabler of spillover effects. We turn next to a review of the IS literature to see examples of these spillover effects, the locations where they can occur, and the possible form these effects can assume.

## Spillover Effects: IS Literature Review

The IS literature contains several examples of process-level spillover effects where the benefits of greater alignment spill over to a different process beyond the point where the IT investment first occurred. Some of these examples speak to the impact of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems that, by design, integrate business processes, including linking to business partners, suppliers, and customers [32]. In Table 1, we provide some general examples of spillover effects from the literature. Based on past IT business value research, we use a set of five generic processes (supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, and customer relations) as focal processes within the firm where spillover effects can originate or into which spillover effects can transfer from elsewhere in the firm. Together, these five processes span the value chain [36, 60, 68]. Following the structure of the generic value chain, these processes are linked from left to right, starting with supplier relations and extending through customer relations. The four links that connect these five separate processes means that there are four potential instances of spillover effects. To the extent that customer relations is the last process in the value chain, we posit that there are no other downstream processes that will receive spillover effects from increased alignment in customer relations.

In the area of supplier relations, for example, Bakos [3] explains how interorganizational systems (IOS) link buyers and suppliers either through an electronic exchange or as a way to facilitate information exchange. IOS improve overall supplier relations by making it easier to search for suppliers with specific characteristics while allowing for improved communications and lower coordination costs. However, the benefits of using IOS to closely align with supplier relations also spill over into the area of production and operations by allowing producers greater certainty in deciding the size and timing of their production runs or their ability to satisfy production goals. Similar arguments appear in the electronic data interchange (EDI) literature where the use of IT for managing supplier relations has led to greater productivity and lower production costs [39, 40, 62]. In terms of systems theory, as claimed by

Nevo and Wade [42], a synergistic link between an IT asset and an organizational resource (similar to alignment between IT and a business process) leads to increased IT business value while reducing the risk that business value will be lost to competitive replication. Owing to requisite variety—meaning systems may have different uses and applicability [2]—alignment between IT and supplier activities may allow firms to take advantage of various production possibilities. For example, if firms need to ramp up or scale back production levels quickly, it helps to be able to coordinate changes in procurement with suppliers. With tight alignment in the area of supplier relations, production managers can anticipate increased throughput, greater responsiveness, and fewer obstacles when reacting to change.

Alignment in the area of production and operations can also have positive spillover effects further downstream within the value chain. The interaction between design or research and development (R&D) and manufacturing has led to concepts such as design for manufacturability and mass customization. CAD/CAM (computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing) systems have allowed greater flexibility in production so firms can now accept smaller orders that were once uneconomical [33]. Virtual design tools such as CATIA—used by Boeing, Ford, NASA, and General Electric—have allowed firms to reduce design and production costs, improve production throughput and operating flexibility, and minimize production errors and rework. While this would suggest that spillover effects move from the area of product and service enhancement (R&D) into production/operations, the reverse is also possible. Flexible manufacturing and the use of IT in production, more broadly, has allowed firms to introduce new products or services and make incremental changes to existing product offerings [49, 73]. For example, credit card operations at Capital One were instrumental in the development of new product offerings such as zero percent balance transfers and card reward offerings. IT can also be embedded in products (OnStar) or can enhance the perceived usefulness of existing products (cell phones, GPS, electronic book readers).

Greater product variety, product customization, and overall product and service enhancement can also spill over into the area of sales and marketing as firms use marketing IT to discover new markets or to expand sales to existing markets [45, 78]. Customer relationship management (CRM) systems typically rely on data from elsewhere in the firm such as the availability of production capacity or materials inventory in order to give customers projected delivery details. Products that contain embedded IT such as automobiles and cell phones increasingly upload performance and usage data that are directly relevant for marketing purposes. When information is transferred to sales and marketing systems from other parts of the firm, there is increased scope for more accurate sales forecasting (which can then feed back into supplier-facing systems), greater responsiveness to market trends (which may also feed back into production and new product design systems), and greater market share. Here, also, theory can help to explain how the effects of alignment in the area of product and service enhancement can spill over into sales and marketing. The resource-based view holds that the value of a system or resource is tied to the variety of uses and adaptability of that system [2, 50]. More recently, IT adaptability has been applied to the study of business agility [58]. Where firms are using IT to plan for, and execute, product innovation or ways to add value to their existing products and services, they may be better able to react to unexpected market threats and opportunities [22, 58]. Success, however, comes not simply from creating new products or services but from marketing and selling those products and services in the marketplace. This may require new and perhaps radical marketing approaches. This may mean that firms that are already using IT to market their products and services obtain an even greater return on their IT spending. Equally, the effect of alignment in sales and marketing can spill over into product and service enhancement. Mining of sales and marketing data or business intelligence more generally can result in the discovery of new product possibilities. Datamining can also help marketing and sales personnel to devise new ways of bundling and selling products as a way to expand the different uses of existing products [59].

IT has long been seen as helping to create, maintain, and improve customer relationships [29]. At a time when firms are collecting enormous amounts of data about customers through sales and marketing systems, their ability to understand and predict customer behavior is rising [53, 54, 65]. Marketing data is helping firms to distinguish between customers. For example, financial services firms such as Fidelity Investments and T. Rowe Price treat incoming calls differently based on their ability to link the incoming phone number to an account balance. Sales and marketing systems can also interface with customer-facing systems so as to allow customers to opt into marketing programs or alerts. The resource-based view would also argue that the flow of data between marketing systems and systems used for customer management create conditions that allow firms to enjoy a sustainable strategic advantage [50]. While it is possible for competitors to buy similar CRM systems, market information remains a vital differentiator that cannot be easily duplicated or acquired by competitors. Lastly, Nakata and Zhen [41] note that specific IT capabilities around information processing, storage, and communications help to bolster marketing information quality. This in turn can help firms to become more customer oriented, but it must also be noted that IT capabilities, in isolation, do not produce the same effect. It is only through marketing data that IT can affect customer orientation [41].

The foregoing arguments confirm the potential for alignment-based spillover effects to impact IT business value in downstream processes. These arguments do not rely on the type of IT investment in the firm—for example, whether a firm is using an ERP system or a best of breed approach. Certainly, an ERP system is intended, by design, to facilitate streamlined interprocess information flows so spillover effects may be amplified by ERP systems, but these effects, we suggest, are equally likely if firms are using best-of-breed systems. The point is that work processes are logically and structurally linked. Different types of IT may affect the extent of alignment in individual processes but that need not undermine the potential for spillover effects in later processes. These arguments culminate in the first of our two research hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: The effects of strategic alignment, as measured at the process level, will spill over and impact IT business value in processes further downstream within the value chain.

## Distance and the Scale of Spillover Effects

To the extent that theory supports the presence of spillover effects, we must ask how far along the value chain those effects are likely to travel and whether these effects will grow or contract as the distance from the focal process increases. Theoretical arguments based on the design of the value chain do not say how pervasive spillover effects are likely to be, only that systems are serially linked in such a way that the effects of alignment in a given process are likely to cascade downstream to other parts of the value chain.

Drawing on our prior arguments outlining the existence of spillover effects (H1), we know that the effects of alignment on IT business value are likely to spill over into at least one downstream process. Depending on a firm’s business strategy, other processes may benefit also. As prior research in the IS and organizational studies domain reveals, strategic choice can motivate firms to put more or less emphasis on different parts of the value chain [38, 47, 66, 70, 77]. For example, firms that view themselves as low-cost producers may not care about alignment effects spilling over into marketing or sales or customer relations as these areas are peripheral to their focus on low-cost activities, standardization, and operating efficiency. Instead, it is preferable to consider the effects of alignment spilling over into areas such as production and operations that lie at the core of their strategy [47]. Similarly, product innovators may be more desirous of spillover effects in product and service enhancement than in other areas while customer-intimate firms care more about spillover effects in customer-facing processes such as sales, marketing, and customer relations. Therefore, there may be a desire among firms for spillover effects to be confined within certain processes.

The structure or design of the value chain recognizes the incremental value of process output that serves as input to the next process—a relationship that is key to the existence of spillover effects. In order for those spillover effects to move down the value chain, that is, for the effects of alignment in a particular process to spill over into other processes that might not be directly linked to it, the output from this anchor process must continue to be of value to these other downstream processes. In its simplest form, if a value chain consists of three processes, configured in a linear fashion as P1 → P2 → P3, for the effects of alignment in P1 to influence IT business value in P3, the output of P1 must be of incremental value to P3. Arguably, as we look downstream from P1, the incremental value of alignment in P1 to other processes is likely to diminish. There are two possible reasons for this. First, IS executives are more likely to select IT investments that solve local process concerns, without expecting that the effects of that IT will be greater elsewhere [30, 74]. Thus, a chief information officer (CIO) is more likely to select a supply chain management system based on what it will do for supplier relations rather than what it might do for marketing or other downstream processes. If, indeed, the performance effects of IT on marketing should increase, it may be long after a supply chain management system has gone live as time lags are a factor while the incremental value is unlikely to be as high as what is possible with a dedicated investment in marketing IT or as important as the effect of IT on supplier relations, which was, as we recall, the original intent of the supply chain management investment. Second, whether a firm’s business processes are arranged as a chain, shop, or network, there is a necessary order to the flow of information—an order that is based on the specific needs of the firm. Recent research by Nevo and Wade [42], drawing on systems theory and the resource-based view, argues how IT business value is a function of realized synergy between IT and organizational resources. At each point in the value chain, shop, or network where information passes from process to process, synergy gives rise to emergent capabilities for how the information created by the systems in one process can be used in another process. IT integration requirements likely mean that this information will be customized to the needs of the next downstream process. Returning to the previous example of a supply chain management system, the output produced by this system is most often shared with production scheduling or assembly systems, rather than passing directly to marketing or customer relations. There may be no obvious need to link every system to every other system (even in value networks) because of the particular sequence of steps needed to deliver a finished product or service to a customer. Hence, any spillover effects of alignment in processes that are not directly linked to the focal process will likely diminish as the usefulness of the information created by the focal process becomes progressively less relevant. These arguments give rise to our second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Spillover effects will diminish in scale as the distance from the focal process increases.

## Methodology

To formally t est our hypot heses, we developed a set of matched surveys (two per firm) targeted at IT and business executives. Questions were assigned to each survey according to each respondent group’s area of expertise. We chose to operationalize alignment as profile deviation, one of three approaches that has been widely used in previous alignment studies [8, 55, 66]. Profile deviation measures, for a particular business strategy, the absolute size of the difference or deviation between the actual and ideal IT strategy, where the ideal IT strategy is what is deemed necessary for perfect alignment. As such, the ingredients for profile deviation should ideally come from two independent groups: business strategy from one group and IT strategy from a second group. Separate respondents can also minimize the risk of respondent bias [46]. Hence, we used strategic planners or a CFO (chief financial officer) if someone with strategic planning responsibility could not be identified to provide data on business strategy while IT executives provide data on IT strategy, as proxied by multiple process-level measure of IT use. Strategic planners or CFOs were identified on Hoovers Web site (www.hoovers.com), an online database of senior management distilled from annual reports. Top IT executives were identified in the Directory of Top Computer Executives compiled by Applied Computer Research. Mindful of fears that CIOs could offer biased views of IT effects at the process level—inflating their opinions in order to feign success—we instead used strategic planners/CFOs to report IT business value in different processes.

Data collection proceeded in two phases. First, in 2000, we conducted a survey of publicly traded U.S. and EU firms (headquartered in English-speaking countries: the United Kingdom and Ireland) in the Standard & Poors (S&P) Compustat database with the stipulation that each firm should comprise a single segment or a single line of business. In survey pretest discussions with several IT and business executives, we noted that multisegment firms often had different business strategies and, hence, different alignment profiles in each business unit. There was no uniform firm-wide strategy and IT resources were often shared among business units, introducing a potential source of measurement bias. Our population was further restricted by requiring that all firms in our sample be included in the Computer Intelligence Infocorp (CII) database of annual IT expenditure and IT capital stock.<sup>1</sup> From a population of 5,557 single-segment firms in S&P Compustat, 541 were matched to the CII data set. Matched surveys were subsequently returned by 76 firms, yielding a 14 percent response rate.

For the second phase of data collection in 2003, we focused on small and mediumsize publicly traded U.S. firms with sales ranging from \$100 million to \$3 billion, omitting small firms and the Fortune 500 as many of the latter were surveyed in the first phase of data collection in 2000. The sample frame for the second phase of data collection comprised 2,826 firms, of which 1,600 were randomly surveyed. From this second group, we received 241 matched surveys, yielding a 15 percent response rate. Across both phases of data collection, we received a total of 317 matched surveys for a combined 14.8 percent response rate. This rate is on a par with other matched surveys of business and IT executives in the IS literature. Average sales for the overall sample was \$2.1 billion, S.D. (standard deviation) \$7.3 billion (2000: mean \$6.4 billion; 2003: mean \$798 million). Survey sample characteristics are shown in Table 2.

Tests for nonresponse bias included comparisons of respondents and nonrespondents on various financial measures (sales, profit margin, and total assets). No significant differences were noted. We also contacted IT executives (one side of the match) at a random sample of 3 percent of nonrespondent firms as part of each phase of data collection. Reasons for nonparticipation included travel commitments, confidentiality concerns, lack of interest, and internal company/IS policies prohibiting participation in academic surveys.

## Measuring Business and IT Strategy

While business strategy has been measured in a variety of ways, we use the Treacy and Wiersema [70] typology of operational excellence, customer intimacy, and product leadership as a way to identify if a firm has one or more strategic foci. This approach has been successfully used in prior alignment studies [44]. Respondents, namely, strategic planners, were asked to allocate 100 points among the different value disciplines with the highest allocation going to the focus that they most associated with their corporation.<sup>2</sup> While this approach does not explicitly identify the range or type of activities that a firm might implement in each process, Treacy and Wiersema [70] note that different value disciplines are likely to prompt firms to focus on different processes within the firm. For example, operationally excellent firms are more likely to focus on operations while customer-intimate firms focus on sales, marketing, and customer service and product leaders focus on product and service enhancement. By the same token, different value disciplines mean firms will likely emphasize IT in different processes, potentially revealing an ideal configuration for process-level use of IT within the firm. Thus, for example, operationally excellent firms are more likely to invest in IT that promotes productivity and efficiency within supplier relations or production and operations rather than in IT that promotes product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, or customer relations.

## Measuring Strategic IT Alignment as Profile Deviation

Consistent with arguments in Venkatraman [71], alignment between IT and business strategy has been measured in three primary ways in previous research. Matching is perhaps the simplest approach but it fails to look past strategy labeling to see how strategies are implemented [44]. Moderation uses product or interaction terms formed from measures of IT and business strategy, but it is difficult to interpret since different combinations of IT and business strategy can create identical product scores [11, 13, 66]. Profile deviation, meanwhile, yields an easily understandable measure of the distance between an actual and ideal IT strategy where the ideal IT strategy is the level of IT use that ensures perfect alignment for the current or actual business strategy. There is some debate as to how the ideal IT strategy should be decided: if it is acceptable to use a subgroup of the highest-performing firms in a sample that is under review or whether an expert panel should be convened to decide what might constitute an ideal level of IT use for a business strategy [55]. We chose to follow the latter approach as a way to avoid introducing bias into our analysis.

To decide this ideal IT use configuration, we solicited input from nine experts (two IT directors at IBM Global Services, a BearingPoint senior consultant, five IS academics, and an IT director at a Fortune 50 firm). Academic experts were chosen on the basis of a research interest in alignment while practitioner experts were chosen on the basis of alignment-related comments appearing in the trade press. Each expert was given a tutorial on value disciplines using definitions in Treacy and Wiersema [70] alongside a series of examples of firms in each value discipline. The panel were then asked to independently consider IT use in each of the five primary processes in the value chain for firms using each of the three value disciplines, assigning a value to each of +1, 0, or –1 depending on whether the expert believed the firm’s ideal level of IT use in that process to be above average, average, or below average when comparing firms with different value disciplines. This exercise was repeated for each process with scores being entered in a 5 × 3 table. In Table 3, we show the modal scores for the nine experts and the author. Analysis revealed a high degree of interrater agreement among the group, particularly as to the locus of above average ratings where nine of ten raters (including the author) were in total agreement. In the case of below average and average scores, seven of the ten raters were in agreement in each process. Based on this level of interrater agreement, we opted to use modal ratings to create our alignment scores rather than averages of all individuals ratings.<sup>3</sup>

To expand these expert ratings into customized measures of ideal IT use for each of the processes in the value chain, we multiplied the scores in each row of Table 3 by the corresponding value disciplines data collected from the strategic planner. This approach generates a weighted average measure of ideal IT use per process, recognizing that while many firms may have a dominant value discipline, it is highly unlikely that firms will pursue one and only one value discipline to the exclusion of all others. Next, to identify the difference between actual and ideal IT use in each process, we standardized each firm’s IT use data using means and standard deviations for the firms in their industry (using the industry categories in Table 2) and then took the absolute value of the difference. Higher absolute values suggest greater misalignment and so to convert these values into an alignment score, we subtracted the absolute value from one. This approach to computing alignment as profile deviation is consistent with what other IS researchers have used in prior studies [8, 55, 66]. ## Controls

For estimation purposes, we introduce a number of controls. We control first for the year in which data were collected in case there were events that affected IT business value in each period (Y2K activity in 2000 or more advanced IT innovations in 2003). We also control for location of the firm (European Union or United States) in order to isolate region-specific regulation or cultural factors that might cause IT business value to vary by location. We also control for firm-specific factors such as IT budget as a percentage of sales, firm size (log of sales), and industry (manufacturing or services).<sup>5</sup>

As noted earlier, differences in business strategy could account for differences in spillover effects as firms place greater emphasis on some areas of the value chain where spillover effects are most likely to materialize. Accordingly, we used the value disciplines data to assign firms to one of four strategy groups.

Using a rule of thumb, firms were said to have a dominant focus if they allocated 50 or more points to one and only one value discipline. As shown in Table 5, this rule yielded 117 operationally excellent firms, 53 customer intimate firms, and 45 product leaders. The remaining 102 firms were labeled as having a mixed focus. Discriminant analysis was used to test the robustness of this allocation. There were 98.7 percent or 313 or the 317 allocations validated. Data in Table 5 show that firms labeled OE, CI, or PL had 60.2 percent, 55.9 percent, and 54.0 percent, respectively, of the points assigned by respondents. Dummy variables (0, 1) were created for each firm on the basis of the above results with mixed firms serving as a baseline.

## Survey Instrument Validation

Exploratory factor analysis was first used to validate the selection of 25 IT business value items. As shown in Table 6, principal components analysis with varimax rotation yielded a five-factor structure, explaining 71.6 percent of the total variance. No single factor explained more than half of the total variance. All 25 items factored under their respective process headings. As a further robustness check, since these items had been tested and validated in previous research, we performed a confirmatory factor analysis in EQS. The results of that analysis reveal that a five-factor structure is appropriate and that the individual survey items are correctly evaluating IT business value within each of the five key processes of the value chain.

Checks for construct validity were also conducted on the 25 IT business value items. Convergent validity identifies if the indicators of a factor correlate higher with other indicators of the same factor than with indicators of a different factor, whereas discriminant validity tests whether the indicators of a specific factor load higher on that factor than on any other factor. Convergent validity is assessed by ensuring that the average variance extracted (AVE) for each factor exceeds 0.50 [21]. Discriminant validity is assessed by ensuring that the squared multiple correlation between each factor pair is less than the AVE for each of the two factors in the pair [23]. As seen in Table 7, the five-factor structure is found to be valid under both tests. A reliability test of each factor (Cronbach’s alpha) also exceeds a suggested minimum of 0.80 [43]. Confirmation of validity and reliability means that for hypothesis testing purposes, we were able to create proxy scores for each process-level IT business value factor as the average of their respective indicators.<sup>6</sup>

## Model Estimation

To formally evaluate the presence of spillover effects of strategic alignment on IT business value, we tested a set of five separate regression models in which IT business value (the dependent variable) can be affected by alignment in the same process or in any upstream process. We first assumed a formal value chain structure (this assumption will later be relaxed) containing the following sequence of links: supplier relations → production/operations → product/service enhancement → sales and marketing → customer relations. Using this structure, IT business value in supplier relations can only be affected by alignment in supplier relations. Since there are no upstream processes (by design), there can be no spillover effects into supplier relations from alignment in other processes. As we move along the value chain, the possibility of spillover effects grows. For example, since production/operations is directly linked to supplier relations, IT business value in this area of the value chain is a function of alignment in both production/operations (a direct effect) and supplier relations (a spillover effect). By the end of the value chain, IT business value in customer relations is a function of alignment within customer relations and in each of the four upstream processes, in which case there are four possible spillover effects.

As reported in Table 8, alignment in each process is a significant predictor of IT business value in each process, consistent with previous findings in Tallon [66]. More importantly, there is strong evidence of spillover effects. Of the ten instances where alignment-based spillover effects of alignment are possible (specifically, alignment in supplier relations can spill over into four downstream processes, production/ operations: three processes; product and service enhancement: two processes; and sales and marketing: one process), all but two are significant at the 0.05 level. Alignment in product and service enhancement has a very small effect at the 0.1 level on IT business value in sales and marketing while alignment in sales and marketing has no significant spillover effect on IT business value in the area of customer relations. ## Direction of Spillover Effects

The above analysis assumes a somewhat rigid, albeit generic, value chain structure. As previously explained, firms are under no obligation to follow this structure in designing their value chain. In practice, firms can also deploy value shops that follow partially flexible but largely preset process patterns or value networks where process linkages are entirely flexible. On this basis, the results of our previous analysis might not generalize to firms with these more flexible value chain structures. Hence, we tested an entirely flexible value chain structure in which any process is free to link to any other process. What this allows us to do is to assess the direction of spillover effects—upstream and downstream—rather than assuming that spillover effects must always flow down the value chain. In our earlier estimation in Table 8, we assumed, for example, that IT business value in supplier relations was a function of alignment in supplier relations, but by relaxing the assumption of an imposed value chain structure, we can now evaluate the potential for alignment-based spillover effects on supplier relations from any process in the value chain. The only part of our earlier analysis in Table 8 that is unaffected by relaxing the assumption of a formalized value chain structure involves IT business value in customer relations. Since customer relations is the final process in the generic value chain, it is already open to spillover effects from all other upstream processes. Relaxing the assumption of a structured value chain means that IT business value in customer relations is still open to spillover effects from all other processes.

## Discussion and Implications

In t he same way t hat research on t he organizat ional effect s of IT evolved from a dominant focus on firm-level measures of performance to a narrow focus on first-order effects at the process level [4, 37, 68], research on alignment evolved from focusing on alignment as matching of strategy labels at the firm level or cross referencing between business and IT plans to recognition of the view that alignment derives from how IT supports critical business activities at the process level [13, 55, 66]. Given this reorientation, researchers have an opportunity to look beyond what we know in general of the associative links between alignment and firm performance in order to better understand how alignment generates value, not just that alignment is valuable. Arguably, it is acceptable to use firm-level measures of performance if alignment is operationalized at the firm level. If over time, however, there is a growing acceptance among researchers that alignment is implemented at the process level and should be operationalized at this level since this is likely to be the locus of any first-order effects, there needs to be some consideration for how these effects unfold. The evolution of research on the business value effects of IT progressed in a similar manner [37]. From an initial assessment of IT effects at the firm level—research that ultimately debunked the myth of the IT productivity paradox [9, 20]—research refocused quickly around IT effects at the process level [4, 68]. Research on the alignment effects of IT is evolving in a similar direction but with one very important distinction—whereas IT business value is an output variable, alignment is an input [14]. An assessment of the spillover effects of alignment recognizes that, as an input, the effects of alignment need not be limited to a single process. Indeed, IT executives may want to pursue alignment in a way that facilitates spillover effects. Thus, spillover effects need not be serendipitous and unplanned but rather deliberate and planned.

## How Alignment Creates Value

As research begins to evaluate alignment at the process level, there is a need to consider the locus of alignment (what processes are most aligned) and to recognize that alignment can vary in intensity from one process to another based on differences in strategic foci [55, 66]. Alignment creates value by virtue of how IT is used to support critical activities in each process and how those activities evolve to take account of existing IT capabilities. Hence, the first-order effects of alignment occur at the process level but, more precisely, within those processes that are the focus of greater IT use. Thus, if a firm wants, for example, to enhance the fit between IT and supplier activities by giving suppliers access to critical data or by allowing the firm to have increased visibility into its supply chain partners’ inventory or production system (as Dell has done for its build-to-order model [34]), we can expect the first-order effects of alignment to be within the inbound logistics or supplier relations part of the value chain. However, what our analysis also shows is that spillover effects are both positive and significant. In this case, the benefits of alignment in supplier relations spill over into several downstream processes, in fact spanning the entire length of the value chain so that the overall effects of alignment may be much larger than originally anticipated. By the same token, the effects of misalignment in supplier relations could trickle down the value chain, leading to higher costs of production and poor customer relations. Hence, the process-level effects of alignment are not localized.

Our results show that the flow of spillover effects of alignment is almost entirely in a downstream direction. In the case of alignment in product and service enhancement, which can be generalized to R&D, innovation, and design, there is some evidence of upstream spillover effects migrating into production and operations. The widespread use of CAD systems and their ability to integrate with manufacturing systems has likely meant that systems used for product design can boost manufacturing throughput if design errors can be caught and corrected early on. Equally, design for manufacturability has meant that CAD tools can enhance production scheduling in ways that accommodate mass customization or small lot sizes (often as small as a single item as is the case with Nike’s NIKEiD system) that would otherwise be economically infeasible.<sup>8</sup>

The principle of spillover effects is well established in the literature insofar as it concerns spillover effects of IT investment between sectors of the economy or between producers to consumers [6, 19]. IS research has, in the past, made reference to the potential for spillover effects of IT investments within the firm or between firms, but this is the first time that spillover effects have been applied to alignment at the process level (or indeed to alignment at any level). Results from two antithetical models note that spillover effects apply to each process in the value chain and that these effects do not dissipate as we move further downstream and away from the origination point. Regardless of whether firms have adopted a highly formal value chain or a less formal (more flexible) value shop or value network, the terminal point of most firms’ value systems involves customer relations. If spillover effects move downstream from each process and each process is, in some way, capable of generating spillover effects, it stands to reason that customer relations will be the ultimate beneficiary of alignment in upstream processes or the point where misalignment is most limiting.

## Research Contribution and Limitations

This research extends the literature on both strategic IT alignment and IT business value. We take an important step toward using a process approach to study alignment and its effects on firm performance. Our data is cross sectional and so we lack the ability to map out the step-by-step causal linkages between alignment and firm performance. Nevertheless, by modeling a sequence of primary processes in the value chain and the spillover effects of alignment between those processes, we generate new and useful insights into the way that alignment influences firm performance. We reveal that spillover effects are a significant source of value as the benefits of alignment in upstream processes cascade downstream to later processes. We also determine that there is no prima facie requirement for processes to be directly linked in order for downstream processes to benefit from spillover effects. While spillover effects tend to move downstream, eventually reaching customer relations, there are a few signs that in rare cases spillover effects can trickle upstream. The key lesson for managers is to recognize that if spillover effects cascade downstream toward customer relations as the final point on the value chain, it is essential to optimize alignment in the earliest possible stages of the value chain, beginning with supplier relations. Similarly, managers must realize that pockets of misalignment earlier in the value chain, indicative of failure to use IT to support key activities, could pose significant performance problems in later downstream processes and, indeed, at the firm level.

We also contribute to research on IT business value at a time when executives are facing renewed pressure to prove that IT creates value [35]. The IS literature has repeatedly shown that alignment matters to firm performance, but we lack a clear understanding of how this relationship unfolds. Using ideas from the value chain, we show that the interconnected nature of technologies that span organizational processes provides a foundation for value creation. Spillover effects (or network externalities as it is described in the economics literature) are a useful way for the benefits of technology to diffuse across a sector or between businesses and consumers. Knowing that alignment-induced spillover effects exist and are significant can add to our knowledge of how IT creates value and to deliberately build IT systems that have these effects. Research on IT business value, whether focused on the productivity effects of IT or on how IT investment affects financial measures of firm performance, has tended to focus only on the direct effects of IT. Our findings show that indirect or spillover effects should not be ignored. By moving from a firm- to a process-level analysis of IT business value, spillover effects can be explicitly identified, modeled, and measured.

Our study also suffers from four specific limitations. First, besides our reliance on cross-sectional data, we use executives’ perceptions of IT business value as proxy measures of performance. Although IT research has found that perceptions are an accurate and reliable barometer of actual performance, it would be preferable to adopt objective and independently verifiable process-specific measures (where available). Second, we use five specific process headings. This may be appropriate when dealing with a large sample of firms. Individual firms may vary in the range and specificity of their processes and how these processes are linked. Third, the cross-sectional nature of our study does not account for the fact that spillover effects take time. Doubtless, there is a time dimension to the pace with which spillovers occur; there is a time lag between when the direct effects of alignment are felt within a given process and when those effects begin to move downstream. Although we used a matched survey approach, collecting data from different groups of respondents (CIOs and strategic planners), we collected data from each group simultaneously. As such, our regression models do not apply a time lag that might account for differences in the timing of spillover effects within firms. The timing and nature of IT investment could also affect the pace of spillover effects. Fourth, as part of our data collection efforts, we excluded firms with multiple lines of business or business units as this complicates the task of relating alignment, which varies by business unit, to firm performance, which is a firm-level aggregate. Spillover effects could behave differently in such firms if they share IT or business processes and so we caution against generalizing our results to conglomerates or similar entities.

## Implications for Future Research

The existence of statistically significant spillover effects signals an opportunity for researchers to ask how can these effects be maximized or extended to other parts of the value chain. Insofar as recent IS research has explored the antecedents of alignment—focusing especially on knowledge sharing among IS and business executives [51]—there is the question of context or conditions under which spillover effects are likely to occur. Spillover effects may be deliberate but, as Alter [1] notes, spillover effects occasioned by systems interactions may also be accidental. If so, what can firms do to better plan for spillover effects, to minimize the risk that misalignment could precipitate a cascade of negative spillover effects, to include spillover effects (positive or negative) in IT cost–benefit analyses, and to manage these effects over time?

Our findings also have implications for research on the resource-based view of the firm where the interaction between IT and non-IT resources is seen as an important source of competitive advantage. The manner in which alignment leads to spillover effects may be a source of causal ambiguity for firms trying to duplicate an IT-led strategic advantage. Research that extends the resource-based view to an analysis of IT capabilities could also consider what technologies are most likely to generate positive spillover effects.

Finally, as research moves to a dynamic understanding of alignment, efforts to look beyond cross-sectional or variance-based studies to longitudinal or process-based studies could benefit from seeing the sequencing of organizational efforts that help to create alignment [28, 56]. Knowing that spillover effects are positive and significant, future research could develop and test process models that identify the timing and locus of spillover effects [57]. Case studies could help to dissect the timing of these effects while also probing technological or organizational factors that might restrict or facilitate the flow of spillover effects.

## Implications for Practice

Our results confirm that alignment is a critical source of value and that top executives are justified in regarding alignment as a top priority. Knowing that spillover effects are an important source of value is likely to spur managers to cast a wider net when trying to evaluate the implications of a particular system. Performing a thorough cost–benefit analysis for a new IT project is already difficult when project-specific IT effects defy precise measurement. Spillover effects can amplify the effects of a new IT project, albeit in processes that may seem distant and unconnected to the current project. If, as our analysis shows, there are spillover effects from alignment in supplier relations in distant customer-facing processes, managers may need to identify how new process-specific IT investments can impact other areas of the organization. If alignment can create positive spillover effects, then managers must recognize that misalignment can just as easily impede performance in areas of the value chain that might be strategically important to the firm.

Our results also reveal that process managers, IT project managers, and other IT executives would be wrong to assume that spillover effects are negligible and ought to be discounted either in validating the ex ante or ex post value of IT. While spillover effects might be small in an absolute sense (as noted by the incremental R<sup>2</sup> in Tables 8 and 10), spillover effects still account for 8.6 percent to 14.6 percent of the total explained variance in IT business value at the process level. We may deduce from this that the incremental financial rewards of spillover effects are well worth considering in any assessment of IT performance. By the same token, the absence of value in a particular process could point to alignment problems further upstream in the value chain. IT executives might not be concerned with pockets of misalignment in processes that are regarded as peripheral to their business strategy. Our findings clearly question the wisdom of this decision if the negative consequences of misalignment, even in peripheral parts of the value chain, can still flow to areas further downstream that might be seen as critical to the success of the firm and where any reduction in process performance could have detrimental effects for the entire organization. In cost–benefit analyses where IT executives are asked to consider the likely effects of particular IT investments—often selecting from investment alternatives that produce identical or near-identical value—it may be useful to consider if the spillover effects of each investment alternative are identical. Since spillover effects can be significant, it could be that IT investment decisions are made on the basis of what investment will generate the largest spillover effect. Indeed, it could also happen that an investment that seems to be having little direct effect within its focal process could still be considered a success once all possible spillover effects are included.

## Conclusion

Theoret ical argument s based on t he int erconnect ed design of the value chain provide an important backdrop against which to investigate spillover effects. These effects are triggered, in part, by the need for improved coordination and information sharing across process boundaries and between business partners. The flow of work between processes benefits from the reduction in uncertainty that is normally associated with bottlenecks and other forms of inefficiency. These arguments underscore the presence and usefulness of spillover effects. Alignment remains a primary challenge for organizations. Knowing that the effects of alignment in a particular process can spill over into other areas—as predicted by the value chain—adds to our knowledge and understanding of how alignment creates business value. This shows, for example, that the solution to removing a bottleneck may lie outside the immediate process in some upstream part of the value chain. Business processes are increasingly interconnected but equally vulnerable to misalignment in upstream processes. Resolving these instances of misalignment may be even more critical if the effects of misalignment can be far reaching. As research on the fit between IT and business strategy takes on a new direction, extending the nomological net of antecedents and consequences of alignment, the measurement and management of alignment-based spillover effects may become increasingly necessary and important. IT managers may also benefit from identifying and managing the size and locus of these effects knowing that spillover effects could comprise a significant portion of the total performance effects attributed to IT.
