# RUCKUS IN THE RENTALS, SEEKING NEW ARRANGEMENTS: REMEDYING THE IMPACT OF HOME-SHARING ON URBAN NOISE<sup>1</sup>

Yi Ding Group of Information Systems Management and Analytics, Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, U.K. {Y.Ding.1@wbs.ac.uk}

Moksh Matta Department of Management Information Systems, Beedie School of Business, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, BC, CANADA, {moksh\_matta@sfu.ca}

Ram Gopal Group of Information Systems Management and Analytics, Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, U.K. {Ram.Gopal@wbs.ac.uk}

Haifeng Xu Department of Information, Technology, and Innovation, Antai College of Economics and Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, CHINA {haifengxu@sjtu.edu.cn}

Drawing underutilized residential assets into the tourism economy, home-sharing platforms create tremendous economic value for their owners and users. They also, however, generate negative externalities. This study examines the impact of home-sharing property use on an externality that has been increasingly understood to have adverse consequences for human health and well-being—noise. Based on empirical analyses using a large sample of transactions from a popular platform, we show that property use increases noise, which adversely affects neighbors. More interestingly, we show that the increase in noise is mitigated when property use is spatially or temporally concentrated. Our analyses reveal that a high concentration of property use can enhance the effectiveness of deterrence created through enforcement action against noise complaints. We further conduct empirically informed simulation experiments and propose a nudging algorithm that helps platforms mitigate noise externalities while also fulfilling user preferences and maintaining revenue growth. Contributing to the burgeoning literature on platform externalities, our work highlights the need for further research on potential complementarities between platform and regulatory governance and provides platforms with an alternative approach to the reputationally expensive guest penalties for addressing noise externalities.

Keywords: Home-sharing, noise pollution, digital platforms, externalities, governance

## Introduction

Market transactions often create negative externalities— unintended harmful consequences experienced by third parties (Pigou, 1924). There is a substantial body of research on how transactions associated with traditional business models create negative externalities (e.g., Girotra & Netessine, 2013; Kleindorfer et al., 2005). However, research examining the relationship between digital platforms and negative externalities is just emerging (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018; Chan & Ghose, 2014). Consistent with this line of inquiry, our study attempts to address the following research questions: Does the use of home-sharing platforms (hereafter platforms) increase urban noise (hereafter noise)? If so, how, and what can platforms do to mitigate the noise?

The adverse consequences of noise on humans can range from annoyance, anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances in the short term to cardiovascular and auto-immune problems in the long term (Basner et al., 2014; Fan & Weinhold, 2022). While these adverse effects also exist in rural areas, they are more pronounced in urban contexts due to the higher density of noise sources (Payer, 2007). Studying the role of platforms in contributing to and mitigating noise thus has important implications for the health and well-being of urban societies.

Platforms can be a particularly potent source of noise generation. They alter land use by drawing a vast amount of underutilized residential assets into the tourism economy through reduced transaction costs and network effects (Coase, 1937/1952; Mi & Coffman, 2019, p. 1). Land that was previously used by owners and long-term renters for residential purposes has begun to be used for hedonistic tourist activities with a strong potential for noise generation (Einav et al., 2016; Gurran et al., 2020). Thus, in line with prior research on platforms and other externalities, such as the suppression of entrepreneurship (Burtch et al., 2018) and hotel revenues (Zervas et al., 2017), an investigation into the impact of platform entry into a region on noise generation may be warranted.

However, focusing on platform entry can overlook the considerable heterogeneity in externalities that arise within an operating region. For example, while the overall noise in New York City (NYC) may increase following platform entry, the increase may vary across different neighborhoods and over time. In addition to improving our theoretical understanding of the underlying mechanisms that contribute to such heterogeneity, examining factors that influence it also enables the generation of policy insights for more nuanced governance actions (as opposed to a region-wide ban on platform entry). Motivated by this potential to improve our theoretical understanding and generate targeted policy insights, our study delves into examining how the extent and patterns of homesharing property (hereafter property) use following a platform’s entry into a geographic region affects noise generation within the region.

While prior studies have largely underscored the role of regulatory governance in addressing platform externalities, some research has highlighted the potential of self-regulation by platforms for tackling externalities (Cohen & Sundararajan, 2015; Han et al., 2022). This potential derives from the informational advantage that platforms have over regulators in tracking and shaping the supply and demand for properties. However, in designing governance mechanisms platforms, like traditional business models, are confronted by fundamental trade-offs between societal well-being and revenue growth (Le Grand, 1990). On the one hand, platforms have a moral and social obligation to mitigate negative externalities and uphold societal well-being (Hosmer, 1994; Mintzberg, 1983). On the other hand, they also have a fiduciary responsibility towards shareholders expecting revenue growth. Effective platform governance thus requires striking an optimal balance between societal well-being and revenue growth. Therefore, our study also explores how platforms can achieve an optimal balance in mitigating their impact on noise generation.

Using a sample of platform transactions from New York City (NYC), we found strong empirical evidence to suggest that noise is positively and significantly affected by the extent of property use within neighborhoods. More interestingly, however, we found that these positive and significant effects weaken with an increase in the spatial and temporal concentration of properties. A deeper examination of the mechanism underlying these results revealed that spatially and temporally concentrated properties increase the effectiveness of deterrence or “chilling” effects (e.g., Ariel et al., 2020; Sherman & Weisburd, 1995) arising from law enforcement action against noise complaints. By improving the visibility and awareness of law enforcement actions among property hosts and guests, concentrated properties tend to deter potential noise incidents.

Motivated by our empirical findings and prior literature highlighting the potential of information provisioning as a platform governance mechanism for reducing negative effects (e.g., Cui et al., 2020; Edelman & Luca, 2014), we conducted empirically informed simulation experiments. Our findings reveal that platforms can indeed strike an optimal balance between noise reduction and revenue growth by implementing a nudging algorithm that directs prospective guests towards properties that, while satisfying the guest’s property preferences along dimensions such as price, quality and functionality, are also proximal to an existing concentration of properties.

## Literature Review

## Sharing Platforms and Negative Externalities

A growing body of research has examined the negative externalities of sharing platforms. Focusing on ridesharing, Ge et al. (2016) found evidence of discrimination against individuals with African American sounding names. Berger et al. (2018) found that Uber’s entry in metropolitan areas resulted in a relative earnings decline for taxi drivers. Burtch et al. (2018) found that Uber’s entry adversely affected entrepreneurial activity in the targeted region. Barrios et al. (2020) showed that the entry of ridesharing is associated with an increase in the number of motor vehicle fatalities. Erhardt et al. (2019) found that the arrival of ridesharing worsens the problem of traffic congestion in cities.

Focusing on home-sharing platforms, evidence of discrimination against Black Airbnb hosts and guests has been documented by Edelman and Luca (2014), Edelman et al. (2017), and Cui et al. (2020). These studies found that Black hosts are not able to charge the same rates as non-Black hosts and that accommodation requests from Black guests are more likely to be turned down. Horn and Merante (2017), Barron et al. (2021), and Liang et al. (2022) found that Airbnb increases rents through its suppression of the supply of rental units. Zervas et al. (2017), Chang and Sokol (2022), and Dogru et al. (2020) found that Airbnb’s entry has a negative impact on hotel revenues. Han et al. (2022) and Xu et al. (2019) found a positive association between Airbnb use and crime rates. This literature, however, reveals three gaps—(1) While the impact of platforms on a variety of negative externalities has been examined, research exploring their impact on noise is still in its nascent stages. (2) While the impact of platform entry on the overall externality in a region has been examined, the theoretical mechanisms shaping the heterogeneity in externalities within an operating region have remained largely overlooked. (3) While the role of institutional governance in mitigating platform externalities has been highlighted, the role and effectiveness of self-governance by platforms for tackling externalities remains understudied. Our study attempts to address these specific gaps.

## Sharing Platforms and Urban Noise

The adverse effects of noise on human well-being have been extensively demonstrated in controlled lab settings (e.g., Li et al., 2014; Rylander et al., 1977). In urban contexts in particular, prior literature examining different sources of noise, such as airport (e.g., Janssen et al., 2011), railway (Lim et al., 2006), industrial (e.g., Ali, 2011), military (e.g., Nykaza et al., 2013), traffic (e.g., Ali & Tamura, 2003), and construction (Lee et al., 2015) noise, has demonstrated the negative association between noise exposure and human well-being. In this literature on urban noise sources, however, platforms have been largely overlooked as a potential source of noise. This research gap is particularly glaring, as platforms fundamentally alter the intended purpose of land use (Espinosa, 2016). Large swaths of land expected to be used for residential activities have been transformed into land used for tourist activities. The scale and scope of such a transformation may be substantial, given the lowered transaction costs and network effects induced by platforms (Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005). Platforms, may thus be a potent source of noise in urban contexts and warrant deeper consideration as an antecedent. Our study also addresses this gap in the extant literature on urban noise.

## Data, Variables, and Model

Data: We drew on data from multiple sources. To capture our dependent variable—noise, we used data on noise complaints from NYC Open Data. In this dataset, each noise complaint is associated with a location, a time stamp, and a brief description. Once a noise complaint is logged, an officer (primarily from the NYC Police Department) is dispatched to the location for enforcement. Our independent variable is property use, which was measured based on Airbnb transaction data from AirDNA. This dataset contains transactions for each Airbnb property in NYC since October 2014.

Dependent and independent variables: We aggregated our data at the zip code-daily level. Our sample covers 205 zip code areas spanning from October 2014 to December 2019. The dependent variable is the number of noise complaints reported in zip code area i on day t (Noise\_Complaints). Noise complaints to government agencies are widely used as an indicator of urban noise exposure (e.g., Hume et al., 2003; Nykaza et al., 2013). The independent variable is the number of properties reserved for actual occupancy in zip code area i on day t (Reservations).

Spatial and temporal concentration of properties: We used Ripley’s K-function (e.g., Diggle & Chetwynd, 1991; Giuliani et al., 2014; Stamp & Lucas, 1990) to measure spatial concentration of reserved properties and an entropybased measure of clumpiness to measure temporal concentration. The details and visual illustrations are presented in Table 1.

Control variables: We incorporated a wide range of control variables into our model estimation. These variables relate to the categories—(1) property listing, (2) community conditions, (3) housing conditions, (4) tourism and traffic conditions, and (5) demographic conditions. Taken together, these controls account for the general attractiveness of a property or neighborhood, and their noise-generating potential. ## Robustness Tests

## Tests for Endogeneity

Main relationship: To address potential endogeneity concerns affecting the main relationship between the independent variable and dependent variable, we used an instrumental variable (IV) approach. We employed four IVs: (1) number of arrival flight cancelations (Cancelation), (2) the occurrence of hurricane Irma (Irma), (3) the average response rate of Airbnb hosts addressing guest inquiries (Response\_Rate), and (4) an exogenous one-time targeted purge of over 1000 illegal listings by Airbnb in November 2015 (Listing\_Purge). Detailed descriptions of all these instrumental variables can be found in Table 4.

We conducted three sets of IV analyses. First, Cancelation and Response\_Rate were employed as IVs, utilizing the full sample for estimation. The second set narrowed its focus to a specific case of flight cancelations attributed to Hurricane Irma. By integrating the IV Irma with Response\_Rate, we confined the sample to the third-quarter observations to ensure comparable tourist arrival numbers. Lastly, using a more complete set of IVs (Cancelation, Response\_Rate and Listing\_Purge), we restricted our sample to the observations of the last quarter of each year to accommodate the characteristics of the fourth IV (Listing\_Purge). Considering that both of our endogenous variable and dependent variable were in the form of counts, we employed a control function approach recommended by Lin and Wooldridge (2019). In this method, the generalized residual gained from the first stage is inserted into the second-stage estimation.

Moderating relationships: To address endogeneity concerns related to moderators, we employed four IVs. For spatial concentration of property reservations, we used the IVs of Dist\_Waterbody and Spat\_NewListing. For temporal concentration of property reservations, we used SD\_AirPassenger and Temp\_NewListing. Detailed descriptions of all IVs can be found in Table 4. For moderation analyses, we also applied the control function approach because it accounts for nonlinear nature of moderation effects (Wooldridge, 2015).

Difference-in-differences (DiD) analysis at building level: We also estimated the impact of property use on noise complaints at the building level using a staggered DiD design. Airbnb entering different buildings at distinct times created an ideal natural experimental setting. Based on the shortest geographic distance, we tied each Airbnb property and noise complaint, to a particular building (7- digit building identifier number (BIN) and geographical coordinates were acquired from NYC Open Data). In NYC, there were 860,303 buildings in total, with 11.49% having Airbnb entered. Considering the sparsity in daily noise complaints from each building, we aggregated the data at the building-monthly level and estimated the DiD model as follows

$$
N o i s e \_ C o m p l a i n t s _ {i, t} = \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \beta E n t r y _ {i, t} + \lambda C o n t r o l s _ {i, t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t},\tag{4}
$$

where i indexes building. Noise\_Complaints<sub>i,t</sub> is the number of noise complaints filed in building i in month t. α<sub>i</sub> captures the building fixed effects. Entry<sub>i,t</sub> is a binary variable with a value of “1” indicating that the platform entered building i in month t and “0” otherwise.

Table 6 presents the results of the building-level analyses. Model (1) is based on the full sample and Models (2) and (3) are based on a restrictive sample with a more “comparable” control group consisting of buildings that are within close proximity to the building the platform has entered (≤500m and ≤200m respectively). All three models show that the entry of the platform resulted in more noise complaints $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 9 , p <$ 0.01 in Model 1; $; \beta = 0 . 0 3 0 , p < 0 . 0 1$ in Model $2 ; \beta = 0 . 0 3 2 , p <$ 0.01 in Model 3).

As a robustness check, we replaced Entry with a series of binary variables indicating the chronological distance of an observation period with respect to the timing of platform entry (in years). The results are reported in Models (4)-(6) of Table 6. As expected, the coefficients for the pre-entry dummies are not significant, thereby validating the parallel trend assumption; the coefficients’ post-entry dummies highlight the positive effects of property use on noise complaints over time.

Moreover, to address the potential treatment heterogeneity in evaluating the parallel trend within the staggered DiD setting, we also employed the doubly robust DiD estimator based on stabilized inverse probability weighting (Callaway & Sant’Anna, 2021). ## Tests for Mechanisms

Main relationship: Although we found that property use does increase noise, the channel through which such an increase occurs is unclear. Tourists using properties may exercise their hedonistic needs by, for example, engaging in loud conversations, loud sex, singing, dancing, or playing games (in residential and/or commercial areas). They may also exercise their need for mobility by walking or ride-hailing in order to travel between different locations. Prior studies have highlighted how property use is associated with “crowding, traffic” (Petruzzi et al., 2020, p. 5), and “parking problems” (Gurran & Phibbs, 2017, p. 85). Given the potential of noise generated by such activities, we conducted additional analyses to discern which of these needs contributes significantly to noise. We did so by splitting noise complaints into those related to (1) hedonistic activities in residential locations, identified by tax classes of properties in NYC with Class 1 and 2 designated as residential properties; (2) hedonistic activities in commercial locations such as bars and restaurants, informed by restaurant inspection data; and (3) mobility activities occurring on streets or sidewalks, identified by the specific mention of these locations or vehicles in noise descriptions. We found that the observed increase in noise is likely a result of hedonistic tourist activities in residential locations (see Models 1-3 in Table 8).

Moderating relationship: Prior literature reveals two potential mechanisms that can explain the negative moderating effects of the spatiotemporal concentration of properties. First, it is possible that law enforcement actions taken by local authorities (e.g., the New York Police Department) may create a deterrence or chilling effect in the vicinity (Sherman & Weisburd, 1995; Ariel et al., 2020) on the same day or subsequent days and thereby discourage noise generation. In this regard, a greater spatial or temporal concentration of property reservations could indicate that more property hosts/guests are subject to the deterrence effect. Second, as reserved properties become more concentrated and noise becomes more intense, community members may psychologically or behaviorally adapt to the noise (e.g., keeping windows closed) (Maris et al., 2007; Stansfeld et al., 2000). To ascertain which mechanism is at work, we conducted six tests, which are explained in Table 7.

Overall, we found evidence in support of deterrence effects being responsible for the negative moderation effects of spatiotemporally concentrated properties. We did not find support for the alternative argument, which suggests that the effects may be a result of community adaptation.

Excluding public events: The relationship between property use and noise complaints may be confounded by public events held in the area. To address this concern, we excluded observations on the days on which public events took place in a focal zip code area from our analysis sample. We first excluded outdoor park events, and then further excluded street events (e.g., parades and farmers markets) and indoor events (e.g., film screenings) (see Models 4-9 in Table 9). All results are consistent with our main findings.

Sensitivity of concentration measures: In the main analysis, we used a 0.01 degree of latitude/longitude as the search radius for the spatial concentration measure (Ripley’s K). In this analysis, we changed this parameter to a 0.001 degree (0.11 km), 0.0025 degree (0.28 km), 0.005 degree (0.56 km), and 0.0075 degree (0.83 km) of latitude/longitude (see Models 1-2 in Table 10). Similarly, in the main analysis, we used 30 days before day t as the observation period for the temporal concentration measure (Clumpiness). In this analysis, we changed the observation period length to 15 days, 45 days, 60 days, and 90 days before day t (see Models 3-4 in Table 10).

Pre-post analysis: To further establish that property use is a significant contributor to noise, we conducted an additional test by exploiting the launch of “Airbnb Neighbors” (www.airbnb.com/neighbors) in June 2016, a platform feature that allows neighbors to report noise caused by Airbnb guests (Newton, 2016). The feature essentially serves as an alternative channel for neighbors to file complaints. If the property use is indeed responsible for an increase in noise (rather than other omitted factors), then, upon the launch of the feature, one would expect some of the noise complaints to be diverted from government agencies to Airbnb Neighbors, reducing the number of complaints to government agencies. We thus constructed a binary variable, Post\_Feature<sub>t</sub>, with the value of “1” indicating that day t is after June 2016 and “0” otherwise. We replaced the moderator in Specification (3) with Post\_Feature to compare the effects of property use before and after the feature launch. Models (5)-(6) in Table 10 show that the effects of the property use on noise were weakened after the launch of the feature, thereby further validating our claim that the increases in noise were indeed likely a result of property use.

## Simulation Experiments

With the objective of proposing a platform governance mechanism that strikes a balance between revenue growth and noise reduction, we conducted empirically informed simulation experiments. Motivated by studies that highlight the role of strategic information provisioning in mitigating the negative consequences of the platform (e.g., Cui et al., 2020; Edelman et al., 2017), we demonstrate the effectiveness of another form of information provisioning— rank-ordering of property listings as a nudge mechanism for addressing noise. By doing this, platforms can reduce noise without hurting their revenue growth while also aligning with guests’ preferences.

Simulation experiments: We conducted two experiments. Both were based on data from zip code area 11211 on December 31, 2019, a vibrant community of properties in Brooklyn. 1,219 out of 5,454 properties were reserved on that chosen day. The majority of the properties were located within a longitude range of 3.3 km and a latitude range of 1.9 km (Figure 1a). In Experiment 1, we proposed a viable approach for guiding customers in their property selection and established the effectiveness of the nudging mechanism in reducing noise. In Experiment 2, we employed estimates from the first experiment to analyze the effect size of the nudging mechanism on a larger scale—(1) throughout NYC, and (2) beyond a day to a week/month.

Nudging algorithm: The ability to nudge guests towards a target property depends on (1) increased visibility and (2) enhanced probability of selection (Richardson et al., 2007). To achieve the first objective, we considered changing the rank order of properties displayed on the platform. Based on eye-tracking studies on online search, the majority of users’ eye-movement activity concentrates at the top of the results page (PRweb, 2005; Schwartz, 2014). Therefore, pushing target properties to the top can likely improve their visibility. To attain the second objective, properties aligned with the guest’s preferences were chosen as top-ranked. Two variables were constructed to guide the selection—(1) pairwise property similarity score and (2) guests’ choice preference score specific to each property.

The similarity score measured the cosine similarity between two properties based on attributes related to properties only (e.g., property type, number of bedrooms, and amenities). The cosine similarity was transformed into a 0-1 range, where a score of 0 represented total dissimilarity, and 1 indicated identical properties (the score was cross-validated by human coders). After computing pairwise similarity scores for all properties, we obtained a $5 , 4 1 4 \ \times \ 5 , 4 1 4$ similarity score matrix.

The preference score, which decides guest purchases, involves factors beyond the desirable attributes of a property—in particular, the value-for-money proposition. Hence, in addition to the above property attributes, we also included price and quality indicators (annual revenue, overall rating, and number of reviews). Based on these variables and the properties chosen for our sample (1,219 out of the 5,414 properties), we employed a Probit model to predict a preference score for each property. This produced a 5,414 × 1 vector of preference scores, ranging from 0 (highly unlikely to be preferred) to 1 (highly likely to be preferred).

Simulation procedure: We selected the first 200 properties reserved by guests based on their booking time and retained them in order to obtain a preliminary understanding of their spatial distribution (Figure 1a). During each iteration of a subsequent reservation made by a guest, whose actual choice of property (X) was known, two constraints were applied to select the property as top-ranked (Y). First, a similarity score above w $( w \in ( 0 . 5 5 , 0 . 6 0 , 0 . 6 5 , 0 . 7 0 , 0 . 7 5 , 0 . 8 0 , 0 . 8 5 , 0 . 9 0 ,$ 0.95)) was required between the top-ranked property (Y) and the chosen property (X). Second, the preference score of the top-ranked property had to exceed that of the guest’s chosen property. The first constraint ensured that the nudged property satisfied the desirable attributes required by the guest, while the second constraint enhanced the likelihood of the guest selecting the property. Given the two constraints, we opted for the property yielding the highest Ripley’s K based on properties that were already reserved, indicating the greatest degree of spatial concentration. This procedure was repeated 1,019 times, from the 201st booking guest to the 1,219th booking guest. Figures 1b-1d illustrate the spatial distributions of actual reserved properties (Xs) and properties after nudging (Ys).

Results of Experiment 1: Based on properties after nudging (Ys), we predicted the number of noise complaints and compared it with the predicted number of noise complaints based on actual reserved properties (Xs). Using December 2019 data, we trained 11 models and assessed their performance using RMSE and MAE through a 10-time 10- fold cross-validation. SVR was chosen for its superior and efficient performance. For noise complaint prediction in area 11211 on December 31, 2019, the recorded number was 9, while the SVR prediction was 9.18.

Figure 2a shows that with a high w value (0.95), the predicted decrease in noise complaints is minimal (0.01%; with nudged properties mirroring actual reservations). As w decreases from 0.95 to 0.55, there is a marked increase in predicted noise reduction, peaking at 9.72% at w = 0.55. A critical turning point is noted at w = 0.75: noise reduction increases noticeably until this point (reaching 7.89% at w = 0.75), but the rate of increase moderates thereafter. Figure 2b reveals an inverted U-shape between the similarity score threshold (w) and the growth in preference score. At w = 0.75, the average percentage increase in preference score peaks at 25.3%, indicating notable guest preference elevation upon nudging. The low improvement in preference score at two ends is because nudged properties are identical to guests’ reserved properties (w = 0.95) or because nudged properties are not very similar to guests’ reserved properties (w = 0.55).

Importantly, this nudging approach did not adversely affect the platform’s revenue. The revenue differences across different similarity score thresholds range from -1.75% to 3.14%, with a revenue difference of only 0.31% observed at the threshold of 0.75. In sum, this nudging algorithm benefits all three parties (i.e., guests, platforms, and community). As long as guests preferences are well understood (through their past reservation history or a prebooking survey), the nudging algorithm ensures that guests’ property requirements can be met—guests are even provided with better options that have higher preference scores. Platforms contribute positively to the community by generating less noise without incurring additional financial costs.

Based on the simulated data, we further validated the “deterrent” effect of enforcement actions. We identified the top 10 locations with the most complaints filed and calculated the average number of hypothetical properties within a proximity of 100 meters to these locations. People in these hypothetical properties could potentially be aware of the noise complaint investigation. Figure 2c shows that the average number of neighbor properties exposed to the investigation increases from 8.7 (w = 0.95) to 10.8 (w = 0.55). This suggests that the deterrent effects of a noise complaint investigation are more pronounced when reserved properties are spatially clustered. Moreover, the increasing trend runs approximately counter to the noise reduction trend in Figure 2a, which strengthens the notion that spatial clustering of properties mitigates noise, potentially due to the deterrent exerted by noise complaint investigations.

Results of Experiment 2: Building upon the results of Experiment 1, we extrapolated the potential effects of implementing the nudging mechanism across all zip code areas of NYC. Specifically, we utilized the estimates obtained with a similarity score threshold of 0.75, which yielded a 33.2% increase in Ripley’s K from 0.0164 to 0.0219 in Experiment 1. By assuming the same increase in Ripley’s K across all zip code areas, we predicted the percentage change in noise complaints in NYC on December 31, 2019, and found that the predicted number of noise complaints decreased by 6.6% in the nudged scenario compared to the actual scenario (the noise complaint number decreased from 786 to 734). When we further extended our estimation beyond a single day to a week (December 25-31, 2019), we found a 5.6% decrease in noise complaints in the nudged scenario (the complaints number decreased from 7,227 to 6,822). Furthermore, when we extended our analysis to the entire month of December 2019, we predicted a 6.2% reduction in noise complaints with the implementation of the nudging strategy, and the number of noise complaints decreased from 28,300 to 26,534.

## Implications and Limitations

Our findings have implications for several streams of research. First, there is growing interest in understanding how technology innovations such as social media and artificial intelligence harm society and politics (e.g., Berente et al., 2021; Haidt & Allen, 2020). Tying into this literature on the “dark side” of technological innovations, our findings specifically demonstrate how platforms can also harm societal well-being. Second, the literature on platform governance is dominated by internal concerns about increasing trust, generativity, and innovation (e.g., Cennamo & Santalo, 2013; Rai et al., 2019). Our findings, however, highlight the need to also understand how platform governance can address externalities such as noise. Third, there is also growing research interest in understanding the legitimacy challenges that confront sharing platforms’ entry into new institutional contexts (e.g., Garud et al., 2022; Uzunca et al., 2018). Our work establishes noise externalities as a potential legitimacy challenge and highlights how platforms can work with institutions to address this challenge.

Our study also contributes by establishing the spatiotemporal distribution of properties as a key boundary condition in the link between property use and noise. Studies are increasingly examining the question of designing governance mechanisms for reducing the negative consequences of platforms, such as racial discrimination (Cui et al., 2020; Edelman & Luca, 2014; Edelman et al., 2017). Complementing such studies, our findings underscore the importance of the spatiotemporal concentration of properties as a key parameter around which governance can be designed. For guiding the design of platform governance, our study also demonstrates the effectiveness of a nudging algorithm. The proposed nudging algorithm specifically helps platforms to effectively address the trade-off between noise reduction and revenue growth.

Among growing calls to address the negative externalities of sharing economy platforms, an ongoing debate in the literature relates to the use of platform self-regulation versus government regulation (Cohen & Sundararajan, 2015; Han et al., 2022). Against this backdrop, our finding offers evidence in support of a more hybrid approach. It demonstrates how internal platform governance (through information provisioning) can complement external institutional governance (noise complaint investigations) to achieve reductions in negative externalities. Finally, in addition to addressing the trade-off between revenue growth and noise reduction, our proposed mechanism presents platforms with an alternative to more drastic measures—such as the banning of hedonistic tourist activities or the implementation of a Pigouvian-style (Pigou, 1924) noise tax, which might permit the offender to continue committing harm as long as a fee is paid (Masur & Posner, 2015).

Our study does have some limitations. First, our study examines the effects of the patterns of property use on noise in a major metropolitan city. This limits our ability to examine how diverse institutional environments interact with platform governance to influence externalities. Future studies could explore such interactions between regulatory and platform governance by studying our focal relationship across different jurisdictions. Second, although our study demonstrates the adverse effects of property use on noise, it does not explore the reputational impact of this externality on the platform. Examining the reputational impact could help platforms better calibrate their governance response to the externality. Third, our study does not examine the psychological factors that influence guests’ propensity to make noise or neighbors’ propensity to file complaints. Understanding these psychological factors could further enrich our understanding of heterogeneity in the effects of property use on noise.

Finally, although our study proposes using rank-ordering in property listings as a nudge mechanism, such nudging comes with the risk of lowered customer satisfaction and growth. We believe that investigating how platforms can address this risk offers several lucrative avenues for future research. Future studies could empirically explore the trade-offs between social and legitimacy gains from this nudging strategy and the potential economic loss associated with it. Studies could also examine platform and environmental contingencies where the value of social (e.g., CSR scores) and legitimacy gains (e.g., regulatory leniency, community support) for the platform outweigh the risk of economic loss, and vice versa. Studies might also investigate how strategic changes to the timing and frequency of nudges can affect such trade-offs. Studies could also explore how offering incentive mechanisms (e.g., discounts, upgrades) along with nudges and disclosures of expected social gains to customers (e.g., fair trade practices) might influence their purchase decisions. Ultimately, we note that platforms regularly deploy similar nudging strategies with trade-offs (e.g., Uber’s driver nudges extend driving hours but increase the risk of driver attrition, traffic, and accidents).
