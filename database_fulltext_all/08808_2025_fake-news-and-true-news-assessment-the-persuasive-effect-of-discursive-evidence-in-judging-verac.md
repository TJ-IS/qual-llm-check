# FAKE NEWS AND TRUE NEWS ASSESSMENT: THE PERSUASIVE EFFECT OF DISCURSIVE EVIDENCE IN JUDGING VERACITY<sup>1</sup>

Abayomi Baiyere Digital Technology Area Group, Smith School of Business, Queen’s University Kingston, CANADA, and Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, DENMARK {a.baiyere@queensu.ca}

Jan M. Bauer Department of Management, Society and Communication, Copenhagen Business School Frederiksberg, DENMARK {jmb.msc@cbs.dk}

Ioanna Constantiou Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School Frederiksberg, DENMARK {ic.digi@cbs.dk}

Daniel Hardt Department of Management, Society and Communication, Copenhagen Business School Frederiksberg, DENMARK {dha.msc@cbs.dk}

Individuals are often unable to assess the veracity of news claims—especially on social media platforms. Recent research has suggested that interventions indicating normative signals, such as flagging false claims, are not always effective. We propose an approach in which users are provided with discursive evidence to consider in determining the veracity of claims rather than depending on normative true or false flags. We conducted a series of experiments to explore the effects of different forms of discursive evidence on individual judgments of the veracity of news claims. We found that providing such evidence can significantly improve individuals’ judgment of both true and false news claims—with certain caveats. Providing discursive evidence with high evidence strength leads to a general increase in veracity judgment. Discursive evidence containing items with lower evidence strength may shift believability— thus improving judgments for either true or false claims but degrading them for the other. We also identify important asymmetries between true and false claims, finding that the effect of some evidence may be improved if people are in a more critical mindset—for example, by priming them to think about the concept of truth and lies. Taken together, these results extend knowledge on the problem of fake news and may suggest effective approaches to address the problem without diminishing attention to true news.

Keywords: Fake news, true news, discursive evidence, veracity judgment, accuracy judgment, evidence strength, detection, persuasion, priming, misinformation, experimental study

## Introduction

The issues related to fake news have become an important concern for both academics and society more generally with the rise of digital technologies (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Kim et al., 2019; Lazer et al., 2018; Moravec et al., 2019).

The role of digital technologies such as social media in increasing the scale, reach, rapidity, and professional appearance of fake news (Bakir & McStay, 2018) has made it a pertinent topic of concern for IS scholars (Baiyere et al., 2020; Horner et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2022; Kim & Dennis, 2019). In social media, user-generated content is delivered on a mass scale, regardless of its veracity (Conroy et al., 2015; Kane et al., 2014). This deluge of content has made it a challenging task for individuals to determine which news claims are true and which are false.

The rise of social media platforms as a means of news consumption has not favored legacy news organizations, which are required to uphold journalistic standards of veracity in their reporting. This has created fertile ground for fake news to flourish (Bakir & McStay, 2018). As the traditional barriers of entry have been reduced (e.g., cost of generating, editing, and distributing news), legacy news outlets that are traditionally associated with high levels of public trust and credibility have been further undermined (Lazer et al., 2018; Matherly & Greenwood, 2024). These developments have changed the conversation about the veracity of news claims. People have become polarized and are increasingly drifting away from a shared account of important developments or historical events. Consequently, effective strategies to support people in correctly assessing the veracity of claims circulating in online media are needed.

Many recent empirical studies have explored normative interventions such as flags or warning tags (Machete & Turpin, 2020; Moravec et al., 2019), while others have advocated non-normative interventions such as pre-bunking (Roozenbeek & van der Linden, 2019a, 2019b), nudging accuracy considerations (Pennycook et al., 2021), or increasing cognitive engagement (Bago et al., 2020). While many non-normative approaches lack informational value and have limited persuasiveness, the normative flags, which are dominant in prior IS literature, have shown mixed results. Indeed, recent studies and practical interventions (Andersen & Søe, 2020) have used flags to aid people in recognizing fake news by providing a clear normative indication to individuals that a claim is false. The use of such warning flags has found support in some previous studies that have demonstrated a reduced effect of false claims (Cook et al., 2017; Moravec et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2020b). However, other studies have shown that such warnings may be ineffective for various reasons, such as confirmation bias (Moravec et al., 2019). In fact, in some cases, warning flags have been reported to backfire (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010; Schaffner & Roche, 2017). Since extant studies indicate mixed results, the literature on the effect of flag warnings is, at best, unclear (Moravec et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2021; Pennycook et al., 2020a). Consequently, Moravec et al. (2019), who found that flags have no effect on judgments about truth, called for a novel framework to better warn social media users of fake news.

We respond to this call and shift the focus from normative warnings to engaging people more in the judgment process with targeted evidence that has informational value. We suggest that one important reason for the inconclusive findings about flags is that they pass judgment on a claim. This sort of normative approach risks dividing people into groups in that the warning either confirms or refutes a person’s prior stance vis-à-vis the claim. When the flag contradicts prior beliefs, it may backfire, prompting people to believe the exact opposite of the flag’s warning (Schaffner & Roche, 2017). Underpinning this effect, we argue, is that flags do not typically provide evidence or justification for a judgment call on a claim. Notably, the underlying assumption is that people are expected to accept the predefined judgment without the ability to weigh the evidence behind the judgment.

In this regard, we propose discursive evidence as a complementary approach because it provides informational value while avoiding any explicit normative message. We define discursive evidence as a form of evidence with content that can be considered in determining the veracity of a news claim without necessarily imposing a judgment. This view draws from the notion of dianoia, which captures the faculty of independently drawing inferences from a premise to arrive at a conclusion (Kaluori, 2005). As an intervention, discursive evidence may potentially improve an individual’s judgment of news claims while addressing some limitations of flags and other non-normative interventions. Hence, in this paper, we investigate the value of discursive evidence as a potential contribution to addressing the problem of fake news and ask: How can an individual’s judgment of the veracity of news claims be improved by discursive evidence?

To answer this question, we performed three experimental studies. We tested the effectiveness of different types of discursive evidence in supporting users’ judgment of the veracity of news claims. We began with two forms of discursive evidence. We call the first form “curated” evidence, which is produced by a fact-checking website. We call the second form “uncurated,” which is produced by a simple web search of the news claim itself. The results of our first study show that curated evidence leads to improvements in aggregate veracity judgments while uncurated evidence does not. However, in a second study, we found that the limited overall effectiveness of uncurated evidence on veracity judgments could be increased by priming the concept of truth and lies. These two studies show that discursive evidence may result in statistically significant improvements in the judgment of news claims. However, this raises another question: What is it specifically about discursive evidence that leads to these changes in judgment—especially, in the uncurated category? We designed a third study to explore the specific effects of different types of evidence to test different levels of evidence strength—which we define along the dimensions of helpfulness and relevance and which draws on the notion of argument strength in the literature on persuasion (O’Keefe, 2002; Perfors et al., 2018; Reinard, 1988). In this third study, we replicated most core results from the previous studies and uncovered the specific effect of particular evidence items on the ability of individuals to judge the veracity of a claim.

Our study makes four main contributions. First, regarding discursive evidence and veracity judgment, our study responds to the call in the IS field by Moravec et al. (2019) by showing how discursive evidence may significantly improve judgment of the veracity of news claims. When the discursive evidence provided has high strength, improvements are comparably large and robust. With low evidence strength, discursive evidence improves veracity judgment under specific conditions but also degrades veracity judgment under other conditions. Second, regarding asymmetry between true and false claims, we show that there are important asymmetries in how discursive evidence supports judgments involving true and false news claims. On one hand, if an item has high evidence strength, it is easier to convince an individual to believe a true claim than to disbelieve a false claim. On the other hand, items with low evidence strength may affect the believability of both true and false news claims regardless of their veracity. Third, regarding priming and discursive evidence, we demonstrate that individuals primed to adopt a more critical mindset tend to utilize uncurated discursive evidence more effectively, resulting in significantly improved judgments. Specifically, a critical mindset leads to more accurate veracity judgments on aggregate. Fourth, regarding improving judgments despite prior inclinations, contrary to prior studies in the field (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019), our results clearly show that people do not “always believe what they want to believe.” We find that people show significant improvements in judgments based on discursive evidence with high evidence strength, even when this goes against their prior inclinations.

## Individual Judgments in Fake News Research

There is extensive literature addressing the problem of fake news from various perspectives (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Horner et al., 2021; Turel & Osatuyi, 2022). We focus on what George et al. (2021) termed “persuasion” in a recent review. Persuasion influences an individual’s judgment of the veracity of a news claim, whether true or false—i.e., enhancing the veracity judgment. There is also an important strand of persuasion research that focuses exclusively on ways of reducing belief in false claims—that is, decreasing the believability of false claims (Moravec et al., 2020).

Interestingly, a close look at the current IS literature on fake news in our leading journals revealed a dominant focus on the believability of false claims rather than enhancing veracity judgments of both true and false claims. For example, in an influential study, Moravec et al. (2019) explored the use of a fake news flag to alert users by working with a set of constructed, fictional news claims treated as fake news claims. Moravec et al. (2020) conducted a followup study with the same dataset of news claims, using two different interventions, a stop sign and a textual notification (e.g., “Declared Fake by 3<sup>rd</sup> Party Fact-Checkers”). Similarly, Kim et al. (2019) and Kim and Dennis (2019) found positive effects in reducing the believability of false claims by presenting users with ratings given by experts or users. Additionally, Turel and Osatuyi (2022) explored what they called the peer context of users in which they primed users in order to induce them to be more conscious of what they called self-objectivity, while Horner et al. (2021) looked at the effect of emotions in their study of believability. Taken together, these IS studies have proven to be valuable in illuminating ways to attend to the believability of false news claims.

On the other hand, it is a challenge in veracity assessment studies to sort claims into the categories of true and false. For example, Ng et al. (2021), and Wei et al. (2022) used data from the Weibo social media site and engaged with both the true and false dimensions of fake news. In our study, we argue that there is much value to be gained by focusing on improving veracity judgments and examining both the true and false claims that characterize news contexts; it is not only false news that people encounter online (London et al., 2022). While reducing the believability of false claims is important, we are rapidly approaching an era in which veracity judgment becomes critical. For example, the rise of novel means of generating fake news, such as generative AI (Sabherwal & Grover, 2024), heralds a time in which false claims may begin to drown out true claims. In such scenarios, users might become excessively skeptical of news claims, thereby impeding the believability of true claims. Considering the importance of this, our study takes a perspective that focuses on veracity judgments, considering both true and false claims jointly.

Studies adopting the veracity judgment perspective assess interventions not only by whether they decrease the belief in false claims but also by whether they increase the belief in true claims independently (George et al., 2018; London et al., 2022). Some other studies approach veracity judgments by looking at true and false claims in aggregate—what some call “truth discernment” (e.g., Pennycook et al., 2021). In our study, we distinguish among three different ways of assessing veracity: (1) aggregate veracity judgment—our main outcome variable, which assesses the overall effect on the veracity judgments and includes a balanced ratio of true and false claims combined; (2) unidirectional veracity judgment—the effect on true claims or false claims independently; and (3) bidirectional veracity judgment—the effect on both true and false claims concurrently. For simplicity, we refer to these as aggregate veracity, unidirectional veracity, and bidirectional veracity.

The key distinction between bidirectional and aggregate veracity is that an improvement in bidirectional veracity implies improvements in the assessment of both true and false claims, while an improvement in aggregate veracity simply means that there is a total improvement. For instance, consider two individuals, A and B, who are initially able to correctly assess only one true and one false claim out of 10 news claims. If, after an intervention, Person A is now able to assess three true and three false claims correctly, while Person B is now able to assess five true and one false claims correctly, then the intervention increases the bidirectional veracity of Person A but not Person B—even though the aggregate veracity is increased for both persons. This highlights the salience and nuanced nature of the distinction between both, as we will further demonstrate.

Proponents of the aggregate veracity approach stress the potential adverse effects on true news claims if interventions are only tested by attempting to reduce beliefs in false news claims independently. Nevertheless, it is important to note that looking at aggregate veracity alone may be insufficient, since an aggregate improvement may result from an improvement on just one type of claim (either true or false) and thereby mask null effects or even decrease the veracity judgment in the other type of claim. In different news contexts, the ratio of false to true claims might vary widely. For interventions to be robust against highly skewed claim ratios, they need to improve true and false claims independently and simultaneously, thus increasing bidirectional veracity. While we focus on aggregate veracity in our main analyses (by testing effects on the combined sample of true and false claims), we also show the effects on true or false claims independently, allowing us to assess bidirectional veracity when significant effects for true and false claims are observed to go in the same direction. ## Hypothesis Development: Discursive Evidence and Fake News

In this paper, we investigate the conditions under which discursive evidence improves individuals’ judgment by persuading them about the veracity of news claims. Persuasion can be defined as a process of convincing other people to change their attitudes, beliefs, and behavior (Perloff, 2003), and it is at the core of the elaboration likelihood model (ELM). The persuasion literature emphasizes that people’s mental reaction to a message, such as discursive evidence, plays a critical role in the persuasion effort (George et al., 2021; Perloff, 2003). Directly communicating to people that their intuitive assessment is wrong may trigger psychological mechanisms that interfere with higher cognitive engagement (Loewenstein, 1996) and prompt rejection despite the strongest scientific evidence (Philipp-Muller et al., 2022). In fact, many approaches to countering fake news share normative characteristics that may signal to individuals that their intuitions are (likely) wrong. Brehm (1966) stated that, in such situations, people might exhibit psychological reactance, which is motivated by a need for self-determination, a desire to retain a sense of control of one’s actions, and beliefs that can trigger rebellious behavior and negative emotions, e.g., anger. For these reasons, we stipulate that the evidence provided should abstain from strongly normative messages about the news claim. Rather, it should be framed in a way that allows participants to make up their minds.

Following the ELM (O’Keefe, 2002), evidence can persuade people through two different processes: the central route relies on reflective and mindful thinking, while the peripheral route uses heuristics and mental shortcuts. These routes are not mutually exclusive, and their use depends on the individual degree of elaboration, which should be conceptualized as a continuum. Persuasion through the central route of relatively high elaboration occurs if the provided evidence induces more favorable thoughts about the advocated position, which mainly depends on the actual strength of the argument (Levitan & Visser, 2007). Under low elaboration conditions, persuasion may occur through different heuristics that might arise from the provided information, such as a judgment based on the credibility of the source or the likeability of the message.

A core insight of the ELM model is that any relevant variable might affect the persuasion process in different ways—even at the same time, making it hard to predict ex ante what effect might be expected when manipulating a persuasion process in a specific context (Perloff, 2003). Consequently, it is challenging to determine which discursive evidence is the most persuasive a priori since effects depend on the individual elaboration likelihood. For instance, while discursive evidence that is objectively helpful and relevant should be persuasive under high elaboration conditions, the same evidence might lose effectiveness under low elaboration conditions because people do not engage with it. We stipulate that the effectiveness of discursive evidence depends on a match between the curation of the provided evidence and the elaboration conditions of the individual receiving it.

In view of these theoretical considerations (Brehm, 1966; Perloff, 2003), we tested different forms of discursive evidence. The first was an excerpt from an assessment of a news rating site. The excerpt included a summarized conclusion of the assessment, omitting the final rating. We considered this to have high evidence strength (Cummings & Dennis, 2018) and to be unambiguous, although it still required interpretation since it lacked the final rating itself. We termed this curated evidence because it provided a narrative expert summary of the available evidence regarding a specific news claim. As opposed to flags (Moravec et al., 2019), curated evidence does not impose a judgment but provides compelling evidence that is helpful and relevant in determining the veracity of a claim (Perloff, 2003). From the theoretical perspective of ELM, information only changes attitudes through the central route of higher elaboration if the information persuades the person with the evidence upon which she is elaborating intensely. Hence, we expect that curated evidence will encourage individuals to engage with the argument itself and make up their minds. We thus hypothesize:

H1a: The presence of curated evidence improves individuals’ judgment of the aggregate veracity of news claims.

The second form of discursive evidence, which we termed uncurated evidence, was a result of a search engine query based on the text of the news claim. Prior research shows that, when faced with a gap in our knowledge (for example, whether a claim is true or false), people turn to online search engines (e.g., Google) for information (Sparrow et al., 2011). Furthermore, some fact-checking sites that provide expert reviews of fake news and journalists reportedly rely on online research as a core part of their verification process (Brandtzaeg et al., 2018). Given that the issue of fake news is a large-scale problem, effective evidence design cannot be fully separated from practical considerations. Curated evidence will likely be more persuasive for a given news claim. However, it is difficult to provide such evidence on a large scale in a timely fashion. Hence, potentially less persuasive, automatically produced evidence might be generally effective in combating the fake news problem at scale—especially, if individuals do not engage in high levels of elaboration, making the argument strength less important. While uncurated evidence is generally less informative and noisier than curated evidence, we expect that it will still provide useful information. We thus hypothesize:

H1b: The presence of uncurated evidence improves individuals’ judgment of the aggregate veracity of news claims.

## Study 1

## Methods

Study design and experimental procedure: The study used a within- and between-subject design to assess the effect of the experimental manipulations. Participants entered the experiment online platform, designed with Qualtrics, and were shown a welcome page that asked for informed consent. Thereafter, each participant assessed the same set of news claims twice. The first assessment was the same for all participants, while the evidence provided in the second assessment differed between the two randomly assigned treatment groups (see Figure 1).

News claims: Participants were asked to perform their first assessment of 22 news claims using a binary indicator, indicating whether the claims were either “true” or “fake.” The news claims were collected from the MultiFC dataset (Augenstein et al., 2019), which is an aggregate of data from many news rating sites. We randomly selected 11 claims rated by PolitiFact as True and 11 rated as “pants-on-fire.” PolitiFact describes its “pants-on-fire” rating as the least truthful rating, indicating that “the statement is not accurate and makes a ridiculous claim” (Drobnic, 2018)—we used this to indicate fake news. News claims in MultiFC are U.S.-focused and contemporary and they have an assessment from a rating site such as PolitiFact. For the second assessment, participants were provided discursive evidence as detailed below.

Evidence treatments: For the curated evidence, we provided the original text from PolitiFact (often labeled “PunditFact”) as an excerpt but omitted their summary rating (i.e., a 6-point Likert scale from “true” to “pants-on-fire”). These excerpts were informative and unambiguous, requiring interpretation but leaving the decision open to the reader (since they lacked the actual rating). PolitiFact has been demonstrated to be useful in recent empirical studies about fake news (Jiang & Wilson, 2018). While the ultimate truth is hard to assess for all claims, we adopted the extreme end of the ratings (i.e., “pants on fire”—“true”) because prior studies have shown that the accuracy of the evaluations by PolitiFact, when compared with other similar databases, were outright falsehoods (i.e., “pants on fire”) or obvious truths (Lim, 2018). Typically, this text provides a clear and succinct statement of why the claim is rated as it is. However, the explicit rating was removed from the material presented to the participant. Thus, the participant was still required to interpret the text and infer the rating (see Figure A1 in Appendix A).

For the uncurated evidence, we provided support in the form of search snippets, which were performed as part of constructing the MultiFC dataset. As with the curated evidence, we removed items, mostly referring to factchecking websites, that indicated an explicit normative judgment of whether the claim was true or fake (see examples in Figure A2).

Measurement: The main outcome variable for the analysis was the calculated proportion of accurately assessed news claims—meaning that our dependent variable ranged from 0 (for those judging all claims incorrectly) to 1 (for those judging correctly). In a post-experimental survey, participants rated their political ideology (very left to very right) and interest in politics (very little to very much) on a 7-point Likert scale. Additionally, participants were asked for their gender (male, female, others), age (numeric), and education (eight-category variable).

Participants: Participants were recruited via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. The initial sample was 327 participants from the U.S. We implemented several quality requirements, detailed in Appendix A (Data Quality section), to enhance data quality. Participants failing these quality requirements were excluded from the analysis sample, which left 289 valid responses.

This final sample consisted of 48% males with an average age of 38 years. Taken together with a mean self-assessed political ideology of 3.5, the participants were slightly left of center. The baseline accuracy of the first news claims was 0.6, meaning that, without any evidence, participants, on average, rated 13 of the 22 claims correctly. We did not observe any systematic differences between the two treatment groups, implying that there is no evidence of selective attrition by participants in either treatment group. See Table B1 in Appendix B for a more detailed description of the sample.

Analysis strategy: To arrive at the veracity judgment for the main analysis, we calculated the changes for each individual by subtracting their value in the first rating from the second rating. Data was analyzed on the individual level, and we relied mainly on OLS regression to test for significant differences between the two ratings and between the two treatments. To assess aggregate veracity, we tested for significant differences in true and false combined. We tested them separately to assess unidirectional and bidirectional veracity.

## Results 1

The linear regression results support H1a, indicating that the curated evidence significantly improved the aggregate veracity of the participants’ judgments by 6%, compared to their unsupported responses (see Table 2 and Figure 2 for a summary of the results). This increase was significantly different from zero and when compared to the uncurated evidence (beta = 0.06, t = 3.53, p < 0.01, 95% CI [0.03, 0.09]), which showed no improvement in aggregate veracity compared to the first assessment. Hence, we found no support for H1b.

One might surmise that the uncurated evidence had no effect on veracity judgments when looking at the aggregate measure. However, we could see two significant effects of the uncurated evidence when we examined veracity judgment independently. Participants increased veracity judgment for true claims by 5.5%. False claims were also more likely to be judged as true, which reduced veracity judgment with a comparable effect size. The positive effect on aggregate veracity for curated evidence was mostly driven by the strong effect for true claims of 8.5%, with a weaker effect for false claims, which was not statistically significant.

## Discussion 1

Study 1 provides support for H1a. Curated evidence helped participants judge the aggregate veracity of news claims. This effect was primarily driven by the improved unidirectional veracity judgments of true claims and, to a lesser but not statistically significant degree, of false claims.

We did not find support for H1b that providing uncurated evidence improves judging the veracity of news claims; the hypothesized main effect of aggregate veracity was virtually zero. However, the uncurated evidence significantly improved the judgment of true claims while showing a similar but negative effect on false claims. In other words, it generally increased the overall believability, leading to a tendency to judge more claims as true, regardless of their actual veracity—akin to the truth bias (Clementson, 2017). The result is in line with what has been termed the “illusory truth effect,” suggesting that repeated exposure to a false claim increases belief in the claim being true (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010; Pennycook et al., 2018). For our design of the uncurated evidence as search results, we deliberately searched for the original claim. This implies that these claims, even when false, were likely to appear repeatedly in the search results (e.g., as quotes). While speculative, this effect would be consistent with peripheral route processing in the ELM: the illusory truth effect is a heuristic that should not affect judgments when evidence is processed mindfully through the central route.

Study 2 was subsequently conducted to shed additional light on the conditions under which discursive evidence can improve the veracity judgment of news claims. In this study, we manipulated participants to increase information processing and provide a better understanding of the boundary conditions for the positive effects of discursive evidence.

## Study 2

Study 2 aimed to replicate the findings from Study 1 and to understand how and under which conditions discursive evidence is effective. While curated evidence was effective in terms of aggregate veracity, uncurated evidence showed no main effect in Study 1. As this was primarily caused by the negative effects of false news claims, we follow the ELM and stipulate that increasing the motivation and ability for information processing could potentially mitigate the effect of counterproductive heuristics. To do this and test the overall sensitivity of the provided evidence to differences in elaboration likelihood, we added a priming manipulation in

Study 2. The effects of priming have been observed in different research fields, which indicate that priming interventions affect information processing in various ways (Cesario, 2014). In fake news research, several previous studies have tested priming as an experimental manipulation (Turel & Osatuyi, 2022; Van Duyn & Collier, 2019). Within the debate, several scholars have highlighted that people who engage with news online are likely to do so with a more hedonic and less critical mindset (Kim & Dennis, 2019)— particularly when claims are read on social media. Such a mindset is characterized by hedonic intentions and pleasureseeking, which likely reduces critical reflection and focus on veracity (Kim & Dennis, 2019).

We expected that priming the concept of truth and lies, and thereby inducing a more critical mindset, would improve the effect of discursive evidence, allowing participants to combat peripheral processes such as the illusory truth effect and boost the persuasion process through the central route (Lutzke et al., 2019; Machete & Turpin, 2020; Van Duyn & Collier, 2019). Hence, we formulated the following hypothesis, which we tested for curated and uncurated evidence separately:

H2a: Priming the concept of truth and lies improves the effect of discursive evidence on individuals’ judgment of the aggregate veracity of news claims.

As the effect of the uncurated evidence particularly differed between true and false claims, we stipulate that priming effects might also differ. The ELM model suggests that manipulating any variable might affect the persuasion process in different ways. If the decreased veracity judgment of false claims in the uncurated evidence is caused by a heuristic, i.e., the illusory truth effect, higher levels of elaboration and engagement of more cognitive effort caused by the prime should result in a debiasing (Moravec et al., 2019). In this case, the effects for false claims should improve, reducing the effects of the illusory truth effect, while the changes for true claims would remain theoretically ambivalent: if veracity judgment improvements are also linked to the same heuristic—which happens to improve judgments—then increased elaboration might not necessarily lead to better judgments but rather shift the persuasion process to the same outcome. However, it is also possible that the priming intervention might induce a more critical mindset and make participants more skeptical of the evidence. If the persuasion process does not move towards higher elaboration but merely shifts the peripheral process heuristic, we would expect a reduction in the observed improvements for true news claims but increased veracity judgment for false claims. Given these ambivalent theoretical predictions, we formulate the following undirected hypothesis:

H2b: Priming the concept of truth and lies influences the effect of discursive evidence on the veracity judgment of news claims, depending on whether the claim is true or false.

## Methods

Study design and experimental procedure: The design of Study 2 was nearly identical to Study 1, using the same procedure and evidence treatments. However, unlike Study 1, all participants were offered a financial reward for correctly assessing each of the 44 news claims. This reward was offered to test whether a lack of participant engagement could explain the results in Study 1 and to ensure that participants properly engaged with the priming task. Participants were first randomized into a prime or a filler task before judging the first set of news claims. This randomization was independent of the informational treatment arms, leading to a 2×2 design for the second assessment (see Figure 3).

Priming task: To activate a more critical mindset, we relied on a priming task similar to that of Mussweiler and Neumann (2000), which triggers self-generated thoughts and memories about the relevant construct—which, in our case, involved truth and lies. This approach, in which people come up with the relevant priming words by themselves, reduces one’s ability to attribute the origin of these thoughts to an external source. This is more likely if the relevant concept is primed directly by, e.g., a fake news flag that can be interpreted as an external request to think about the veracity of the news claim.

Prior research has shown that people detecting such external influence—in this case, a priming procedure with respect to their thoughts about a news claim—are more likely to actively resist the external influence of the prime (Mussweiler & Neumann, 2000). The operationalization of our priming task was based on Mussweiler and Neumann (2000). It consisted of 15 words presented as three sets of five words. The participants were asked to find antonyms to the words. Participants could give up to three antonyms per word and were required to provide at least one to receive their financial compensation. The priming and filler task had 10 identical words, such as hot, heavy, and soft. For the priming group, we selected the five remaining words so that the antonyms related to the concept of falsehood or fakes. These words were true, real, honest, sincere, and correct. The filler task in the control condition used five more words unrelated to any relevant concepts.

Participants: Participants were recruited via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. The initial sample was 408 participants from the U.S. People already participating in Study 1 were not allowed to participate in Study 2. We implemented similar quality requirements as in Study 1, detailed in Appendix A (Data Quality section), to enhance data quality. Participants who failed any quality requirement or did not provide at least one antonym per word in the priming task were excluded from the analysis sample, which ultimately consisted of 361 valid responses.

The final sample comprised 40% males with an average age of 41. Political ideology and interest were comparable to Study 1. No major differences were observed between the different treatment groups. See Table B3 for a more detailed description of the sample.

## Discussion 2

The results show that priming a more critical mindset against fake news increases the effect of uncurated discursive evidence on aggregate veracity judgments. On priming the concept of truth and lies, it was nearly as effective as curated evidence—on aggregate, which suggests that a more critical mindset may, at least in some cases, compensate for a lack of curation of the provided evidence. The priming task shifted the effect of false and true claims in a positive direction. Although the point estimate for false claims remained negative, it was not statistically significant. This could indicate residual evidence for the illusory true effect, which was reduced but not eliminated by priming the concept of truth and lies. While the activation of a critical mindset (Lazer et al., 2018; Lutzke et al., 2019) has previously been associated with an increase in skepticism (Clayton et al., 2019; Van Duyn & Collier, 2019), we did not obtain significant differences for true or false claims. Study 2 was also a successful replication of the results from Study 1. One noticeable difference is the significant effects of false claims in the unprimed, curated condition. The effects in Study 1 were in the same direction but not statistically significant. We can only speculate about the reasons for this difference, which might be linked to the introduced financial incentive or reflect a Type 2 error in Study 1. Pooling the data from both studies yielded statistically significant results.

In Studies 1 and 2, the uncurated evidence was composed of several different evidence items. Some of these items contained helpful information, some were related to the claim but not necessarily helpful, while others were completely irrelevant. Participants needed to engage sufficiently to identify and evaluate them. In line with the ELM, we stipulated that the lack of curation made this task challenging and might have facilitated reliance on peripheral route processing that made individuals more susceptible to potentially biased heuristics, such as the illusory truth effect. Our results from the priming condition are consistent with the theory in that our results indicate an increase in the level of elaboration by which more participants scrutinized the uncurated evidence and were able to distill relevant information rather than applying a heuristic in a state of low elaboration. Given the design of Studies 1 and 2, we were unable to determine whether a more critical mindset would help individuals make better use of helpful information or prevent them from changing their beliefs in response to misleading or factually irrelevant information when evidence is uncurated.

Interestingly, no priming effect was observed for the first rating before participants were provided with discursive evidence. This contrasts with recent findings (Clayton et al., 2019; Van Duyn & Collier, 2019) but demonstrates that priming did not induce additional thought processes that effectively altered judgments without discursive evidence. Our results thus suggest that while activating a critical mindset through priming may not be sufficient in itself, the priming effect can be bolstered with discursive evidence. Furthermore, activating a critical mindset may be particularly important in improving veracity judgment in cases where people are exposed to uncurated evidence.

## Study 3

We performed Study 3 to sharpen the insights and strengthen the validity of the findings of Studies 1 and 2. Specifically, we designed Study 3 with three objectives: first, to address the limitations of the previous two studies while solidifying the replicability of the findings; second, to understand the specific effect of varying types of discursive evidence— whereby evidence is categorized according to whether it is helpful and relevant; and, third, to empirically confirm if discursive evidence could induce individuals to change their judgments despite prior inclinations.

Objective 1—Replication and improvements: We sought to rule out several potential confounds in Studies 1 and 2. First, it is possible that judgments may have changed simply because participants were reading the same news claims twice. Hence, we added a clear control group to Study 3 that presented participants with the same claim twice but with no evidence provided. Second, in the previous studies, the curated and uncurated treatments not only varied in content but also in length. These differences might have confounded the observed effects if participants were less likely to read and elaborate on a longer text (lower elaboration likelihood) or mistook the length of an argument for its strength (O’Keefe, 2002). In Study 3, the evidence provided to the participants was of similar length to eliminate this potential confound. Third, in the previous studies, the presence of the source of the evidence items might have introduced bias in the participant’s judgment. In Study 3, we avoided displaying an explicit source for the evidence. Some evidence items, however, cited specific individuals or official statistics where necessary. We considered these to be different because they represented a part of the evidence as opposed to the media source (e.g., CNN or Fox). For example, “October data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggests…”. We did this because the presence of a source that either aligns or contrasts prior beliefs is a known heuristic under peripheral processing that may lead to biased judgments (Pornpitakpan, 2004).

Furthermore, as in Studies 1 and 2, we used news claims as rated by fact-checking sites—predominantly PolitiFact. While PolitiFact is a widely respected fact-checking organization, there is a possibility that their ratings might be unreliable in some cases or might even display a general ideological bias. These are serious concerns that could cast doubt on the validity of our results. To address this, we performed a detailed independent validation assessment of the claims collected for Study 3. The results of the validation exercise strongly supported the fact-checking sites’ assessments, with the ratings assessed as correct in all cases—except one. In the one exception, the claim was correct at the time of PolitiFact’s rating and during our study but is no longer true today due to the evolving and timedependent nature of the claim. (See Table A2 in Appendix A for details of this assessment.)

Objective 2—Testing the effect of varying evidence strength: The difference between the two types of discursive evidence in Studies 1 and 2 may be categorized in different ways. While the curated evidence only included information that was relevant to assess the claim, the information provided in the uncurated evidence varied greatly. To identify the essential elements of effective discursive evidence, it is necessary to categorize and experimentally manipulate the constituent items of the evidence and their influence. Building on related research about evidence used to persuade individuals in decisionmaking (Ecker et al., 2019), we advance the notion of evidence strength. We conceptualize evidence strength along two dimensions: the evidence’s helpfulness in assessing a claim’s veracity (Perfors et al., 2018) and the general relevance of the evidence to the claim (Ecker et al., 2019). Two main approaches can be distinguished in ELM research (O’Keefe, 2002) and the persuasion literature (Perfors et al., 2018; Reinard, 1988) to assess the strength of persuasive arguments. While one tries to objectively assess the strength of arguments based on factors such as logical coherence and relevance (Ecker et al., 2019), the other assesses the strength of evidence based on its effectiveness in changing people’s attitudes and beliefs (Zhao et al., 2011). The conceptual distinction between evidence strength and argument strength is that—in the discursive sense— evidence is not normative, whereas argument is. While evidence provides information in support of an argument, an argument uses evidence normatively to support a particular conclusion (Spigelman, 2001).

Some prior experimental persuasion research has categorized evidence along a categorical and single dimension of weak and strong evidence (Fernbach et al., 2011; Reinard, 1988). However, these categorizations did not map onto the evidence items in the uncurated evidence used in Studies 1 and 2, which we aimed to explore. Hence, we operationalized evidence strength in our study along two dimensions that are not independent. Since we view relevance as a necessary condition for helpfulness (see quadrant “x” in Figure 5), we distinguished between (1) evidence that is irrelevant to the claim, (2) evidence that is related to the claim but does not help in accurately assessing it, and (3) evidence that is actually helpful in determining the veracity of the claim.

First, based on this conceptualization of helpfulness (see Figure 5), we captured the perceived importance of evidence in relation to judging a news claim, which is similar to perceived importance measured in relation to argument strength for the ELM (Zhao et al., 2011) and, implicitly, its perceived persuasiveness. Second, we captured aspects of the informational value provided in the evidence through its relevance to the news claim (Ecker et al., 2019). These categories have been considered in the literature, and it has been argued that using evidence—especially if it is more relevant, specific, and consistent with other evidence—tends to increase a claim’s acceptance (Reinard, 1988). Hence, we created an ordinal measure of evidence strength based on the assumption that evidence will be more persuasive if it is relevant (vs. irrelevant) and that strength may be increased by making relevant evidence helpful for assessing the veracity of a news claim. We elaborate on the three categories.

Irrelevant evidence items have no connection to the claim and possess no relevant informational value that can help determine the veracity of a claim. We argue that irrelevant evidence has the lowest strength—that is, it is unhelpful for assessing the veracity of a news claim and is the least persuasive form of evidence. Hence, with a high level of elaboration, irrelevant evidence items should not affect veracity judgments. Prior research has empirically tested the provision of similar types of evidence, showing mixed results on veracity judgments (Ecker et al., 2019). A related phenomenon was identified by Pennycook et al. (2020a), who showed that people misinterpret the absence of fake news flags as evidence of the truthfulness of a claim. Building on these findings, we expect that the exposure to irrelevant evidence under a low elaboration process may affect veracity judgments based on prior beliefs about whether a claim is true or false.

Related evidence items provide information about the topic or object of interest in the claim but do not contain information that is directly helpful in assessing the claim’s veracity. Under low levels of elaboration, participants may have misconstrued related items as evidence with actual informational quality— possibly invoking heuristics involving the likeability of the claim or its alignment with prior beliefs (Moravec et al., 2019). However, related evidence might also have broadened the mental image participants had about the subject of the claim and, therefore, deepened participants’ elaboration about the claim’s veracity. This might have helped them recall relevant details about the claim, which would have been mentally unavailable without such related information.

Finally, helpful evidence items contain information supporting a specific judgment of a claim. Hence, helpful evidence should directly improve people’s judgment of a claim’s veracity. Moreover, helpful items are characterized by high evidence strength that should persuade people to rethink their assessments if they made mistakes in the first assessment. This process, however, requires a high level of elaboration. Participants engaged in low elaboration might not have been persuaded, may have relied on other possible heuristics, or may have completely disregarded the evidence.

The search for discursive evidence to help demystify a claim can be messy in real contexts, as people will encounter different evidence, ranging from helpful to related to irrelevant and combinations thereof (Brandtzaeg et al., 2018). Therefore, our study design was set up to model the different forms of evidence that people are likely to encounter (e.g., from a Google search). In sum, we conceptualize increasing evidence strength as ordinal increments ranging from irrelevant to related to helpful along the two dimensions of helpfulness and relevance. We thus hypothesize that, along both dimensions, higher evidence strength will lead to improved judgments:

H3a: The higher the evidence strength, the more discursive evidence will improve individuals’ judgment of a claim’s veracity.

Objective 3—Changing judgment despite prior inclinations: Given that the results of Studies 1 and 2 show that discursive evidence can persuade people to change their judgments, we designed Study 3 to examine whether discursive evidence is persuasive enough to have the same effect in cases in which the change in judgment conflicts with one’s prior inclinations (e.g., partisanship). This is driven by two considerations. First, recent IS scholarship on fake news indicates that people believe what they want to believe even when it makes no sense (Moravec et al., 2019). Our study aims to contribute to this discourse by testing whether discursive evidence could be a potential avenue for changing people’s judgments despite their prior inclinations. Second, and relatedly, the polarization that results from fake news suggests the importance of finding approaches that can help people shift their previous views and soften the ongoing polarization that currently defines certain issues (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017; Bakir & McStay, 2018). Thus, we selected the evidence items in Study 3 to represent a balance between apolitical and partisan claims related to both political parties in the U.S. (i.e., Republicans and Democrats). This allowed us to investigate the importance of prior inclinations— specifically, political partisanship—for the effectiveness of discursive evidence, which was not specifically investigated in Studies 1 and 2. In line with previous findings that people believe what they want to believe and the predictions from the ELM highlighted earlier, we hypothesize that:

H3b: Discursive evidence is less likely to improve judgments if the news claim opposes an individual’s prior inclination.

## Methods

Study design and experimental procedure: The design of the Qualtrics-administered experiment was similar to Studies 1 and 2, in which participants rated the same news claims twice. The first task was identical for all participants—claim assessment without evidence. To deconstruct the effect of evidence strength, we opted for a within-person design where the strength of the evidence differed for the same participant between claim ratings in the second assessment. This allowed us to independently assess the effect of two evidence items of the three categories (irrelevant, related, helpful) on six different types of news claims (true vs. false; apolitical, Republican, Democratic). This led to a fully orthogonal experimental design with 54 claim-evidence combinations (see Hensher et al., 2005): 2×3 related to types of news claims and 3×3 possible combinations of the two evidence items with three categories (see Figure A3 and Table A1 for examples). To keep the workload for participants manageable, we broke the 54 combinations into three blocks of 18 claims per participant (see Table A4). In addition, we added a control group where news claims were rated twice without exposure to any evidence. As in Study 2, participants were randomly assigned to a priming task before the first rating (Figure 6).

News claims. Participants rated 18 new news claims as to their truthfulness. The items were balanced and had nine true and nine false statements. For both categories, we selected three news claims that were (1) apolitical, (2) Republican, or (3) Democratic. We categorized a claim as Republican if it expressed a view generally favored by Republicans, e.g., a claim supporting Donald Trump. Similarly, claims generally favored by Democrats, e.g., criticism of Trump, were categorized as Democratic. If a claim was categorizable neither as Democratic nor Republican, it was classified as apolitical. While this study is interested in assessing the effect of evidence strength, it is essential to adequately incorporate and balance different types of news claims in the design to avoid confounding from a potential link between evidence strength and claim type because the “natural” uncurated evidence in previous studies, for instance, could have been stronger for specific types of news claims.

Evidence treatments: For the second assessment, people were randomized into an evidence treatment group or a control condition that withheld evidence to eliminate any potential confound from rating a claim twice. Participants in the treatment group were assigned to one of three blocks with different combinations of two evidence items (see Table A4). As outlined above, we categorized the evidence items into helpful, related, and irrelevant. In our two previous studies, the uncurated evidence (i.e., Google search snippets) contained varying combinations of helpful, related, and irrelevant items, while the curated evidence only contained helpful evidence items curated in a persuasive narrative by a dedicated expert. Given the nature of the uncurated evidence tested in Studies 1 and 2 and the fact that evidence rarely consists of a single argument, we opted to provide two evidence items simultaneously to explore how different combinations of the three categories would affect each other. Within these nine possible evidence combinations, we viewed evidence items containing two helpful items as being closest to the curated evidence of Studies 1 and 2, although they still lacked the textual coherence of the curated evidence. Accordingly, combinations of different evidence items represented intermediate levels of evidence strength, while two irrelevant items represented the lowest evidence strength.

Claims and evidence validation: We tested five news claims per category (true vs. false; apolitical, Republican, Democratic) and three evidence items (helpful, related, and irrelevant) per category and claim. The validation process included three pre-tests on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk to continuously improve the used materials (total number of participants per pre-test: N<sub>1</sub> = 32, N<sub>2</sub> = 60, N<sub>3</sub> = 102). Evidence items that had been correctly validated by more than 70% of pre-testers were used in the study. All remaining items that did not meet this criterion underwent an independent review by three researchers for additional clarification to remove any ambiguity. Each researcher assessed the edits by the other reviewers, and a final solution for each item was agreed upon after a joint session with the entire team. In the final step, we selected the 18 news claims with the best-rated evidence items and discarded the evidence items with the lowest rating for each of these claims.

Measurement: We changed the measurement of participant assessment from the binary indicator used in Studies 1 and 2 to a 7-point Likert scale assessing the claim’s truthfulness, ranging from “Not at all” to “Very much.” This measure, which has been used in various studies on fake news (Moravec et al., 2020), allowed us to assess a more nuanced perception of the truthfulness of a claim. The experiment ended with the same questions about individuals’ demographics and policy-related questions as in the two previous studies.

Participants: Participants were recruited via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. The initial sample was 998 participants from the U.S. who had not participated in Studies 1 or 2. Given the minimal differences between Studies 1 and 2, we decided not to incentivize participants for accurate judgments in Study 3. We decided to boost the sample size for Study 3 to keep the individual cell size (here, 20 with nine evidence treatment combinations plus the control split by the priming intervention) comparable to our prior studies. The experimental survey included data quality requirements similar to those of Studies 1 and 2; see Appendix A (Data Quality section) for details.

This led to 715 valid responses, implying a relatively larger loss of quality participants compared to Study 2, which we attribute to the lack of a financial incentive for correct answers present in Study 3. This final sample was comprised of 46% males with an average age of 42. Taken together with a mean self-assessed political ideology of 3.8, the participants were slightly left of center. See Table B5 for a more detailed description of the sample.

Analysis strategy: Since our design varied the combination of evidence types between participants and news claims, the data were analyzed on the participant-item level. We subtracted truthfulness ratings for the first assessment from the second for each true news claim, resulting in more positive values for larger improvements in truthfulness. For false claims, we subtracted the second rating from the first to obtain more positive values for larger reductions in truthfulness. Improvements in veracity judgment were estimated via OLS. Given that the treatment now varied within individual participants, we analyze the data on the participant-claim level and cluster the standard errors by participants. We opted to display the experimental results of our evidence manipulations as group differences rather than decomposing them into main and interaction effects. We only observed minor differences in the order of the provided evidence—e.g., showing a helpful item first or second. Differences were either statistically insignificant or small enough not to warrant an alternative interpretation. Hence, we opted to simplify the presentation of our findings from the 3×3 design and pooled mixed evidence conditions, ignoring the order in which they were presented. This approach led to six distinct types of evidence strengths plus the control group without any evidence as a reference in the estimation (see Figure 7 & Table B7).

## Discussion 3

The results of Study 3 validated our previous findings by showing that discursive evidence with high evidence strength can improve individuals’ judgment of the veracity of news claims. They also replicated the effect of priming the concept of truth and lies: Priming may increase the effects of discursive evidence on aggregate veracity. In addition, we observed an asymmetry in how the effects of the interventions vary between true and false claims. Table 5 above compares the findings from all three studies and delineates the results for unidirectional, aggregate, and bidirectional veracity.

Furthermore, we observed a relation between evidence strength and veracity judgment in general. As in the first two studies, we saw interesting differences in the unidirectional veracity effect on true and false claims. In the first two studies, uncurated evidence improved judgment on true claims while judgment on false claims was degraded. This seems suggestive of a heuristic—potentially, the “illusory truth effect,” the idea that simply continuing to mention or discuss a claim can tend to bolster its credibility. However, in Study 3, the provided evidence did not repeat the claims, and we observed that something quite different was going on. There were, in fact, two distinct effects, reflecting an asymmetry of true and false claims.

First, unhelpful evidence items increased doubts or skepticism, leading participants to perform worse on true claims and better on false claims (that is, we observed a decrease in bidirectional believability). Given that the negative effects for true claims exceeded the positive effects for false claims, aggregate veracity was significantly reduced. As in Studies 1 and 2, this can be explained by referencing ELM’s peripheral route and the application of a heuristic known as the “weak evidence effect,” whereby people reduce their belief in certain claims if the provided evidence is weaker than they expected (Fernbach et al., 2011). However, the increased veracity judgment in false claims was limited to unhelpful evidence of which at least one item was irrelevant. When both evidence items were unhelpful but related to false news claims, veracity judgment was unaffected. Second, helpful evidence improved bidirectional veracity, that is, an improvement on both true and false claims. These improvements were systematically greater for true than for false claims.

Finally, we observed that participants were willing to change their judgments even when they went against their prior inclination. When participants were provided with discursive evidence, no difference was observed in aggregate veracity between claims that matched participants’ political positions and those that opposed them. Interestingly, while we did not observe any changes in the aggregate veracity for rating the claims twice, a pattern emerged in the analysis of this prior inclination subsample. Participants rating claims that opposed their inclinations seemed to become more doubtful while, for matched claims, they seemed to become slightly more confident.

## General Discussion and Implications

The main contribution of this study is to show how discursive evidence may be an effective means to improve an individual’s veracity judgment of news claims. We summarize the results from all three studies in Table 5 and highlight the following main findings:

1. Discursive evidence and veracity judgment: Discursive evidence can significantly improve individuals’ judgment of news claims. The strength of the provided evidence is crucial for the robustness of these effects. Discursive evidence with low evidence strength is only effective in specific situations and needs to be applied carefully to do more good than harm.

2. Asymmetry between judgment of true and false claims: Our findings reveal important asymmetries in how discursive evidence supports judgments involving true vs. false claims.

3. Priming and discursive evidence: Activating a critical mindset through priming can help individuals use uncurated discursive evidence better on the aggregate. Given that people frequently consume news in a hedonic, less critical state, our study highlights priming as a potential mechanism to improve the veracity judgment of news claims.

4. Changing judgments despite prior inclinations: We show that individuals are willing to shift their judgment in the face of discursive evidence, even when this contradicts their prior inclinations. This challenges a view in prior IS research that people believe what they want to believe even when it makes no sense (Moravec et al., 2019). This observation is consistent with our assumption that discursive evidence is less likely to trigger reactance when normative interventions challenge an individual’s prior inclinations.

## Discursive Evidence and Veracity Judgment

Our results show that providing individuals with discursive evidence can improve the veracity judgment of news claims. Specifically, our findings suggest that discursive evidence with high evidence strength can improve people’s (unidirectional, bidirectional, and aggregate) veracity judgment—i.e., false claims are more likely to be categorized as false, and true claims are more likely to be categorized as true. When discursive evidence is characterized by low evidence strength, its effectiveness partially depends on different caveats related to the strength of the evidence, individual engagement in processing the evidence, and whether a claim is true or false. Hence, we identify a trade-off between discursive evidence curation and narrower boundary conditions for the effectiveness of discursive evidence.

Regarding the size of these effects, in Study 1, the provision of curated evidence improved overall judgments by an absolute increase of 6-7 percentage points (relative increase: Study 1: 9.7%, Study 2: 12%). This corresponds to a standardized effect size of Cohen’s D = 0.4, which is comparable to or exceeds many effects found in previous studies (Chan & Albarracín, 2023; Moravec et al., 2019; Pennycook et al., 2021; Roozenbeek et al., 2022). Notably, our results contrast with studies in which flags had little or no effect on user judgments (Andersen & Søe, 2020; Clayton et al., 2019; Moravec et al., 2019).

The specific nature of the evidence is crucial: With uncurated evidence in Studies 1 and 2, we observed an absolute decline of around 5 percentage points on false claims, and, in the case of unhelpful evidence in Study 3, we observed a decline for true claims (average absolute decline of -0.4 steps on a 7-point Likert scale, mean = 4.4). We closely associated the risk of reducing veracity judgment through uncurated and unhelpful discursive evidence with the use of heuristics and peripheral route processing according to the ELM. Our results are consistent with the theoretical prediction that the evidence strength is particularly relevant to the quality of judgments when processed through the central route. With low evidence strength, heuristics are more likely to govern the change in judgments. However, these heuristics, which are more simplistic in nature, seem unable to improve veracity judgment but rather either increase or reduce bidirectional believability—supposedly the “illusory true effect” in Studies 1 and 2 and the “weak evidence effect” in Study 3. Therefore, the resulting change in aggregate veracity is governed by whether the unidirectional veracity effects on true vs. false claims are equivalent (uncurated evidence in Studies 1 and 2) or differ (irrelevant evidence in Study 3).

It is also worth noting that the ratio between true and false claims was balanced in the present study, which is unlikely to be the case in the real world. Any imbalance in the frequency of occurrence between the true vs. false claims would affect the overall effectiveness of evidence with low strength. Hence, discursive evidence needs to have sufficiently high evidence strength to be robustly persuasive under all conditions. Low evidence strength only improves veracity judgment if encountered with a sufficiently critical mindset and the right type of news claim. If not targeted well, the heuristics associated with low evidence strength may also backfire and reduce veracity judgments. This points to a related ethical dilemma. Reflective persuasion with discursive evidence of high evidence strength is unlikely to face objections on the grounds of being manipulative (Sunstein, 2016). However, this is not the case for low evidence strength when using heuristics, e.g., providing someone deliberately with irrelevant evidence to reduce believability in a false claim. Such heuristics may be abused if leveraged to distort veracity judgments—providing a cautionary tale related to our results.

Overall, our findings suggest a delicate interplay between news claims, the strength of the discursive evidence, and the individual’s mindset as they engage with a claim. We see these as a call for future research to illuminate the intricacies surrounding why, when, and how discursive evidence improves an individual’s judgment.

## Asymmetry Between True and False Claims: From Believability to Veracity Judgment

In supporting individuals in detecting fake news, it is important not to swing the pendulum of doubt so far that people begin to doubt the truth. Our study sheds light on this by juxtaposing veracity judgment and believability. People may not always find definitive evidence when judging a news claim. Thus, we examined the effect of discursive evidence on veracity judgment and believability.

While some discursive evidence may improve bidirectional veracity, as shown in Table 5, effects frequently differed between true and false claims. Overall, we found that, in most cases, shifting veracity judgment for true claims is easier than for false claims. We consistently observed larger effects for true claims except in the case of curated evidence in Study 2. These large effects were mostly positive and can potentially be explained by the truth bias, a commonly accepted concept in deception detection research that stipulates that people are, by default, more likely to believe that a claim is true than false (Clementson, 2017; Levine et al., 1999). Study 3 showed that although helpful evidence improved bidirectional veracity, the improvement was greater for true than false claims.

However, with unhelpful evidence, participants generally tended to become doubtful (decreasing bidirectional believability) of both true and false claims. These effects were larger in magnitude for true news but negative, suggesting overall higher importance for evidence strength in judging true claims accurately. These results show that the truth bias can be mitigated (with helpful evidence) or inverted (with unhelpful evidence) depending on the nature of the discursive evidence provided. Conversely, judgments on false claims in Study 3 were improved with nearly all types of discursive evidence. Our findings suggest that people respond to low evidence strength items by shifting believability, which might affect aggregate veracity, depending on whether the effects on true claims are similar to those on false claims. In Studies 1 and 2 (unprimed), we observed a relatively symmetric increase in believability, while believability was reduced for unhelpful evidence in Study 3. For the latter, the asymmetric response led to reduced veracity judgment in aggregate. In particular, the finding that providing unhelpful (especially irrelevant) evidence items can decrease believability holds a double-edged sword implication for how we approach the believability of not only false claims but also true claims. This is important because the dominant focus of the literature in IS has been on investigating false claims (Moravec et al., 2020; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Turel & Osatuyi, 2022).

These results open new opportunities for future research and have direct practical implications. In the wrong hands, this knowledge might be intentionally used as a disinformation tool to induce people to discount true claims. This may explain, in part, some of the mystery as to why people fail to believe true claims, as witnessed in the COVID-19 pandemic and recent U.S. politics. It also raises pertinent questions for future research—for example: How can we navigate the thin line between skepticism and credulity as we strive to deal with fake news? Our study provides a starting point, and we call for future research to explore how to increase skepticism without sacrificing credulity when it is warranted.

## Priming and Discursive Evidence

In addition to the insights about the effectiveness of discursive evidence, our results also show that priming may be leveraged to bolster the effect of uncurated discursive evidence on aggregate. Indeed, in both Studies 2 and 3, we observed significant improvements in aggregate veracity in the context of priming. In Study 2, this effect was found with uncurated evidence but not with curated evidence. In contrast, in Study 3, we saw a significant improvement in aggregate veracity when examining all types of evidence combined. From the theoretical perspective of the ELM (O’Keefe, 2002), the observed effects of priming in Studies 2 and 3 are consistent with an increase in central route processing. Activating a critical mindset heightens the motivation with which people engage with noisier and less curated discursive evidence. It also makes it more persuasive in the aggregate. If evidence is curated, as in Study 2, a more critical mindset does not improve persuasiveness. In general, our findings are consistent with prior research, which suggests that priming may induce people to think critically (Lazer et al., 2018; Lutzke et al., 2019; Machete & Turpin, 2020), and this enables them to make better use of discursive evidence in the judgment of news claims. In addition, the fact that priming was not effective without discursive evidence in our studies suggests that a critical mindset without supporting evidence may not be helpful in combating fake news.

Furthermore, although Study 3 replicated the aggregate results of Study 2 with regard to priming, our results do not reveal which subcombination of evidence items drove this observed effect. Our analysis shows that the effect estimates for aggregate veracity increased with higher evidence strength but were only statistically significant for the two helpful items among the false claims. Hence, we cannot make definitive statements about the influence of specific evidence items on how priming affects the persuasion process. Similarly, we did not observe significant differences in contrasting true and false claims. All point estimates are positive and suggest larger effects on true claims in Study 2, while the estimates in Study 3 are larger for false claims. Hence, our results are only indicative of the detailed mechanisms behind the interaction between discursive evidence and priming a reader to adopt a more critical mindset. While unpacking these is not the focus of this research, we submit that these findings raise interesting questions that call for exploration by future research.

## Changing Judgments Despite Prior Inclinations

In consonance with recent studies (Pennycook et al., 2020, 2021), our study departs from the narrative that people are so tightly bound to their prior inclinations and views that an intervention is unlikely to sway them (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019). Although there is value in this viewpoint in that it brings to the fore a need to address confirmation bias in the attempt to equip individuals better to detect fake news (Lazer et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2017), our study joins emerging research in this area to bring to light mechanisms that point to more effective potential interventions.

While our research is not the first to show that interventions may shift judgments despite prior inclinations (Ecker et al., 2022), our research indicates that discursive evidence’s power is not limited to changing judgments. It may also do so even when the evidence contradicts prior political inclinations. This is a departure from the coherence theory of truth, which suggests that establishing a claim’s truthfulness lies in the claim’s consistency with pre-existing beliefs (Roberts & Qahri-Saremi, 2023). We suggest that the theoretical notion of reactance may be an important explanation for the effectiveness of discursive evidence and the ineffectiveness of flags. The results (broken down by explicit party affiliation in Figure B1) suggest that potential asymmetric responses of prior inclination might be conflated with general differences in the openness to evidence along people’s self-reported political orientation.

This opens a vista for future studies to unpack the triggers of reactance that may inhibit people from modifying their initial judgments. Our study shows that discursive evidence may unlock people’s propensity to change their views even when this goes against prior beliefs. Within this lies the potential to soften the menace posed by the rise of polarization and echo chambers.

## Implications for Future Research on Fake News

The emphasis in the emerging literature on IS has been to focus on false news. This is a sensible focus that helps attend to the scourge of fake news. However, our discursive evidence perspective shifts the discipline’s gaze from the dominant attention to false claims and invites future research to consider the value of judging true claims (see also London et al., 2022; Pennycook et al., 2021). This is particularly important given the recent polarization and echo chambers that characterize many online discourses in social media. These problems have resulted in true claims being dismissed for lack of evidence or because they deviate from prior beliefs or assumptions. We argue that this is as important a challenge as the need to address false claims. As the recent difficulty in getting many people to believe accurate information about COVID-19 has demonstrated, providing an apparatus to help people discern both true and false claims (bidirectional veracity) is a pertinent concern that should occupy fake news scholarship now and in the future. In this regard, our results show that discursive evidence generally makes people do better (i.e., correct themselves) on true claims than on false ones. In cases such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the implications of this finding could be a matter of life and death.

Our contributions in this regard are modest yet potent. Our work provides a stepping stone and a call to action for future research to help individuals better judge the veracity of both true and false news with discursive evidence. While we acknowledge that a highly curated approach faces practical challenges, we note that any intervention flagging fake news claims also needs to include an assessment of its veracity. The evidence used to justify flagging a claim might be used as the basis of discursive evidence as an intervention tool. Our results provide valuable insights for curating such discursive evidence and show that, on some occasions, the efforts of creating helpful curated evidence might be outweighed by the increased effectiveness of providing uncurated evidence at a broader scale or for selected claims—when done properly, as indicated by our findings.

Consequently, given that IS research sits at the junction of management, technology, and social research, it can play a vital role in providing conceptual and practical insights for addressing this issue. Specifically, our results provide a segue for research streams such as social media and digital platforms to contribute to the ongoing fake news discourse—particularly in judging fake news. Fake news detection is concerned with categorizing news in terms of veracity (Conroy et al., 2015). However, with the rise of social media, we live in a digital age in which individuals cannot solely rely on expert factchecking of social media, as it is increasingly difficult to keep up with the flood of material from fake news generators (Conroy et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2024). Determining the reliability of news claims (particularly on social media) remains a daunting but critical challenge.

Our study calls for additional inquiry into the balance between embracing the hedonic attraction of social media (Kim et al., 2019; Pennycook et al., 2020b; van der Heijden, 2004) and maintaining a mindful engagement with social media content. While prior research has elaborated on the hedonic aspects, as well as their implications, the context of fake news, as highlighted by our findings, suggests that it is crucial for future research to engage in critical discourse on social media with an emphasis on how to incorporate mechanisms that trigger a critical mindset without diminishing the hedonic value. Such research would not be trivial but would hold important implications for how we design and engage with social media content going forward. This is important, as our study and decades of deception detection research have taught us that humans are often not good at detecting lies (Conroy et al., 2015). Our priming task was employed to shift individual mindsets and show the importance of more critical thinking on the persuasiveness of different evidence types. If social media platforms were to adopt the provision of discursive evidence to address the fake news problem, our results suggest that these efforts might be amplified by fostering critical reflection. While future research needs to test how such priming could be best implemented as an intervention tool, we note that our priming task implementation was not very different from frequently used reCAPTCHA tasks and could be implemented in a way that is akin to how they leverage advertisement and other levers to prime user behavior. Our study contributes to knowledge on addressing this issue by providing discursive evidence in conjunction with priming as a plausible path forward and by opening an opportunity for future research to reveal how individuals can be equipped to perform significantly better than chance.

Our study has additional implications for research streams such as artificial intelligence (AI) and design science research (DSR). AI and DSR scholars could draw on the insights from this study to create digital artifacts that can address the problem of fake news by supporting people in judging the veracity of news claims. What is perhaps most remarkable is that, in our study, the automatically produced uncurated evidence improved judgments nearly as much as the curated evidence in the context of a priming manipulation. The effectiveness of automatically produced evidence is key to providing an effective intervention at scale. Our priming results suggest that such interventions may require proper attention to the cognitive states of users (Bago et al., 2020; Pennycook et al., 2020b, 2021). This suggests multiple avenues of investigation in future AI and DSR research on fake news involving discursive evidence and priming. The automatically provided discursive evidence in our study was produced by a simple search engine query. Recent research in AI—especially natural language processing (NLP)—has shown dramatic progress in this sort of semantically guided selection of text material: Large language models such as BERT and GPT-3 (Dale, 2021) have been applied to a wide variety of tasks requiring such semantic understanding, including question answering, natural language inference, and sentiment analysis. Our results show that the crucial distinction is between helpful and unhelpful discursive evidence items. This is a challenging distinction to perform automatically. However, we suspect that current large language models are making some strides on the long road of meeting this challenge. That is, we envision a news judgment support system in which a standard search engine would produce a very large set of potentially relevant evidence items for a given news claim. Then, the helpful items could be automatically extracted from that set and presented to the user as they judge the news claim. Our results also show potential risks if the provided evidence lacks sufficient strength. At this point, it is important to remind the reader of the need for future research to engage in exploring and capturing what is considered “proof” in contemporary academic and public discourses. Our study only begins to scratch the surface. Given the findings of our research and the ever-expanding potentials of digital technology and the increasingly polarized political climate, there is a critical need to conduct studies that consider such phenomenological and epistemological issues from a variety of perspectives, leveraging different research approaches and mixed methods.

Our study provides initial steps indicating that future studies may benefit from leveraging automatically produced evidence, such as the uncurated evidence used in Studies 1 and 2, to support people’s judgment of news claims with priming more effectively. This could remedy the constraints of depending on curated evidence from an expert—particularly considering the velocity at which fake news can be generated and spread via digital platforms. Sensitizing future research to this may open avenues for IS scholarship on fake news and contribute theoretical insights and practical relevance and utility (Baskerville et al., 2018). We call on future research to consider the creation of digital artifacts that engage meaningfully with the challenge of fake news. Furthermore, we extend the call by Moravec et al. (2019) for future IS research to continue the quest to develop better approaches for enabling individuals to judge fake news effectively.

## Limitations

The findings presented in this paper should be viewed with certain limitations in mind. First, we assessed the efficacy of discursive evidence based on individuals’ judgment of a claim’s veracity. While this assessment is an important precursor for behavior, we did not measure behavior directly. Previous studies have assessed individual intentions to read, like, or share a claim and have found believability measures to be highly correlated (Kim et al., 2019; Kim & Dennis, 2019). Additionally, given that such behaviors have been repeatedly associated with more automated processes, actual behavioral responses are likely to depend on complex situational factors for which explicit intention measures are often less informative.

A related issue is the level of engagement with the news claims in our experiments. While we do not see major differences between the performance in unincentivized Study 1 and the performance in incentivized Study 2, it is difficult to determine whether participants’ levels of motivation and elaboration in our experiment match those in the real world. Reading news claims on social media platforms has been associated with a hedonic mindset (Moravec et al., 2019; van der Heijden, 2004). Our research did not investigate the users’ mindsets. Instead, we focused more broadly on fake news appearing on various digital media platforms, suggesting that the introduction of our priming task might help activate a more critical mindset in judging veracity.

Furthermore, we did not investigate the potential unintended negative effect of fact-checking or labeling news claims on unlabeled news claims (Pennycook et al., 2020a). In our case, we provided evidence for all the news claims and observed that the veracity judgment increased. Interestingly, the results of Study 3 align with this conclusion because we observed a similar implied truth effect (Pennycook et al., 2020a) with irrelevant evidence. This calls for more research into the mechanisms through which the absence of (valid) evidence might induce such effects. We note that irrelevant evidence may be encountered more in online media than in spoken conversation, where a person confronted by a claim responds with irrelevant evidence. Nonetheless, irrelevant evidence represents the polar opposite of helpful evidence for empirical comparison purposes. Relatedly, we focused on what Roberts and Qahri-Saremi (2023) called factual evidence with our notions of helpful, irrelevant, and related evidence. We did not explicitly explore what they called illusory evidence per se. Illusory evidence, as they defined it, is evidence curated to be misleading. Given the observations in our study of the potential negative influence of unhelpful evidence, this area of scholarly inquiry deserves further attention.

An additional issue concerns the categorization of claims as true or fake. PolitiFact, which is the source for most of our claims, is a recognized source of news assessments. However, there is always a possibility of error. News claims are ambiguous and vague by their nature. The categorization of a news claim rests on an assessment of real-world conditions, which either support or contradict the claim; this categorization is something that never admits complete certainty. Hence, we carried out a validation exercise to mitigate this issue (see Table A2) while acknowledging the inherent limitation that for some claims in our study, an absolute assessment of its veracity beyond any reasonable doubt remains elusive. Future research should focus on what is considered to be evidence or proof of a claim and how such considerations are shaped and changed in academic and public discourses about fake news. Those are difficult questions, and future studies should aim at richer data collection—for example, by building on qualitative and/or mixed methods studies to capture different dimensions of the phenomenon of fake news and attempting to sort out these terms as they relate to fake news.

Lastly, the participant sample only allowed for inferences about the U.S. population, and the news claims were related to issues specific to the U.S. context. Hence, we urge caution in generalizing these findings to other countries and encourage additional research to assess the efficacy of discursive evidence outside the U.S.

## Data Quality

For this project, we relied on three samples collected through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk). MTurk has been used as a data source for numerous studies on fake news (e.g., Pennycook et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; van der Linden, 2017). The quality of data collected via such online platforms has been the focus of several dedicated studies, with MTurk receiving particular attention (e.g., Douglas et al., 2022). These studies provide mixed conclusions regarding data quality, which has been found to vary over time and with study design (Webb & Tangney, 2024; Chmielewski & Kucker, 2020; Buhrmester et al., 2018). Several methods have been suggested to increase data quality (e.g., Barends & Vries, 2019; Berry et al., 2022; Chandler & Shapiro, 2016). For the three samples, we implemented several of these checks and quality requirements to screen out inattentive or unengaged respondents. We aimed for consistent data quality over all three samples and hence adapted the quality requirements to account for the differences in the length and designs between the three survey experiments.

For all samples, participants were required to be from the U.S. and have a prior approval rate of MTurk HITs of 98%. We also implemented active attention questions within the surveys. Studies 1 and 2 included the following: First, participants were shown an image of a banana and asked to enter the name of the object in an open text field. Second, a categorical item asked for the color of a banana from a multiplechoice set, with yellow being the only reasonable color. In Study 3, we only applied the latter active check as the categorial nature of the response question allowed for an extra quality check. We assessed veracity via a Likert scale rather than a binary indicator and removed all participants whose answers in the news ratings showed no variance, e.g., fully believing all news claims. In Studies 2 and 3, we also removed participants who did not provide at least one reasonable response for the 15 antonyms. We further set lower and higher time limits for the surveys. The lower limit was adapted to the workload. We increased the lower threshold for Study 2 because the priming task was added. Study 3 could be completed faster as we reduced the number of news claims. These cut-off times were similar to using the next full minute beyond the fastest percentile and close to other criteria, e.g., one third of the median time (for each study in seconds: Study 1 = 208; Study 2 = 382; Study 3 =289). The upper limit was kept steady at 50 minutes (see Table A3 for a list of the criteria).

These criteria led to an exclusion of 12-28% of the initial sample. While we can only speculate about the absolute level or relative differences for exclusion, the first two studies, with their binary ratings, made it easy for participants to complete the study. As noted in the main text, we assume the higher loss in Study 3, mainly coming from a failure to provide antonyms, is linked to the absence of response-dependent financial incentives, which might have encouraged participants in Study 2 to conduct this task more carefully. Overall, we paid our participants: Study 1, \$0.70 for successful completion; Study 2, \$0.50 for successful completion plus \$0.02 for each correctly assessed news claim (\$0.88 max.); Study 3, \$1 for successful completion.

Contrasting these data quality considerations with other fake news studies published in reputable academic journals, our approach can be considered conservative, as some neither report using time limits nor implementing active attention checks and hence retain a larger share of the original target sample (e.g., see Pennycook et al., 2021). Other studies removed similar or larger shares of their initially collected sample (e.g., see Wang et a. 2022). It is worth noting that many studies on data quality suggest that a lack of quality control is likely to introduce noise and therefore reduce treatment effects in between-subject designs (e.g., Craft et al., 2022). However, for our studies, a too-conservative approach in quality control might have shifted our sample towards particularly attentive participants, who would have been less representative of our target sample of people browsing news sites or social media. Given this risk, we reestimated our main finding with the raw sample, where the relevant variables were available. The results remained qualitatively similar, with only minor differences. Among the most notable deviations was the replication of the priming effect in Study 3, where the raw sample yielded an insignificant main effect with p = 0.051 (see Table B6, Model 4, Panel 1 for comparison). This seems, however, unsurprising as the raw sample included more than 200 participants who did not properly engage with the priming task.
