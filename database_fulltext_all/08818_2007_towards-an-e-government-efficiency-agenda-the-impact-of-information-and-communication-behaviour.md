# Towards an e-Government efficiency agenda: the impact of information and communication behaviour on e-Reverse auctions in public sector procurement

Ray Hackney<sup>1</sup>, Steve Jones<sup>2</sup> and Andrea Lo¨sch<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Brunel University, Business School, Oxbridge, Middlesex, UB83PH, U.K.; <sup>2</sup>Conwy County Borough Council, Wales, U.K.; Geoffrey Manton Building, Manchester M15 6LL, <sup>3</sup>Manchester Metropolitan University, Geoffrey Manton Building, Manchester M15 6LL, U.K.

Correspondence: R. Hackney, Brunel University, U.K. E-mail: ray.hackney@googlemail.com

## Abstract

The research in this paper explores how the general context and management information and communication behaviour in electronic Reverse Auctions (e-RAs) may impact on organizational efficiency to ensure procurement excellence. There is evidence that e-RAs improve procurement success by achieving considerable price reductions of externally sourced goods through direct, real-time and transparent competition between suppliers. This paper investigates the conditions for the successful adoption of e-RAs in the U.K. public sector generally and Welsh Unitary Authorities (WUAs) in particular. The research illustrates how e-RAs can support the procurement function and thus, can contribute to organizational efficiency in the context of electronic government (e-Government). A national U.K. survey and an exploratory case analysis in a WUA (Conwy) were undertaken to determine the conditions for the e-RA process. The findings underline the critical importance of the adequate preparation of the e-RA through appropriate information and communication behaviour. Consequently, the paper identifies a set of enabling factors and corresponding guidelines that could be used for the successful adoption of e-RAs in the public sector. The contribution of the research will be of benefit to both academics and practitioners engaged in procurement research and implementation.

## Introduction

Several companies (e.g. BMW and RAC) and public sector organizations (e.g. NHS and universities) have started using electronic-Reverse Auctions (e-RAs) to achieve direct cost reductions of externally sourced goods (B2B Research Centre, 2003; Wagner and Schwab, 2004). In e-RAs, buying organizations invite pre-selected suppliers who compete against each other to supply a specified good or service, thus driving down the supply price through direct competition (De Boer et al., 2002). The potential savings range from 5 to 30%, or in some cases even more (Turbin et al., 2000; Barling, 2001; Sashi and O’Leary, 2002; Beall et al., 2003). By considerably driving down the cost of the procurement function, e-RAs can make a significant contribution to the overall organizational efficiency of e-Government. Given the advances in the public sector to reduce costs through efficient purchasing (Office of the Deputy Minister, 2003; Gershon, 2004), e-RAs may present a useful initiative to further improve purchasing operations in the public sector.

However, in spite of the perceived benefits of e-RAs, recent studies (Jap, 2002; Beall et al., 2003; Jap, 2003; B2B Research Centre, 2003; Emiliani, 2006) indicate potential pitfalls of e-RAs. Emiliani (2006, p. 6) points out that if not managed properly, ‘poor sourcing decisions, higher costs, and less cooperative supplier relationships are a common result of e-RAs – the opposite of what buyers hoped to achieve from e-RAs’. Lapiedra et al. (2004), for instance, found that suppliers who had won an e-RA by bidding below their margins could quickly recover these initial losses in later negotiations. Thus, the question arises how e-RAs can be used successfully and deliver sustainable savings for public sector bodies such as governmental institutions. Both contextual conditions and appropriate information and communication behaviour supposedly contribute to the development of efficient buyer–supplier relationships (Williamson, 1981; Beall et al., 2003). This study will therefore investigate how both the general context and the information and communication behaviour in e-RAs may impact on organizational efficiency in the public sector, particularly Welsh Unitary Authorities (WUAs).

## Theoretical stance: information and communication behaviour

Williamson’s (1981) Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) is one of the most commonly used theoretical backgrounds for analysing an organization’s choice of buyer–supplier relationships and its efficiency (Olsen and Ellram, 1997). Generally, organizations can choose between two alternative governance structures (Williamson, 1981). As each governance structure is associated with certain transaction costs, the goal is to choose the ‘right’, that is, most efficient structure and relationship, respectively. The decision of the right governance structure and buyer– supplier relationship, however, does not only depend on contextual factors (e.g. the level of competition, uncertainty, transaction specificity or the frequency of the transaction (Williamson, 1981)). Behavioural factors such as bounded rationality of the decision maker or opportunism equally affect the decision by directly influencing the information available and taking into account when making the decision. Despite giving useful insights into the formation and efficiency of buyer–supplier relationships, the TCE approach has several limitations: (1) TCE neither specifies how buyer–supplier relationships can be characterized nor how their efficiency can be judged apart from the vague indicator ‘transaction costs’ (Bianchi, 1995). (2) The exact role and concept of information and related behaviours remains unclear and (3) The role of different contextual variables remains arguable (Kay, 1992). The following sub-sections will therefore provide a critical review of the literature in order to identify useful adaptations and extensions of the TCE framework for the conceptual background of this study.

## Buyer–supplier relationships

Within the literature, various classifications and models of buyer–supplier relationships have been suggested (Gibbs, 1998; Cousins, 2002). Despite this diversity, several recurrent intrinsic and extrinsic characteristics of buyer–supplier relationships can be found (Lamming et al., 1996). Whereas intrinsic characteristics (i.e. trust, commitment, long-term orientation, investments and dependency) tend to be regarded as inherent in any relationship and describe their inner nature, extrinsic characteristics (e.g. success) tend to represent certain outcomes or results. The function of extrinsic characteristics is not merely descriptive, but directly judgmental in terms of indicating the general quality and efficiency of a relationship. However, the evaluation of relationship performance is still unclear (Boddy et al., 1998). Generally, the perception of the quality of a relationship is a very subjective affair (Lamming et al., 1996; Ambrose, 2005). O’Toole and Donaldson (2002) found that particularly intangible factors play an important role for determining the actual performance of a relationship including satisfaction, conflict level and perceived successfulness (e.g. Mohr and Spekman, 1994; Jap and Ganesan, 2000). Overall, buyer–supplier relationships in this research will be assessed both in terms of their intrinsic (i.e. trust, commitment, long-term orientation, investments and dependency) and extrinsic facets (i.e. perceived successfulness, satisfaction and level of conflict).

## Information and communication behaviour

The literature review also reveals adequate ‘information and communication behaviour’ as a seemingly indispensable pre-condition for well functioning and effective buyer–supplier relationships (Wilson, 1999). Several studies indicate that the information behaviour between buyers and suppliers may have a significant influence on buyer–supplier relationships (Spekman, 1988; Helper, 1991; Mohr and Spekman, 1994). For instance, improvements in the information flow between buyers and suppliers in a supply chain may increase the effectiveness of their relationship (Lewis and Talalayevsky, 2004). Helper (1991) reported that information behaviour and commitment are closely related and have a major impact on relationship performance. However, even though buyers and suppliers do realize the importance of adequate information and communication exchange, they often fail to actually address the criticality of this dimension in practice (Kern and Willcocks, 2002). Generally, human information behaviour consists of three inter-related processes: seeking information, exchanging information and actually using the information (Wilson, 1981, 1999). Applying this model to the purchasing context, a purchasing manager requires information to make a decision, which is why he seeks and exchanges information. Using the information refers to actually deciding from which supplier to source (i.e. the supplier selection). Research (Spekman, 1988; Choy et al., 2004) has shown that various pieces of information and decision criteria may be considered by the buyer as part of the supplier selection process (e.g. delivery, price, service, past experience, warranties and claims, quality, location, technological capability and the supplier’s financial situation). Ndubisi et al. (2005) claim that the supplier selection strategy is directly related to the effective building of the buyer–seller relations. However, e-RAs may have brought changes to the traditional selection process and the related information seeking, exchange and decision-making behaviour (see Weber et al., 1991), which may affect the efficiency of the resulting relationships.

## Context and applicability of e-RAs

E-RAs are not applicable to all purchasing situations. It is reported that they tend to work particularly well and are desirable when the ability to specify the products is high, that is, when the product has clearly defined attributes that suppliers can translate into unambiguous specifications. Furthermore, e-RAs are primarily used for stable purchases of non-strategic products. Also, successful e-RAs generally involve a greater number of suppliers (four or more) than unsuccessful auctions (Jap, 2002; Mabert and Skeels, 2002; Smeltzer and Karr, 2003). However, all suppliers need to be equally qualified suppliers if e-RAs are to be used efficiently (Beall et al., 2003). Generally, the particular industry sector (Sako, 1992) may also play an important role for the applicability of e-RAs. ## Context of the research

The focus of this paper is to determine the impact of e-RA within a WUA. The Gershon (2004) report and the Office Deputy Prime Minister (2003) national procurement strategy aim at improving purchasing procedures in the local government. The Welsh Assembly is particularly committed to maximizing value for money in order to deliver better quality public services in Wales. A recent initiative, ‘Making the Connections’ seeks to maximize efficiency gains through more effective cooperation and coordination between agencies across the Welsh public sector. The Assembly has set a target of achieving d600 million of value for money improvements across the Welsh public service by 2010, which includes a distinct target for procurement savings of d120 million by 2008, with the possibility of more by 2010. Greater use of collaborative procurement engendered in e-RAs may significantly contribute to the achievement of this goal.

While e-RAs have a track record in the private sector, they are relatively new to local government. Therefore, they should be carefully evaluated. Particularly for smaller authorities, benefits of e-RAs might be available at less risk if they join a consortium, as was the case in Wales. By collaborating in contract negotiations and aggregating demand, public sector organizations may generally reduce prices and deliver economies of scale in procurement processes. Local authorities like the WUA, many of which buy the same goods from the same suppliers, thus are in a prime position to join together in procurement negotiations and reduce prices in this way.

Several authorities now take a more corporate approach to purchasing and do not only provide staff with professional training but are also developing their own procurement strategies (Irani et al., 2005). Figure 2 notes differences between the corporate and service-specific approach to procurement.

The implications of this new approach are diverse. For example, the Office of Government Commerce (OGC) has reached a number of agreements with major IT suppliers that allow all public sector organizations to buy their products at significantly cheaper prices. However, recent audit reports have found that these suppliers are not used as widely as might be expected, particularly in local government. By aggregating demand and involving all interested parties in the procurement process from the outset, the savings should be greater and both the initial capital investment and ongoing running costs should be cheaper. This paper will illustrate how such efficiency gains can be achieved through the use of e-RAs.

## Methodology

Taking into account the novelty and the little existing evidence about the role of information and its impact on the effectiveness of buyer–supplier relationships in e-RAs, the purpose of this research is mainly explorative (Stake, 1995, #124). Following Creswell (1994), the study combines both quantitative and qualitative research methods in a two-phase design with a survey among public sector buyers followed by a single case (focus group) study of a local government authority (within WUA). While the survey allowed for identifying existing differences between e-RAs and other sourcing events, the use of a case study provided additional insights into the properties of and inter-relations between the concepts under study, and their importance for e-RA efficiency. Due to the limited amount of both empirical and theoretical evidence, the study employs an inductive approach aiming at the development of theory grounded in data which, as Glaser and Strauss (1967) claim, should be more likely to resemble the ‘reality’ than theory deduced from a series of concepts based on limited evidence or speculation. Consequently, sampling in this research refers to theoretical sampling rather than statistical sampling, that is, it is undertaken so that additional information can be obtained to help in generating conceptual categories (Glaser, 1994; Stake, 1995; Robson, 2003).

The major aim of the survey was to obtain insights into potential differences between e-RAs and other sourcing scenarios in the public sector. The choice was made in favour of an online questionnaire because this would have two major advantages: (1) The flexibility of the online format would make it possible to include and directly compare different groups (i.e. both buyers and suppliers with and without e-RA experience) in terms of contextual factors, information behaviour and buyer– supplier relationships. (2) The online format would also allow for overcoming the lack of anonymity that often presents a major barrier in the collection of sensitive data. The content of the questionnaire was based on the conceptual assumptions that emerged from the literature. In order to prevent possible problems with questionwording, answer-choices and answer-coding (see Robson, 2003), the questionnaire was formally piloted by purchasing and supply professionals at the IPSERA 2005 conference.

The survey data presented in this paper includes the responses from 56 public sector buyers. The distribution of the survey was supported by various organizations that would allow access to both e-RA users and non-users, including e-bidding software provider CombineNet, e-sourcing consultancy e-Three, the Chartered Institute of Purchasing and Supply (CIPS) and their Policy Advisory Network (PAN), the NHS, the Southern (SUPC) and North Western Universities Purchasing Consortium (NWUPC). Following the pilot study in April 2005, the actual collection of the survey data took place between May and July 2005. Participants were first asked to identify the most recent e-RA or sourcing event they had recently undertaken. They were then requested to provide information (1) about the context and information behaviour in this particular e-RA or sourcing event and (2) about the relationship to one supplier of their choice in this event. The survey questions and corresponding scales are summarized in the Appendix.

The identification of differences between e-RA users and non-users is based on (1) the analysis of the mean values in the two groups and the consistency of the mean differences (i.e. $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { e - R A ~ u s e r } } \mathrm { - M e a n } _ { \mathrm { n o n - u s e r } } )$ and (2) the effect sizes obtained from the Mann–Whitney U-statistic. Regarding the latter, the Mann–Whitney U-test is a nonparametric test for identifying differences between two independent groups on a continuous measure; it provides a score (the Z-score) from which the effect size of the differences can be calculated. It is generally pointed out that the estimation of the size of such effects is generally more important than significance tests. In fact, even the smallest of differences would be deemed as statistically significant if a big enough sample is used. The solution to this criticism is to measure the size of the effect in a standardized way which does not depend on sample size. Consequently, the effect size in this research are classified as small (values between 0.10 and 0.30), medium (values between 0.30 and 0.50) and large effects (values between 0.50 and 1.00).

In addition, the emerging differences in mean values between e-RA users and non-users were systematically analysed in terms of their consistency. Glaser (1994) points out that testing for statistical significance or merely focussing on effect sizes may present a strong barrier to the generation of theory as these tests direct attention away from theoretically interesting relationships that are not of sufficient magnitude to be statistically significant or are simply small in their size. Here, the analysis of differences for consistency can usefully aid the development of grounded theory (Glaser, 1994): If, for instance, e-RA users consistently score lower values on all relationship items than non-users (i.e. Mean<sub>e-RA user</sub>Mean<sub>non-user</sub> is negative for all items), it can be assumed that the buyer–supplier relationship tends to be affected in a negative way in e-RAs – even if the observed differences are only small. As such, the consistency of such differences presents another important indicator for the existence and importance of effects.

The subsequent case study was undertaken in the context of local government, that is, a WUA. The use of a case approach in the second data collection phase was appropriate considering that the research aims to investigate e-RA events within an empirical context (Yin, 2003). It would also allow for taking a more indepth view of underlying issues of e-RAs in addition to the largely descriptive survey findings. The data derived both from a substantive case analysis within Conwy Borough Council where interviews with the purchasing manager (referred to as PM) and discussions with information technology managers (referred to ITMs) and available documentary evidence. The interviews followed Flanagan’s (1954) critical incident technique (CIT), which has been successfully applied to research process efficiency in various fields including IS development, purchasing (Carter and Dresner, 2001) and buyer–supplier relationships (Wong, 1999). Respondents are asked to initially identify the general aim of the e-RA process and then focus on ‘critical incidents’, that is, incidents where the outcome was particularly effective or ineffective (Fountain, 1999). The critical incidents identified in this study were two e-RAs: one for IT equipment and one for stationary.

Following the grounded theory approach (Glaser, 1994; Strauss and Corbin, 1998), broad initial coding categories were developed based on the areas of interest for this research, that is, context, information behaviour and buyer–supplier relationships. The subsequent analysis of the interview transcripts and documentary evidence closely followed the suggestions for axial and selective coding and the general suggestions given for the analysis of case study materials (Yin, 2003). After allocating each text segment to the initial categories, the allocated fragments of the text were further analysed for interesting or conspicuous characteristics and relations to other segments that led to the creation of more detailed, and often more explanatory codes. Both critical incidents reports were also analysed for commonalities and differences using the method of cross-case synthesis as suggested by Yin (2003), which helped validate the observed enabling factors and inter-relations.

The survey data also appear to confirm the assumption that e-RAs have a negative impact on buyer–supplier relationships, as shown in Table 2. All intrinsic facets (i.e. trust, commitment, dependency, investments and longterm orientation) received lower scores from the buyers in the context of e-RAs. Interestingly, the perceived conflict level too tends to be lower in e-RAs than in other sourcing events. Whereas participating buyers characterized other sourcing events such as tenders as lengthy, cumbersome and complex, the very straightforward, highly standardized process of e-RAs may simply not leave enough room for discussions and potentially evolving conflicts that might explain the lower conflict level in e-RAs.

It is also useful to note that e-RAs do not appear to have any impact on the satisfaction with or the success of the relationship (see Table 2). Considering the widely acclaimed savings and efficiency of e-RAs (Turbin et al., 2000; Barling, 2001; Sashi and O’Leary, 2002; Beall et al., 2003), one would expect that buyers are more satisfied in e-RAs and that they judge their successfulness to be higher. As Emiliani and Stec (2004) suggest ‘[e-RAs] are y a diversive purchasing tool designed principally to drive down supply prices without adequate consideration given to other important measures’, which may then limit their potential efficiency, thus explaining the above findings on satisfaction and successfulness.

The survey data only partially support Emiliani and Stec’s (2004) claim that the importance of several types of information buyers collect in the preparation stages of a purchasing event is hardly lower in e-RAs than in other sourcing events (see Table 3). Only the importance of market information is noticeably higher in e-RAs, possibly indicating the high importance of the existing market conditions for the feasibility of e-RAs.

In addition, the frequency of face-to-face communication does not appear to suffer in the context of eRAs as shown in Table 4: buyers communicate with their buyers as often as they do in other sourcing situations. However, the information exchange via email and telephone is slightly reduced.

Regarding the quality of the information exchange and communication between buyers and suppliers, no consistent effects could be found, as shown in Table 5. Only the accuracy and usefulness of the information exchanged as well as the openness of the communication seem to be noticeably reduced in the context of e-RAs. It may be assumed that the slight reduction in both quality and the frequency of the information exchange are due to the tight time constraints and pressures the buyer is exposed in the highly standardized e-RA process.

Within the final supplier selection, the data suggest that price is clearly more important in e-RAs than in other sourcing situations as shown in Table 6. All remaining criteria are either as important as in other sourcing situations or considerably less important.

Interestingly, the past experience with the particular supplier is practically as important in e-RAs as in other sourcing situations. The analysis of the cross-tabulations confirms that in e-RAs previously known suppliers are more likely to be awarded the contract than in the other sourcing scenarios: in e-RAs, 73.9% of all suppliers who were awarded the contract in the e-RA were previously known suppliers. In other sourcing situations, only 54.8% of all suppliers who were awarded the contract were previously known suppliers. Even though the conflict level is generally lower, relationships in e-RAs are neither more successful nor more satisfactory for the buyers, which seems to support the assumption that e-RAs indeed may be detrimental to an effective building and management of buyer–supplier relationships. Even though buyers do not seem to disregard any information prior to the e-RA, it is possible that particularly the slightly reduced communication via phone and email as well as the reduced accuracy of the information, usefulness of the information and openness of the communication contribute to such negative effects. In addition, the over-emphasizing of price and the corresponding under-estimation of other decision criteria might present another reason for the negative effects of e-RAs on buyer–supplier relations and their reduced efficiency.

## Case analysis – WUA Conwy

The analysis of the case study within the WUA helped answer the questions regarding the how and why of the above survey findings and observed inefficiencies Yin, 2003, #516. The case study identifies one central requirement for the efficiency of e-RAs: a clear specification that is perfectly understood by all participants. Only when suppliers know exactly what they are competing for (i.e. in terms of product and conditions of the contract), are they able to calculate a sustainable bid representing the best market price. Any uncertainty regarding specification and contract directly impacts upon the efficiency of the e-RA. The following results illustrate how this central requirement of clarity can be achieved (or threatened) by the (in)adequate consideration of contextual conditions and (in)effective information behaviour.

## Adequate consideration of context and general conditions

As already indicated by the survey findings, e-RAs appear to take part under specific contextual conditions. An inadequate consideration or misinterpretation of these conditions may reduce the efficiency of the auction.

Market characteristics The first step for buyers consists of identifying whether there is sufficient market place interest for an e-RA. If there is not, the idea of conducting an e-RA should be abandoned. The stability of the market requires attention too, as PM stresses: ‘You and [the suppliers] need to know what the market is going to do y . [I]f you want to tie them on a fixed price on the commodity, they will look to you to pay the risk rather than themselves so you end up paying a high price’. Also, switching costs may be significant and inhibit the overall effectiveness of an e-RA: ‘If you are wanting to buy y on a three month contract, then an e-auction might be a very good way of doing it. But you got to be prepared for the cost of switching your supplier every three months’. PM further notes that e-RAs drive down the prices in two ways: (1) via a sufficient volume allowing suppliers to lower their profit margins because they are guaranteed a lot of business, or (2) via excess capacities:‘[I]f we are buying a marginal capacity rather than prime capacity from the supplier y [the supplier] can win business by charging less than the normal price for that commodity because he’s recovered his fixed costs elsewhere’.

Industry/sector The sector in which the buying organization is operating in may reduce the level of clarity of both specification and contract, thus limit the efficiency of e-RAs. As public sector body, WUAs underlie the EU Procurement Rules (Arrowsmith, 2002) thus needing to advertise the contract in the European Journal and going through the EU tender process that is often very lengthy and expensive. As such, it is often not feasible for single public sector organizations to conduct e-RAs on their own; rather, they need to combine their spend with other public sector bodies. Also, due to the way the public sector operates, e-RAs can only arrive at the so-called Framework Agreements which, as PM notes, has the disadvantage that ‘there are no guarantees within the contract if we buy any or how many we will buy. So there are no values or quantities within the contract’. This uncertainty poses a risk for suppliers, which they translate into higher bids.

Despite these difficulties, the use of e-RAs in the WUA was found to have considerable benefits. Generally, the WUA strategic procurement partnership offered specific programme scope and networking opportunities to improve procurement practice across the Wales public sector. The case study illustrates that procuring one large contract through an e-RA generally costs less and takes less time than negotiating a number of small contracts. The ITM stated that ‘major suppliers were more attracted by one large contract than a number of smaller contracts’ and this resonates with the literature. The ITM also stated that ‘time saved negotiating this major contract meant that other important IT work could be undertaken’. Prior to the e-RA, Local authorities were paying different prices for the same equipment from the same supplier. Through the e-RA, cashable savings have been achieved and financial benefits have been identified and tracked, which have contributed to the good financial health of the WUA. Now, all authorities are paying the same reduced price and there is a clear pricing structure. The ITM stated that ‘there are also opportunities to identify and undertake other e-RA activities’.

Product characteristics As the survey data suggest, not all products are equally suitable for e-RAs. The type of good directly impacts on its specifiability: complex goods are more difficult to translate into unambiguous specifications than, for example, commodities – which has direct consequences for the efficiency of the auction. Also, different products are often related to a different organization or structure of purchasing, as PM explains: ‘We’ve got several hundred people out there in this council y who may want to be buying stationary’. Such a diffuse structure may cause difficulties to accurately identify the needs. In contrast to that, the purchasing structure for IT facilitates the collection of needs information and the development of an accurate specification, as PM points out: ‘IT is dealt with differently in most public sector bodies from stationary. Most public sector bodies will have a single person in the organisation responsible for IT’.

## Informational excellence throughout the entire e-RA process

Having carefully considered whether the contextual conditions are right for conducting an e-RA, buyers need to carefully collect, analyse, and adequately communicate all relevant information at any stage of the e-RA, that is, needs analysis, specification and final decision.

Needs analysis E-RAs start with a thorough needs analysis (e.g. via questionnaire) leading to the development of a baseline specification. However, the accurate identification of the needs requires an extensive search, collection and analysis of needs information as well as direct information exchange and communication with the users or their representatives. For the unsuccessful stationary auction, PM noted that ‘the people who had put the bid together hadn’t really done enough preliminary work with councils y and said ‘‘What are your needs really going to be’’ So the indicative quantities that went into the tender and the weightings in the basket of goods were hopelessly inaccurate. And that has not helped’.

Specification In addition, the buyers in the unsuccessful e-RA failed to clearly communicate the meaning of both specification and contract terms. This had severe consequences for the efficiency of the e-RA – on both the buyers’ and the suppliers’ side. As in the IT equipment auction, the lowest bidders obtained the status of qualified suppliers under the new Framework Agreement. The stationary contract was done as a ‘basket of goods’ detailing several commodities and their indicative volumes and values. The suppliers assumed that once being a qualified supplier, a council who chose to buy from them would automatically need to buy all goods contained in the basket from them at the prices bid. As a consequence, the bids obtained in the stationary auction turned out to be entirely inappropriate: having misunderstood the terms and conditions of the contract, two suppliers took a fixed percentage off all their prices. ‘The third [one] took the view that a significant volume of the contract lay with paper. So it said, in terms of its bidding strategy, we will keep our price for paper fixed, but we will reduce the price of everything else in the shopping basket’. When the councils realized that the suppliers had made mistakes in their pricing strategy, they started what PM describes as ‘cherry picking’. For instance, buyers bought their paper at a ‘normal’ price from the first and the second supplier, but everything else for next to nothing from the third supplier.

The consequences of the suppliers’ misunderstanding were horrendous as PM illustrates: ‘The suppliers suddenly woke up to the fact that they may have exposed themselves and while trying to persuade councils that it was a ‘‘buy all or nothing’’ bid, councils were cherry picking’. PM admits it is not only the suppliers to blame but also the people who had put the specification together who ‘really had not done enough preliminary work’ and ‘of course, we did not help in terms of the preparation’. He concludes that ‘what we are seeing now is that the prices are all an awful lot closer to reality than at the beginning of the contract. For individual users this has caused all sorts of problems because with our financial having seen that we could buy stationary literally for 80% less than we were buying it before have gone around slashing budgets taking money out of people’s budgets because you don’t need it any more. And then, the prices have gone back upy . We have seen that some of the suppliers in the first year put their prices up by 50 or 60% across the lot of their commoditiesy . So that one is a nightmare. It cost us a huge amount – also in time and in credibility’.

Final decision Often, the best suppliers are not the ones winning the auction and better results can be achieved through the adequate consideration of existing relationships and both appropriate communication and management of suppliers as PM illustrates for the successful IT auction: ‘Wrexham also for a lot of technical reasons decided to stay with Fujitsu, but went to Fujitsu saying: ‘‘Look, this is the price we could buy our equipment for under the current all Wales framework agreement. This is your current price under the OGC contract. What is going to happen?’’ and Fujitsu said: ‘‘We will find you a better price’’’. Even though Fujitsu’s prices are still slightly more expensive than the prices of the winning suppliers, Wrexham managed to get the overall best value for money. This achievement is largely due to the accurate handling of the situation and buyers’ way to communicate the situation to the supplier in an appropriate way. Thus, purely price-based decisions may not necessarily provide the optimal solution. Rather, a best value solution based on an existing good relationship with the supplier as well as the supplier’s willingness to enhance the efficiency of the relationship may often outmatch the lowest price solutions. This, however, is only possible if a good relationship and supplier management are already in place: ‘[W]e normally have account managers set up for us and they will deal [with us] faceto-face y . So all the auction does is drive down the price. It doesn’t change anything down the stream in terms of contract or supplier management’.

However, the ITM highlights that ‘it can become very difficult to incentivize the supplier to provide an appropriate level of performance towards the end of long-term contractual arrangements’. There is a danger of lack of value for money if the procurer becomes tied to one supplier for a long period of time and another e-RA is not undertaken. In addition, several other risks associated with the contract award have been identified, such as insufficient contractual flexibility to meet particular changing or new partnership needs or a lack of capacity to resource any future change process or future requirements. In the successful IT auction, a great deal of work was therefore undertaken to ensure that project and e-RA process did not adopt fundamentally poor processes: the contract was properly and proactively managed to ensure that problems do not emerge. This is especially important as there will be a significant cost and lengthy timescale to make major changes to the existing contract or to undertake another e-RA to overcome any problems with the existing supplier. As such, it is important for buyers to understand that efficient relationships are a ‘separate, ongoing business’ and not the result of the e-RA. Only a combination of e-RA combined with strategic supplier management is likely to deliver sustainable benefits.

Finally, it is important to ensure appropriate internal communication prior and during the decision-making phase in order to ensure that the approved suppliers indeed are the most suitable ones for all parties involved in the e-RAs. In the IT auction, there has been a lack of effective engagement with stakeholders, especially the users of the IT equipment in the case organization. The ITM stated that ‘the new supplier and associated kit has been forced upon users without consultation or a trial of the equipment’. The implementation has not been split into manageable steps. Instead, a big-bang approach has been adopted that resulted in social and human issues emerging, such as resistance to using the new equipment and resistance to change.

## Discussion and synthesis

In accordance with the existing literature, the findings obtained from both survey and case studies indicate that e-RAs tend to take place under certain contextual conditions (Jap, 2002; Mabert and Skeels, 2002; Beall et al., 2003; Smeltzer and Karr, 2003). Of particular importance for e-RA success are a high contract volume (e.g. through the combining spend), the existence of favourable market conditions (i.e. excess capacities and high level of competition) and the use of easily specifiable products (e.g. with a manageable purchasing structure and with low complexity). Generally, the survey findings support assumption that e-RAs affect buyer– supplier relationships in a negative way. Also, the information exchange and communication behaviour as well as the decision making seemed to have several drawbacks in the context of e-RAs. This is consistent with Beall et al. (2003) who found that regarding the information exchange in e-RAs, many suppliers suspect that buyers withhold information and/or only use the e-RA to drive down the price of the supplier they actually want to source. Particularly new suppliers tend to be disadvantaged in this respect, thus reducing the chances for developing a relationship with the buyer (see Lo¨sch, 2005). Insufficient information and communication behaviour present the potentially most severe problem of e-RAs: missing out on participants’ concerns and/or important facts regarding the specification might easily lead to complications after the auction. Figure 3 summarizes the general factors that may help buyers to achieve e-RA and relationship efficiency as found in this research.

While the survey data provide important insights into the general characteristics of e-RAs, the case study interviews provided important insights into how context and information behaviour actually contribute to efficient buyer–supplier relationships and e-RA success. The resulting practical implications for the appropriate management of e-RAs are therefore critically important. It is crucial to define the scope of the e-RA and set clear specifications and realistic targets. Local authorities need to identify the type of products that are conducive to successful e-RAs and the processes that can be consolidated to achieve economies of scale. They should ensure that standardization is the rule for any e-RA specification, with customization only in exceptional and properly considered and authorized cases. Also, each step of the process needs to be prepared, supported, and executed with thorough information behaviour. This does not only include the external information behaviour between buyers and suppliers, but also the internal informational efficiency between the buyer(s) responsible for the e-RA and other buyers or stakeholders. Consequently, there needs to be a high level of trust between the lead procurer and other procurers to ensure that there is confidence that the contract can be managed remotely without risk to the quality of the supply service provided. Also, service users should be involved in all stages of development to ensure that the procurement service and equipment meets their requirements.

Regarding the selection process, care needs to be taken to ensure that all of the terms of supplier’s best and final offer are fully reflected in the eventual contract. It is important to note that the success of the contract ultimately depends on the quality of services provided by the supplier. Although value assurance is incorporated, there may be a need for service level agreements in the contract to, as noted, ‘incentivize’ good performance. For example, targets to prevent delays in the renewal of equipment and mechanisms to maintain good communication. Others could include factors that measure the quality of services, measure user satisfaction with the supply contract and assess the status of the project. Future contracts should contain robust value for money mechanisms to enable costs to be benchmarked and externally validated. These mechanisms should extend to contractual change provisions. Also, there needs to be an effective management structure for the partnership that focusses on the job and avoids corporate leaders in the detail. This will result in an effective governance structure with clear service commitments, performance measurement and accountability. The way individuals undertake their roles and responsibilities in the procurement partnership, is of paramount importance and will heavily influence the degree of success of the project. Direction and ownership is an important aspect of the project.

Overall, the research reports that the Conwy e-RA procurement process was satisfactory and delivered a transparent framework capable of meeting the Welsh Assembly recommendations to improve major procurements, to improve the performance of WUA’s and to support the procurer/supplier relationship in the future. It is useful to highlight the importance of lowest cost and affordability, as value for money should always be given weight when negotiating contracts. There is a need to include a break clause under which the regime is reviewed after each technology refresh. Public sector officials need to rigorously analyse the costs and benefits of the selected option and ensure that there is a clear value for money justification for the eventual decision. The relationship between the procurer and supplier needs to be evaluated to ensure it is operationally effective, and perhaps further enhanced by a more effective strategic partnership. An ITM states that ‘there is scope to develop future initiatives benefiting both supplier and procurer and therefore improving the initial project’. The partnership needs to satisfy itself that the decision to proceed with the e-RA a strategy was soundly based, that the procurement process is operationally efficient and effective and that the framework in place for the partnership is appropriate and will work in the coming years.

## Conclusions

This study attempted to (1) to explore how both the general context and the information and communication behaviour in e-RAs may impact on organizational efficiency and (2) identify cornerstones for public sector efficiency through procurement excellence. Overall, the research in this paper clearly demonstrates that the preparation of the e-RA directly influences its efficiency. Critically, buyers need to carefully consider whether the conditions for conducting an e-RA are right, and if so, ensure that their specification is crystal clear and accurate – and clearly understood by the suppliers. To achieve purchasing efficiency through e-RA, purchasing managers in the public sector need to make sure that the following conditions are applied:

\- the right type of product (highly standardized and easy to specify);

\- the right organization/structure of purchasing (key informants);

\- appropriate market conditions (sufficient competition, price fluctuations, market interest, switching costs and excess capacities);

\- adequate process management (i.e. accurate needs analysis; clear specification detailing accurate indicative quantities/volume and values, adequate prequalification and final selection of suppliers) and

\- existing supplier management including a clear, open and candid communication;

\- one large contract rather than a number of small contracts (to attract the supplier);

\- flexible commercial arrangements that takes account business needs;

\- value for money mechanisms to enable costs to be benchmarked and externally validated and

\- affordability and value for money issues are properly considered and weighted when negotiating contracts.

If conducted correctly, taking into account the enabling factors and guidelines identified in this research, e-RA may indeed lead to significant opportunities for achieving value for money in public sector procurement events. The study therefore suggests that e-RA’s provide an important approach to procurement, which is appropriate within a public sector context and will support an e-Government efficiency agenda.

Clearly, further research is necessary to determine public sector organizations’ approaches to e-RA design and implementation. For example, the use of robust scenario planning when letting major contracts via e-RA. The adoption of software modelling techniques for business case analysis and subsequent formal evaluation processes.
