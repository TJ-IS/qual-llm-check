# Mediators between coordination and IS project performance

Neeraj Parolia <sup>a,\*</sup>, Stephen Goodman <sup>a</sup>, Yuzhu Li <sup>a</sup>, James J. Jiang h

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, University of Central Florida, Orlando,

Received 30 March 2006; accepted 10 June 2007 Available online 21 August 2007

## Abstract

Successful IS projects result from coordination among team members and stakeholders. We examined the effects of horizontal and vertical coordination on project performance. A total of 169 responses from IS project managers listed in the Project Management Institute of the USA were solicited, obtained, and analyzed. The results indicated that horizontal coordination can enhance the level of leadership empowerment and knowledge transfer, and help to clarify the mission and objectives among team members, while vertical coordination can enhance knowledge transfer. The results further suggested that project performance was improved by empowering team members, promoting knowledge transfer among team members and specifying clear mission and project objectives.

## 1. Introduction

Coordination is a central problem in IS development (ISD) projects. Failure to meet deadlines is endemic in the IS industry. During the execution of project tasks, information exchange is essential. Coordination mechanisms can be classified into two categories: vertical or horizontal. The mechanisms for vertical coordination are usually implemented through project managers, a steering committee/and management. In horizontal coordination, the linkage is provided by an individual team member who communicates directly with other members or users on a one-to-one basis. Vertical coordination can reduce project uncertainty by controlling project scope change; horizontal coordination, measured in terms of mutual adjustments and lateral communication, provides improved project performance through learning [31]. Extensive horizontal coordination between users and IS staff often leads to knowledge transfer and organizational learning. Due to the nature of IS tasks, horizontal coordination may be most important [5].

However coordination has not been the subject of extensive study in the IS literature. Most empirical studies have attempted to establish a direct link between coordination and project outcomes [1,24,33]. Unfortunately, there are few empirical results and these are inconclusive. For example, Andres and Zmud found that horizontal coordination strategy had positive main effects on software development productivity and user satisfaction. However, Nidumolu concluded that there is no direct link between horizontal coordination and final project outcome. Furthermore, Nidumolu suspected that the two coordination mechanisms play different roles in the IS development process. While vertical coordination has a positive impact on process control, horizontal coordination may help IS staff and users explore more system alternatives.

We attempted to answer the question:

How do horizontal and vertical coordination affect the performance of IS projects?

Thus trying to explain how coordination leads to improvement in the final IS project through leadership, knowledge transfer, and a clear mission statement with objectives.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

IT projects face peculiar challenges, such as requirement uncertainty, change in project scope, phased implementation, distributed implementation teams, and personnel working on several tasks. These problems demand effective coordination mechanisms. In fact, the effectiveness of coordination is a critical success factor of IT project implementation.

Coordination theory [40] posits that task interdependence and uncertainty determine the use of the modes of coordination. There are two general ways in which organizations can be coordinated: by programming or feedback. Coordination by programming is relatively static, involving such means as division of labor, regulation, or standard operating procedures that align individual actions through structure or directive. Coordination by feedback (or mutual adjustment) is more informal and unstructured; individuals exchange information about their state and adjust their behavior to ensure compatibility between the goals and their actions. In the IS context, coordination by feedback is often adopted. Either individual role occupants serve as the way of making mutual task adjustments through vertical or horizontal channels of communication or the group participates in staff or committee meetings.

In our work we focused on the personal mode where, due to uncertainty, mutual work adjustment occurs via horizontal communication channels and group meetings. When task interdependence is high, horizontal direct contact between unit members occurs and the coordination modes are expanded.

Past research focused primarily on antecedents, strategies, contingencies and outcomes of coordination. For example, trained teams demonstrated significantly greater proficiency than untrained ones. Coordination was found to be influenced by balance of communication, shared cognitions about the distribution of expertise in the team, time working together, interactions and frequency of communication and interactions between shared cognition and team history. Coordination is achieved through IT tools and the power of the project leader over the functional manager. Coordination becomes difficult as project size and complexity increase. However, it leads to personal success in team members [17] and increases project performance [39]. Unfortunately, limited attention has been paid to the impacts of coordination on learning and knowledge transfer.

Tasks in organization are coordinated using organizational structures, which consist of information linkages possessing different capacity for carrying information. Vertical information linkages coordinate activities between the organization hierarchies and increase efficiency and control while horizontal linkages coordinate across departments and help to overcome barriers.

The members of IS project teams are often selected from different functional areas. and the teams can adopt either vertical or horizontal coordination. In the IS context, vertical coordination is the extent to which coordination between users and IS staff is performed by designated authorities, such as steering committees and project managers. Horizontal coordination is the extent to which coordination between users and IS staff is undertaken through mutual adjustment and communication. Horizontal coordination creates an environment for learning and unity of effort. Past literature has shown that the transfer of knowledge, clear mission and objectives, and team empowerment are critical for team learning [4,36]; members then have a clear vision of the team and understand how they can contribute to its success. Collective mind theory argues that individuals develop shared understandings of the group’s tasks and one another, and that thus facilitates group performance [28]. When people coordinate, the team’s goal will be well articulated and each individual’s roles and responsibilities are clarified, resulting in high performance.

Leadership commitment and empowerment of team leaders will help members learn and elicit behaviors consistent with an experimenting and changing culture with a positive impact on project performance [13]. The culture of learning and experimenting supported by team leaders will positively influence team members, resulting in enhanced project performance.

Transfer of knowledge is facilitated by helping team members learn from one another, including knowledge of past failures. Interpersonal coordination provides a means for team members to interact and learn. Faraj and Sproull [9] investigated the coordination of knowledge in an ISD context and argued that expertise coordination generated positive effects on project performance that transcended general routine coordination benefits.

## 2.1. Hypotheses

Horizontal coordination mechanisms involve tools to increase communication and decision making across organizational boundaries. Horizontal coordination instills a sense of autonomy in IS team members; it allows them to communicate directly with team members during IS development and resolve potential differences. Organization theory posits that horizontal structure in organizations leads to shared task identity and empowerment.

A large number of horizontally coordinated team members in three industries (steel, apparel, and medical) were studied by Appelbaum and Berg [2]; the workers were dealing with quality control of their output, performing routine maintenance on their machinery, assigning daily tasks to team members, and dealing with scheduled absence of members. Appelbaum [3] argued that workers employed at firms utilizing horizontal coordination and self-directed teams should exhibited more internal motivation to learn firm specific skills, had higher job involvement, and displayed higher levels of organizational commit ment. Supporting this, Lincoln and Kalleberg [26] found strong hierarchies to be negatively related to employee commitment.

Thus our first hypothesis was:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ . Horizontal coordination will positively enhance the levels of leadership commitment and empowerment in the project team.

Organization theory postulates that horizontal information linkages result in collaborative integration. It leads to the project team setting higher goals across activities [7]. Organizations whose structures parallel a functional and multidivisional organization architecture have been found to be less appropriate for knowledge creation [16]. On the other hand, organizations whose structures contain internal networks have an environment that increases knowledge creation. If group members work jointly on tasks, they share knowledge and modify their activities without a need for central control [20].

Firms are then more likely to learn and diffuse knowledge. Wijk et al. [43] found that employee rotation across functions and organizational units helped them gain experience and also increased knowledge transfer.

Based on the theoretical and empirical evidence, we therefore hypothesized:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ . Horizontal coordination will positively enhance the levels of the knowledge transfer in the project team.

Horizontal coordination reduces the time for feedback among team members; it can also reduce the degree of role asymmetry, by disclosing task roles to the relevant actors. In complex IS development, there is an equivocality of information, goal, and roles. To deal with this, mutual adjustment, or horizontal coordination, is a significant factor in organizational design. Organization theory posits that horizontal information linkage overcomes barriers and provides opportunities for coordination among employees to achieve unity of effort and organizational objectives.

A large proportion of the horizontally coordinated team members in industries studied by Appelbaum and Berg participated in setting performance goals for the team, and solved work-related problems. Workers in the teams developed problem solving capabilities by increasing communication [34].

Vertical coordination through managerial control can help establish goals and clarify the roles of the individual team members; this can be controlled either by monitoring behaviors or outcomes [35].

Based on this we hypothesized:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 \mathbf { a } } .$ Horizontal coordination will positively enhance the levels of the clear mission and objectives in the project team.

$\mathbf { H _ { 3 b } } .$ . Vertical coordination will positively enhance the levels of the clear mission and objectives in the project team.

Leaders need to be committed to the accomplishment of organizational goals and learning. Specifically, leaders need to help identify performance gaps and help set goals that encourage the search for knowledge to narrow the gaps. Leaders should cultivate a learning climate through their behavior; e.g., seeking feedback, being open to criticisms, admitting mistakes, and empowering employees to make decisions and take risks [11,29]. When leaders create a democratic climate, there is a free exchange of ideas, cross-functional knowledge fertilization, and tacit knowledge is converted into explicit knowledge shared by team members. Empowered IS professionals acquire new knowledge and skills that will help them progress in their career [14]. Staff members are then more likely to direct greater effort toward accomplishing their work, and sustain a greater effort over time.

Based on this, we hypothesized:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Leadership commitment and empowerment will positively improve IS project performance.

Effective project team members are those who are knowledgeable about the technology and the business and can integrate their knowledge with that of others. Knowledge sharing is fundamental to the development of any complex product. Team performance varies as a function of the strength of connection between the team and the broader organization, and also as a function of the kind of knowledge being transferred. The importance of effective knowledge transfer is underscored by empirical evidence that indicates higher rates of productivity and survival for organizations that transfer knowledge effectively.

Based on this, we hypothesized:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ Knowledge transfer will positively improve IS project performance.

The organization as a whole and each unit need to have a clearly articulated purpose. Employees must understand this and how they contribute to the mission of the organization. Senge [37] and others have stated that ‘‘building a shared vision’’ creates tension that leads to learning. Employees can then strive to overcome any gap. In the IS context, project managers need to define a clear mission statement about teamwork in the organization. Jassawalla and Sashittal [21] stated that senior management had to define a clear overall strategy for new product development projects. Hutt et al. [19] concurred that a fundamental task for leaders was to create a strategy map with which organizational members could identify. We posit that clarity of mission and objectives will reduce the degree of role uncertainty and speed the development effort. Thus:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 6 } .$ Clear mission and objectives will positively improve IS project performance.

## 3. Research methodology

A survey was used to collect data and, from, it to test the hypotheses.

## 3.1. Measures

All the items were measured on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from complete (5) to not at all (1). The constructs were:

a. Vertical coordination. The extent to which coordination between users and IS staff was undertaken using vertical means, such as authorized entities. The scale for this construct was that used in [32]; it included specific elements responsible for vertical coordination, such as a project manager, the steering committee, and management.

b. Horizontal coordination. The extent to which coordination between users and IS staff was undertaken through mutual adjustments and communication using personal means. The scale for this construct was adopted from Nidumolu. It included specific horizontal coordination elements, such as oral and written communication across users and inhouse IT groups.

c. Clear mission and objectives. The degree to which employees had a clear vision of the organization and understand how they could contribute to its success and achievement. The scale adopted was that of Goh and Richards applied to IS teams. This included specific elements stating the IS team members’ perceptions of the clarity of the goals and objectives of the project.

## 3.2. Data collection

The sample we used was drawn from IS project managers listed in the Project Management Institute (PMI) in the USA. The sample was chosen because members of PMI are a good sample of IS staff. A questionnaire package, including a cover letter and the questionnaire, was sent to 1000 randomly selected members. Procedures, suggested by Dillman [8], to increase response rate were used, These included careful design and pilot testing of the instrument; careful wording of the cover letter; addressing respondents by name; immediate follow-up on undelivered questionnaires (by calling companies for correct addresses or names); and a reminder letter along with a follow-up questionnaire sent 3 weeks after the initial mailing. A response rate of 16.9% resulted (i.e., 169 respondents). This response rate is comparable to surveys on similar topics.

To understand whether respondents actually reflected the population parameters (to exclude nonrespondent bias), we compared the demographic data with that in prior studies [22,25]. This showed that respondent characteristics were similar. Harman’s single factor test was used to test the common method variance. Our results indicated that more than one factor was extracted, with a total variance extracted of 68%, and the first factor accounting for 35.7% of variance only. Thus since no one factor represented all indicators, common method variance was not evident.

## 4.2. PLS analysis

Hypotheses were tested and verified by using partial least squares (PLS) analysis [27]. This is a latent structural equation modeling technique that uses a component-based approach to estimation; it contains two steps. The first examines the measurement model and the second assesses the structural model. When using PLS, researchers must pay attention to three concerns: (1) the reliability and validity of measures; (2) the appropriate nature of the relationship between measures and constructs; and (3) path coefficient, model adequacy, and the final model from the available set of alternatives [18]. PLS-Graph Version 3.01 was used to test the hypotheses.

## 4.2.1. Measurement model

Item reliability, convergent validity, and discrimi nant validity tests are often used to test the measurement model in PLS. Individual item reliability can be examined by observing the factor loading of each item. High loading implies that the shared variance between the construct and its measurement is higher than the error variance. Factor loading higher than 0.7 can be viewed as highly reliable and factor loadings less than 0.5 should be dropped.

Convergent validity is assured when multiple indicators are used to measure one construct. It can be examined by insistence on high reliability of the questions, composite reliability of the constructs, and consideration of the variance extracted by constructs (AVE) [10]. Construct reliability is demonstrated by Cronbach’s alpha. Composite reliability of constructs is calculated by squaring the sum of loadings then dividing it by the sum of squared loadings plus the sum of the error terms [42]. AVE, proposed by Fornell and Larcker, considers the variance captured by the indicators. If the AVE is less than 0.5, the variance captured by the construct is less than the measurement error and the validity of the single indicator and construct is questionable.

For the convergent validity, the variance extracted for each construct must be larger than 0.5, and the itemconstruct correlations must all be more than 0.7. All these showed that the measurement had high convergent validity. Composite reliability of each construct was also above 0.7 which was acceptable. Except for the clear mission, the Cronbach alpha of each construct was also above 0.7 which indicated high internal consistency.

Discriminant validity determines whether the measures of constructs are different from each other [30]. It can be assessed by testing whether the square root of AVE is larger than correlation coefficients. Another way to determine it is to verify the factor loading of indicators [6]. To have discriminant validity, indicators should have higher loading in interesting than in other constructs. Because PLS graphs only provide factor loading on one construct, procedures suggested by Smith et al. [38] were used to generate cross-loading values, as shown in Table 3. Loading values for each indicator (shown in bold font) exceeded 0.7, which indicated high and significant discriminant validity. ## 4.2.2. Structural model

Basic information about each variable is given in Table 4, including means, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. For each variable the skewness was less than 2 and the kurtosis less than 5, indicating no significant violation of normal distribution [12]. Five missing values were found and replaced by the mean [15]. Table 5 shows the path analysis. The powers of testing the proposed links were all above the recommended value of 0.80. Mediating effect can be shown in two different parts. The first is from independent variable to mediators and the second part is from mediators to the dependent variable. The independent variables to mediators’ relationships are shown in the first two rows of Table 5. These indicated that horizontal coordination had a positive and significant effect on clear mission, leadership, and knowledge transfer. Vertical coordination had positive and significant effect on clear mission. Mediators to dependent variable relationships were shown in the last column of Table 5. These indicated that all mediators had a positive and significant effect on project performance. Therefore, all the hypotheses were supported.

In order to further explore the data set, we examined the direct effect of horizontal coordination (coefficient = 0.11 and p-value > 0.05) and vertical coordination (coefficient = 0.09 and p-value > 0.05) on project performance; no significant effect was found. Furthermore, the links between vertical coordination and leadership as well as between vertical coordination and knowledge transfer were explored. The results indicated that there was a significant relationship between vertical coordination and knowledge transfer (coefficient = .15 and p-value < 0.05) but no significant relationship between vertical coordination and leadership (coefficient = 0.06 and p-value > 0.05).

## 5. Conclusions

The purpose of our study was to examine and document the effects of the horizontal coordination mechanism in ISD projects, and to determine whether vertical coordination had similar effects. As predicted, horizontal coordination was observed to produce clarity in mission and objectives. This is consistent with previous research. Vertical coordination was also found to have a significant relationship since managerial control can establish goals and clarify roles. Both the mechanisms explained 19.9% of the variance in mission and objectives.

As posited, horizontal coordination produced leadership commitment and empowerment. On the other hand, vertical coordination did not have a significant effect on it. Both coordination mechanisms explained 25.3% of the variance in leadership commitment and empowerment.

One may argue that a high degree of knowledge sharing is difficult in organizations with a rigid hierarchy. Vertical coordination might then restrict the interaction between the team members. Surprisingly, however, the exploratory results indicated that vertical coordination does lead to knowledge transfer in teams. The items for knowledge transfer that we measured leveraged existing knowledge. This may explain support for relationship between vertical and horizontal coordination with knowledge transfer. Both the coordination mechanisms explained 19.7% of the variance in knowledge transfer.

As in any study, one limitation is that it only examined a limited set of mediators. Nevertheless, the exploratory results indicated that direct links between horizontal and vertical coordination were fully mediated by the selected factors—indicating they had accounted for most of the effects.

What differentiated our study from previous efforts was that we argued that horizontal coordination leads to certain mediating outcomes (commitment and empowerment, clear mission, and knowledge transfer), which, in turn, lead to better project performance. Furthermore, horizontal coordination could enhance the empowerment of team members to collect information and produce better work.

Vertical coordination through managerial control can contribute by establishing goals and clarifying the roles of the individual team members. But adopting a particular coordination mechanism in lieu of another can have harmful results. Top management teams should view their role as one of creating a framework, establishing performance standards, and promoting horizontal coordination at those IS professionals who are responsible for individual modules of the project. Project managers must be able to populate the project team with IS professionals who possess the requisite skills, attributes, and behaviors that facilitate effective horizontal coordination.

One might suggest that a limitation of our study was in our adopting items from the IS literature to measure vertical and horizontal coordination. However this technique has been varied used in several contexts and level of analysis and has been applied to a range of disciplines including physiology, biology, organizational behavior, and computer science.
