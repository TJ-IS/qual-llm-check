# A socio-technical approach to business process simulation

Andreas Gregoriades <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Alistair Sutcliffe <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, University of Surrey, UK

<sup>b</sup> Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, UK

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 24 May 2007 Received in revised form 29 February 2008 Accepted 6 April 2008 Available online 12 April 2008

Keywords: Business process simulation Human performance Bayesian Belief Networks

## a b s t r a c t

This paper describes a socio-technical approach to business process redesign through the investigation of complex interactions and dependencies among humans and IT systems of organisations. The focus is on the need to assess business process performance early in the redesign process, to prevent organisational failures. The method is based on human performance quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation and is supported by a tool that enables scenario-based evaluation of prospective organisational processes through simulation. The approach combines probabilistic and subjective measures of tasks and communication acts in business processes to quantify business performance in terms of cycle time. The approach models business processes as a set of scenarios of sequential activities where the dependencies between actors, IT systems and tasks are explicitly de<sup>fi</sup>ned. Business process performance assessment is achieved through a systematic walkthrough of the process model using these scenarios. Human performance constitutes an important parameter to business process performance and is modelled based on Human Performance Shaping Factors (PSF) and assessed using Bayesian Belief Networks (BBN). Process cycle time is calculated using aggregates of task and communication completion times, and calibrated using performance estimates of each of the agents in the scenario. The method enables trade-off analysis among candidate process models and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of performance bottlenecks early in the design phase. A radiology process improvement case study is presented that demonstrates the use of the method and the tool.

## 1. Introduction

To maintain the competitive edge in ever-changing business environments, organisations must keep on modifying or recon<sup>fi</sup>guring their business processes and the enterprise application systems which complement them, in time to meet changing business requirements [24]. Business processes, are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a structured, measured set of activities designed to produce a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed output for a particular customer or market [9], which constitute the critical success factors for organisational competitive advantage. This realisation drives improvement such as benchmarking, business process reengineering (BPR) and continuous process improvement [23]. BPR, relates to the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of an entire business system in order to achieve signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements in the performance [10]. Change, however, is risky due to the impact that it might have on processes and components of an organisation. Therefore, reengineering efforts usually fail due to the degree of change and uncertainty that they introduced. According to Hammer, the rate of failure in reengineering projects was over 50% [21,22]. This has been attributed to a list of causes among which the inability to accurately predict the outcome of a radical change and the insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient modelling of the dynamic aspects of processes. Paolucci et al. [41] warn that one of the most important issues that contribute to the high failure rate in business change projects is the lack of tools for evaluating the effects of design alternatives before their implementation. Thus, mistakes can only be recognised once the redesigned processes have been implemented, when it is too late to correct a wrong decision. Business process simulation [33] can assist decision maker's understanding of the differences in performance between candidate business process designs and provide insights into the implications of alternative trade-offs prior to implementation.

However, despite the consensus that simulating organisations can optimise business performance, the focus has been on the transactional aspects of the organisation, which describe the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information and entities among processing stations, while the softer aspects remain unconsidered. Organisations, however, are composed of people who interact with IT systems to achieve goals [29]. Hence, the behaviour of both components needs to be considered when simulating processes, for a holistic representation of organisational dynamics and subsequently the assessment of their performance. Traditional process redesign efforts ignore the psychological and social factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence process performance, e.g. IDEF [34] and the ARIS toolset [46]. Role Interaction Nets [48] and Role Activity Diagrams (RADs) [27] simulate organisational processes analogous to Petri nets in an attempt to detect con<sup>fl</sup>icts, but again the social context is missing. On the other hand, methods that stemmed from the socio-technical school of thought such as ETHICS [37], Soft Systems Methodology [7], and Multiview [3] incorporate the people dimension, but lack quantitative and analytic capabilities necessary for assessing business process performance.

Another important characteristic that has been overlooked in business process simulation is the analysis of interaction among IT systems and human actors. According to Wickens [55], human performance is directly related to use of IT systems, hence, organisations need to promote designs that demonstrate optimum performance of both the human and technical components. Our approach extends traditional business process modelling with constructs from both the functional and socio-technical perspectives similar to Biazzo's [59]. Similarly, Speech Act Theory [2,47,20] has been used to model businesses in terms of patterns composed of interrelated communication acts. Flores and Ludlow [15] also viewed businesses as networks of interrelated commitments. Winograd [57] and the approaches: Action Work<sup>fl</sup>ow (AW) [36], Dynamic Essential Modelling (DEMO) [11,43], and Business Action Theory (BAT) [17,32] are also based on human communication. In DEMO transactions are modelled in three phases during which actors agree on the execution of an action, execute it and negotiate the results. AW models the conversation <sup>fl</sup>ow among participants in terms of an action-work<sup>fl</sup>ow-loop, with request, agreement, performance and evaluation phases. BAT is a six-phase process that describes generic business interaction in terms of customer–supplier communications. These methods, however, provide only frameworks with limited automated assistance for the business analyst.

The method and tool described in this paper provides business analysts with performance indicators and trade-off analyses of proposed changes in organisational processes. Business processes are modelled using the constructs of: human agent, tasks and technology. Scenarios, which are pathways through the business process model, describe its dynamics and enable the evaluation of its performance. The approach employs event-based simulation in accordance with probabilistic assessments of human performance for the estimation of process cycle time expressed in temporal requirements of tasks and communication acts in a business process. Trade-off analysis between the “as-is” and “to-be” models enables the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of weak points and provides performance comparisons of alternative business processes.

The paper is organised as follows: initially the approach for modelling communication acts, test scenarios and business process models is explained, followed by description of the tool that enables quantitative assessment of business process performance. The model for assessing human performance using BBN technology is portrayed along with a technique to parameterise prior knowledge (conditional probability tables). Subsequently, an application of the method and tool for the redesign of a radiology process in the healthcare domain is presented. The paper concludes with the results from the case study and a discussion.

## 2. Modelling communication

According to Stalk and Hout [49], process cycle time constitutes a key success factor for attaining competitive advantage and its measurement is critical prior to deciding the most appropriate process change. However, predicting cycle time is a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult problem that can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by many parameters. Traditionally, business processes have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ordered execution of tasks to produce goods or provide services that have added value in the organisations environment or to the organisation itself. However, people in business processes interact with technology to complete tasks; therefore, interactions among people and between people and computers should also be modelled. Modelling communications and tasks early in the process redesign phase enables the holistic analysis of prospective business processes prior to their enactment and minimises the risk associated with the change.

Design of the human performance in business processes involves communication in two ways. First is design of communication channels and procedures between people; and second communication between people and computers. While the second concern has been traditionally treated as straightforward input commands and output messages in simple models of interaction [39,51], with the advances in intelligent agents and their increased prevalence, the design of human–computer communication require richer models of discourse. The grounding theory of communication modelling in this paper is Clark's [8] theory of language. Clark argues that people communicate to establish and achieve shared goals. Meaning develops through a cycle of exchanges between the conversees that establish a common ground of shared understanding. Clark proposes a ladder of action that moves from initial recognition to comprehension of intent, elaboration of implications, planning actions and <sup>fi</sup>nally executing plans. However, meaning is construed not only through language but also via reference to mutually accessible artefacts (e.g. pointing to diagrams or objects in a room) and via shared knowledge of the communication context and the roles of conversees.

## 3. Business process and scenario modelling

We developed modelling constructs of discourse acts to explicitly describe communications among participating actors and between actors and information systems (Table 1a) [50]. The complete modelling framework of our method includes the following components: the human agents involved in the process, the IT systems used, the tasks undertaken, the dependencies between tasks-human roles-technologies and the discourse acts required to complete these tasks. Tasks are characterised by best and worst time durations, complexity weightings (Table 1b) and communication acts required to undertake them. IT system properties include: usability, reliability, functionality, performance and information support. Communicative acts are categorised into in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows with weightings that represent the dependency between the originator of the message and the receiver in terms of implication for action and decision making by the receiver, following Clark's [8] theory and discourse action theories [57]. Generic Task weightings (Table 1b) and durations are based on quantitative scales produced by researchers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of human performance [1,35,54]. Therefore, simple tasks receive low weights whereas complex, knowledge-based tasks which require reasoning receive higher weights, hence increased duration. Generic Task durations are used and the blueprint is re<sup>fi</sup>ned for speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks using domain knowledge elicited from subject matter experts. Task and communication durations constitute the idealised times for the completion of an activity and discourse act in the process. These times are subsequently altered based on the performance level of the human agent undertaking the task. This property changes with regard to in<sup>fl</sup>uences from the environment, the technology, the organisations etc., as illustrated in subsequent section.

Using these modelling constructs analysts can develop the Dependencies Process Model (DPM) that explicitly shows the associations between humans, IT systems, their tasks and the discourse activities between them. Upon development of the DPM, business analysts aim to specify the most plausible scenarios that can emerge in the business process under consideration. Scenarios by de<sup>fi</sup>nition constitute narrative descriptions of events that can occur in an organisation, which are used to envisage the future of a prospective system. They can describe in detail activities undertaken by users or system components and provide the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for analysing or enacting a business process. Business processes are described by one or more scenarios occurring sequentially or concurrently. These explicitly portray all the activities in a business process. Since scenarios constitute the cornerstone of our business process performance assessment method, it was important to develop a technique that would enable their modelling in a format that could be automatically executed in a software simulation. ## 4. Human performance modelling with BBN

Our approach employs Bayesian Belief Networks (BBN) technology to assess human performance that directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences task and communication completion times, which subsequently change process cycle time. Due to the inherent uncertainty that characterises human behaviour, it is necessary to use a probabilistic technique. BBNs are based on the concept of Bayesian probability, and provide a language and calculus for reasoning under uncertainty. BBNs are useful for inferring the probabilities of future events, on the basis of observations or other evidence that may have a causal relationship to the event in question [28]. The two main components of BBN are the causal network model (topology) and the conditional probability tables (CPT). The model characterises causal relationships, expressed as directed acyclic graphs. Variables can have any number of states, so the choice of measurement scale is left to the analyst's discretion. CPTs describe prior knowledge about the various causal dependencies in terms of conditional distributions. BBN can be used in two reasoning modes, bottom-up/diagnostic and top-down/predictive. The former infers the most likely cause given evidence of an effect, while the latter deduces the probability that a certain cause would have, given a speci<sup>fi</sup>c effect.

BBN strength resides in their ability to provide an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient factorisation of the joint probability distribution (JPD) over a set of variables with de<sup>fi</sup>ned states that describe the modelled problem. If the JPD is known, the posterior probabilities given an observation can be calculated. However, the calculation of the JPD becomes intractable with the increase of the variables included in the model. The key to ef<sup>fi</sup>cient representation of JPD is to reduce the number of probabilities that are involved.

This is achieved with the introduction of conditional independence. This states that a variable is independent of all its non-descendants given its parents. This factors the JPD into several component distributions that are easier to compute because they depend on a smaller set of variables. This property is realised with the use of directed acyclic graphs and their corresponding CPTs.

## 5. CPT parameterisation

Since the BBN precision is directly related to the prior knowledge embedded as probability distributions describing the likelihood of each event occurring, it was imperative to provide a technique to accurately model the prior knowledge and its representation in the CPTs. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of CPT generally requires an exhaustive pair-wise combination of all parent node states (Fig. 3). Hence, for each variable A we have to specify P(A|c) for each con<sup>fi</sup>guration c of its parents. However, this imposes a considerable con<sup>fi</sup>guration load when the number of parents is large. In complex nets, the process becomes intractable. The potentially huge effort in constructing an accurate CPT is sometimes referred to as the knowledge acquisition bottleneck. When data is not available based on which inference can be made, usually the CPT parameterisation is left to human experts to specify. However, when the number of nodes and their states increases this process again becomes intractable. An approach that makes this tractable is the application of the noisy-OR method proposed by Pearl [42]. Noisy-OR distributions approximate CPTs using fewer parameters and the resulting distributions are as reliable [16,58]. Currently, there is no evidence that shows that specifying the CPT using exhaustive pair-wise combinations of input variables results in a better performing network [13]. The noisy-Or approach is based on the principle that specifying conditional probabilities for each parent in turn is easier than for all simultaneously, i.e. to come up with P(child|parent1) and P(child|parent2) is easier than P(child|parent1,parent2). The problem though is that we can not compute P(child|parent1,parent2) from P(child|parent1) and P(child|parent2) suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently in a non-noisy-OR model. The benefit of the noisy-OR approach resides in the fact that these probabilities can be estimated suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently accurately without having to revert to human experts to explicitly de<sup>fi</sup>ne all of them. Instead, the only requirement by the domain expert is to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the impact probabilities, which specify the impact that a cause has on an effect and ranges from 0 to 1.

Fig. 3. Parent child relationships in a BBN model with their CPTs in “true” and “false” states.

Parents and children nodes in noisy-OR gates are represented as Boolean variables and each parent is independent of any other parent [31,40]. Hence, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition noisy-OR gates describe cases where a child node is false only if its true parents are inhibited. Therefore, to represent a noisy-OR CPT we only need the inhibition probability for each parent node. The term noisy indicates that the causal interaction is non-deterministic. So, i $\mathbf { \dot { x } } _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \dots x _ { n }$ are causes of an effect y then each of the causes has a probability p suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to produce the effect in the absence of all other causes. These constitute the impact probabilities that allow the parameterisation of the entire CPT using only n parameters $p _ { 1 } . . . p _ { n }$ representing the probability of the effect occurring given x is present and all others absent. In other words,

$$
p _ {i} = P (y | \neg x _ {1}, \neg x _ {2}, \neg x _ {3}... x _ {i}... \neg x _ {n})\tag{1}
$$

and the probability of y given a subset $x _ { j }$ of $x _ { i } ^ { \prime } s$ is calculated using:

$$
P (y | x _ {j}) = 1 - \prod_ {i: X _ {i} \ni X _ {j}} (1 - p _ {i}).\tag{2}
$$

Hence, considering the example topology of Fig. 4, the estimated impact probabilities of the three parents are: P (Good Human Performance=True/Good Agent Capability= - True)=0.7, P(Good Human Performance=True/Good Task Support=True)=0.5 and P(Good Human Performance=True/Low Task Complexity=True)=0.6. The inhibition probabilities are 0.3, 0.5 and 0.4 respectively. Based on these and using Eq. (2) we can calculate the probability distributions for the required node. Therefore, the probability that human performance is high given that task support, agent capability and task complexity are high, is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (\text { good\_human\_performance } = \text { True } | \text { good\_task\_support } \\ \quad = \text { True }, \text { good\_agent\_capability } = \text { True }, \text { low\_task\_complexity } \\ \quad = \text { true }) = 1 - [ (1 - 0. 5) (1 - 0. 7) (1 - 0. 6) ] = 0. 9 4. \end{array}
$$

The impact probabilities of the in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors to our human performance BBN model are based on the THERP database of human error probabilities included in chapter 20 of the techniques handbook [53] and the weighting factor of the Error Producing Conditions (EPC) of the HEART method [56] plus human factors literature [45].

## 6. BBN model topology

Since our approach employs performance-based evaluation of business processes using BBNs the <sup>fi</sup>rst problem we faced was to develop the network topology (Fig. 4). For this we initially investigated the causes associated with human performance through an initial literature review [53,4,25,26]. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed causes constitute the Performance Shaping Factors affecting the operator actions. The model is composed of <sup>fi</sup>ve categories of in<sup>fl</sup>uences which describe the technology, the environment, the organisation, the task and the agent. Technological in<sup>fl</sup>uences include usability, reliability, functionality, maintenance and machine performance. To illustrate some of the causal relationships, poor technological features have an adverse in<sup>fl</sup>uence on human performance due to increased demand for cognitive resources required to recognise, interpret and operate the technology [18]. According to Bailey [4] the environmental context, i.e. noise, lighting, comfort, etc., has an indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence on an individual's stress through increasing fatigue and subsequently performance. Wickens [55] also warns of the negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the environmental context on an actor's performance. Organisational factors such as management practices and culture have a positive effect on individuals' enthusiasm and motivation, which consequently improve an agent's capability and inherently his/her performance. Finally, the agent's properties, such as inherent ability, training and experience, affect the agent's capability by acting as antidotes to stress. Hence, adequate training and experience could act as resistors to increased stress and normalisers to human performance.

## 7. Preliminary validation of BBN

Validation constitutes an important activity in BBN model development, which aims in evaluating the diagnostic and predictive performance of the model. However, in our case the validation process is not ideally solvable since domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c data sets, against which our model performance can be compared, are never available or likely to be. Therefore, the answer is to use generic <sup>fi</sup>gures of human performance estimates as these are described in THERP and compare these against the results from the BBN. An important point to make is that that the model and the tool that utilises it are not producing values that are 100% accurate; instead the method enables the process designer to perform sensitivity analysis to identify differences between candidate designs. This preliminary validation study concentrated comparing results produced from running the BBN model with all possible permutations of input scenario parameters against known estimates from generic performance indicators. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, after running the BBN model with all possible pair-wise combinations of input scenario conditions, we conducted a relevance analysis between input combinations and output data to identify the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential parameters in the model and subsequently validate this against rankings of impact factors from THERP database. The process was repeated until all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant deviations from the desired state have been resolved. At each reiteration of the process the model's CPTs were recon<sup>fi</sup>gured.

## 8. Assessing human performance and task/communication completion time

The BBN model of Fig. 5 estimates the human agent's performance based on prior knowledge embedded in the model in the form of CPTs and evidence entered during scenario analysis. Hence, at each task step of the scenario analysis, information concerning the agent, the task, the environment and the technology is used as input evidence to the BBN. Input nodes for the human operational environment include the short-term effects of duty-time, which can be estimated from narrative scenarios, to the longer-term in<sup>fl</sup>uences of management culture, incentives, training and experience which are judged from the operational context. The <sup>fi</sup>nal input nodes describe aspects of the system's environment, the task and the technology used. All the inputs are held on databases that link the variables to human, machine, operational and system environment components of the model. Since the underlying BBN model uses Boolean input nodes, the corresponding numerical value of each input node in the database is converted into discrete input evidence that belongs to one of the two states of each input node. Therefore, values that fall below 5 in a scale of 1 to 10 are modelled as negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences (input evidence set to low-state) on the operator's performance. Quantitative measures of properties that describe each input node of the model are normalised in a 0–10 scale, where 0 denotes low and 10 high satisfaction of the property.

The Human Performance BBN produces probabilities based on input evidence entered on the leaf nodes. Input propagates down the model to give rise to an estimate of human performance in a form of probability distribution among possible states of the node (High, Low). These estimates are used, in conjunction with task/communication duration estimates, to give an indication of the level of increase to the idealised task/ communications completion times. Hence, the assigned best and worst-case completion times, obtained from domain experts or historical data, re<sup>fl</sup>ect the shortest/longest possible duration of a task. The estimated task/communication completion time is calculated using the following formula:

$$
\mathrm{AT} = \left(P ^ {\text { low }} * \mathrm{WT}\right) + \left(P ^ {\text { high }}\right) * \mathrm{BT}\tag{3}
$$

where,

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {AT} & \text {Adjusted time} \\ p ^ {\text {high}} & \text {Probability of human performance being high} \\ \text {BT} & \text {Best task/communication completion time} \\ p ^ {\text {low}} & \text {Probability of human performance being low} \\ \text {WT} & \text {Worst task/communication completion time} \end{array}
$$

Hence, if the probability of high human performance is equa to 1 then the probability of low human performance will be 0 (best-case); this will result in a best-case completion time. On the other hand, if the probability of a low human performance time is 0.62 and the best and worst times are 4 and 7 s respectively, then the adjusted time is $( 0 . 6 2 * 7 ) + ( 0 . 3 8 * 4 ) =$ 5.86 s.

An alternative approach to task time assessment is the incorporation of the calculations in the BBN itself using multistate variables. However, this would limit the portability of the method and tool, since different domains have different tasks and hence durations. Moreover, putting time estimates in the BBN would limit the tool's accuracy due to the discretisation of the input and output. An alternative would be the application of a dynamic discretisation algorithms such as the one described in [38].

## 9. The business process analyser (BPA) tool

The BPA software tool, programmed in JAVA, helps to diagnose problems in business processes and assess cycle times for comparative evaluation of prospective redesign efforts. The tool's architecture is illustrated in Fig. 6.

The business process model editor enables the user to specify the actors, tasks, IT systems involved in the process along with their dependencies and interactions, for the development of the DPM. This model is used next during the scenario speci<sup>fi</sup>cation phase where detailed descriptions of the activities are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as sequences of event tuples (actor, task, technology, communications). The tool performs analysis of business processes using properties of each scenario event tuple that describe the actors, tasks, communications and technology used at each scenario step. This analysis is performed by the Dynamic Analyser which partitions scenarios into user de<sup>fi</sup>ned intervals, to enable analysis at different level of granularity. Fig. 7 depicts analytically the intervals and time required by the Radiologist agent in a typical radiology process. For each scenario, the required information is retrieved from a database and supplied to the BBN assessor with input that describes properties of each tuple component. Information concerning the environment and the organisational culture is also used as input to the BBN assessor. Input values from the database initially undergo a discretisation process to convert them into discrete input for the BBN. This evidence is propagated down the BBN model for each scenario step, to give estimates of human performance. This is used to adjust task and communication times. Aggregated task and communication times for each scenario step are used to calculate overall scenario completion time. Since each business process can be described by more than one scenario, the process cycle time is the aggregate of all scenario times. However, to avoid counting twice task durations for activities in scenarios that occur concurrently, overlap times between two or more scenarios are subtracted from the overall process cycle time.

![](/api/attachments/JUEQA4SP/fulltext/images/1052c086f312718613d4517222d8e207c5a1a939665b7abdb126cfff44c66dcb.jpg)  
Fig. 6. The business process analyser architecture.

Formally the aggregated communication time for each agent in a scenario is expressed as a cumulative sum of HCI and HH communication times.

$$
C t = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} C _ {H C I} t _ {i} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} C _ {H H} t _ {i}.\tag{4}
$$

Similarly, agent's overall task completion time is expressed as an accumulation of adjusted task times for each agent in each scenario. Hence, accumulated task times $T _ { t }$ is calculated as an aggregate of all adjusted task durations Td :

$$
T _ {t} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} T d _ {i}.\tag{5}
$$

Therefore, overall scenario time ST is expressed as the sum of communication and task times for each agent after these have been adjusted based on human performance estimates. Overall business process cycle time is an accumulation of the completion times of all scenarios involved in the process, minus the overlap times between concurrent scenario tasks.

To enable the analyst to test processes against required speci<sup>fi</sup>cations the tool allows the user to set threshold values for desired completion times for scenario intervals. These de<sup>fi</sup>ne the maximum required time to complete a set of tasks. Scenarios can also be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed with different starting times to enable the modelling of task sequences commenced at different times. The horizontal sequences of shaded boxes in Fig. 7 correspond to scenario task step timelines initiated at different time intervals. The size of each box indicates the duration of each task step in the scenario. The example illustrates the typical activities in a radiology process. The analysis is conducted using three concurrent scenarios, initiated at different times as depicted in Fig. 7. The bar chart on top of the timeline depicts the time spent by the Radiologist at each scenario interval while performing tasks and communicating with other agents and IT systems. The shaded top part of each bar corresponds to the extra time caused due to reduced human performance and is the aggregate of all extra times of each task undertaken by the Radiologist in each interval. The interval time is user defined and enables the analyst to refine his/hers analysis to the level he/she considers appropriate.

To assist business process analysts in evaluating the goodness of prospective process models and ease the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the critical components (agents, technology, communications) in the model that cause reduced human performance or increased cycle time, the tool provides a mechanism to group environmental and organisational in<sup>fl</sup>uences together and accordingly set them to either best or worst case before simulating the process. This enables the analyst to stress test prospective business process models using best/worst-case environmental/organisational input.

## 10. Case study

This case study illustrates use of the method and tool for redesigning business processes in the healthcare domain by evaluating the performance of prospective process designs. With advances in IT modern healthcare establishments are seeking to introduce technologies in an attempt to improve their performance. This case study demonstrates the application of the method and tool in supporting the decision making process of business analysts while studying the performance of a typical radiology process. The method is composed of the following steps: (1) model the “as-is” DPM and the corresponding scenarios that describe the process dynamics (2) simulate the process with the BPA tool to identify de<sup>fi</sup>cient agents, ITcomponents and tasks (3) identify possible solutions to the problem and develop the corresponding “to-be” DPMs and scenarios (4) simulate and evaluate the performance of each prospective solution using best and worst-case conditions (5) compare results of each alternative design against existing process performance to justify the change effort.

## 10.1. The scenario of use

The following scenario explains the steps and the actors participating in a typical ankle X-ray process.

10.1.1. Scenario: ankle X-ray using film-based and manual transcription

In a typical ankle X-ray scenario, the patient initially registers with the receptionist (REC) on the RIS and relevant patient information is retrieved from Hospital Information System (HIS). The patient is subsequently guided into the radiology room and placed on the examination table. Next, the radiographer (RHR) inspects the patient's ankle to determine the best position of the X-ray tube. She places the tube above the patient's ankle, sets the parameters on the control panel and makes the scan. The <sup>fi</sup>lm cassette is then taken to the darkroom for development. In the darkroom the radiographer operates the <sup>fi</sup>lm processor to develop the exposed <sup>fi</sup>lm. The processor contains chemicals that act on the exposed <sup>fi</sup>lm and develop the radiograph. The radiographer places the produced images on the view-box for quality check and forwards them on to the Radiologist (RST) for analysis. Subsequently, the radiographer prepares the billing data and submits it to the secretary (SEC) for processing. The Radiologist reads the images, makes his diagnosis and dictates his interpretation. Subsequently the Radiologist prepares a preliminary report and forwards it to the secretary for transcription. The <sup>fi</sup>nal report is returned to the Radiologist along with the billing data, who authorises it for submission.

For the radiology process, the “as-is” DPM is depicted in Fig. 2. The above scenario is decomposed into three scenario threads each describing the activities of agents in the process in a sequential manner. These scenarios are created using the scenario modeller. Each thread corresponds to a walkthrough on the DPM. For this case study there are three scenario threads that occur concurrently in the X-ray process (Fig. 7). The <sup>fi</sup>rst thread includes the following steps: registration of patients in the radiology unit, fetching of previous images, analysis of past images, patient examination with a modality, evaluation of generated images' quality, analysis of images and generation of preliminary report. The second thread is initiated after the X-ray images have been generated and includes the preparation of billing data. The third thread start after the RST completes the preliminary report and the RHR <sup>fi</sup>nishes the billing data and describes the transcription of the preliminary report to produce the <sup>fi</sup>nal report to be authorised by the RST.

![](/api/attachments/JUEQA4SP/fulltext/images/e2d1de0dce952d89518bf629eb85211365bf00e2f4fb303f1dba8495e998a289.jpg)  
Fig. 8. “To-be” design 3 Dependencies Process Model. Arrows point to the introduction of PACS and Speech Recognition (Dictation Sys) technologies.

Typical problems in the healthcare domain include how to reduce costs by saving labour and resources, i.e. development <sup>fi</sup>lm, plus ways to decrease time to perform an examination (cycle time), therefore, reduce patient waiting and increase process throughput. The digitisation of the radiology process is considered a revolutionary approach for improving performance due to the complexities of traditional processes while storing, retrieving and reporting diagnostic information in large hospitals. Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) promised reduced cycle time with the introduction of <sup>fi</sup>lm-less imaging that eliminates manual processing of images in the dark room with the introduction of electronic capture, <sup>fi</sup>ltering and communication of images among actors in the radiology process. However, despite their advantages, these technologies are not guaranteed to improve process performance without being evaluated through simulation. Identifying the best combination of technologies, tasks, and people remains a challenge in any process redesign effort. Our method and tool employ a socio-technical perspective to process modelling and simulation that widens the scope of analysis during redesign. The BPA tool assists business analysts evaluate the goodness of each alternative redesign solution in terms of performance. In this case study, three redesign alternatives are considered, each one using an envelope of technological, environmental, organisational and human properties. The aim is to <sup>fi</sup>nd the solution that maximises process throughput and minimises cycle time (Fig. 8).

## 11. Results

Initially the method begins with the simulation of the current business process model. Results from this step pinpoint areas of concern as these are depicted in Fig. 2. Highlighted agents denote those actors in the model that consume most of the process time while communicating and undertaking tasks. The threshold for each scenario analysis is pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the designer. The BPA tool identi<sup>fi</sup>es these actors by systematically assessing the performance and times spent by each, while undertaking their allocated tasks and interacting with colleagues or technologies. Critical performance indicators for each of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed critical agents can be interrogated further by selecting them with the mouse.

![](/api/attachments/JUEQA4SP/fulltext/images/3e87da1b71e4ffe226a10bceaaebd392ba95766ec1a2448f84c119f74ac4dfef.jpg)

Essentially the simulation revealed that the RST and RHR agents are critical. This is due to low performance indications at certain scenario intervals, plus increased task-load due to either concurrent execution of tasks, increased task complexity or communication load. Homing in the activities of these agents enables the analyst to identify the discourse acts and the generic tasks that they perform most frequently, and therefore consume most of the agent's time. The results of Fig. 9 indicate that the RST spends most of his time performing the “analysis”, “decision” and “action” tasks. These are required during the analysis of the X-ray images, the diagnosis and the transcription of the generation of the preliminary report. Additionally, the discourse actions consuming most of the RSTs time are “Liaise”, performed while interacting with the RHR, and “Command” performed while interacting with the technology. On the other hand, RHR spends most of the time operating the <sup>fi</sup>lm processor while “developing” and “pre-processing” X-ray images in the darkroom. Based on this analysis of the “as-is” process, three candidate “to-be” solutions are considered: (1) use <sup>fi</sup>lm-based diagnosis with speech recognition to reduce the temporal demand for the “action” tasks performed while transcribing the diagnosis report (2) use <sup>fi</sup>lm-less diagnosis with manual transcription to reduce the time while analysing, deciding and acting upon decisions, plus minimise interaction time among actors and technology (3) use <sup>fi</sup>lm-less diagnosis with speech recognition (Fig. 8). To test that any candidate solution will result in better performance than the “as-is” con<sup>fi</sup>guration, the method compares each candidate against the best achievable performance from the “as-is” model with the environmental, organisational and human settings set to best-case. This is essential since the cost associated with each candidate solution is higher than the cost associated with improving the environment, the management style or retraining the personnel of the “as-is” model. Therefore, if all of the proposed solutions fail to outperform the results from an improved “as-is” model then there is no cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t in investing in any of them.

Results from this analysis revealed that the overall cycle time of the “as-is” design was considerably higher that that of the three candidate solutions even with best-case settings, hence the need to proceed in evaluating the three candidate solutions.

After modelling and simulating each candidate solution, the tool revealed that the overall cycle time of design 2 (D2) produces the best results, as depicted in Fig. 9. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the time required to complete the activities of the RST and RHR actors has been reduced signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Moreover, the analyst can identify the scenario steps and the components in the process that contribute the most towards performance. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the tool provides the analyst with human performance estimates for all scenario steps as depicted in Fig.10. This enables the analyst to pinpoint the best technology at each process step. This analysis is performed with worst-case environmental, organisational and human properties to eliminate in<sup>fl</sup>uence from these components and focus on the human performance gain associated with technological characteristics. Results from this analysis, as depicted in Fig. 10, revealed that the positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence to human performance from the PACS technology outperforms that of DP, view-box and modality at task steps 5–8, 10–12 and 17. Moreover, at task step 13 Voice recorder outperforms the dictation system. This indicates that PACS and manual transcription are the best technologies for these task steps.

Concluding, all results indicate that the introduction of the PACS along with manual transcription is the best course of action since it will result in the best process cycle time and human performance. This is translated into more patient visits per shift and consequently increased income for the hospital. It is interesting to note that the combination of PACS and Speech

Recognition technology does not offer the best process performance. This apparent paradox is attributed to the low reliability of the transcriptions produced by this technology. Therefore, the Radiologist tends to double check produced reports which results in no improvement in overall process time

## 12. Discussion

In order to remain competitive, organisations realise the importance of their human recourses and started to work around user-centred business process designs. While the term business process management (BPM) is most frequently used to describe technologies for business process automation, in recent times, business leaders are increasingly recognising the need for a more holistic approach to BPM [5] that incorporates the people dimension in the analysis of business processes. The effort in BPM points towards methods, policies, metrics, management practice and software tools to discover, model, simulate, execute, analyse, optimise and govern ongoing adjustments to processes. for improved business agility and operational performance. BPMG de<sup>fi</sup>nes BPM as an ongoing process of understanding, designing, executing and optimisation of enterprise-wide business activities that incorporate people, processes, systems and strategy. Despite the clear speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of people properties in the business process modelling effort, current BPM research remains mainly focused on technology. Business performance assessment constitutes the cornerstone for successful business change. This stresses the need for tools and methods to assess prospective business model perfor mance prior to their enactment. Techniques such as Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats (SWOT), and getting constructive feedback from those who are closest to the business are important: however, more formal performance measurement techniques are needed that account for humans, tasks and the technology used in business processes. Results from the application of Systems Dynamics in business process simulation [19] were encouraging; however, the method proposed had minimum use of people's properties. Properties of business process actors such as productivity constitute the foundation for successful business change. This however is directly related to human performance, which when not dealt with appropriately may result in reduced business performance that leads to increased delays, and errors, that can cause missed deadlines and customer dissatisfaction.

This paper employs a socio-technical approach to business process modelling and simulation that contributes towards improved quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of business performance of prospective change efforts. The need for a socio-technical approach to business processes has also been addressed by Katzensten [29]; however, no tool was provided to support the method. Our approach uses dynamic assessment of human performance through a BBN model for improved quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of business process cycle time, using properties of the technology, the environment and the organisation. This approach escapes from the traditional deterministic measure of task completion times and hence improves the accuracy of the business process performance assessment. Moreover, the method enables diagnostic analysis of process merits and helps decision makers identify tasks, technologies and agents in the process that cause reduced performance and need to be addressed during the redesign. Work by Kumar and Becerra-Fernandez [30] also stresses the need to support business process redesign; however, their efforts concentrate on managing con<sup>fl</sup>icts among goals of organisational members using cognitive maps. A limitation of their method is the lack of the time dimension. Also, the development of cognitive maps and the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of causal values among causal concepts are complex tasks and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to be achieved by an organisational member. Other approaches to organisational redesign such as the business value complementarity method [6] concentrate on the business value gains and the way these are affected by changes in technological and organisational factors. Again, the development of the business value model is a complex process that requires automated support.

![](/api/attachments/JUEQA4SP/fulltext/images/8e4ed4d1429a4ae72aeb55fb8741188bf6b2b80bec7bb858d04c294af7418b60.jpg)  
Fig. 10. Human performance estimates per scenario step for each IT component with worst case settings of environmental, human and organisational properties

Our method is based on the socio-technical perspective, and hence we introduced communication patterns in the business process modelling, to address the people dimension. Despite the fact that communication patterns constitute an important aspect in business process redesign, there is no analytical technique to date that integrates communication acts with business performance. Research efforts use communication acts to model businesses; however, these attempts remain conceptual. Our approach combines in<sup>fl</sup>uences from the Social Networks Analysis (SNA) that explicitly address relationships among actors in a team and model communication acts among participating agents [12]. However, SNA focus is on determining important agents in the network rather than their performance and do not provide support for business change decisions. Work by Kumar and Becerra-Fernandez [30] also recognises the importance of communication acts in organisational business process assessment. However, their efforts concentrate on the need for modelling collaborations among business actors in an attempt to make trust within and across organisations explicit and consequently improve interorganisational relationships. Their method uses a taxonomy of speech acts and a conversation for action loop based on which interactions among actors are modelled.

The use of simulation in our method enables the evaluation of prospective business process models early in the design phase. Similar approaches that employ business process simulation to support organisational change, such as MICRO SAINT [48], Protos [60] and ARIS [61], also use cycle time as one of their metrics of process performance. However, they mainly concentrate on the functional aspects of the process and fail to consider inter-operator communications and human factors.

This paper presented a novel method and a tool that enables the validation of business process model performance prior to implementation. The contribution of the method goes beyond traditional business process simulation that concentrates on functional properties of the process, to include organisational, environmental, human and technological properties of the human element of such processes. The introduction of Bayesian probability in the simulation enables the modelling of softer aspects of business processes that are characterised by uncertainty. Limitations of our approach concentrate on the fact that only one dimension of business performance is addressed, namely cycle time. Other properties that de<sup>fi</sup>ne organisational performance such as costs, quality and <sup>fl</sup>exibility are missing. In the future we aim to expand our method to incorporate the remaining dimensions of business performance. Additionally, we intend to augment the method with optimisation capabilities using Monte Carlo simulation. This will enable the analyst to specify properties of each design alternative, based on which the system will <sup>fi</sup>nd optimal combinations that will result in best process performance. As far as the human performance BBN model is concerned, in order to improve its accuracy and eliminate the limitations of Boolean variables we aim to augment the model's granularity with multi-state nodes. For the parameterisation of the CPTs we will use the method and tool developed by [14], suitable for ranked nodes with multiple states.
