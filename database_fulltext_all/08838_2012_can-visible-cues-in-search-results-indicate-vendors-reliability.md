# Can visible cues in search results indicate vendors' reliability?

## a b s t r a c t

Search engines play a critical role in the diffusion of online information because they determine what content is easily visible to Web users. Major search engines, such as Google, Microsoft Live Search, and Yahoo!, provide two distinct types of results, organic and paid, each of which uses different mechanisms for selecting and ranking relevant Web pages. Using a third-party trust assurance program from BBB (Better Business Bureau) Online we <sup>fi</sup>nd that vendors represented by websites in organic and paid results have varying reliability ratings. These ratings, based on overall customer experiences, may range from satisfactory to unsatisfactory. We empirically examine how vendors' reliability ratings from BBB Online are associated with cues (such as type of search result, relative price of a product, and number of sites selling the product) that can be observed or derived from organic and paid search results. Further, we apply a data mining technique to predict the ven dors' BBB reliability ratings using those cues and achieve good performance.

## 1. Introduction

Due to the large number of websites and overwhelming amount of information available online, search engines have played a critical role in information search because they act as a central conduit between the producers and consumers of information [26]. According to Feng et al. [7], over 40% of product searches were initiated via search engines. In order to identify a particular website for the desired service or product, Web consumers typically <sup>fi</sup>nd and compare a large number of retailers from results in search engine result pages (SERPs). As consumers become increasingly dependent on search results from commercial search engines for their information needs, it is important for consumers to know that vendors that are represented by websites in SERPs are indeed reliable. According to BBB (Better Business Bureau) Online (http://www.bbb.org), an unreliable company may engage in unscrupulous methods (such as high pressure sales tactics and false statements about products) when dealing with customers. Identifying the reliability of vendors in search results is important and has practical implications for Web consumers.

Major search engines, such as Google, Microsoft Live Search, and Yahoo!, show two types (i.e., organic and paid) of search results on the same SERP. Organic results are generated by search engine's pro prietary algorithms which rank a site by considering many factors such as link structure and content relevance [3,10]. Paid results appear on the top, right, and sometimes bottom of a SERP, and they are produced because content providers or advertisers pay search engine companies by placing bids on one or more terms in search queries [13,15]. Paid search has enormous economic impact and has become a primary business model for Web search engines [13]. Given the differences in mechanisms for producing organic versus paid results, it may be the case that it is easier in terms of time and effort for a company to be listed in the top positions of paid results than in the top positions of organic results. This is especially true for lesser-known or lesser-established <sup>fi</sup>rms with a small number of loyal customers. These companies, however, can try to offset their low pro<sup>fi</sup>le by bidding high prices for relevant keywords and appear in the paid results to attain a greater number of Web consumers.

In this study, using the reliability reports from BBB Online we identify the reliability ratings for resellers, vendors who buy products from manufacturers and resell them to consumers. Fig. 1 is a screenshot for part of a BBB reliability report for a digital camera reseller. The company name (which is blacked out in this example) and its BBB rating (F, which means unsatisfactory) are displayed on top of the report. Following that, BBB discloses the numbers of complaints received about this company, how many of them the company failed to respond to, and states the main tactics the company engages in with its customers. To be consistent with BBB, we choose the term reliability to qualify resellers and further we categorize them into one of two categories, satisfactory and unsatisfactory, according to their BBB ratings.

For relevant search results in SERPs, Web consumers can observe certain cues such as the types of results (organic and paid), page numbers, position of results (i.e., the index numbers of organic and paid results), and prices. After programmatically submitting a large number of e-commerce queries to the three major search engine sites, Google (www.google.com), Microsoft (search.live.com), and Yahoo! (www.yahoo.com), we empirically examine how a vendor's reliability is associated with various cues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from search results. And further we apply a decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er to predict each vendor's reliability using those cues. The results from our empirical analysis contribute to online reliability and trust literature. The performance of the predictive model for reseller reliability con<sup>fi</sup>rms that it can become a viable consumer product search aid. Findings from this investigation have important implications for the search engine companies, search advertising market providers, and more importantly for search engine consumers.

## 2. Literature review

Consumers' trust is critical to the success of online retailers [27], because whether consumers trust a vendor ultimately affects their purchasing decisions [17,20]. Prior work has examined the relationships between trust in companies and features of their websites. Nöteberg et al. [24] compare trust assurance seals from two different sources — an assurance from a third party and a store's selfproclaimed assurance. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that a third party's assurance is more effective than a store's self-proclaimed assurance in increasing the purchase likelihood, while either assurance is more effective than without one. Hu et al. [12] study the effects of privacy, security, and transaction-integrity assurance on consumer's initial trust in online vendors. They report interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings that multiple assurance seals, such as both privacy and security assurance or both privacy and transaction-integrity assurance, may not enhance consumers' initial online trust more than a single assurance seal does. By analyzing salient online trust antecedents, Chau et al. [6] investigate the relationship between customers' trust in an online vendor and their dropouts in different stages of the purchase process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they <sup>fi</sup>nd that the salient trust antecedents can change in different stages of the consumer purchasing process. For example, the ease of use of a vendor's site is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in an early state of the purchase process but becomes less important in later stages. According to Waker<sup>fi</sup>eld and Whitten [32], consumer perception on cognitive cues (i.e., product brand equity, website attractiveness, communication, opportunism) in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer's initial trust in an e-retailer. Third-party endorsement, presence of physical store, and clarity of warranty can reduce customers' perceived risk and enhance their intention to purchase from new e-tailers [35]. After studying signaling roles of observable cues (e.g., security disclosures, awards from neutral sources, seals of approval) in websites of small online retailers, Wang et al. [33] <sup>fi</sup>nd that security disclosures and awards from neutral sources enhance consumers' trust. However, none of these studies have considered the type of search result as a signal of trust. In addition, different from their selected website features, such as privacy statement, third-party endorsement, and warranty, cues used in our study are more conducive to automated processing and are less “semantic” in nature. Moreover, instead of recruiting subjects and observing their behaviors in experiments, we identify company reliability ratings from a third-party trust assurance program (i.e., BBB Online) and examine how the ratings are associated with and can be further inferred by cues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from search results.

After comparing organic search results with paid results, Ma et al. [22] <sup>fi</sup>nd that unsatisfactory companies tend to offer lower average prices than satisfactory ones, and paid results contain more unsatisfactory companies than do organic ones. Different from their work which is mainly to <sup>fi</sup>nd and report the differences between organic and paid results in terms of price and number of vendors, we focus on studying how visible cues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from search results are associated with vendors' reliability and further predicting the reliability using those cues.

Jansen [14] shows that, for e-commerce queries, paid results are more relevant than organic ones. Jansen et al. [15] <sup>fi</sup>nd that Web searchers appreciate sponsored links if they are relevant and are unconcerned if the search engines disclose them as sponsored links. Unlike these works, we study the relation between the visible cues of search results and the reliability of vendors.

Many researchers have studied issues related to prices of online vendors. The research problems include the relationships between price and trust-assuring arguments, price and post-purchase service quality, the price difference between e-tailers and hybrid retailers (selling through both websites and physical locations), and price dispersion among online merchants [2,5,8,12,16,31]. Provided that consumers do not have complete information about an online retailer's credibility, price is important in retailer selections of the consumers [29]. Because the cost of picking an un-trustworthy store is higher for high-price than low-price products, Kim and Benbasat [16] reveal that customers are more in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the content of assurance arguments when purchasing high-price than low-price products. Mai et al. [23] <sup>fi</sup>nd that book vendors using privacy seals charge a premium for products compared to vendors not bearing a seal. Koças [18] demonstrates that <sup>fi</sup>rms, especially those with small number of loyal customers and large number of comparison shoppers (i.e., switchers who pursue the lowest prices instead of being loyal to any company), use price promotions to attract comparison shoppers. Cao et al. [5] separate customers' satisfaction associated with placing an order and satisfaction with order ful<sup>fi</sup>llment because placing an order online and receiving the order happen at different time. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that price satisfaction is negatively associated with satisfaction with the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment process. This situation suggests that higher pricesatisfaction (re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a lower price) comes probably at the cost of lower level of service in shipping and customer support. This lower level of service can be expected to negatively affect satisfaction with the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment process, which can lead to poor overall customer ratings, such as an unsatisfactory rating by BBB Online. Similarly, Cao and Gruca [4] <sup>fi</sup>nd that in online book market, e-tailers having higher satisfaction with post purchase services charge signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher prices. Gan et al. [8] study the pricing strategies of dot-bams (brickand-mortars) and dot-coms. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that online stores show higher price dispersion than brick-and-mortars, and they do not see a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in the average price level between dotbams and dot-coms. Price differences between pure e-retailers and hybrid retailers have been reported [e.g., 2, 31]. Tang and Lu [31] <sup>fi</sup>nd that in CD market prices by e-tailers are generally lower than prices by hybrid retailers. Bock et al. [2] compare three categories of products (books, CDs, and digital cameras) in two countries (China and the U. S.) with different levels of Internet maturity, and they claim that in both countries, e-tailers have lower price levels and price dispersion than hybrid retailers. These past studies do not consider the impact of prices or price dispersion on an online vendor's reliability. Our study attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap empirically.

## 3. Our approach

In this study we focus on the reliability analysis and prediction for the products of digital cameras because digital cameras represent a very common category of consumer electronics. A large number of consumers can afford and own such products and many people buy them online. They are homogeneous products and thus buying from a satisfactory vendor becomes important. Moreover, since prices of digital cameras are typically much higher than prices for products like books or CDs, the losses for digital camera consumers would be greater due to the frauds of unreliable vendors. Some past studies have chosen (digital) cameras for their research e.g., [1,2,28]. To conduct our investigation we gathered the following data: (1) queries used to search for digital cameras, (2) SERPs for each of the queries to obtain data about resellers, (3) BBB ratings for the resellers in the SERPs, and (4) visible cues (or attributes) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from SERPs.

## 3.1. Search queries

Using a keyword research tool from Google Adwords, with the initial seed keyword “digital camera” we ranked the Google-suggested keywords (e.g., “canon digital camera”) by search volume (the relative number of users searching for that keyword on Google) and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed eight major camera manufacturers (Canon, Nikon, Panasonic, Sony, Kodak, Olympus, Samsung, and Fuji<sup>fi</sup>lm). From each manufacturer's U.S. website we manually identi<sup>fi</sup>ed all the listed digital camera models. Using the combination of a maker and a model, we create different search queries. For example, for the query “canon eos 5d”, the model (eos 5d) speci<sup>fi</sup>es what speci<sup>fi</sup>c model of camera we look for and the camera maker (Canon) provides a context to help improve the relevance of the search results. With this procedure we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a total of 243 queries, which represented all the 243 different models available from the eight major digital camera manufacturers' websites in December 2007.

## 3.2. SERPs and resellers

To obtain a sample snapshot of query results, we programmatically submitted each of the 243 keywords to each of three commercial search engines and fetched the content of the top three SERPs and all of the landing pages corresponding to the URLs in the three SERPs during a weekend in February 2008. While examining the search results in these SERPs, we considered only those results that were from websites of digital camera resellers (e.g., www.newegg. com) selling brand new products, and discarded links from other sites, such as camera review websites (e.g., www.dpreview.com), price comparison websites (e.g., www.pricescan.com), auction sites (e.g. www.ebay.com), image-sharing sites (e.g., www.pbase.com), and personal websites or blogs. We also discarded results corresponding to non-US domains (e.g., amazon.com.uk). In this study, a product refers to a camera, a camera body only, or a camera kit (e.g., including lens). We considered only brand new products to make comparisons (e.g., price comparison) fair.

If a result showed that a reseller's website had shopping information for the corresponding model of camera, we recorded the type of result (i.e., organic or paid), page number (i.e., 1, 2, or 3), (paid or organic) result's index (i.e., order) in the SERP, URL of the link, and price of the product. In addition, we recorded whether a dollar sign (\$) appeared in the title of a search result. The price was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the reseller's website and did not include shipping cost or tax. Table 1 summarizes the number of all distinct websites for resellers and the number of price observations (i.e., search results pointing to reseller's websites selling the corresponding camera models) for the 243 queries. For instance, from the Google search results we identi <sup>fi</sup>ed 86 distinct reseller websites and 1545 price observations.

## 3.3. BBB ratings

In the online environment, vendors' reliability is important to Web consumers, because they are often reluctant to make purchases from unknown merchants due to uncertainty about vendor behavior [32]. However, it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for consumers to know the reliability of online retailers. Thus some third-party sources try to aid consumers in identifying trustworthiness of online vendors [21]. Prior work has shown that the use of third-party sources affects consumer trust of online retailers and their intention to purchase [19,32]. The BBB (Better Business Bureau) Online deals with consumers and businesses and provides a reliability report on a company on the basis of several factors, such as any advertising issues found by BBB Online, the number and type of complaints, and how the company handled complaints. Most of the time, the BBB Online provides a company with an overall rating, either satisfactory or unsatisfactory, in its reliability report.

Occasionally BBB Online issues a reliability score according to a <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained scheme, ranging from A+ to F. We convert these <sup>fi</sup>ner ratings such that those equal to or better than ${ \mathsf { C } } - { \mathsf { \Omega } } ( { \mathrm { i } } . { \mathsf { e } } .$ , BBB acceptable rating) are labeled satisfactory and those below C− are labeled unsatisfactory. We manually identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the reliability record for each reseller's website in our data sets. Table 2 shows the number of distinct websites and the number of price observations after discarding the corresponding search results which did not have a BBB reliability rating (i.e., no rating) or were not listed (i.e., not found) by BBB Online. For our regression and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation analyses, we used only those ob servations reported in Table 2.

BizRate (http://www.bizrate.com/) is also commonly known for providing ratings for e-commerce vendors. Different from BBB Online, BizRate collects ratings online from individual consumers after they complete their transactions and reports the aggregated results. A potential problem is that such comments can be misleading and incorrect, as the comments may not be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed. Another reason for not using BizRate ratings is the mechanism for showing BizRate surveys to users. Only after a vendor contracts BizRate for using its services, will some randomly selected consumers see the BizRate survey after they <sup>fi</sup>nish their transactions in that company's website. It is reasonable to think that compared to a satisfactory company, an unsatisfactory company is less likely to allow customers to provide feedback which eventually would be available to the public. In fact among all websites/vendors we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from our data sets, BizRate gave outstanding or good ratings for 15 companies and rated none as poor or unsatisfactory (BizRate provides four different ratings: outstanding, good, satisfactory, and poor). In other words, if we had used BizRate in our study the number of negative (unsatisfactory) instances will be zero, which would not allow for meaningful analyses of the independent variables or reliability prediction.

## 3.4. Visible cues and derived attributes

The selection and ranking of organic search results are based on search engine algorithms that consider various factors related to websites, such as the number and rank of in-links and content relevance [e.g., 3, 10]. The selection of paid results depends on the bidding on keywords and the ranking of paid results is generally determined by the bid price and the click-through-rate [30]. Ma et al. [22] <sup>fi</sup>nd that organic results contain fewer unsatisfactory companies than do paid ones. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rst we choose the obvious visible cue, resultType (i.e., organic or paid), as an attribute for distinguishing satisfactory and unsatisfactory companies. We noted that some vendors appearing in organic results are also shown in paid search as they also pay for sponsored search. The dual appearance gives the vendor additional exposure to Web consumers. In a recent work, Hu and Sheng [11] <sup>fi</sup>nd the dual appearance increases clickthroughs and e-tailer's revenues. Thus we derive a Boolean attribute, inBothTypes, to represent whether the same vendor appears in both organic and paid results of the top three SERPs for the same query from the same search engine.

The search result index is another simple visible cue, and it represents the ranking of the result in SERP. We denote this attribute as index. According to prior research, the index (i.e., rank) is critical for both companies and consumers because higher ranked (i.e., lower index values) links would attract more customer attention and higher click-throughs [9]. When identifying the index for a (paid or organic) result on a SERP, we consider/accumulate the number of results in the previous SERP(s). The index of organic results is straight forward: it always starts from 1 for the <sup>fi</sup>rst result in the <sup>fi</sup>rst SERP, and the index of the <sup>fi</sup>rst organic result on the second SERP is the number of all organic results in the <sup>fi</sup>rst SERP plus 1. For paid results on the same SERP, we consider results listed on top to be ranked higher than those appearing on the right side, and those on the right to be ranked higher than those on the bottom. For example, suppose there are eight sponsored links in the <sup>fi</sup>rst SERP, for the second top paid result on the second SERP, its index is 10 which is the number of all paid results on the <sup>fi</sup>rst SERP plus 2.

According to Cao et al. [5], a lower price may result in a lower level of service. Also, Ma et al. [22] <sup>fi</sup>nd that the average price from unsatisfactory companies tends to be lower than that by satisfactory companies. Thus for the same product, we estimate that a (relatively) lower price could indicate a poor reliability of the vendor. We derive the following normalized metric, $P _ { r e l } ,$ to measure the relative price for the same product. We do not use absolute price in dollar amount because there is no low or high for a price by itself (without comparison) and also because for different camera models the prices can vary dramatically.

$$
p _ {r e l} = \frac {\mathrm{P} - \mathrm{P} _ {\min}}{\mathrm{P} _ {\max} - \mathrm{P} _ {m i n}}
$$

In the above metric, $\mathrm { P _ { m i n } }$ and $\mathrm { \Delta P _ { m a x } }$ represent the lowest and highest price for the same model from all resellers in the top three SERPs from a search engine, and P is the price by the reseller of the current search result. $P _ { r e l }$ is normalized between 0 and 1. The larger the $\mathrm { \sf P } _ { \mathrm { r e l } } ,$ the higher the reseller's price is compared with prices from other vendors. If $\mathrm { P _ { m i n } } { = } \mathrm { P _ { m a x } }$ we set $\mathrm { \sf P } _ { \mathrm { r e l } }$ at 0.5 because the price is equally close to the lowest and highest price.

Intuitively, a company gains more pro<sup>fi</sup>t by selling a more expensive product. We would like to examine whether it is true that the price range can become a factor to help distinguish reliability of vendors. We divide the 243 digital camera models in our data set into low, medium, and high ends according to their average prices. We are interested in <sup>fi</sup>nding whether an unsatisfactory company tends to make more money by selling the high and medium ends of cameras, so we consider such a price range an indicator for distinguishing satisfactory and unsatisfactory reseller. Therefore for each of the camera models and results from each search engine, we computed an average price by averaging all prices from different resellers in the top three SERPs, sorted the average prices for all models, and evenly divided them into three categories, low, medium, and high. We denote this price range attribute as $\mathbf { P } _ { r a n }$

We expect that when a product is popular (i.e., more people buy it), there are more companies selling it, and so the number of companies (sellers) can represent the popularity of a product. Moreover, if an unsatisfactory company offers a lower price for a popular product, it would have a chance to lure more customers. Therefore, whether a product is popular may be an indicator for distinguishing satisfactory resellers from unsatisfactory ones. Since a website URL can represent a vendor, we use the number of distinct websites (for resellers in the top three SERPs) to measure the popularity of a product, and denote this metric as numDistinctSite. In order to identify a site/reseller from search results we extracted the second-level domain name from the URL. For example, jr is the second-level domain name for the site, www.jr.com. We observed that for some queries the same vendor appeared multiple times in the top three SERPs, and this is especially true for paid search results when there were not many different resellers for the product. We denote siteFrequency as the number of times a site/reseller appears in (paid and organic) search results in the top three SERPs for a given query. For example, for a site, the site-Frequency of 2 means this reseller/site appeared in two different search results (either one in paid and one in organic, or both in paid, or both in organic results) in the top three SERPs. So the siteFrequency attribute re<sup>fl</sup>ects the visibility of a vendor and we would like to see whether it has the same effect as inBothTypes which also signi<sup>fi</sup>es visibility.

Last, for a search result from SERPs we can easily identify whether or not a dollar sign, \$, appears in the title of the search result. As we have observed, dollar signs often appeared in the titles of sponsored results and were shown next to relatively lower prices; vendors may be using the sign to attract customers' attention. We denote this boolean attribute, whether a dollar sign shown as a price in a result title in SERP, as dollarSign.

The dependent variable, DV, or class label is the vendor's BBB reliability rating which has a binary value: 0 for unsatisfactory and 1 for satisfactory.

## 4.1. Significant variables and discussions

For the analyses, we <sup>fi</sup>rst convert the four categorical IVs into dummy variables. In particular,

• resultType: 0 for organic result and 1 for paid result

• inBothTypes: 0 for the vendor not appearing in both organic and paid results and 1 for appearing in both

• dollarSign: 0 for no dollar sign appearing in the result title and 1 for otherwise

$\mathrm { \Delta P _ { r a n } : }$ Because of its three categorical levels, by choosing the high end as the reference category, we produced the following two dummy variables from $\mathrm { P _ { r a n } }$

${ \dot { \mathsf { d } } } _ { \mathsf { l o w } } \colon 1$ for low, 0 for medium and high price products

$\mathtt { d } _ { \mathrm { { m e d } } }$ : 1 for medium, and 0 for low and high price products

Since the DV is a binary variable, we choose logistic regression to examine the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of those IVs (we use the SPSS 15.0). Next we discuss how coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in Table 4 are associated with the DV.

• The variable of resultType is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant across all data sets from the three search engines which means the type of search result, which was ignored by prior research, is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicator when judging a vendor's reliability. We think that this situation is due to differences in vendors' strategic selections of both camera products and search engines for their paid advertisement needs.

## 4.2. Predicting reliability

With the eight attributes in Table 3, how well can we predict the reliability of a website/reseller? Using a publicly available data analysis tool, Weka [34], we use a decision tree (i.e., J48 classi<sup>fi</sup>er in Weka) model because this classi<sup>fi</sup>er has been found to perform well for binary classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. Furthermore, it does not impose assumptions on the distribution of data, and its results are well suited for human interpretation [25]. In the following Table 5 we show the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation performance that is based on ten-fold cross validation with default parameters in Weka.

The second column displays the prior distributions of satisfactory and unsatisfactory companies in the observations. Unsatisfactory companies exist in over a third (i.e., 35.5–38.9%) of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed search results from all the three search engines, which demonstrates the general existence of unreliable companies in search results and indicates the importance of this study. The decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er achieves accuracy between 85% and 89%. Among the three data sets from the three search engines, the performance from Microsoft and Yahoo! data sets is slightly higher than that from the Google data set.

## 5. Conclusions

Search engines have become a necessary and convenient tool for people to <sup>fi</sup>nd information and shop online. However, the results in SERPs do not disclose any information about a company's reliability to Web searchers. This information is critical because vendor's reliability is one major concern for Web consumers, especially considering the facts that unsatisfactory companies widely exist in search results in general and in paid results in particular and they tend to offer lower prices to lure customers [22]. In this study we propose an approach to predicting vendor's reliability on the basis of cues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from search results. We empirically examine thousands of price observations generated by 243 e-commerce (i.e., digital camera) search queries from three major commercial search engines (Google, Microsoft, and Yahoo!). We <sup>fi</sup>nd that several variables, that are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed or derived from search engine results and therefore can be considered as cues visible to a Web surfer or an automated crawler, are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in inferring a company's reliability. Those variables include the search result type, relative price, total number of resellers, and whether the same reseller appears in both organic and paid results. Further we employ decision tree classi<sup>fi</sup>er to predict the reliability on the basis of these cues and achieve accuracy between 85% and 89% for the data sets from the three search engines. For the three search engine data sets, many of the attributes appearing in the top-4 levels of the trained decision trees are common. Moreover, the four signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by regression analysis are almost always chosen by all of the decision trees in their top-4 levels of nodes. Using our approach, an interesting practical application, embedded in a Web browser but independent of a search engine, can be developed to predict a site/reseller's reliability for Web searchers.

Results of this study have implications for different stakeholders. Currently SERPs, especially the sponsored links, do contain some (e.g., over one-third) unsatisfactory companies but don't disclose to consumers anything about the vendors' reliability. This situation raises concern for search engine consumers. Given that sponsored search has become the dominant revenue source for search engine companies, they may consider providing Web consumers vendors' reliability information in SERPs, such as that extracted from a reliable third-party assurance program, or raise the bar for vendors with unsatisfactory ratings in sponsored search and prevent them from dominating paid search results. The implication for vendors is that companies with satisfactory ratings should consider disclosing their ratings on SERPs in order to distinguish them from those unsatisfactory companies. Finally the implication for consumers is that they should not only be aware of the existence of unsatisfactory vendors in search results, but also be receptive to suggestions to look up a vendor reliability record from a third-party assurance program before purchasing from the vendor. In addition, consumers can actively use tools built using predictive models such as ours to estimate the reliability of vendors. Not only can such tools easily integrate reliability estimates with search results, but they can also provide reliability estimates for companies whose reliability cannot be found from existing assurance programs.

When identifying relevant search results (i.e., a reseller site that sells the right camera), our software program fetched the top three SERPs and the landing pages for the results in these SERPs, and thus later we could (manually) examine those pages. If the landing page corresponding to a search result did not contain the relevant product, then we assumed that the site did not sell the product. Thus our approach can be considered strict in terms of identifying relevant results.

We test our approach for the product category of digital cameras using search results from three major search engines. Interesting future directions include studying different product categories, considering multiple time points, including “semantic” website features (such as third-party endorsement and warranty on vendors' Web pages) as attributes/IVs, and using site ratings from different sources to further test our approach. Investigating, comparing, and classifying different company-rating websites can be an insightful future direction as well. While our process for creating search queries is reasonable for producing relevant results, we realize that to search for a camera of interest, Web consumers can use other types of queries besides combining the manufacturer name and product model. Hence expanding the empirical analysis and prediction evaluation to a larger variety of relevant keywords could also be a future direction.
