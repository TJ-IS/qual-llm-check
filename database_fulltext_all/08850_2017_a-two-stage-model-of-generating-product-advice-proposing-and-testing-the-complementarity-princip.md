# A Two-Stage Model of Generating Product Advice: Proposing and Testing the Complementarity Principle

DAVID JINGJUN XU, IZAK BENBASAT, AND RONALD T. CENFETELLI

ABSTRACT: Most extant research into product recommendations focuses on how advice from recommendation agents (RAs), consumers, or experts facilitates an initial (or single-stage) screening of available products and provides relevant product recommendations. The literature has largely overlooked the possibility and effects of the second stage of product advice using a recommendation improvement (RI) functionality, during which users can refine and improve the accuracy of the firststage product recommendations. Thus, our understanding of how users make product choices is incomplete. To rectify this, we propose a two-stage model of generating product advice, and we use it to test what we propose as the complementarity principle. This principle posits that the first-stage recommendations (personalized or nonpersonalized) influence the impact of different types of second-stage RI functionality, which augment the first stage by facilitating either alternative-based or attribute-based processing. Results show that the complementary synergies between the two stages result in higher perceived decision quality, but at the expense of higher perceived decision effort. We contribute to the literature by helping researchers better understand users’ adoption of the second-stage RI functionality in conjunction with first-stage recommendations. In addition, e-commerce designers are advised to provide different and complementary types of recommendation sources and RI functionalities to facilitate online consumers’ decision making.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: online recommenders, perceived decision effort, perceived decision quality, personalized recommendations, product recommendations, recommendation improvement, recommendation sources.

Given the immense volume and diversity of information available on the Internet, customers rely on different types of product recommendations from various sources to manage a daunting volume of data in a limited period. Information sources—such as word of mouth and recommendations from experts—influence consumers’ decision making [10, 16, 21, 61, 66]. To assist customers in narrowing down their options from among the very wide variety available on the Internet, popular websites provide different types of product recommendations. Some provide consumer recommendations (e.g., Expedia’s hotel ratings) and expert recommendations (e.g., CollegeXpress.com’s Experts’ Choice). Others provide their customers with advicegiving software to assist them in choosing the “right” products [57]. These online recommendation agents (RAs) assist consumers by eliciting their product needs and then making product recommendations that satisfy those needs [49, 77, 78]. RAs have become increasingly important to the success of online commerce [1, 6, 32, 33, 34, 37, 67, 73, 77, 78]. To counter intense pressures from competitors [1], retailers with an online presence—such as BestBuy and Sears—have developed RAs that help consumers select a product that best matches their needs.

Much of the academic literature on recommendation provision assumes a single stage of consumers’ product evaluation, resulting in an incomplete understanding of how users reach their product choices. In such single-stage or first-stage product advice with an RA, consumers may provide an RA with information regarding their product attribute preferences, and the RA, in turn, provides a consideration set of product alternatives from which the consumer can choose.

However, it might be difficult to satisfy customers’ needs in just one cycle of recommendations. In fact, Amazon since 2009 has provided a way for users to finetune the recommendations first received (“Fix this recommendation”). Similarly, the popular movie recommender MovieLens in 2010 added a “Movie Tuner” function so that users can adjust the movies initially recommended by the site by requesting movies possessing more or fewer particular attributes (e.g., “Similar to ‘Predestination,” but more realistic”). The site then recommends movies that meet the user’s more refined requirements. We label this second-stage feature as recommendation improvement (RI) functionality. With an RI functionality, a decision aid can build a more comprehensive understanding of a user’s product preferences and requirements, and adapt product recommendations to the particular user’s needs [45]. Surprisingly, the literature has largely overlooked the possibility, availability, and effects of second-stage product advice using an RI functionality, by which users can refine and improve the accuracy of first-stage product recommendations. For this reason, in their comprehensive review of RA research, Xiao and Benbasat [77, 78] did not mention RI functionality. Although a few pioneer practitioners have adopted RI functionality in e-commerce websites, we contend that it is important to understand under what circumstances which RI functionality can be best used to enhance users’ performance and how to prevent its misuse.

Given the lack of empirical research on RI functionality and the importance attached to RI functionality by scholars (e.g., [45, 75]) and practitioners (e.g., Amazon.com, MovieLens), we propose to study the efficacy of second-stage RI functionality in an e-commerce setting and its effects on perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort, two of the most important constructs in a decisionmaking context. According to the effort–accuracy framework [53, 54], a consumer’s decision-making process is often influenced by the trade-off between the perceived quality of the decision and the perceived effort required to make that decision, since more accurate decisions come at the expense of expending more effort. In this study, perceived decision quality is the degree of a consumer’s confidence in his/her product choice, and perceived decision effort is the amount of effort the consumer feels he/she has exerted in processing product information, evaluating product alternatives, and arriving at choice decision [77]. Studying users’ perceptions is important in information systems literature [13, 18, 25, 30, 33, 34, 79] because many studies have revealed that individuals’ choice of information systems are typically affected by their perceptions of the capabilities that an information system can offer. If an individual does not perceive higher decision quality or reduced decision effort, then he or she will not be interested in continuing to use a decision aid. Extensive research has applied the effort–accuracy framework to evaluate users’ perceptions of decision quality and decision effort in the design and use of technology-based decision-making tools (e.g., [2, 40, 70, 73, 74, 82, 83]).

Preferential choice tasks entail selecting among alternatives based on one’s preferences of attributes describing those alternatives [28, 33, 34, 55]. This indicates that alternatives and attributes are two of the key components in preferential choice problems. Thus, we study RI functionality from the perspectives of attributes, alternatives, and the combination of attributes and alternatives:

1. Attribute-based RI functionality allows users to provide or update their preferences on one or more product attributes after they have examined the recommendations (from consumers, experts, or an RA) they received during the first-stage product advice. The attribute-based RI functionality then returns those products that best fit a user’s preferences based on those indicated product attributes.

2. Alternative-based RI functionality allows users to indicate a preference for one product (alternative) from the recommendations provided by the firststage product advice (again from sources such as consumers, experts, or an RA). The alternative-based RI functionality then returns those products most similar to the one for which the user indicates a preference.

3. As an integration of the attribute-based and alternative-based RI functionality, an integrated RI allows users to indicate a preference for one of the products recommended during the first-stage product advice, and it optionally allows users to provide or update their preferences on one or more individual attributes for the preferred product alternative indicated.

The major research question we answer in this study is: Given the nature of the first-stage product recommendation, what is the best second-stage RI functionality for product choice decision making? To elaborate, first-stage recommendations can be described in terms of degree of personalization. Sources such as consumer’s preference lists (e.g., “most popular,” “Top 5,” etc.) or expert advice (e.g., Consumer Reports) lack the personalization of RA recommendations. What is most popular or most recommended may not be the best fit for a given individual consumer. Therefore, for users of consumers’ and experts’ recommendations in the first stage, the question is whether the addition of an attribute-based, alternative-based or integrated-RI functionality in the second stage would enhance the user’s perceived decision quality. Conversely, RAs are explicitly designed to be personalized, seeking input from a user for the sake of providing recommendations that fit the needs or desires of the user. Hence, for users of an RA, an additional question is whether an attribute-based RI or alternative-based RI functionality is more helpful in the second stage in improving preference matching capability.

In this study, we propose and empirically evaluate a two-stage model of generating product advice using recommendations in the first stage and RI functionality in the second stage.<sup>1</sup> The main proposition we advance and test in this model is the complementarity principle, which posits: For higher perceived decision quality, nonpersonalized first-stage recommendations (e.g., from consumers or experts) are best matched with attribute-based RI functionality in the second stage. Conversely, personalized first-stage recommendations (e.g., RAs) are best matched with alternative-based RI functionality in the second stage.

The rationale behind this prediction (elucidated further in the Hypotheses Development section) is that on the one hand, initial nonpersonalized recommendations, either from experts or consumers, recommend a select few products, but users have not yet actively customized the associated product attributes of these recommended products for themselves. Thus, perceived decision quality will be low without the attribute-based RI functionality. On the other hand, an RA provides personalized recommendations with products that possess good fit with the user’s stated product attribute preferences. However, with compensatory strategies,<sup>2</sup> all product attributes simultaneously contribute to the calculation of an aggregate fit value [3, 77] and the recommended products may not have the exact attribute values the user had in mind. Thus, based on the theory of preferential choice[12, 58], the user may wish to explore alternatives similar to the ones recommended but that may have better values for the desired attributes, thereby leading to higher perceived decision quality. However, higher perceived decision quality likely comes at the cost of higher perceived decision effort. This is due to the nature of complementarity, which requires two types of decision processing (attribute-based and alternativebased processing). Switching from one to the other demands more effort, compared to using the same type of processing throughout both the first and second stages of recommendations.

The two-stage model of generating product advice can be applied to online business by assisting consumers to make better product choices. For example, BestBuy’s “Find the Perfect TV” personalized RA elicits online users’ TV preferences (e.g., price, size) and then recommends a list of TVs. <sup>3</sup> However, BestBuy’s product recommendations are limited to the first stage. If users want to look for products similar to the one recommended, there is no such functionality available. Applying our proposed two-stage model, BestBuy could add the alternative-based RI to the second stage to allow customers to refine the first-stage product advice. The alternative-based RI is not limited to the TV RA mentioned, but also other products such as musical instruments sold in BestBuy, and medical equipment sold by medicalsupplydepot.com. In addition, users of a website providing nonpersonalized advice (e.g., Yelp.com’s consumer ratings or CollegeXpress.com’s Experts’ Choice) will benefit by having an attribute-based or integrated RI to generate the second-stage recommendations subsequent to the current recommendations in the first stage.

## Literature Review

## First Stage: Personalized vs. Nonpersonalized Recommendations

Literature suggests that customers use information sources to reduce the uncertainty and risk associated with product purchase [16, 52]. Recommendations in the first stage can be categorized as personalized vs. nonpersonalized. Types of nonpersonalized recommendations include those from stores, consumers, and experts. Consumer and expert recommendations appear in general to be preferred over nonexperiential sources, such as those from an online store [21, 43], given that consumers have experienced the products for themselves [18] and experts have tested the products. However, results about the superior effect of the consumer over expert recommendations have been mixed. For example, experts were viewed as more credible in some studies [61, 72], while the reverse was found in others (e.g., [9]). Therefore, in this research, we include both types of nonpersonal recommendations: consumers (such as the five “most popular products” by computershopper.com) and experts (such as the “Editor’s Pick” by shopstyle.com).

One type of personalized recommendation is the content-filtering RAs that elicit users’ preferences and incorporate those preferences toward making product recommendations [33, 69, 77, 78].<sup>4</sup> RAs are becoming more important in providing personalized support and reducing information overload for customers in their online decision-making process [32, 74]. They have been incorporated in many websites (such as BestBuy.com, Sears.com, and Projectorcentral.com). The common characteristics that distinguish consumer and expert recommendations from RA recommendations are that consumer and expert recommendations require no input (i.e., product preference indication) from users and, thus, they provide nonpersonalized recommendations. In contrast, to obtain an RA’s recommendation, a user needs to provide the RA with input regarding his or her preference for each product attribute, and the importance assigned to each attribute, thus leading to personalized recommendations. In this study, both first-stage consumer and expert recommendations are grouped into one category as a counterpart to first-stage RA advice.

## Second Stage: Recommendation Improvement (RI) Functionalities

Second-stage RI functionality—that is, user feedback on the desirability of the firststage recommendations—is an important component of decision making in general, and of RAs in particular [45]. RI functionality is one way to supplement expert and consumer recommendations, because the latter two do not account for an individual user’s product preferences. Through the RI process, preference matching recommendations can then be generated. While RI functionality brings an element of personalization to expert and consumer recommendations, it is also useful in the case of an RA. As asserted by West et al. [75], it would be valuable for an RA to elicit users’ preferences, and, after providing a set of initial product recommendations, allow users to provide RI suggestions to fine-tune those initial product offerings. RI functionality plays a critical role in many RAs [44, 75]. However, this assertion has not been tested empirically, which thus necessitates the current research. Because preferential choice problems entail the analyses of data by alternatives and/or attributes [33, 34, 55], we examine three types of RI functionality:

● Attribute-based RI functionality allows users to provide preference information on each attribute of a recommended alternative (e.g., “Show me products with a price between \$200 and \$400 and a hard drive between 200GB and 300GB”).

● Alternative-based RI functionality allows the user to indicate a preference for one of the recommended alternatives and to ask to view those similar to the one for which the user indicates a preference (e.g., “Show me more products like product B”).

● Integrated RI functionality, as an integration of the alternative-based and attribute-based RI, allows a user to indicate a preference for one of the recommended products, and an optional improvement on the value of one or more attributes of the preferred product (e.g., “Show me more products like product A but with price between \$200 and \$400”).

To our knowledge, there have been no empirical studies comparing the differences among these three types of RI functionality, and how they complement the first-stage recommendations based on how personalized those first-stage recommendations are. Payne et al. [54] proposed a two-stage decision process in which a consumer first forms a product consideration set and then evaluates it in-depth before making a final choice. Applying Payne et al.’s [54] framework, Häubl and Trifts [23] evaluated the role of RAs in assisting users to form a consideration set in the first stage, combined with the role of a comparison matrix to assist users in organizing product information in the second stage. However, neither study examined RI functionality. In these studies, users did not have the opportunity to refine the initial product recommendations. Thus no further product advice was generated at the second stage. We address this gap in the literature.

By examining the heretofore unstudied role of RI functionality, we expect to make the following two theoretical contributions. First, we extend prior RA research by proposing a two-stage model of generating product advice. This is important because previous research has primarily focused on the first (and often only) stage of product recommendations (e.g., [25, 33, 34, 74, 82]). Prior research has presumed that everything begins and ends at a first stage [77, 78] when, in fact, a user’s product evaluation is an iterative process [15, 59]. Ignoring the second stage may limit the potential to achieve higher decision quality. Second, we not only examine both stages of product recommendations but also evaluate three types of RI functionality at the second stage to better understand which one is the best, under what conditions, and why. By including the second stage, we address the possibility of complementarity of decision-making tools between the first stage and second stage of product recommendations that has thus far been ignored. This in turn allows us to make a contribution to the growing literature in behavioral decision making that seeks to understand and explain how customers use multiple tools complementarily to make a better product selection.

## Theory of Preferential Choice and Decision-Processing Strategies

Preferential choice is a well-developed research area in many disciplines, including marketing, sociology, psychology, and economics. It is a pervasive and remarkably important human behavior [8]. Preferential choice problems normally entail many alternatives and attributes [54, 55]. The theory of preferential choice posits that an individual may have an ideal point (value) for an attribute of a product alternative (such as, the capacity of a hard drive on a laptop computer), and that his or her utility regarding the product alternative falls off as an attribute value either increases or decreases [12, 51]. The assumption that each individual has a preference for a certain attribute is consistent with the concept of rationality used by economic theorists. Most economists define rationality in terms of consistency principles and evaluate the rationality of behavior using principles of consistency within a system of preferences and beliefs [58]. The most important principle of preferential choice is perfect consistency or invariant preferences across occasions [58]. That is, a person’s choice of a given alternative is largely based on what he or she prefers. Prior research has shown that consumers give greater consideration to, and are ultimately more likely to choose, highly preference-matched personalized content [26, 68, 80]. They are also more prepared to pay for preference-matched goods and services [56].

While users’ preferences for certain products can change, it is less likely in our study. “Preferences are not always constructed; people do have firm and stable preferences for some objects” [5, p. 188]. According to Bettman et al. [5], users are most likely to have stable product preferences when they are familiar and experienced with the preference object. In such cases, users may simply retrieve these previously formed preferences from memory and select their preferred product. Consumers often have high product knowledge about products used on a daily basis. For example, 90 percent of U.S. households have a laptop computer (census.gov 2014).<sup>5</sup> Thus, our assumption is that users’ preferences regarding the attributes of these products will be relatively stable.

To make a preferential choice, users may leverage two types of decision-processing strategies—attribute-based or alternative-based processing—depending on whether the search and evaluation of alternatives proceeds within or across attributes [48, 54]. With attribute-based processing, a single attribute of several alternatives is processed before information about a second attribute is processed. In alternativebased processing, multiple attributes of a single alternative are considered before information about a second alternative is processed.

During a decision-making process, a consumer may switch between attributebased and alternative-based processing from time to time [62]. For example, a consumer presented with a product comparison matrix might use alternative-based processing at the beginning of the decision process for product exploration, switch to attribute-based processing in the middle to eliminate some products, and eventually use alternative-based processing at the end to finalize the preference formation and justify the decision. The switch between these two processes could also be influenced by the user’s goal (accuracy maximization or effort minimization) [62], and the product presentation formats (vertical or horizontal) [4].

In our study context, RAs are likely to induce attribute-based processing because a user has to think about his or her product attribute preferences and indicate them one by one to the RA to receive personalized recommendations. On the other hand, experts and consumers directly recommending product alternatives are more likely to facilitate alternative-based processing [39]. Likewise, during second-stage RI functionality, attribute-based RI is expected to enable attribute-based processing while alternative-based RI is expected to enable alternative-based processing.

## Hypotheses Development

Effect of RI Functionality on Perceived Decision Quality (in the Presence of Nonpersonalized Recommendations)

When nonpersonalized recommendations (e.g., experts and consumers) are presented at the first stage of a product choice evaluation process, users will see a list of recommended product alternatives. Given that product advice is not personalized for each specific user, a user may not necessarily wish to choose the product alternatives recommended. Based on the theory of preferential choice, users will likely have a desire to indicate their specific preferences regarding product attributes to choose a product that best fits their preferences.

The second-stage attribute-based RI functionality allows users to express preferences so that the recommendations reflect their overall desires [33], which is expected to lead to higher perceived decision quality. Specifically, it reduces the amount of information (i.e., poorly matched products) users have to process. This in turn frees up human information-processing capabilities [82], thus also having a positive impact on perceived decision quality. In addition, as the attribute-based RI functionality provides users with a set of recommendations that are highly matched to their preferences, users can focus on a smaller number of high-quality recommended products [33] and thus be less concerned about the chance of overlooking unexamined high-quality ones.

In contrast, the second-stage alternative-based RI functionality in the presence of nonpersonalized recommendations may not necessarily improve users’ decision quality to the degree enabled by attribute-based RI. Certainly, we acknowledge that alternative-based RI functionality can improve perceived decision quality as compared to the absence of any RI functionality, because it provides users more possibly similar alternatives to a recommended product that users already have an interest in. However, alternative-based RI functionality does not provide users the same degree of precision to express their preferences for specific product attributes [33]. Instead, it enables users to express their preference for one recommended product over others in the recommendation set. However, each of the product alternatives recommended by nonpersonalized recommendations (experts or consumers) is a bundling of various attributes that may or may not be relevant to the user. The lack of specificity of user attribute preferences with alternative-based RI functionality is inherently ambiguous with respect to the user’s intent because it has only limited capacity for guiding the recommendation process [45]. For example, a product with five attributes, each possessing four values, can lead to 1,024 different potential product versions. Someone using an alternative-based RI functionality can only indicate his or her preference through one of the few recommended product alternatives, which is less precise toward fulfilling a user’s specific preferences. As these alternatives do not necessarily match all of a user’s attribute preferences, using an alternative-based RI functionality is less likely to return product alternatives that closely match the user’s true preferences. Furthermore, although users can formulate their attribute preferences by comparing the alternatives,<sup>6</sup> they might still wonder if there are other possible attribute values that they have not been exposed to in the recommended product alternatives.

Compared to the alternative-based RI functionality, the integrated RI functionality enables users to additionally express their preferences on each specific product attribute. For example, users may state that “Overall, I like product B, but can you show me some products similar to it with a price between \$200 and \$400?” In fact, at one extreme, users can use the integrated RI functionality in a fashion similar to the attribute-based RI by anchoring on any one product and then requiring the RI to show products similar to it with a price between “\$200 and \$400,” a hard drive capacity between “100GB and 200GB,” screen size “10–12 inches,” and so on. Thus, a user’s use of integrated RI functionality will be similar to that of attributebased RI.

To summarize, as compared to the alternative-based RI functionality, an attributebased RI or integrated RI allows users to indicate their product attribute preference (s) more precisely, thereby fulfilling their desire to express their preferences, as postulated by the theory of preferential choice. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: When the first-stage product recommendations are from nonpersonalized sources (consumers or experts), an attribute-based RI (H1a) or an integrated RI functionality (H1b) will lead to higher perceived decision quality than an alternative-based RI functionality.

## Effect of RI Functionality on Perceived Decision Quality (in the Presence of Personalized Recommendations)

The theory of preferential choice indicates that users have a desire to express what they want. If the value of a product attribute (e.g., the screen size of a laptop) deviates from their ideal preference, this product will be less desirable than one having the ideal value, assuming the values of other attributes remain constant. RAs offer personalized recommendations because they have a preference elicitation function that asks for user preferences on product attributes and the importance (weight) a user puts on each product attribute. Then the RA screens a large number of alternatives, narrows down the consideration set, and provides a list of products that matches each user’s attribute preferences [64].

Nevertheless, not all products recommended by an RA with compensatory strategies can exactly match users’ needs for two reasons. First, with compensatory strategies, all product attributes simultaneously contribute to the calculation of a preference value [77]. Thus, products with the same aggregated preference value may have different product attribute values. For example, one laptop with a high price and high-speed processor and another laptop with a medium price and moderate-speed processor may have the same preference value to a user, and both could be recommended by an RA. Second, while noncompensatory strategies, in theory, might recommend products that exactly match users’ needs, it is not always the case due to the user’s preference for product attributes with conflicting values (e.g., many advanced product features vs. low price) and weights assigned [82]. For example, if a user indicates a preference for a laptop with a very low price (e.g., \$120) and a very powerful 4.0 GHz processor speed, then obviously an RA will not be able to recommend a product with a perfect match, and may instead end up offering unmatched products. For these two reasons, not all preference-matching products returned by the RA may match users’ precise needs.

However, in contrast to the nonpersonalized product recommendations offered by consumers and experts, the RA recommends a plausibly acceptable consideration set to users [33, 74]. Under this circumstance, a second-stage alternative-based RI functionality (e.g., “Show me more products like product B”) or integrated RI (e.g., “Show me more products like product B, with optional update on one or more attributes on product B”) can direct the RA to return another set of product alternatives that are similar to the one a user identifies.

Specifically, the initial products recommended by an RA help users find alternatives close to their preferences, and the alternative-based RI or integrated RI functionality complements and fine-tunes those initial recommendations. That is, the RA guides the users to indicate their preferences regarding product attributes (attribute-based processing) and then recommends product alternatives. The personalized alternatives present products as bundled attributes, leading users to perform alternative-based processing [39]. This processing is even likely with the presence of the alternative-based or integrated RI. Users might come to realize the availability of certain combinations of product attributes that are not clearly accessible to them earlier when indicating their product attribute preferences to the RA. The attributebased processing and alternative-based processing complement each other and result in higher perceived decision quality. An additional explanation is that these two types of complementary processing strategies give users a better sense of control. Prior research has found that people tend to perceive their decision performance more positively if they have control over their decision-making process [13]. Thus, even if an RA cannot recommend a better product with the alternative-based RI functionality, users might still perceive that they make a better decision with more similar product alternatives to consider.

When the first-stage product recommendations are from RAs, attribute-based RI functionality in the second stage is less likely to further improve perceived decision quality for two reasons. First, the attribute-based RI does not offer users any greater capability beyond the RA’s initial preference elicitation, because they both enable users to indicate their preferences on product attributes. As noted earlier, comparing two complementary processing types, using a single processing type (attributedbased processing in this case) gives users a limited level of control. Second, using the attribute-based RI functionality would only provide the user the ability to change a preferential value for one or more attribute(s) that the user had already indicated to the RA at the first stage. According to the theory of preferential choice, most users have preferred values for product attributes, and they might not want to significantly deviate from these values.

Based on the preceding arguments that alternative-based RI or integrated RI functionality can assist users in further refining the preference-matching products returned by the RA, and the limited additive function of an attribute-based RI functionality to an RA’s initial preference elicitation function, we propose:

Hypothesis 2: When the first-stage product recommendations are from personalized sources (RAs), an alternative-based RI (H2a) or an integrated RI (H2b) functionality will lead to higher perceived decision quality than an attributebased RI functionality.

Effect of RI Functionality on Perceived Decision Effort (in the Presence of Nonpersonalized Recommendations)

In this subsection, we predict the complementary effects of initial recommendation and later RI functionality on perceived decision effort, the other central variable in the effort–accuracy framework. As noted earlier, users are more likely to use alternative-based processing when they are presented with nonpersonalized recommendations as compared to personalized ones from an RA. With alternative-based processing, users are more likely to consider multiple attributes of a single recommended alternative before they consider a second alternative [48, 54]. Alternativebased RI functionality gives users the option to indicate a preferred product alternative over the others, as well as submit a request to “show me more products similar to that.” To use an alternative-based RI functionality, a user needs to form an overall impression about each product alternative to indicate a preference for one product in the recommended set over others. Thus, the decision-processing strategy used to evaluate either expert or consumer recommendations is likely to be the same as with the subsequent use of an alternative-based RI functionality.

In contrast, first evaluating consumers’ or experts’ nonpersonalized recommendations (vs. RA’s recommendations) and then using an attribute-based RI functionality likely requires users to switch their decision process from alternativebased processing to attribute-based processing. The processing switch is expected to result in higher perceived decision effort. This can be explained by the concept of processing fluency, which is defined as the ease with which people identify a stimulus and process its meaning [38]. A user’s subjective familiarity with a stimulus can lead to processing fluency [60]. As a user’s eyes jump from a vertical orientation (e.g., two attributes for the same product) to a horizontal orientation (e.g., two products for the same attribute), the processing fluency will be disrupted. Furthermore, the resulting disfluency will lead to greater cognitive elaboration and more effortful processing [63]. In other words, it is the degree of elaboration (from switching the information-processing strategy) that determines the perceived decision effort. Prior research also revealed that switching information-acquisition strategies lengthens decision time [62]. Thus, we believe that a user who switches information–processing strategies will spend more mental effort than one who uses a single processing strategy from beginning to end.

This is also consistent with the gist of the effort–accuracy framework, positing that trade-off exists between the accuracy of the decision and the effort required to make the decision: that is, more accurate decisions come at the expense of more effort [54]. Since perceived decision quality is higher with a combination of two different decision-processing strategies, the perceived decision effort will be higher. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 3: When the first-stage product recommendations are from nonpersonalized sources (consumers or experts), an attribute-based RI functionality will lead to higher perceived decision effort as compared to alternative-based RI (H3a) or integrated RI (H3b) functionality.

Effect of RI Functionality on Perceived Decision Effort (in the Presence of Personalized Recommendations)

Using reasoning similar to that employed above, when the first-stage recommendations are personalized (by using an RA that elicits users’ preferred attributes of a product before providing product advice), using an attributebased RI functionality in the second stage will consume less decision effort because a user can use the same attribute-based information-processing strategy for both first-stage and second-stage recommendations. However, the use of alternative-based RI functionality or integrated RI functionality in the second stage will demand a change of processing strategies (i.e., from attribute-based processing at the first stage to alterative-based processing at the second stage), and will, therefore, require more decision effort. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 4: When the first-stage product recommendations are from personalized sources (RAs), an attribute-based RI functionality will lead to lower perceived decision effort than will an alternative-based RI (H4a) or an integrated RI (H4b) functionality.

## Methodology

The hypotheses proposed were tested through an online experiment with a $3 ~ \times$ 4 between-subject design. The first factor was the three types of personalized and nonpersonalized initial recommendations (i.e., RA, consumers, and experts). The second factor was the four types of RI functionality (i.e., no RI, alternative-based RI, attribute-based RI, and integrated RI). Accordingly, twelve simulated shopping websites were constructed. According to power analysis for between-subject design, 192 subjects can assure a sufficient statistical power of 0.8 for a medium effect size (f = .25) [11].

## Procedures, Tasks, Subjects, and Incentives

The task given to the subjects was to select a product from one of 12 shopping websites specifically designed for this study. Before the subjects were assigned to one of the 12 shopping websites, each subject went through a baseline website, which served as a benchmark for evaluating particular websites (treatments) based on the adaptation theory [24]. This theory suggests that people’s judgments are based on three criteria: (1) the sum of their past experiences; (2) the context and background of a particular experience, and (3) a stimulus. Keeping this in mind, we randomly assigned subjects to different treatment conditions to ensure that the subjects’ past experiences were averaged out and thus homogeneous across conditions. In addition, by having a common benchmark for all subjects, we could be more confident that the context and background of their experimental experiences were equivalent, and that any disparities across different conditions were caused only by different treatment stimuli. This approach of using a common benchmark is consistent with prior studies [30, 31, 33, 81].

The baseline website was designed with products recommended by a store without any RI functionality. The store recommended five products in the form of a comparison matrix, which provided a convenient summary of the product information, with rows displaying product attributes and columns displaying product models. Subjects could also see the rest of the 83 products in the store,<sup>7</sup> which were displayed 5 products at a time on the subsequent pages. In the first task, using the baseline website, subjects were required to shop for a laptop computer for a family member. In the second task, using one of the experimental websites, subjects were required to buy a laptop for themselves. After finishing the two shopping tasks, the subjects were asked to judge the experimental site, using the baseline website as a benchmark.<sup>8</sup>

E-commerce shoppers were recruited from a North American panel and accessed via a marketing research firm. Individuals were provided with a point-based incentive (redeemable for various prizes available through the marketing firm) for their assistance in the study. In addition, they were told that serious and honest participants would be awarded a \$15 Visa Gift Card, the value of which is more than three times the value of the points subjects received. The criteria used for rewarding the \$15 Visa Gift Card were how logical and convincing their answers were to the survey questions, including their justification of their product choices. The participants were further told, “There are no right or wrong answers here; we are just interested in getting an honest description of your perception.” Previous research (e.g., [33]) has indicated that the incentive is important, as it serves to motivate subjects to view the experiment as a serious online experience session and to increase their involvement.

## Experimental Website Design

As with the baseline website, each of the 12 experimental websites featured the same 88 laptop computers, which were available from leading online commercial websites at the time of the study. Based on these commercial websites, 8 key product attributes (price, hard drive, processor, memory, screen size, video card, weight, and battery) were provided for each product. The rationale behind using 8 product attributes was that providing 10 or more tends to significantly decrease the human ability to cognitively process alternatives [44]; the general rule of thumb is that the span of immediate memory is about 7 plus or minus 2 items [47].

## Manipulation of First-Stage Personalized vs. Nonpersonalized Recommendations

Users having access to personalized recommendations via an RA in the first stage needed to indicate their preferred product attributes and importance of each preferred attribute before they could get the top-five product recommendations from the RA. For example, a subject may have specified that the desired hard drive attribute should fall into the range of “401GB to 500GB,” the price should fall into the range of “\$400 to \$600,” and finally, how important these requirements are to him or her (see Appendix Figure A1).The RA then ranked the products based on the user’s indicated preferences and presented the top five recommendations that best matched the user’s preferences on the first page of the recommendations. Subjects could also see the ranking of the rest of the 83 products; these were displayed five products at a time on the subsequent pages in decreasing levels of the match. Since users had the option of reviewing on their own all the product alternatives available in the comparison matrix, they could voluntarily decide whether or not to follow the RA’s advice.

Users in the nonpersonalized recommendation treatment groups—that is, having access to experts’ and consumers’ advice in the first stage—were presented with five product recommendations on the website (see Appendix Figure A2 for the expert condition). These five products were identical in the expert and consumer conditions, and were carefully selected to make sure that they were both popular products in the market (i.e., based on high consumer rating in CNET) and recommended by computer experts (i.e., based on the CNET editors’ recommendations). Users were informed that these alternatives were based on consumers’ or experts’ opinions. This way of manipulating the consumer and expert sources eliminated the confounding effect between recommended products and recommendation sources.<sup>9</sup> Detailed treatments are presented in Table 1. Similar to those in the RA treatment groups, subjects here could also see the rest of the 83 products and could voluntarily decide whether or not to follow the source’s advice.

## Manipulation of Second-Stage RI Functionality

In each of the RI conditions, subjects could use the RI functionality to indicate or update their product preferences. Examples of the three different types of RI functionality are shown in Table 2 and Appendix Figures A3, A4, and A5. In terms of the specific decision strategy supported by the RI functionality, both attribute-based RI and alternative-based RI used an equal weight (EQW) decision strategy.<sup>10</sup> For attribute-based RI functionality, it is based on the value of the product attributes indicated (see Appendix Figure A3). For alternative-based RI, it is based on the attribute values of the preferred alternative indicated (see Appendix Figure A4).

## Construct Measurement

The measures for all constructs were adopted from scales validated in prior studies. The measurements of perceived decision quality were based on Xiao and Benbasat’s [77] comprehensive review summary;<sup>11</sup> their measures have been widely used in prior studies (e.g., [23, 27, 50, 65, 71). The measurements for perceived decision effort were faithfully taken from Lilien et al. [42] and Kayande et al. [36],<sup>12</sup> whose studies examined the effect of decision support systems. Product knowledge [17] and task involvement [46] were used as control variables. Based on the adaptation theory [24] discussed earlier, values of the dependent variables are comparative values based on a common reference point—that is, the baseline website in the first shopping task. Perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort were measured using a Likert scale ranging from the baseline website (1) to the experimental website (11). The neutral point (6) indicates that the subject perceives that the second shopping website does not differ from the first one, and 11 points indicate the subject believes the measurement items reflects his or her experience in the experimental website much more strongly than the baseline one.

## Data Analysis

## Subject Background Information

Participants in this study were 197 e-commerce shoppers. Females formed 64.3 percent of the participants. The mean age of the participants was 38.8 years, with 30.2 percent between 18 and 30 years old, 22.4 percent between 31 and 40, and the remaining participants (47.4 percent) between 41 and 50. Of the participants, 42 percent had a bachelor degree or higher, 26 percent had a two-year college degree, and the rest, a high school diploma or lower.

Subjects had been using the Internet for 12.8 years on average, spending over 29 hours on the Internet each week. Over 79 percent of the participants used the Internet for at least 15 hours each week. The level of participants’ familiarity with online shopping was 6.26/7.0. The average reported knowledge level of the product used in the task—laptop computers—was 5.02/7.0. Subjects’ task involvement was 5.65/7.0 on average,<sup>13</sup> indicating that subjects were serious about the experimental task. No significant differences were found across the treatment conditions regarding these five factors: Internet usage years, Internet usage hours per week, familiarity with online shopping, product knowledge, and task involvement.

## Construct Reliability and Validity

Construct reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The alphas for perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort are 0.89 and 0.87, respectively. Both met suggested tolerances (> 0.70) [20]. The correlation between perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort is 0.52. To aid in interpreting the results, Table 3 reports an average of the measurement items used in the scale for each dependent variable. To test construct validity, exploratory factor analysis of the perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort was performed with varimax rotation (Table 4). Without exception, all items load highly (loading ≥ 0.70) on their associated factors, confirming the convergent validity of the factors. Two factors emerged with no cross-construct loadings exceeding 0.40. In other words, the loadings of a given construct’s indicators are higher than the loadings of any other. In all cases among the cross loadings, the differences are greater than 0.30. This lends further support to discriminant validity.

## Results Regarding Initial Recommendations and RI Functionality

As a manipulation check, we asked whether subjects were aware of the recommendation sources available (e.g., experts, consumers, RAs) in the study. Computer logs were checked to determine whether the subjects used the RI functionality. Two subjects who did not notice the recommendation source and three others who did not use their respective RI functionality were excluded from the data analysis, resulting in the final sample size of 192. Thus, the RI was effective in attracting participant’s attention and utilization. Results of analysis of variance (ANOVA) show that the treatment for the first-stage personalized recommendation was successful.<sup>14</sup> Participants perceived that the RA is more personalized than are the experts (mean difference = 1.21, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ or consumers (mean difference = 1.32, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , but there was no difference found between experts’ and consumers’ recommendations (mean difference $= 0 . 1 1$ $p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . In addition, we found no significant differences 2 between these two treatments (experts vs. consumers) in terms of perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort. The results were robust regardless of the types of RI functionality provided in the consumer and expert treatments. Thus, we aggregated the consumer and expert treatments into one condition (nonpersonalized). Results of independent t-tests show that the treatment for RI functionality was also successful. Subjects in the RI functionality conditions perceived that the website was more effective in gathering their feedback to provide better recommendations as compared to those in the control condition (mean difference = 1.03, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Furthermore, we asked subjects whether the website allowed them to specify the attribute preference. Subjects in the alternative-based RI functionality condition reported a lower mean as compared to those in the integrated (mean difference = –1.1, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and attribute-based RI functionality conditions (mean difference = –0.60, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5$ , one-tailed), and no difference was found between the attribute-based and integrated RI functionality (mean difference = 0.51, $p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . Alternative-based RI does not allow users to indicate specific attribute preference, yet both attribute-based and integrated RI do. Thus the manipulation check of three types of RI functionality was successful.

To check whether subjects in the RA treatment are more likely to use attributebased processing and those in expert or consumer treatments are more likely to use the alternative-based processing, we asked subjects what types of decision-processing strategies they adopted.<sup>15</sup> The findings for the control group (no RI functionality) showed that RA treatment subjects used attribute-based processing more than consumer and expert treatment subjects $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . In addition, subjects in the RAs with attribute-based RI functionality treatment used attribute-based processing more than subjects in the expert and consumer treatment with alternative-based RI functionality $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , consistent with what was discussed in the Decision-Processing Strategies section.

Perceived Decision Quality. There were significant effects both of RI functionality and the interaction between the initial (i.e., stage 1) recommendations and later (i.e., stage 2) RI functionality on perceived decision quality (see Table 5). Since a significant main effect of RI functionality was found, we further compared participants assigned to conditions with RI functionalities and those without. The results suggest that the former perceived a higher decision quality than those assigned to the control condition (no RI functionality), with the mean difference of 0.69 $( p = 0 . 0 2 )$ demonstrating the effectiveness of the RI functionalities. The significant interaction effect suggests that the effects of RI functionality were moderated by the initial recommendations being personalized vs. nonpersonalized (Figure 1); therefore, it was investigated in more detail [22, 29] as shown in Table 6. For users with personalized first-stage recommendations (RA), both alternative-based $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and integrated RI functionality $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ led to significantly higher perceived decision quality as compared to attribute-based RI, supporting H2a and H2b.

Unlike the findings for perceived decision quality, the ANCOVA results suggest insignificant interaction effects $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ between first-stage sources (personalized vs. nonpersonalized sources) and three types of RI functionalities in terms of recommendation acceptance. The discrepancy between the actual behavior of recommendation acceptance and the perception of decision quality can be explained.

Recall that one of the superior effects of RI functionality is that users are exposed to more selection of attribute values and are accordingly less concerned about the possibility of overlooking unexamined high-quality ones. The possibility to double check whether there are better products or not with the assistance of RI can boost users’ perception of decision quality about the product they chose, be it from the RI’s top five recommendations or not. An independent t-test for participants using RIs reveals no significant difference (mean difference $= 0 . 1 8 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ of perceived decision quality between participants who followed RI’s top five recommendations $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 8 . 1 0 )$ and those who did not $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 7 . 9 2 )$ , supporting our theorization. This explanation is also consistent with prior research showing that people tend to perceive their decision performance more positively if they have control over their decision-making process [13]. Given the different effects of RI functionality on recommendation acceptance and perceived decision quality, we believe that its effect on perceived decision quality matters more as perceived quality of decision influences many aspects of behavior such as planning subsequent decisions, communicating the decisions to others [84], and devoting resources to pursue an outcome [35].

We also investigate other behavioral data including the number of product pages viewed and the decision time. Since each product page displayed five products, the 88 products employed in the experiment were covered in 18 pages. Users in the RI functionality conditions (mean = 2.61 pages) viewed fewer product pages than those in the no RI functionality condition (mean = 7.91 pages), with a mean difference of 5.3 pages $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Consistent with these findings, we found that participants with RI functionality spent less time (mean = 3.85 minutes) than those assigned to the control condition (no RI functionality, mean = 5.95 minutes) $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , demonstrating the effectiveness of the RI functionality in reducing decision time. Taken together, although users with RI functionality viewed fewer pages and spent less time, they did not perceive less decision effort spent (no main effect in perceived decision effort as shown in Table 7). One possibility is that while RI users saved time in evaluating product recommendations because of the better matching products recommended by the RI, they also spent greater effort in using RI functionality and deploying information-processing strategy, which is more cognitively demanding. Thus, the effort saved through fewer product pages viewed was offset with the effort spent in using RI functionality.

After analyzing the main effect of RI functionality on product pages viewed and decision time, we ran a MANOVA test for these two dependent variables (pages viewed and decision time) among the three types of RI functionality and the two recommendation sources (personalized vs. nonpersonalized). No significant difference exists between the RI functionality types and the two sources $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . The only significant main effect is that users in the personalized source condition (i.e., RA users) spent more time than those in the nonpersonalized source condition (mean difference = 1.78 minutes, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , probably due to the additional preference elicitation process users need to go through to receive the personalized recommendations.

## Supplementary Analysis of the Integrated RI Functionality

As noted earlier, for the subjects in the personalized treatment (RA), the use of second-stage integrated RI functionality led them to perceive higher decision quality than the attribute-based RI functionality and control condition. And for subjects in the nonpersonalized treatments (consumer or expert), the integrated RI functionality led them to perceive higher decision quality as compared to the second-stage alternative-based RI and control conditions. In using integrated RI functionality, we found that 67 percent of the subjects used the optional attribute preference update feature, with 81 percent in the nonpersonalized treatments (consumer or expert) and 47 percent in the personalized treatment (RA). Furthermore, we found that subjects in the personalized treatment (RA) made an average of 1.1 attribute requests (i.e., “but change” function, see Appendix Figure A5) when they indicated their preferences on one alternative (i.e., show products similar to this model), but users in the nonpersonalized treatments (consumer or expert) conditions made an average of 2.3 attribute requests. This indicates that how users used the integrated RI functionality in the second stage differed depending on their first-stage recommendation source. Users used integrated RI functionality in a fashion more like an alternative-based RI when the initial recommendations were from personalized first-stage sources (RAs), but used it in a manner more similar to an attribute-based RI when the initial recommendations were from nonpersonalized sources (consumers or experts). This adds further support to the Complementarity Principle by showing that users, when they had a choice of what RI functionality to employ, behaved in a way that is consistent with this principle.

## Discussion

The results convey two key messages. First, it is important to go beyond the firststage recommendations to study the second-stage RI functionality as certain types of RI functionality can further improve perceived decision quality. Second, the integrated RI functionality appears to be the best RI overall, regardless of the types of initial recommendations.

RAs assist users in performing the tedious and process-intensive work of screening and sorting products based on users’ expressed preferences, and offer users product recommendations that match their preferences. Under this condition, alternativebased RI and integrated RI functionality are more desirable than attribute-based RI in helping users explore other product alternatives and find new products that still stay close to their initial preferences. With alternative-based RI functionality, product attribute preferences that are not directly accessible to a user might be discovered through the presentation of product alternatives as bundled attributes. Thus, alternative-based RI functionality helps users to clarify their implicit attribute preferences, explore more product alternatives, and reach better-perceived decision quality. While attribute-based RI functionality allows users to update their preference, users are likely to find it less useful, as they may not want to deviate significantly from their originally indicated preferences to the RA.

On the other hand, with consumer or expert recommendations, users were not exposed to a personalized set of products. Under this condition, attribute-based and integrated RI functionality are more appropriate than alternative-based RI in helping users express their precise preference on product attributes. In our operationalization of attribute-based RI functionality, the laptop with eight product attributes of four values each can have 65,536 different product versions. Users of the attribute-based RI functionality could indicate one version of the 65,536 different preference combinations, while those using an alternative-based RI could only indicate their preference through one of the five recommended products. Thus, attribute-based RI functionality provides a better opportunity (65,536 vs. 5) for users to more precisely indicate their attribute preference. On the other hand, alternative-based RI functionality prevents users from providing an exact preferred value for an attribute. Particularly when none of the initial recommended products are preferred by a user, alternative-based RI functionality does not assist users to identify a good fit.

The results related to perceived decision effort are aligned with the findings for perceived decision quality. As hypothesized, better-perceived decision quality is a result of the adoption of two different but complementary types of decision-processing strategies (attribute-based and alternative-based). The higher associated perceived decision effort also reflects users’ use of these two different decisionprocessing modes in their decision-making task. The results are consistent with the effort–accuracy framework, maintaining that effort and accuracy are an inherent trade-off in the consumer’s decision-making process [54].

Users of integrated RI functionality need to contemplate both the preferred product alternatives and the preferred attributes. However, they did not perceive the usage of integrated RI functionality as requiring significantly more effort. As analyzed earlier, the way a user uses integrated RI functionality is contingent on the first-stage recommendation source: when an RA is the source, integrated RI functionality is used more like an alternative-based RI; when expert or consumers are the sources, integrated RI is used more like an attribute-based RI. This reveals that users prefer to use two types of decision–processing strategies to obtain better decision quality, lending further support to the complementarity principle.

## Contributions and Conclusions

## Theoretical Contributions

There are three major theoretical contributions from this study. First, we proposed a two-stage model of generating product advice by including both first-stage recommendations and second-stage RI functionality. Past research focused on first-stage recommendations and ignored the second stage [25, 33, 34, 74, 82]. That is why in the extensive review of RA research done by Xiao and Benbasat [77, 78], RI functionality was missing in their comprehensive model. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the efficacy of second-stage RI functionality combined with first-stage personalized vs. nonpersonalized recommendations. In proposing this two-stage model, we also advance RA literature by demonstrating that the incorporation of alternative-based RI and integrated RI functionality will lead to better-perceived decision quality as compared to the use of an RA alone. Furthermore, this study will help researchers better understand individuals’ online decision-making behaviors and their use and adoption of RI functionality.

Second, we contribute to the decision-making literature by proposing and testing the complementarity principle: the effectiveness of different types of RI functionality in the second stage of product advice is contingent on the various recommendation sources in the first stage. By incorporating multiple types of RI functionality and multiple types of initial recommendations, we not only contribute to the literature by adding the second stage of generating product advice, but also examine which RI functionality at the second stage is better, and under which condition (i.e., which type of first-stage recommendations). By doing so, we enrich the RA literature by revealing that alternative-based and integrated RI functionality with an RA are superior to attribute-based RI functionality with an RA in achieving better-perceived decision quality. In contrast, attribute-based RI or integrated RI should be combined with consumer and expert recommendations to obtain higher decision quality.

Third, we contribute to the research on first-stage product recommendations. While recent studies have investigated various types of first-stage recommendations, comparisons between human and technological sources have rarely been made. For example, Wang and Doong [72] and Chen [9] investigated experts and consumer recommendations only at the first stage. Although Senecal and Nantel [61] compared RAs to consumers and experts, they did not evaluate these sources’ differences regarding perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort, two central variables in the effort–accuracy framework. In addition, the RAs they adopted in the experiment were not truly interactive online RAs. Instead, subjects were told that the computer system analyzed their answers to the questionnaire that they completed a few days ago, and recommends this product for them. Given that using an online RA is becoming more popular in current e-commerce environments, there was a surprising lack of empirical research investigating the differences among online RAs, human experts, and the “wisdom of crowds.” This study rectifies this knowledge gap by putting together the three types of initial recommendations in one comparison set.

## Practical Contributions

This study also has implications for practitioners. The results of the study suggest that RI functionality can improve users’ perceived decision quality when it appropriately complements the first-stage recommendations. Accordingly, e-commerce designers can provide different types of RI functionality to facilitate online consumers’ decision making based on the complementarity principle revealed in this study.

For RA designers, our results show that while the traditional attribute-based RI functionality does not produce the best results, alternative-based RI or integrated RI will improve users’ perceived decision quality. Thus, when designing an RA interface, practitioners should select alternative-based or integrated RI functionality to augment the capabilities of an RA.

Website retailers that have consumer or expert recommendations in place but do not have RAs should note that consumer or expert recommendations with attributebased RI or integrated RI functionality eventually lead to the same level of decision quality as the RA usage. In particular, users do not need to go through the initial preference elicitation process and indicate the importance weights for products attributes. Thus, as a “short cut” to compete with other merchants with RAs, these website retailers are advised to augment first-stage consumer or expert recommendations with the attribute-based RI or integrated RI functionality to provide users with better matching products.

Our study finds that integrated RI functionality leads to higher perceived decision quality, regardless of the first-stage recommendations provided (personalized or nonpersonalized). This is because users use the integrated RI in different ways depending on the personalization extent of the first-stage recommendations. When the initial recommendations are from consumers or experts, users use more of the integrated RI’s attribute functionality. When the initial recommendations are RA derived, then they use more of the integrated RI’s alternative functionality. Thus, e-commerce designers are advised to provide integrated RI to facilitate online consumers’ decision making.

Experts are expensive, scarce, in high demand and not always available [7]. Thus, the cost to get experts’ product recommendations is probably higher than those of consumers and RAs. Therefore, we do not suggest that websites appoint experts themselves or train their staff to act as experts. Self-appointed experts may be perceived to have an affiliation with those websites and be biased [9]. Instead, websites should leverage experts’ opinions from other publicly available and reputable channels such as CNET and Consumer Reports as endorsements for their products. However, given our results, expert recommendations are not always essential. They can be replaced with recommendations by consumers and RAs combined with the right RI functionality.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with all studies, there are limitations and related opportunities for future research. First, although our experiment’s participants were mostly adults (average age 38.8 years) and therefore more generalizable than typical student subjects, readers should exercise caution in generalizing the results of this study to other demographic groups and those outside of North America. Second, we only investigated three types of RI functionality in the current study. Future research should investigate the effects of other types of RI functionality, such as ratings-based RIs that allow users to rate the initial product recommendations instead of just picking one recommended product as their preferred alternative. Third, the laptop product examined in this study is Microsoft Windows–based and generally considered to be a high involvement product, since it is costly and has multiple product features. Subsequent studies should consider other product attributes (e.g., Mac) and product types (such as low involvement or experiential products) to generalize our findings. In addition, we did not consider participants’ real budget in purchasing a laptop, and we call for future research to replicate the study in a field setting where participants have to spend their own money in purchasing a product. Another possible stream of research is to use our study as a starting point to evaluate whether the results will be changed by using other outcome evaluations such as perceived enjoyment.

## Conclusions

Given a large number of products on e-commerce websites and the large population of users searching and comparing products online [57], online firms are increasingly using decision aids to assist online shoppers in arriving at informed decisions. By examining consumers’ perceptions of decision quality and decision effort toward the use of different types of first-stage recommendations and second-stage online RI functionality, we contribute to theoretical advances in consumer decision making in e-commerce. Notably, our research fills the gap in the recommendation systems literature by highlighting the complementary principle between the first- and second-stage product recommendations. In addition, we fill a void in the literature by investigating why the complementary relationship occurs for both perceived decision quality and perceived decision effort; better decision quality and greater decision effort go hand in hand, and both are due to users using two different types of decision processing (attribute-based and alternative-based). In doing so, we advance the theory of preferential choice in the context of online decision making supported by first-stage recommendations and second-stage RI functionality. And based on our findings, we offer guidelines for providing better online decision support services for consumers.
