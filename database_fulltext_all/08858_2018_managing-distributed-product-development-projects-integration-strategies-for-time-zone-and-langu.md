# Managing Distributed Product Development Projects: Integration Strategies for Time-Zone and Language Barriers

Edward G. Anderson Jr.,<sup>a</sup> Aravind Chandrasekaran,<sup>b</sup> Alison Davis-Blake,<sup>c</sup> Geofrey G. Parker<sup>d</sup>

Abstract. Distributed product development projects encompass product and process development activities that span organizational and country boundaries. The increasing trend toward globalizing projects requires firms to coordinate development eforts made by team members from various functions within the firm, speaking multiple languages, and working in various time zones. We analyze qualitative data from 70 distributed product development projects that span 14 countries and involve cross-functional team members speaking 10 diferent languages. We find that commonly discussed integration strategies such as modular product designs and colocating team members by themselves are insuficient to coordinate project work. Rather, our field interviews suggest that firms invest in design information systems (DIS) with specific features to facilitate product design and empower their project managers to integrate the development eforts. Specifically, our interviews suggest that firms often modify the organization by “unifying” the engineering and purchasing functions into a single supply chain integrator function to increase the scope of responsibilities for these managers. We then test our hypotheses on the benefits of these strategies on project outcomes by using survey data from 55 distributed product development projects in 20 firms. Results indicate that the use of DIS is associated with higher quality and relationship performance when there are diferences between focal and supplier firm personnel languages. Unifying engineering and purchasing functions into a supply chain integrator is associated with improved response time in the presence of time zone diferences. We also find that a unifying strategy is associated with lower cost in the presence of language diferences, but is also associated with a worsening of response time. These results provide guidance to product designers in organizations that must coordinate complex work across time zone barriers and languages. The results also provide guidance to researchers, by showing that diferent integration mechanisms may have diferential efects across various coordination barriers and across multiple dimensions of project performance. We conclude by linking these results to integration mechanisms previously discussed in the coordination literature.

## 1. Introduction

For decades, firms as diverse as Apple, Cisco, Frito-Lay, IBM, 3M, General Electric, Lockheed Martin, Microsoft, and General Motors have outsourced many of the activities they once performed in-house to networks of specialist suppliers all around the world (Tambe and Hitt 2010, Sahay et al. 2003, Winter and Taylor 1996). After some initial successes in the 1990s, these firms and many like them continue to review their product portfolios for opportunities to outsource more complex product development activities. This “distributed product development” approach encompasses product and process development project activities that span across organizational boundaries and require coordination of activities between organizations on a frequent basis (Hinds and Kielser 2002). Unlike repetitive outsourced manufacturing or business process outsourcing work (Mani et al. 2012), distributed product development projects involve high levels of task interdependencies (Eppinger and Chitkara 2006, Sosa et al. 2002). They also depend greatly on tacit knowledge that is notoriously dificult to manage when involving personnel from different functions within the organization (Grant 1996, von Hippel 1994).

For instance, consider the following example of a project from an electronics equipment manufacturing firm in our study. This focal firm decided to involve a supplier in designing and manufacturing a subcomponent for its oilfield sensor equipment. Specifically, the supplier team, together with members from the firm’s design, manufacturing, and purchasing departments, had to design and develop a computer to run the algorithms necessary to process temperature variations obtained from a new sensor design. The supplier was already known for their reliability and quality and was a strategic manufacturing partner for the focal firm. However, the design assignment involved a high level of product complexity that required tighter coordination between the purchasing and the design members within the supplier team. The supplier’s singleboard computer was supposed to be modular, but this was not properly coordinated during the project activity. The computer was installed, along with other components, into a system chassis (outside case). When the prototype testing began, the single-board computer functioned without any problems. However, during a later stage of the development process, the supplier updated the computer without notifying the focal firm, and this update created unwanted physical interaction between the computer and the rest of the system. The computer’s integral cooling fan was moved to a new position, which was partially blocked by a portion of the overall sensor system’s equipment case developed by the focal firm. This led to overheating and eventual computer failure, resulting in extensive delays in the product launch. Firms such as the one just mentioned engage in distributed development eforts because of potential benefits, such as supplementing in-house technical capabilities and providing access to advanced technical expertise or lowering development costs while improving product quality (Doz and Hamel 1998, Eppinger and Chitkara 2006). However, the actual benefits as seen in the above example have often been lower than anticipated (Michaels 2012) because of coordination barriers, such as difering firm standard operating procedures and firm cultures (Anderson et al. 2007).

In the past decade, the extent of external firm involvement in product development activities has begun to reach across multiple geographic regions, further increasing coordination barriers (Carmel and Espinosa 2011, Bailey et al. 2010). For instance, Amaral et al. (2011) report a case in which an Asian customer requested “monkey-proof” equipment from its American supplier, who in turn developed a product that was color coded and highly user-friendly. Alas, the Asian customer was speaking literally: the equipment was intended for use in the jungle populated with fleshand-blood monkeys that tear and gnaw at monitoring equipment and wiring. This amusing tale illustrates the coordination challenges when engaging in global development eforts with team members from various regions. It also resonates well with the findings from Hinds et al. (2014, p. 544) that suggest that even if nonnative English speakers pursue English language instruction to strengthen their capabilities, their command of English engineering jargon may be weak:

Attempting to communicate technical or business topics precisely in English was especially frustrating. One manager told us that, even if people pursue English language instruction, “they don’t really teach engineering jargon there.”

As the example above illustrates, the practice of coordinating product development eforts is especially difficult when dealing with partners separated by continents, oceans, languages, and functions. Nonetheless, there is an increasing trend toward globalizing product development activities that requires engineers, designers, supply chain personnel, and senior leaders to deal with barriers such as language and time-zone diferences on a daily or even hourly basis (Anderson and Parker 2013, Varonis and Gass 1985, Espinosa et al. 2007, Carmel and Espinosa 2011). The purpose of this research is to investigate the following questions: What are some significant coordination challenges when engaging in development activities with suppliers from all over the world? What integration mechanisms can help firms manage these barriers? Are these integration mechanisms diferentially efective against diferent barriers, thus necessitating firms to develop a suite of integration mechanisms to manage product development projects?

Numerous scholars from various disciplines such as information systems, operations and supply chain management, and strategic management have studied challenges associated with distributed product development projects (Espinosa and Carmel 2003, Eppinger and Chitkara 2006, Srikanth and Puranam 2011, Hinds and Mortensen 2005). Through these studies, we now know much about the efects of organizational (Rai et al. 2009), temporal (Espinosa and Carmel 2003, Levina and Vaast 2005), cultural (Neeley et al. 2012), geographic (Kotha and Srikanth 2013), and functional diferences (Watson-Manheim et al. 2012), and how they have an impact on distributed development activities. They have also proposed solutions such as designing modular work (Baldwin and Clark 1997), colocating team members (Kotha and Srikanth 2013, Kotlarsky et al. 2014), bargaining power (Kotha and Srikanth 2013), and using communication technologies such as video conferencing and shared general-purpose information systems (Liu and Aron 2015, Srikanth and Puranam 2011, Kotha and Srikanth 2013) to overcome the coordination challenges. Despite the use of these coordination mechanisms, problems still persist in the distributed project context as evidenced by the examples above. Hence, we build on the prior literature using a multimethods approach employing both qualitative and survey data to improve our understanding of this topic. use this code:

Table 1 outlines the firms studied and the types of data collected and used in this research. The qualitative interview data was collected from 70 global project teams belonging to 30 firms that span 14 diferent countries to identify and better explain the coordination challenges in these settings. These data were collected as part of two studies funded by the National Science Foundation. We use insights from the interviews and iterate them with extant literature to derive hypotheses on the coordination barriers and strategies used by these organizations. This approach of iterating between extant literature and field evidence is recommended for theory refinement, especially when existing theories are not formulated well enough to allow explicit hypothesis testing (Yin 2003). We then triangulate, by using survey data collected from multiple respondents of 55 project teams to test the developed hypotheses (Venkatesh et al. 2013).

As seen in Table 1, about 90% of these projects involved physical product development activities (e.g., automobile products, medical devices, video game consoles, electronic sensors, scrapbooking tools, consumer food products, etc.), which are the focus of this study. We conducted over 100 semistructured interviews with both the project team members and their managers on various topics such as project background, supply chain relationships, product and process development methods, IT tools, development challenges, and coordination strategies (see Appendix A.1 for a subset of the interview protocol). The qualitative data provide rich anecdotes regarding the coordination challenges when working on these projects. These projects span various industry segments and involve coordination with supplier team personnel who are located all around the world. Our interview responses from project team members suggest that the inability to engage in frequent interactions because of time-zone diferences was a common issue that created delays in obtaining feedback during the development process. In addition, we found that project teams often raised significant concerns when dealing with team members who spoke diferent first languages from themselves. Even in countries like India, where English is spoken well and where project teams were multilingual (which might suggest fewer communication problems; Hong 2010), project teams complained about the inherent biases and collaboration challenges when the team members spoke diferent first languages, which again echoes findings from Hinds et al. (2014) and Kane and Levina (2017).

Evidence from our interviews suggests that firms often invested in both information systems and empowering project managers to manage coordination barriers. Specifically, we found that firms invested in specialized design information systems (DIS) that allowed for task interfacing and coordination during product development eforts. DIS involves technologies that allow teams to synchronize resource allocation and change design decisions across development teams. Furthermore, our interviews indicated that in addition to commonly used strategies such as colocation (Kotha and Srikanth 2013, Snow et al. 1999), some firms employed unique strategies such as unifying the engineering and purchasing functions, to create a single empowered project manager through an increased scope of responsibilities, which is referred to as a “supply chain integrator” (Parker and Anderson 2002), to help manage coordination barriers. We refer to this as a unifying strategy.

We then used survey data collected from 55 distributed development projects using multiple informants to test these findings. We measured project performance on multiple dimensions such as quality, project cost, response time, and working relationships during the course of the project. We found that the two coordination mechanisms were diferentially efective against language and time-zone barriers and on diferent dimensions of project performance. In particular, DIS use was associated with improved quality and relationship performance when there were language diferences between focal and supplier firm personnel. A unifying strategy to merge purchasing and engineering into a supply chain integrator to act as a single point of contact between the focal and supplier personnel was associated with reduced response time when there were time-zone diferences; it also helped reduce cost but increased response time when there was a language diference. Taken together, our results suggest that coordination mechanisms are diferentially efective against various coordination barriers. A practical implication is that there is no single “silver bullet” to manage coordination challenges and managers need to be prepared to face some trade-ofs in their project deliverables. This finding also contributes to the emerging theories on integration strategies on the need for project-specific information systems and empowerment of project managers (e.g., Srikanth and Puranam 2011) as researchers will have to consider not only complementary integration mechanisms to manage distributed projects but also how those play out over multiple dimensions of project performance.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we review the existing literature to describe some of the challenges identified by scholars and how they afect product development work in our sample. We then iterate between existing coordination literature and interview data to derive hypotheses on coordination strategies. Section 3 describes the survey data collection process, which is followed by analyses in Section 4. We use qualitative insights to explain some of our empirical results. Finally, we conclude with the theoretical and managerial implications in Section 5.

## 2. Theory Development

In conjunction with our interview data set, we review the larger literature on coordination to first identify two particularly problematic barriers to efective coordination of distributed product development projects, namely, time-zone and language barriers. We then identify two integration mechanisms used by firms to bridge these barriers that emerged during our interviews: (1) DIS tailored to facilitate communication during product development projects, and (2) empowering project managers to serve as supply chain integrators. We position these two mechanisms in the literature and, in particular, use them to extend Srikanth and Puranam’s (2011) model of integrating distributed work. Finally, we iterate between the literature and our interviews to propose hypotheses about the mechanisms’ efectiveness in bridging time-zone and language barriers.

## 2.1. Coordination Barriers to Distributed Product Development Projects

During the past two decades, researchers have observed that, increasingly, firms are outsourcing to their suppliers more complex knowledge work that includes engineering design and the development of new products and processes (Brusoni et al. 2001, Parker and Anderson 2002, Sahay et al. 2003, Eppinger and Chitkara 2006, Espinosa et al. 2007, Kotha and Srikanth 2013). This is because, with increased specialization in specific design and engineering skills, firms are finding it increasingly dificult, costly, or inefective to rely primarily on internal capabilities (Brusoni et al. 2001). This tendency to outsource design and development eforts has resulted in several new boundaries that include geography, time-zone gaps, and organizational and functional diferences between the development teams.

Espinosa and Carmel (2003) ofer a nice summary of the research done on these barriers. They suggest that there is a poor understanding of the implications that arise from work performed across boundaries, especially when boundaries such as organizational identity, time zone, functional expertise, or culture, overlap. In particular, they cite studies investigating time-zone diferences without teasing out the efects of other potentially correlated barriers such as organization identity (shared identity, norms, and practices; Levina and Vaast 2005) that can inhibit identification with the overall project. Similarly, Vlaar et al. (2008) argued that sensemaking was impaired in these settings because of time-zone diferences. The primary result of time-zone diferences is to lessen real-time communication, thus hindering the management of shared task and resource dependencies, particularly as deadlines approach (Kotha and Srikanth 2013, Srikanth and Puranam 2011). Kotlarsky et al. (2014) argue in a similar vein, focusing on the necessity for codifying knowledge-related interdependencies among groups in the context of ofshoring/outsourcing relationships as well as arguing that improvisation must occur via interaction between the supplier and the focal firm.

Levina and Vaast (2005, 2008), using practice theory (Bourdieu 1977), build on Espinosa and Carmel (2003) and argue that both social boundaries and physical distance make it dificult for outsourced projects to create a shared identity between a focal firm or organization and its suppliers. However, their qualitative samples show that trust and joint identity revolving around the project between focal and supplier organizations could ameliorate these issues over time. This resulted in focal firm project managers “protecting” the interests of their supplier team members and supplier team members, in turn, often identifying themselves as part of the focal firm project manager’s “team.” Crucially, Levina and Vaast (2008) noted that project managers in their sample could usually issue proposals and select vendors. In other words, they had taken on some of the responsibilities traditionally associated with most firms’ purchasing organizations. Hong (2010) argued that identification could be expedited by biculturals (personnel who can move easily between two cultures, with bilingualism being an important indicator), who are better at establishing cultural metacognition and hence can act as boundary spanners, helping form shared understanding, integration (including task coordination), and mutual trust. However, a recent study by Kane and Levina (2017) finds that this is not always the case and bicultural managers when leading projects sourced to their home countries may sometimes micromanage work and hinder collaboration especially when they try to distance themselves from their home country identity.

Rai et al. (2009) built on the themes of both trust and joint identity as well as the efect of barriers to collaboration, finding that cost overruns and focal firm satisfaction are improved by information exchange, joint problem solving, and trust. Finally, Watson-Manheim et al. (2012) argue that boundaries in virtual work can evolve over time. For example, time zones can have differential consequences under various circumstances. Furthermore, this is recognized by team members on both sides as requiring conscious efort and attention to manage. They also argue that, while prior work has shown that information technology (IT) is more useful for mitigating negative outcomes of geographic dispersion rather than demographic dispersion (O’Leary and Cummings 2007), researchers have done little to identify the conditions under which IT is used and the specific features of those information systems that help or hinder joint performance of activities. One exception to this is a case study at Boeing by Kotha and Srikanth (2013), which begins to approach this problem by identifying features that can promote visibility into supplier’s current activities. However, Anderson and Parker (2013) call for more research into information systems features that would facilitate the integration of distributed projects as many other features might prove to be of value.

The qualitative interviews with the project teams brought out some of the key coordination barriers when engaging in development activities. Similar to the findings of Watson-Manheim et al. (2012), we found that the inability to engage in spontaneous communication because of time-zone diferences between the focal and supplier teams created many fault lines among the project teams with respect to task coordination. In fact, prior studies show that large time-zone separations cause the coordination of complex product or service development tasks between focal and supplier teams to occur more frequently through media such as the Internet and telephones. These do not allow for the sort of shared interpersonal context enjoyed by development teams from regions in similar time zones (Van den Bulte and Moenaert 1998, Sosa et al. 2002, Gibson and Cohen 2003, Carlile 2004, Espinosa et al. 2007). Additionally, time-zone diferences can create substantial delays in initiating contacts between the focal and supplier organizations that can contribute to overall development costs (Sosa et al. 2004, Carmel and Espinosa 2011), particularly with respect to task coordination and team improvisation to solve problems (Kotlarsky et al. 2014). Twenty out of the 55 project teams in our sample faced more than three hours of time-zone delay between the focal and supplier teams. For instance, a U.S.-based integrated software firm in our sample outsourced a subcomponent’s development work to one of its strategic suppliers in India. The level of interdependency between the main module developed in-house and the subcomponent required engineers to frequently exchange information and feedback on test and design in near real time. However, the time-zone diference between Mountain View, California and Bangalore, India did not allow for these interactions. According to the project supervisor:

Time zones give up efective hours per day. Answers take up to a day from the supplier team. In this business quick feedback is everything. We lose spontaneity in design iterations [which is extremely crucial for our development purposes].

Our interviews also suggest that all of the project teams in our sample often had a diverse functional group of personnel operating on the focal and supplier sides and there was minimal variation on this type of diversity. For instance, a typical team in our sample had not only the engineering personnel but also included financial, supply chain, manufacturing, and—if they did employ a unifying strategy— purchasing team members. Such diverse functions in teams widened the barriers between focal and sup plier firms when they spoke diferent languages. In fact, studies have documented the role of language barriers in distributed team settings with functional diversity. Sosa et al. (2002) discusses the issues of language differences and how it increases the likelihood of engaging in nonverbal communication (e.g., emails) during distributed product development projects involving engineers and manufacturers. The earlier work of Cramton and Hinds (2004), Neeley and Dumas (2016), and Hinds et al. (2014), which studied six product development teams, suggests that asymmetries in language fluency can create fault lines resulting in power struggles between diferent team members. The inclusion of bilingual team members can ameliorate these issues somewhat (Hong 2010), but not eliminate them, as bilinguals also sufer from social identity threats that create collaboration challenges (Kane and Levina 2017). Language diferences can also open up the potential for miscommunication that can create time delays, as clarification is sought to resolve any issues. Moreover, miscommunication between personnel with different language backgrounds can sometimes mean that neither party even realizes a miscommunication has occurred until much later in the project, which, as described in the monkey-proofing example in Section 1, is perhaps even more problematic. The fact that distributed development projects often involve tacit knowledge exchange between the focal and the supplier firms (Kogut and Zander 1992, Subramaniam and Venkatraman 2001, Sosa et al. 2002, Gofin and Koners 2011) exacerbates this problem. Forcing these team members to use a single common language (which for many is not their primary language) can also aggravate misunderstandings resulting in loss in status (Neeley 2013), mistrust, and group conflict (Harzing and Feely 2008). This can interfere with developing the shared context, beliefs, and experiences among team members (Orlikowski 2002) and team familiarity in general (Espinosa et al. 2007) necessary for distributed product development projects. These issues can also prevent supplier personnel’s development of represen tation schemas, which mirror those of focal organizational members (Carlile 2004). Our sample data include interviews with the project teams, in which we found numerous instances of miscommunication due to language diferences between focal and supplier personnel. For instance, when asked about challenges coordinating work with suppliers in China, the project manager of a U.S. firm made the following comment:

There is a language barrier. A lot of artists in China do not understand complex features when they are described on the phone. We also have time diference and also other typical cultural issues. Conveying a different culture when you can only talk from 5–8 p.m. at night becomes quite dificult. And it takes a long time to instill the quality expectations over there that you have here in the sense of work finished.

Even in countries such as India where English is an oficial language and where some of the bilingual team members spoke fluent English, we found instances in our interviews that suggested dificulties in coordinating project activities because of difering interpretation schemas among team members. Our interviews also suggested that these dificulties may be driven by errors made while translating the project requirements into the supplier personnel’s primary language (e.g., Hindi, Telegu, Tamil, etc.). When asked about the issues dealing with Indian suppliers, a French project manager (working in Vancouver), employed in managing gaming industry projects, made the following comments:

The Indians are much more linguistically fluent than the Chinese, but I’m more confident that at the end of the conversation, I’ve gotten my point across to the Chinese in a way that I’m going to see what I want back than I am with the Indians. The Indians will give what often to Americans appears, at least in my experience, sort of a false confidence—oh sure, sure, I get it, no problem, I understand and then you get back something that is completely not what you wanted.

Now in both cases, in India and in China, there are persistent problems with the quality of the software documentation. If you look at the code that someone generates for you it may be just garbage inside like the old stories of someone who buys a Mercedes and it has a lawnmower engine in it or something. I’m more confident of getting good results [and what to expect] out of China than I am out of India these days.

Importantly, our qualitative interviews suggest that both time-zone diferences and language barriers create diferent types of coordination challenges in distributed product development projects. Much work on organizational mechanisms has been done over several decades to counter coordination barriers (e.g., Thompson 1967, Gulati et al. 2005, etc.). Srikanth and Puranam (2011) propose a useful framework that is adapted and represented in Figure 1. As seen in this figure, previous research on coordination mechanisms can be categorized broadly into three streams: (i) reducing the need for coordination by modularizing product development (Baldwin and Clark 1997), and managing the need for coordination through (ii) ongoing communication, and (iii) tacit coordination.

It might seem that relying solely on creating a modular product architecture is, by itself, a viable approach to coordinate distributed product development project eforts. However, increasing levels of modularization can hide system level interdependencies from focal and supplier firm personnel, creating greater dificulties in synchronizing partner eforts across interdependent project tasks (Ethiraj 2007), particularly when the eforts were less routinized or unfamiliar to the focal firm (Mani et al. 2014). Many of our interviewed firms confirmed this viewpoint. They employed modularity in their designs, but still had many coordination issues as exemplified by the oilfield sensor project discussed in Section 1. Moreover, our qualitative interviews suggest that coordination barriers such as time zone and language exist even in the presence of tacit coordination strategies such as colocating personnel. Our interviews did find evidence of the use of ongoing communication facilitated by information systems to minimize coordination barriers. These communication mechanisms were specifically tailored to be used for exchanging design and engineering information (i.e., DIS) during product development. We extend Srikanth and Puranam’s (2011) framework with respect to integrating distributed work to account for these tailored DIS systems (Figure 1). We also propose a newer form of coordination strategy to supplement ongoing and tacit communication, that of expanding the power of the project manager by increasing their scope of responsibilites. Specifically, we argue for unifying purchasing and engineering to combat the sometimes conflicting goals and directives created by the two organizations in these settings as an extension to existing frameworks (Figure 1). In Section 2.2, we iterate between evidence from our interviews and the extant literature, to shed some light on these two specific coordination mechanisms.

## 2.2. Role of Design Information Systems

According to the knowledge-based theory of the firm (Nickerson and Zenger 2004), firms derive competitive advantage by developing capabilities that allow the efective integration of knowledge activities occurring outside their firm boundaries. In the context of distributed projects, research shows that firms typically invest in developing shared information systems capabilities that facilitate communication with supplier partners (Orlikowski 2002, Sosa et al. 2004). For instance, Krishna et al. (2004, p. 65) argue that the benefits of shared information systems include “agreed coordination and control mechanisms, for example, to report on and monitor project progress. They can be further harmonized through common processes, such as systems development methodologies.” During our interviews, we asked project managers and team members about their use of information systems to communicate with supplier partners. Most of the projects we studied made use of generic communication channels such as emails, telephone, videoconferencing, web-based meetings, etc., when working with their suppliers. While these communication channels are omnipresent in today’s business climate, many have argued that information systems tailored to the specific needs of distributed development activities are essential (Berente et al. 2009, Kotha and Srikanth 2013, Krishna et al. 2004, Ragatz et al. 1997). However, Watson-Manheim et al. (2012) point out that the specific features of such systems remain as yet unidentified, because most research in this area only treats general-purpose information systems, particularly email and work collaboration tools such as those in Srikanth and Puranam (2011). Espinosa and Carmel (2003) describe a path forward by noting that task coordination issues become highly problematic in the face of barriers such as time-zone diferences. Kotha and Srikanth (2013), through a qualitative study at Boeing, furthered this idea by beginning to identify specific features such as installing cameras onsite at suppliers, document control, and prioritization of logged help requests and problems to promote visibility into supplier activities to coordinate tasks in their qualitative study within one firm. We build on these ideas by proposing DIS as an example of one such specialized system for coordinating development eforts through project management methodologies such as the critical-path-based methodologies (to control schedule and allocate resources) and document control systems tailored to manage design and process changes (Malone and Crowston 1994, Schmidt and Simonee 1996). We found significant variation in the degree that firms’ teams in our study sample used DIS to coordinate their project activities.

Specifically, we found three distinct components of DIS used by partnering teams to coordinate project activities from our interviews across the firms’ dyadic relationships in our sample. The first component involved a scheduling and checking algorithm such as the critical path method (CPM) or project evaluation and review technique (PERT) that can be used to manage timing, resource utilization, and development cost as well as to monitor the project during product development eforts and anticipate coordination issues in advance (Raymond and Bergeron 2008). The second component involved a document control module tailored to tracking engineering change orders (Gokpinar et al. 2013), which allows better control of product design changes (Berente et al. 2009). Failure to communicate these design changes to all of the involved organizations in a timely manner can result in cost penalties, particularly from rework resulting from one organization not knowing the engineering changes created by a diferent organization (for a survey on this topic, see Abdel-Hamid and Madnick 1991, Lyneis and Ford 2007). Finally, DIS also involves a third component that is used to identify activity interfaces during development eforts (Turner and Speiser 1992). This involved techniques to formally translate user requirements into specifications and identify specification interactions so as to prevent the design of poor interfaces between new product development activities, i.e., to modularize the activities so as to minimize required communication (Baldwin and Clark 1997). This can minimize excessive and unknown interactions between tasks that interfere with the final integration of the entire project (Parker and Anderson 2002, Sosa et al. 2004). The most prominent techniques to manage these issues in the new product development world are the quality function deployment (Grifin and Hauser 1993) and design structure matrices (Eppinger and Browning 2012).

While it might seem possible to deploy various “ofthe-shelf” systems, implementing them in a network of dyadic relationships and then leveraging them to improve distributed product development is dificult because they are a specialized form of project management shared information systems. Such systems take time and investment to put into place (Ahlemann 2009), because they require appropriate systems integration into the firm (Raymond and Bergeron 2008) and, by extension in distributed settings, to their supply chain partners. Moreover, DIS require that individual users functioning in various regions and across firms learn these systems efectively to achieve meaningful use (Raymond and Bergeron 2008). This is unsurprising, as studies have shown that IT deployments are efective only when human resources are aligned with IT systems (Barney 1991, Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997). For instance, Wade and Hulland (2004) argue that only by combining IT assets (for example, CPM, PERT, or design structure matrix software) with capabilities developed over an extended period of time (such as supplier relationships) can firms achieve competitive advantage that cannot simply be bought of the shelf. Wade and Hulland (2004) as well as Bhatt and Grover (2005) argue that this is particularly true when such expertise is embedded within a supply chain of external partners. Srikanth and Puranam (2014), in their study of Boeing’s product development, found that many aspects of DIS in distributed settings would be useful, but could find little evidence of them in acrossfirm settings such as examined in our study. Hence, based on these arguments, introducing DIS into a distributed project setting is a complex matter because of the need to develop organizational capabilities to leverage it appropriately.

We argue that the use of DIS can help project performance in the presence of time-zone diferences during project activities because of the following reasons: DIS, because of its improved scheduling of resources, engineering change order document control, and superior task modularization should reduce the number of coordination conflicts that require timely intervention between focal and supplier firm personnel who operate in diferent time zones. As a result, development eforts can take place with fewer cost or quality (e.g., rework) penalties. Moreover, there is an additional benefit to DIS because time-zone gaps reduce the ability to resolve these conflicts through lateral communication in a timely manner (Sosa et al. 2002, Anderson et al. 2007, O’Leary and Cummings 2007, Kotlarsky et al. 2014). When asked about the use of DIS, a project engineer from the United States working on a distributed development project with suppliers from Japan made the following comment:

We use Agile [the project management software tool, not the methodology] to manage change requirement forms, etc. It helps coordinate how to incorporate the change, can you get the parts, etc. Turns out, we’re the best expediters around.

All these discussions ofer support to the role of DIS in mitigating time-zone barriers. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). A DIS strategy moderates the relationship between time-zone diference and project performance. That is, distributed project performance is stronger when using DIS strategy under time-zone diferences.

The use of DIS can also help when there are language diferences among the distributed product development teams. For example, when project teams speak diferent languages, communication challenges can emerge when they routinely interact across organizational boundaries (McDonough et al. 2001, Carlile 2004). DIS systems, with their emphasis on building schedules and interaction matrices, enhance a shared understanding of project goals and visions by articulating information that would often otherwise be tacit (Petersen et al. 2005, Eppinger and Browning 2012). Thus, these systems can become boundary objects that can create a “common language” of concepts that facilitate communication and the development of group identity and solidarity (Carlile 2004, Levina and Vaast 2005). This should help focal firm-supplier dyads that are separated by diferent first languages on a number of fronts. First, the creation of boundary objects increases the common library of definitions, concepts, and syntax in a dyad that is otherwise disadvantaged as described earlier. Moreover, it exposes hidden gaps in understanding that can lead to later misunderstandings (Carlile 2004), such as when each member of a dyad invests the same word or phrase with a diferent meaning, which is particularly common in dyads that do not share a common first language (Harzing and Feely 2008, Hinds et al. 2014, Neeley 2013). Finally, it can facilitate the shared understanding of tacit knowledge (McDonough et al. 2001, Carlile 2004, Levina and Vaast 2005), which does not easily cross a language gap (Varonis and Gass 1985). Our interviews with a project manager working with a Malaysian supplier made the following remarks with respect to the use of DIS:

Clarity of communication was important especially with our suppliers. You need to see through and deal with the [diferent language] interpretations. I am a big proponent of project management and [IS] tools. We used Microsoft Project [a CPM-based tool] during our development work. Timelines were clearly communicated to them and we got commitments back from them.

Taken together, these arguments suggest the following relationship between DIS and language diferences.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). A DIS strategy moderates the relationship between language diferences and project performance. That is, distributed project performance is stronger when using DIS strategy under language diferences.

## 2.3. Role of Unifying Strategy

Although investments in information systems such as DIS can help manage coordination barriers, interview responses from our project teams show that firms also relied on empowering project managers by increasing their scope of responsibilities. This is also consistent with the extant literature that argues for personnel empowerment as a strategic weapon when coordinating distributed development activities (Krause et al. 1998). One of the most commonly used personnel strategies is the practice of colocation. In a distributed development project, colocation is defined as the ongoing presence of personnel from the focal firm in the supplier organization (i.e., individuals from the focal firm have their primary work site at the supplier organization), or the ongoing presence of supplier individual(s) in the focal organization, or both (McDonough et al. 2001). In fact, studies show that colocation can help improve tacit coordination between team members especially during highly interdependent development activities (Hinds and Mortensen 2005, Espinosa et al. 2007). Most of the project teams in our sample employed this strategy to manage development activities.

Interestingly, however, some of the firms in our sample also invested in other organizational strategies such as giving additional decision-making rights and powers normally reserved for the purchasing function to the project managers. We define this strategy as a unifying strategy. Wynstra et al. (2001, 2003) and Dowlatshahi (1992) argue that purchasing organizations and project managers often work at cross-purposes because of diferent decision time horizons (long-term versus short-term), diferent tasks (building a supply base versus executing a project), and diferent metrics (overall supply chain performance versus an individual product’s technical function). For example, many purchasers in the study by Wynstra et al. (2001) were reluctant to commit early to a given supplier because there was no finished product on which to base their decision. By contrast, project managers at those firms wanted early supplier involvement to facilitate product design. This created tension between the purchasing and engineering entities. Schiele (2010) points out that many purchasing agents want to specialize in a specific commodity or technology, which would result in an impractical number of personnel involved in a new product development project, which typically relies on the integration of numerous component technologies. To resolve these conflicts, Schiele (2010) and Wynstra et al. (2001) call for an integrated product development and sourcing organization, and many studies (Parker and Anderson 2002, Hillebrand and Biemans 2004, Tracey 2004) have found this to be beneficial to project management in product development eforts.

Wynstra et al. (2001) specifically suggest that the permanent merger of purchasing and project management into one organizational unit, such as the purchasingengineering duo they observed at Thompson-CSF (an electronics and defense manufacturer), can help manage focal-firm/supplier conflicts. Parker and Anderson (2002) echo this advice in their extensive study of Hewlett-Packard (HP) by noting that many of the decision rights and powers normally reserved for purchasing organizations were exercised by “supply chain integrators” at HP. They also observed, echoing Levina and Vaast (2008), that middle managers often could span these boundaries to achieve a desired result, but as Parker and Anderson (2002) also pointed out, many of these middle managers have been eliminated in the efort to flatten organizations and reduce personnel costs. By acting as boundary spanners (Levina and Vaast 2005) in a new product development environment, supply chain integrators are focal firm project managers who coordinate product development, manufacturing, and logistics functions across firm boundaries to maintain the integrity of a product’s initial product vision while keeping the project on schedule and on budget (Parker and Anderson 2002). Thus, the unifying strategy involves a single person (or alternately a duo similar to that in Wynstra et al. 2001) who has additional responsibilities and makes decisions regarding both engineering and purchasing tasks for a distributed development project.

Levina and Vaast (2005) noted that the project managers in their sample were generally able to not only specify the selection criteria for their suppliers but also to select their suppliers. Wilcocks and Grifiths (2010) and Parker and Anderson (2002) pointed out that many middle managers who had this power were phased out in recent decades. Hence, in many product development firms, these two extremely important supplier management tasks are controlled at least partially by a separate purchasing department, which remains involved throughout the lifetime of the entire project (Grover and Malhotra 2003). We generalize from Levina and Vaast (2005) and argue that unifying purchasing and project management can help reduce time-zone diferences between the focal and supplier firms, and lack of such unification will exacerbate the efects of these barriers. For instance, a unifying strategy to integrate engineering and purchasing decisions can result in high levels of both technical and functional expertise on the part of the supply chain integrator. While the integrator may not be as “good” at certain types of technical expertise as a dedicated purchasing function’s personnel, under certain conditions— such as high contextual ambiguity—merging these two into one organizational function reduces overall transaction costs for the focal firm with its suppliers in a manner analogous to moving from a market to a hierarchy (Williamson 1991, Grover and Malhotra 2003). Moreover, the presence of barriers will increase information asymmetry between the project manager and purchasing, because the project manager has a more pressing need to make a project-specific investment in building a communications capability with the supplier (Grover and Malhotra 2003), thus driving her to a less-opportunistic and more collaborationist approach with the supplier (McIvor 2008). Given that suppliers tend to react poorly to opportunistic behavior (Parkhe 1993), merging the two functions using a unifying strategy may be particularly appropriate in the presence of coordination barriers.

This tends to be true even when the focal firm has more market power relative to the supplier (Maloni and Benton 2000), because desourcing an important supplier after asset-specific investments have been made can be problematic (Parker and Anderson 2002). As one HP manager put it, recalling Theodore Roosevelt’s dictum of “walk softly and carry a big stick,” that he as a supply chain integrator could only wield a “little stick” with distributed suppliers and had to rely much more on persuasion in this context than he needed to with respect to internal “suppliers.”

Unifying strategies have been shown to follow these patterns anecdotally in distributed project settings by resulting in a single point of contact between the focal and supplier firm that can in turn help better evaluate and communicate the project task requirements across organizational boundaries (Davies and Brady 2000). In their case studies with HP, Parker and Anderson (2002) found that increased geographic distance created a time-zone separation between HP facilities in California and their suppliers in Taiwan, resulting in extensive delays and unnecessary budget extensions in these projects. On the occasions when HP installed a single point of contact with both functional knowledge and purchasing decision rights to coordinate activities, rework decreased and development times improved. Similarly, Sosa et al. (2002, p. 48) found that “synchronous communication” between the interactive team members promotes faster feedback during product development stages.

A unifying strategy can allow such instant feedback across development teams and thus prove particularly efective in geographically distributed product development projects. These points suggest the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). A unifying strategy moderates the relationship between time-zone diference and project performance. That is, distributed project performance is stronger when using a unifying strategy under time-zone diferences.

A unifying strategy can also benefit project teams when they face language separation. For example, when distributed project teams speak diferent languages, communication challenges can emerge when project team members interact routinely across organizational boundaries (McDonough et al. 2001, Carlile 2004). For example, one of the focal firms in our study felt that language issues had contributed to poor communication resulting in “\$100,000 [worth of] scissors that are a mistake.” Supply chain integrators who have engineering expertise can mitigate these problems by creating a shared understanding of project goals and vision (Dougherty 1992) and help teams avoid the miscommunications that are common in day-to-day development activities when language barriers exist (Gefen and Carmel 2008). However, a purchasing arm of the organization, pursuing its own independent agenda of reducing costs without the functional understanding of a supply chain integrator, can easily disrupt this process, creating numerous misunderstandings. This situation can worsen if the supplier and focal personnel speak diferent languages. In these situations, giving the supply chain integrator more responsibilities on purchasing and decision rights creates a single point of contact, even if the integrator speaks a diferent first language from the supplier, allowing consistent communication between the development teams. We found ample evidence for the benefits of a unifying strategy when there were language diferences. As an example, consider the development eforts on an engine control module between a U.S.-based automobile firm and a German supplier firm. According to the project supervisor, the development efort involved substantial tribal (i.e., tacit) knowledge that was simply dificult to communicate between the U.S. and German engineers, especially when involving multiple functional entities. The supervisor finally decided to empower his project manager with both purchasing and project decisionmaking powers. This helped mitigate the coordination challenges during development eforts. According to the project supervisor:

We outsourced to ofload, but we didn’t ofload as much as we thought. We ended up with a [high level engineer/ tech specialist] reporting to the manager who oversaw integration at [supplier]. . . . The only reason this project worked is because of [the supply chain integrator] who was able to coordinate efectively during the project.

This was not the case in another collaboration involving the same firm with another German supplier. The second project did not utilize a unifying strategy, and according to the supervisor it resulted in “our chief engineer beating up their chief engineer.” Thus, the presence of a unifying strategy can benefit project performance, especially when language barriers emerge during distributed product development work, as argued in the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). A unifying strategy moderates the relationship between language diferences and performance. That is, distributed project performance is stronger when using a unifying strategy under language diferences.

## 3. Research Design

## 3.1. Survey Data Collection

In addition to the interview data, we also collected primary survey data from the 55 projects from 20 firms. Data collection took place between 2003 and 2008. Our first step in the data collection process was to identify focal firms that were engaged in distributed product development activities. We identified 42 organizations belonging to seven diferent industry segments and contacted the senior managers to explain our research design (i.e., our interest in collecting data from multiple informants across multiple projects), the research method (i.e., paper and pencil survey instruments and in-person interviews), and the expected time commitment. Three organizations opted not to participate in the study, citing confidentiality concerns. Fifteen could not commit the management time of both supervisors and project engineers (approximately half of these because of employee turnover). Four could not iden tify a project meeting the survey requirements. We dropped two other firms from our sample because they did not meet our sampling requirement (i.e., they manage distinct product development outsourcing activities). The final sample therefore consisted of 20 firms (18 focal firm locations in North America and two focal firm locations in Europe with supplier locations all over the world) belonging to seven major industry segments: aerospace, automotive, consumer prod ucts, electronics hardware, food processing, semiconductor, and embedded software,<sup>1</sup> giving us a response rate of 48%. Comparisons of basic demographics (i.e., sales, research and development expenditures, and the number of employees) between the 20 sampled firms and the industry population (data obtained from the Compustat database) indicated no systematic pattern of diferences in sales and number of employees, minimizing concerns of nonrespondent bias (Chandrasekaran and Mishra 2012). In addition, follow-up emails and telephone conversations with a number of nonrespond ing units indicated that they chose not to participate because of either lack of time commitment or confidentiality concerns. To reduce recollection bias, only projects that were completed during the last three years were sampled (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006). This crite rion also ensured that the integration activities could be related to the production of a clear deliverable (e.g., a specific product, process, or other technological project) that the lead firm was able to evaluate. Our data collection efort involved both onsite meetings and face-to face surveys, which ensured higher quality responses compared with conventional mail or electronic surveys.

For each project, we collected survey data from two types of informant—project supervisors and project engineers. The supervisor completed a brief (threepage) survey that provides basic descriptive data about the project (e.g., percentage of the total project outsourced, timeline, identification of the lead supplier, and length of the relationship and type of contracts with that supplier). Supervisors also assessed project outcomes by comparing final project outcomes to initial project targets. Immediately after completing the survey, the supervisor participated in an individual interview in which they answered open-ended questions about general challenges in managing outsourcing as well as challenges faced in the specific projects discussed in the survey. These interviews were recorded and transcribed for use during our theory development. The supervisor also identified the lead project engineer for each project. In all cases, the lead engineer was blind to the project performance data and all other data provided by the supervisor. The project engineer was the person who served as the key point of contact between the focal firm and the supplier during the project and handled the day-to-day integration of the two firms’ activities. Each project engineer completed an extensive (12-page) survey about the nature of the project (e.g., geographic location of the focal and supplier firm, language spoken by personnel belonging to each side, size and complexity of the project), integration and communication mechanisms used on the project, and their own background and training. The project engineer also answered questions about project performance identical to those answered by the project manager. After completing the survey, the project engineer participated in an interview (alone or with other project engineers) about challenges in managing outsourcing generally and in the specific project for which they completed the survey.

Collecting data from both the project supervisor and the project engineer substantially reduced the feasible number of respondents as compared to a more typical single-level web or mail survey. However, this study design generated many benefits. First, it allowed us to reduce the number of questions per survey informant, increasing the potential for high item response rate and greater accuracy of responses. Second, it allowed us to collect data on specific constructs from the most knowledgeable informant within a project. Third, it reduced concerns regarding common method bias in the data since the outcome data (i.e., the project outcomes) were provided by a diferent informant from the one who provided data on the project’s organization and structure.

## 3.2. Measures

We used Likert-scale items to measure the constructs in our study. The measurement items constituting the individual constructs are reported in Appendix A.1. A description of the individual constructs and of their measurement items follows.

3.2.1. Dependent Variables. Project Performance. We study multiple dimensions of project performance consistent with the product development literature (e.g., Cummings 2004, Hoegl et al. 2004). In particular, we asked the project supervisor to rate the performance of the project with respect to its initial goals. The supervisors specifically rated (i) functionality of the deliverable (quality performance), (ii) working relationship with the supplier (relationship performance), (iii) rampup time of the final product compared to its initial goal (response time, note: a high value of response time indicates more time required than initially thought, hence a lower value is better), and (iv) cost of the deliverable (cost performance, note: high value indicates increase in cost for this measure). A five-point Likert scale was used to measure these performance dimensions (see Appendix A.1 for the scale). A review of descriptive statistics and the histogram for this scale revealed considerable variation in the performance of projects, and thus eliminated the concern that the sample was biased toward high performing projects.

3.2.2. Independent Variables. Time-zone Diference. The project engineer reported on the city and state or province where both the supplier and the focal firms were located. Examples of focal firm locations include Augsburg, Germany; Mountain View, California; and Hoogeveen, Netherlands. Examples of supplier firm locations include Bangalore, India; Singapore; Rustenburg, South Africa; Taipei, Taiwan; Auburn Hills, Michigan; and Minsk, Belarus. These location destinations were used to compute the corresponding time zones in which the focal and supplier firm personnel functioned. Finally we computed the time-zone diference as the adjusted time-zone diference between the focal and supplier firms (Espinosa et al. 2007, O’Leary and Cummings 2007). The time-zone diference calculation used in this study incorporated the common working hours for both the focal firm and its suppliers (O’Leary and Cummings 2007). To illustrate, consider a focal firm in Austin, Texas, and a supplier firm in Tempe, Arizona. The absolute time-zone diference between these regions would be one hour. Using an eight-hour operating time, the common hours that are available to work between these two regions would be seven hours. A negative sign was used to capture the magnitude (i.e., low is better). Our results remain consistent with or without daylight savings correction.

Language Diference. Project engineers reported on the first language of the majority of the supplier personnel that were assigned to this project. They also revealed the first language of the majority of their firm’s personnel who were assigned to this project. Appendix A.1 provides summary statistics on the languages that are part of our research. Languages spoken by focal firm personnel include English and German while languages spoken by supplier personnel include Chinese, Cantonese, Hindi, English, German, Japanese, Spanish, Russian, Tagalog, and Bengali. Researchers studying the efect of language barriers in a cross-cultural context have developed well-established measures that allow us to capture the distance between languages (e.g., West and Graham 2004, Bright 1992). In fact, studies on cross-cultural context have used these measures to understand the coordination barriers between personnel from diferent countries (e.g., Hutchinson 2002). In our study, we relied on a well-established measure proposed by West and Graham (2004) that captures the linguistic distance between languages spoken across 51 cultures. One advantage of using this measure is that it takes into account the primary as well as secondary languages spoken by personnel from a given country (see Appendix A.2 for more details on this measure), and therefore uses a weighted average based on the percentages and the mixes of languages used in these countries. For example, a project that has team members in Germany and the United States with primary languages of German and English, respectively, would have a language diference score of 1, whereas a project with team members in the United States and the Philippines with primary languages of English and Tagalog would have a language diference score of 7.

This approach to measure language diference is superior to other alternative approaches such as using a binary variable to capture diferences (West and Graham 2004). As a robustness check, we repeated our analyses using diferent operationalization of language diference that is explained later in Section 4.4.

3.2.3. Moderators. DIS (Cronbach alpha<sup></sup>0.78). Project engineers reported on the extent of the use of the information system used for product design activities using a five-point Likert scale (1 <sup></sup> not at all, 5 <sup></sup> to a great extent). The specific tools in the survey include use of formal scheduling or project management software utilizing CPM (e.g., the Primavera suite or industry specific project management software), a formally documented process to ensure design and process change information to all project members (e.g., Visual Studio, Omnify PLM, etc.), a formal methodology to translate product/process concepts into design specifications and interfaces (e.g., quality function deployment or design structure matrices). All of these items loaded as a single construct during an exploratory factor analysis and had good internal consistency (Nunnally 1978).

Unifying Strategy. Project engineers reported on whether the purchasing and engineering functions were unified and managed by one person or one organization for their distributed projects. A binary variable (1 <sup></sup> yes; 0 <sup></sup> no) measures whether this strategy was adopted during the project activity. Thirty percent of the projects in our sample adopted this strategy.

3.2.4. Control Variables. External factors pertaining to characteristics of distributed product development projects may be a potential source of heterogeneity in our analysis. In particular, the following factors were controlled in our analyses.

Team Size. Team size has been used as a control variable in many studies (e.g., Atuahene-Gima 2003, Chandrasekaran and Mishra 2012) based on the premise that, up to a point of diminishing returns and excessive complexity associated with large team sizes, bigger teams have access to better resources than smaller teams. We included the natural logarithm of the number of full-time project team members as reported by the project supervisor in the analysis.

Duration. The duration of a project (in months) is an indicator of both the time and efort required to complete the project (Cummings 2004). Project supervisors provided the starting and completion times (month and year) for all our projects. The duration varied between 0.5 months and 30 months (mean: 5.64, std. dev.: 6.64).

Colocation Strategy. Research shows that colocating project team personnel can help create a shared understanding of project task objectives (Carlile 2004) and is often a good approach to manage distributed work (Espinosa et al. 2007). We controlled for this efect in our analyses. Project engineers reported on whether they adopted a colocation strategy to place one or more supplier personnel at their firm or to place one or more of their personnel at the supplier firm. A binary variable (1 <sup></sup> yes; 0 <sup></sup> no) measures whether a colocation strategy was used during the project activity.

Team Conflict (Cronbach alpha <sup></sup> 0.80). Distributing project work can also give rise to team conflicts (Hinds and Mortensen 2005) that have been shown to afect project performance. We asked the project engineers to rate the frequency of disagreements with the supplier personnel on various issues such as responding to changes in project scope, responding to changes in project schedule, building ongoing relationships, and project functionality (see Appendix A.1 for the exact scales). An average of these items was used to measure team conflict.

Our within efects estimation approach accounts for any unobserved efects at the firm levels. However, there could potentially be several project level characteristics that may be unaccounted for in the current model. We did the following qualitative checks to explore these issues.

Since the 55 projects were nested in 20 firms, we used a within-efects (fixed-efects) estimation procedure (Wooldridge 2010). One advantage of using a within-efects estimation procedure is that it allows us to control any omitted variable at the firm level without sacrificing degrees of freedom (Gujarati and Porter 2009). In addition, we also used clustered standard errors at the firm level to account for firm-level interdependencies.

## 4.1. Moderating Efects of Design Information Systems Strategy

H1 posits that a DIS moderates the relationship between time-zone diference and project performance such that the use of this strategy will be more useful with an increase in time-zone diference. Models 2, $6 , 9 ,$ and 14 represent these moderation efects. We centered the variables before creating the interaction terms (Aiken and West 1991). Results from Tables 4 and 5 indicate that the interaction term between DIS and time-zone diference is not associated with any of the four dimensions of performance ofering no support to H1.

H2 posits that a DIS also moderates the relationship between language diference and project performance. Results from Models 2, 6, 9, and 14 show that the interaction term between DIS and language diference is strongly associated with quality $( \beta = 0 . 8 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and relationship $( \beta = 0 . 8 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ performance, but is not associated with response time $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 , \ p > 0 . 2 0 )$ or cost $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 , p > 0 . 5 0 )$ performance. To better interpret the interaction efect of DIS, we created a conditional efects plot (Aiken and West 1991) by plotting language diference against both quality and response time performance at one standard deviation above and below the mean (Aiken and West 1991). Figures 2(a) and 2(b) represent the conditional efects plot. As seen from these plots, with an increase in language diference between the focal and supplier firm personnel, higher levels of DIS are associated with higher quality and relationship performance when compared with low levels of DIS. Without DIS, we find that quality or relationship performance remains the same between low and high language diference. These results suggest that investing in a DIS strategy can benefit certain dimensions of project performance when there are language diferences between focal and supplier firm personnel. They suggest moderation efects of DIS supporting H2.

## 4.2. Moderating Efects of Unifying Strategy

H3 posits that a unifying strategy moderates the relationship between time-zone diference and project performance. Results from Models 3, 7, 11, and 15 suggest that the interaction term between a unifying strategy and time-zone diference is not associated with quality $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 , p > 0 . 2 0 )$ , relationship $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 , p > 0 . 2 0 ) .$ or cost $( \beta = 0 . 0 6 , p > 0 . 2 0 )$ performance, but is associated with improved response time performance $( \beta = - 0 . 1 2 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . That is, with increasing time-zone diference, a unifying strategy is associated with reduced response time in these distributed projects but is not associated with performance measures. To better understand the interaction efect of unifying strategy, we created the conditional efects plot (Aiken and West 1991) by plotting time-zone diference against response time performance with and without the use of a unifying strategy. Figure 3(a) represents this conditional efect plot. As seen from these plots, in the absence of a unifying strategy, an increase in time-zone diference is associated with worsening <sup>(</sup>increases) of response time performance. However, in the presence of a unifying strategy, an increase in time-zone diference is associated with improved (decreases) response time performance. Taken together, these results ofer some support for H3 regarding the moderating efects of unifying strategy on project performance.

Overall, our results ofer important insights on the integration strategies employed in managing distributed product development projects. First, we find that the DIS strategy to manage distributed projects was associated with improved quality and relationship performance when there were language diferences between the focal and supplier firm personnel. This strategy, however, is not efective when managing timezone diferences. Second, we find that a unifying strategy was associated with improved response time in the presence of time-zone diferences and cost performance in the presence of language diferences (but not vice versa). Interestingly, we also found that a unifying strategy actually slowed down response time when there were language diferences between focal and supplier firm personnel. Finally, we found that the efects of the DIS strategy were mostly seen on quality and relationship performance while the efects of unifying strategy were mostly seen on cost and response time performance. ## 4.3. Insights from the Interview Data

We revisited the interviews done with the project engineers and project supervisors to better understand some of these regression results. One result that seemed counterintuitive at first was that higher quality and working relationship outcomes were associated with an increase in language diferences between focal and supplier firms. However, it is crucial to remember that these measures were taken with respect to initial expectations at the beginning of the project as discussed in Section 3.2. One theme that came up repeatedly during the interviews is that the experience of focal firms with suppliers was often better than initially expected. As one American semiconductor focal firm said of an Asian supplier, “At first, we thought they don’t know what they’re doing, but when we visited, we found out that they did know what they were doing.” Another American ofice supplies focal firm said of a supplier based in a diferent Asian nation that over the project, “They are becoming more astute. They are constantly repeating, developing exhaustive feedback loops in an exhaustive task.” That did not mean language issues were still not a problem over the project. As the first focal firm also said, “Sometimes we have language issues. They run a million parts and they don’t work.” In short, while language barriers were not as bad as initially expected, resulting in better project outcomes than initially expected, language problems were still present during the projects.

With respect to the diferentially efective role of coordination mechanisms, our interviews showed that using a DIS helps capture and communicate design requirements between focal and supplier team personnel, increases design and process change control, and improves project schedule monitoring and resource allocation. Using a DIS thus makes many implicit, or perhaps even verbally articulated information, explicit and hence less prone to misunderstanding. This helps explain why our results suggest that a DIS improves both the quality and relationship performance in projects with a diference in first languages between the focal and supplier firm, because there are fewer chances for a critical misunderstanding.

By contrast, investing in a unifying strategy that allows a single person (or potentially two to three people in the same project management organization) to be responsible for both procurement and engineering decisions creates generally, but not wholly, positive results with respect to outcomes. For example, our results indicate that a unifying strategy, unlike a DIS, helps mitigate the response time issues associated with time-zone diferences between the focal firm and supplier. This probably results because fewer people (i.e., no purchasing representatives) have to be included in the tenuous, slow, and often information poor communication loops created by time-zone diferentials. This allows problems to be detected and solved quickly, before they become embedded in the design necessitating rework.

On the other hand, the efect of a unifying strategy with respect to a language barrier is more mixed, because it is associated with improved (reduced) cost but worse response time. We speculate that the reason for this is that, without a unifying strategy, purchasing personnel not only have decision rights separate from the project team, they also have responsibilities to some of the cost issues (Dowlatshahi 1992; Wynstra et al. 2001, 2003; Schiele 2010). Divided responsibilities make it easier for misunderstandings to remain uncaught, which eventually results in issues such as increased cost (e.g., the \$100,000 of useless scissors discussed earlier). This is particularly so since purchasing managers, because they are responsible for many projects, have time to invest in developing a cross-language rapport with the supplier than would supply chain integrators, who typically manage a single project (Parker and Anderson 2002). Thus, when a unifying strategy is employed, more of these issues are resolved as shown by the improvement in cost performance, but such resolution takes time. In other words, without a unifying strategy, a supplier will provide a component more quickly, but that is because various cost issues have not been identified and thus did not require time to correct them. Our semistructured interview protocol bolsters this conclusion by asking an open-ended question regarding coordination challenges created by language barriers. We found a consistent pattern in our interviews voicing the need for personnel who were able to understand both technical (engineering) and purchasing functions simultaneously so as to reduce the number of hidden misunderstandings.

## 4.4. Robustness Checks

We performed the following analyses to test the robustness of our results. First, we used a diferent approach to assess the language diference between the focal and supplier firm personnel. Specifically, project engineers reported on “the normal working language” of the primary supplier contact. We found that in eight projects, the primary supplier contact had a diferent language from the majority of the supplier personnel. These projects had supplier teams in China, India, and Germany and the contacts were reported to speak English (instead of Cantonese, Hindi, or German). This measure is a proxy for multilingual supplier personnel, which has been shown as a strategy that is sometimes used in similar contexts (Hong 2010). We repeated our analyses using this new measure of language diference. The results remained generally consistent but were somewhat weaker in significance. This suggests that even with the help of multilinguals, language barriers still pose some degree of coordination problems in the case of distributed product development projects. This finding echoes a recent study by Kane and Levina (2017) that reports coordination challenges even in the presence of bilinguals.

Second, our within-efects estimation accounts for any omitted variables at the firm level (e.g., firm culture, size, etc.). To account for additional project level factors, we repeated our analyses by adding two other controls. The first one measures the percent of engineering work performed by external suppliers (0%–100%) while the second one measures the type of alliance with the supplier (five-point Likert scale; 1—formal alliance, 2—informal alliance, 3—preferred provider, 4—arm’s length (repeated use), 5—arm’s length (first use)). These variables had some missing values that reduced the overall sample size for the analyses. Our results remain consistent after including these additional controls. However the VIFs for these models were very high because of the high correlation between some of these predictor variables in these models.

Third, we repeated the analyses treating our dependent variable as categorical and used an ordered logit within-efects estimation approach. Interpreting the coeficients from a logit model is somewhat dificult (Kennedy 2003). Nevertheless, the vast majority of the overall conclusions remain unchanged, as seen from Tables A.3 and A.4.

Finally, we carried out additional analysis to check for potential macro efects, namely, national culture of the supplier firm on project performance (Naor et al. 2010). To do this, we performed a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with project performance predicted by 14 countries. Our results showed that countries did not significantly explain cost (F <sup></sup> 0.22, p > 0.50), quality (F <sup></sup> 0.31, p > 0.50), or relationship (F <sup></sup> 0.95, p > 0.51) performance.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions 5.1. Discussion of Results

The past few decades have seen a significant increase in the number of firms that outsource product development work to partners around the globe (e.g., Hinds and Mortensen 2005, Espinosa et al. 2007). Although these studies have begun to address some of the challenges involved in coordinating product development eforts, they still do not capture the complexities of managing distributed project activities. These projects have high levels of task interdependencies and involve a high level of tacit knowledge. Their components interact with each other through electromagnetic, heat, mass, and spatial dimensions, which must all be managed by information flows and decision making across firm boundaries (Ramdas and Taylor 2008, Ulrich and Eppinger 2011). These inherent characteristics impose multiple coordination challenges as firms try to work with partners located in diferent parts of the world.

Studies in the information systems literature have begun to argue about the importance of investing in information systems to manage the coordination challenges created by time-zone and language barriers (Carmel and Espinosa 2011, Neeley et al. 2012). Although information systems ofer a good starting point, it is possible that they may not address all of the coordination challenges experienced in distributed product development projects because of the inherent levels of physical component interactions. In this research, we take a preliminary look at the coordination challenges involved in distributed product development project activities by adopting multiple methods. We employ a unique data set that includes interview data from 70 projects run by 30 firms that outsourced to suppliers in 14 countries speaking 10 different languages. Our interviews show that most of the organizations used the commonly identified strategies by previous studies such as colocating personnel and using information systems when distributing development activities. However, we found that problems still existed because of language and time-zone diferences between the focal and supplier firm even when using these tried-and-true mechanisms. What we discovered was that some of these firms also invested in other mechanisms that are not well understood in the literature. Specifically, we found that a number of firms invested in specialized DIS systems for product development, while others invested in empowering project managers with decision rights more typically associated with purchasing (i.e., the unifying strategy). A DIS communicates product and process design information between the focal and supplier team personnel during product development eforts. A unifying strategy gives the project manager (often termed a supply chain integrator in this case) additional decision-making rights and powers normally reserved for the focal firm’s purchasing (or procurement) organization. We tested these hypotheses using survey data from 55 projects that were hand collected from 20 firms. Results from our survey ofer additional insights into the practice of distributed product development. First, we find that using DIS was associated with improved quality and relationship performance in the presence of a language barrier, but was not associated with any change of outcomes in the presence of time-zone diferences. Fascinatingly, using a unifying strategy is associated with better (reduced) cost and worse response time in the presence of a language barrier, perhaps implying a trade-of between outcomes. Even more interestingly, a unifying strategy actually is associated with improved response time in the presence of a time-zone diference. Taken together, these results suggest that there is no single “silver bullet” to manage coordination challenges, necessitating the use of a portfolio of integration mechanisms. Furthermore, managers ought to be prepared to experience some trade-ofs in their project deliverables outcomes.

## 5.2. Contributions to Theory and Practice

Our study makes three important contributions to the academic literature and practice. First, we iterate between interviews and extant literature to identify the coordination strategies used when managing complex development activities. Previous research has identified several challenges when distributing product development project activities. These challenges include functional diferences (Dowlatshahi 1992; Wynstra et al. 2001, 2003), organizational cultures (Rai et al. 2009), time-zone separation (Espinosa and Carmel 2003), and geographic distance (Kotha and Srikanth 2013) between the partnering teams. In our study, which primarily involved physical product development activities from various industries such as automobile, aerospace, entertainment, and game development, we found that functional diversity was a given because of the nature of the work performed by the teams. Organizational cultural diferences also had very minimal impact on the day-to-day functioning of the project teams across boundaries. However, our interviews found that most teams faced with timezone diferences and language barriers experienced coordination challenges. This in turn resulted in frequent misunderstandings and delays during the development activities. This is especially interesting given the increase in ofshoring physical product development from the United States to Latin American countries instead of Asia (Amaral et al. 2011). For instance, Manning et al. (2008) reports that several organizations including General Electric, Siemens, and Texas Instruments have established technology centers in China, but tend to keep their innovation activities in North America and Europe to reduce delays from timezone diferences. Our research implies that doing so may necessitate a change in coordination strategies because integration mechanisms are diferentially efective against various coordination barriers. For example, a unifying strategy is of less benefit when no timezone barrier is present. Crucially, our research has also revealed that the efects of these mechanisms across a barrier may difer across project outcomes. Hence, we strongly argue that future researchers must design their studies with both of these diferential impacts in mind.

Second, our results on the coordination mechanisms ofer new insights to the existing literature that up to now has mostly prescribed strategies such as reducing the need for coordination through modular product development (Baldwin and Clark 1997), investing in ongoing communication and tacit coordination through colocating personnel (Srikanth and Puranam 2011), or general-purpose information systems without specifying specific features to improve coordination (O’Leary and Cummings 2007). We found that the vast majority of project teams in our study invested in these strategies, yet still experienced coordination issues.

Our analyses further show that some firms invested in a DIS to coordinate design information when focal and supplier firm personnel operated across language barriers and also identified the features of a DIS necessary to accomplish this goal. The use of DIS—such as engineering change order systems, task monitoring with CPM/PERT, and formal translation of user requirements into specifications and their interactions (e.g., through quality function deployment, design structure matrices, or axiomatic design) to improve modularity—can reduce the number of misunderstandings that require frequent intervention between focal and supplier firm personnel with diferent first languages, thereby improving the overall quality of development eforts. It can also help improve relationship performance between the focal and supplier personnel using diferent languages, by reducing misunderstandings. However, our survey results show that DIS strategies are not a panacea. For example, they seem of little help with respect to cost or response time against a language barrier. We speculate that this may be related to the fact that the misunderstandings that create these issues are not addressed by a DIS with the features we specified, i.e., critical path, document and engineering change order tracking, and modular design algorithms. Hence, there may be other features that should be included in a DIS. While employing a DIS is one strategy that exhibited significant variation both in terms of usage and project outcomes in our sample, there could be other information system strategies (not found in our sample) that might be efective for communicating complex component knowledge that is dificult to codify. Examples include the use of “avatar” 3D simulations and 3D printing in testing complex physical product development. Future research might explore the use of such advanced information systems to coordinate distributed project work. This strongly argues that future researchers need to attend to the actual feature set embodied by IT used to coordinate distributed projects.

In addition to DIS, our research introduced a new form of coordination strategy that involves empowering project managers with additional decision rights typically held by purchasing managers, which has been less well recognized by the coordination literature as shown in Figure 1. Specifically, we found that firms often unified purchasing and engineering into one function to coordinate work in distributed settings. We refer to this as a unifying strategy. It transfers decision-making power for purchasing from a purchasing department to the project managers (aka supply chain integrators, Parker and Anderson 2002) when dealing with suppliers around the world. This helped minimize any miscommunication issues between focal firms and their suppliers and facilitated tacit knowledge transfer between these entities, helping prevent deep misunderstandings that would result in significant rework, reducing cost by suppliers. It also improved cost, however, resulting in worse response time, albeit given that a unifying strategy did not damage the focalfirm supplier working relationship, this trade-of must be judged as acceptable to the firms who employed the unifying strategy. On the other hand, a unifying strategy did improve response time in the presence of time-zone barriers by reducing the number of personnel involved in the tedious communications loop associated with distributed product development. This indicates that purchasing departments’ responsibilities be confined to less communication-intensive settings than distributed product development. Overall, these results highlight the need for more research on project manager empowerment strategies to improve the coordination of distributed project work. While the unifying strategy emerged as an important integration mechanism from our interviews, there could be several other organizational modification strategies used to manage these projects. Some examples include colocation (controlled for in our research), using third firms that can act as “network orchestrators” (Hacki and Lighton 2001), and developing governing boards with members drawn from both a focal and its suppliers to negotiate disputes during distributed product development eforts. We urge scholars to further investigate these mechanisms mindfully, as we have shown in this study that integration mechanisms tend to be diferentially efective in bridging various coordination barriers and across several diferent outcomes.

At a higher level, our results suggest that barriers to efective distributed arrangements between focal firms and suppliers are afected diferentially and, hence, may require diferent integration mechanisms. Moreover, our work suggests that specifying the “features” of each integration mechanism studied (whether decision rights or background experience in boundary spanners or tools embedded in DIS) is crucial when studying distributed arrangements such as distributed product development. For example, unifying purchasing and project management, by giving the decision rights typically associated with purchasing to project managers, helped mitigate the efect of language barriers on cost (incurring a penalty in response time). In the presence of time-zone barriers, the eficacy of a unifying strategy was efectively reversed, by actually improving response time without impacting cost. By contrast, employing design information systems improved quality and working relationships, but only across language (not time-zone) barriers. The ultimate consequence of this complexity is that efectively managing projects in distributed settings like those in this study is also complex, and may require a suite of carefully coordinated integration mechanisms, carefully tailored to the particular barriers faced within an individual outsourcing relationship. Furthermore, difering integration mechanisms afect correspondingly diferent dimensions of project performance. We therefore urge product development researchers when studying distributed project performance to (1) use multiple outcome variables (Hoegl et al. 2004) and (2) investigate the suite of integration mechanisms employed as a whole on these projects as well as the feature sets of each of these individual mechanisms.

Finally, our research also ofers numerous insights to practicing managers. For instance, our conclusions on the importance of unifying purchasing and project management suggest that firms involved in distributed project eforts will benefit from training their supply chain integrators in the skill set required to manage the purchasing function. This might require substantial upfront investments from the firms to develop their supply chain managers, but the benefits can outweigh the costs. There are programs such as MIT Leaders for Global Operations as well as internal training in boundary spanning techniques (e.g., one of the authors has conducted extensive executive education for a Fortune-50 multinational on exactly these issues) that can develop these skills. Along similar lines, investing in a DIS can also be expensive given the need to train both their own personnel as well as their supplier personnel on tools such as design structure matrices. Nevertheless, these investments can repay significant dividends by improving quality and relationship performance when dealing with suppliers in various time zones.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our results sufer from a number of limitations that should be noted, one of them being the fact that the data were gathered between 2003 and 2008. Since that time, one would imagine that capabilities to manage these issues have changed, and hopefully improved. However, even with these improvements, the coordination problems have clearly not disappeared, as evidenced by well-known recent projects such as the Boeing 787 Dreamliner (Gates 2013), the Afordable Care Act (ACA or “Obamacare”) websites (Cohen 2014), and Toyota’s recent recalls (Levick 2013). Additionally, two of the authors teach project management at highly ranked Big 10 and Big 12 institutions in executive education to practitioners from various industries. Our conversations with these executives suggest that coordination issues are still prevalent, relating stories that sound remarkably like those in our interview sample with respect to task coordination problems, misunderstandings based on language issues, and arguments with the purchasing departments. Furthermore, they indicate reasons why outsourcing coordination issues have not disappeared. For one, these students seem to indicate that reliance on outsourcing may be increasing. For another, the functionality of CPM-PERT, implementations of engineering change order management systems, and design structure matrices were literally a decade or more old, even in 2008. Yet many of our students are in firms that still do not fully employ these systems in 2015. Hence, we expect that the findings with respect to important features of DIS will still hold. Finally, the technical fixes of virtual conferencing have not advanced much beyond teleconferencing. Admittedly, sometimes this is for unexpected reasons. As one manager from a Fortune-20 firm stated, “half our people have their cameras taped over with duct-tape to avoid being spied upon.” However, this does raise an interesting question concerning the levels of immersive virtual experience that will bring a material jump in improving the “bandwidth” of communication. Will Oculus-Rift really be suficient to remedy these issues or will something much more advanced be required?

We would also acknowledge the small sample size (N <sup></sup> 55 projects) in our empirical analyses. This was necessary, as we were gathering a tremendous amount of qualitative data that involved multiple interviews with the project teams and field visits. It also allowed us to ofer insights that can explain some of the results from the regression analyses. That said, we urge future studies to collect large sample data that can help in generalization of these findings. Finally, we acknowledge that there could be potential sources of uncontrolled endogeneity in our empirical strategy. Although we took all possible eforts through inclusion of relevant controls and interpreting the insights using qualitative interview data, we are aware that no estimation procedure is perfect, and while we believe in the robustness of our findings, we cannot rule out future studies to find diferences in other settings.

All of the above limitations can translate into several rich areas of future research. For example, more research is required to measure the degree of miscommunication that is actually occurring on a day-to-day basis as a result of language barriers. What are the types of miscommunications that can occur during distributed product development eforts? Is there a critical tipping point, as speculated by Taylor and Ford (2007), beyond which miscommunications can actually result in the abandonment of a project? What sorts of techniques can render all project management personnel more efective in avoiding miscommunications in the face of language barriers? Although our study begins to ofer insights on these questions, we are just scratching the surface. Clearly, deeper insights into these issues ofer a promising direction of inquiry for future researchers.

## A.1. Semistructured Protocol Used in the Study (Subset of Protocol)

1. Background. What is your current position? What has been your career history? Who works for you now?

2. Project background. Which product(s)/project(s) are you involved with primarily? Where is your product/project designed? Where is it manufactured (if appropriate)?

3. Supply chain relationships. What interactions with suppliers (or customers) do your job typically require on a regular basis?

4. Product and process development. Using the product/ project you most recently completed as an example, describe how you carried the vision from the original design concept to final release and distribution.

5. Development challenges. What challenges do you face in product development? Can you give an example?

6. Coordination. When there is a design or development conflict between your firm and the supplier, how do you resolve it? Please give an example or two.

7. Personnel. Are some personnel more efective than others at supply chain coordination? Please give an example or two.

## A.2. Measuring Language Diference Using Linguistic Diference

Based on West and Graham (2004), we measure language diference using a linguistic genealogical classification that allowed us to calculate the distance between English and other languages. This approach accounts for both the dominant language as well as the secondary language spoken by the vast majority of people living in the country (based on the CIA World Factbook). One advantage of using this measure is its ability to diferentiate between multilingual countries.

For instance, consider a project from our sample between a focal firm in the United States and a supplier firm in the Philippines. The primary language spoken in the Philippines will be Tagalog while the secondary language spoken in this location is Cebuan. The average linguistic diference between the United States and the Philippines based on this measure is 7. As another example, the linguistic diference between personnel in a U.S. firm interacting with supplier personnel from South Africa will have a score of 1 (the primary language in South Africa is Afrikaans and the secondary language is English). As seen from these descriptions, our approach allows us to capture both variation across diferent language-pairs as well as adjust for multiple languages spoken within a given region.
