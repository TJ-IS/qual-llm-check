# ENTERPRISE SYSTEMS AND M&A OUTCOMES FOR ACQUIRERS AND TARGETS<sup>1</sup>

Chengxin Cao Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College New York, NY, U.S.A. {chengxin.cao@baruch.cuny.edu}

Gautam Ray, Mani Subramani, and Alok Gupta Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {gautamr@umn.edu} {subra010@umn.edu} {alok@umn.edu}

This study examines the impact of coordination capabilities provided by enterprise systems (ES), manifested in ES standardization and extensiveness, on merger and acquisition (M&A) outcomes in the short and long term. Specifically, we examine the extent to which the ES standardization and ES extensiveness of the acquiring and target firms contribute to value creation in M&A initiatives. We also study the relationship between the ES standardization and ES extensiveness of the acquiring and target firms and M&A offer premiums. The empirical analysis suggests that the ES standardization of acquirers is related to lower offer premiums and a higher market response to the acquisition for the acquirer. However, it is the ES extensiveness of the acquirer that improves long-term performance i.e., decreases goodwill impairment and increases operating performance. The analysis also indicates that the target’s ES standardization increases the premium for the target firm and generates a positive market response to the acquisition for the target firm. Overall, the analysis indicates that the ES standardization likely affects the integration cost that influences market response to the M&A and to the M&A premium in the short term, but it is ES extensiveness that affects the realized synergy from the M&A that affects longterm performance.

Keywords: Mergers and acquisitions (M&As), cumulative abnormal return (CAR), offer premium, goodwill impairment, operating performance, enterprise system (ES) extensiveness, enterprise system (ES) standardization

## Introduction

Mergers and acquisitions (M&As) have become increasingly popular over the last few decades. In 2017, 50,600 M&A transactions were completed around the world with a total deal value of 350 trillion USD (IMAA 2018). Acquiring firms typically pay a 30-50% premium above the market value of target firms to the shareholders of the target firm (Laamanen, 2007). Further, there is considerable evidence from prior M&A events that 70-90% of deals destroyed shareholder value for the acquiring firm (Christensen et al., 2011). Why do acquiring firms pay significant amounts above the value that the market assigns to the target firm and what are the circumstances of M&A deals that create value for acquirers are both questions of great interest for researchers and practitioners.

M&As may allow firms to extend the deployment of their existing resources and capabilities to novel contexts (Capron, 1999; Capron & Shen, 2007; Kaul, 2012), creating value by improving the performance of the acquired firm (Berchicci et al., 2012). This improvement in outcomes for the joint firm can arise from a variety of sources. In some instances, the combined firm can achieve better asset and knowledge utilization by allocating production more efficiently across multiple geographies (e.g., McGuckin & Nguyen, 1995). In other instances, the joint firm can make better decisions by pooling information and sharing knowledge across operating units (Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005). In the IS literature, Du (2015) highlights the role of M&As in enabling hospital chains to create value by deploying their industry-specific digital capabilities in other hospitals that they acquire. In addition to the resource deployment perspective, acquisitions are also a means for acquirers to obtain new resources and capabilities (Kaul & Wu, 2016; Puranam et al., 2009), allowing them to bridge capability gaps (Capron & Mitchell, 2009) and enter new markets (Lee & Lieberman, 2010).

Whether an M&A is executed to derive value from deploying current resources to novel contexts or for new resource acquisition, the ability to appropriately integrate the processes of target and acquiring firms is the single most important factor in realizing synergy (Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999; Wheelwright et al., 2000) and enhancing the performance of the combined firm (Zollo & Singh, 2004). In this vein, Capron (1999) relates integration to resource redeployment and knowledge transfer between the acquiring and target firms and found that resource redeployment and knowledge transfer increased performance. To this end, this research focuses on value creation and premiums in M&As through the lens of resource coordination—a capability that is enhanced by the firm’s adoption of enterprise systems (henceforth, ES) (Roberts et al., 2012; Sarazzin & West, 2011; Hedman & Sarker, 2015).

Increasingly, the ability of firms to integrate and redeploy resources both within and across business units arises largely from the coordination capabilities provided by ES (Hedman & Sarker, 2015; Cao et al., 2022). Insights based on detailed case studies in the IS literature suggest that complex business transformations involve a range of choices regarding the standardization and integration of business processes across functional areas and business units (Wheelwright et al., 2000; Beath & Ross, 2010). As information systems become central to the management and operational control of enterprises, the outcomes of M&A initiatives hinge on the challenge of merging the information systems of target and acquiring firms (Yoo et al., 2007; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007). Consistent with the resource-based and capabilities perspective of competition (Teece et al., 1997; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Barua et al., 2004), we view ES installed at acquiring and target firms as key capabilities for value creation from M&A initiatives. Specifically, we focus on two aspects of ES in this paper: (1) ES standardization—the compatibility of different ES modules across the member sites of the organization, and (2) ES extensiveness—the functional and organizational breadth of the implementation of the ES modules. We contend that the capital market reaction, value realized, and premium paid are likely to be influenced by the integration and coordination capabilities provided by ES in acquiring and target firms.

The theoretical contribution of this paper is the argument that ES standardization provides resource coordination capabilities that enable short-term value creation from the M&A through accurate estimation of the synergy and cost of integrating the target and by reducing the cost of integrating the target, while resource coordination capabilities from ES extensiveness impact long-term value creation from the M&A by helping to realize the opportunities for value creation by redeploying the resources of the acquiring and target firms. The empirical analysis supports our theory and suggests that the ES standardization of acquirers is related to a lower offer premium and a higher market response to the acquisition for the acquirer, whereas the ES extensiveness of the acquirer is associated with lower goodwill impairment and higher operating performance. The analysis also indicates that the target’s ES standardization increases the premium and generates a positive market response for the target. Overall, the analysis suggests that it is the ES standardization of the acquiring and target firms that likely reduces the cost of integration and thus affects the market response to the M&A for the acquiring and target firms, respectively. Similarly, the ES standardization of the acquiring and target firms also likely improves the estimates of the potential synergy and integration cost of the M&A, and thus influences the M&A premium in the short term for the acquiring and target firms, respectively. However, it is the ES extensiveness of the acquirer that affects the realized synergy from the M&A and thus affects long-term M&A performance.

## Theory and Hypotheses

M&As are contests between firms in the market for corporate control over the productive resources of potential targets. The central argument underlying this view is that the lower the stock price of the target, relative to what it could be with more efficient resource utilization and management (after acquisition), the more attractive the takeover becomes to potential acquirers who believe that they can manage the two firms combined more efficiently than currently possible with the target firm being a stand-alone entity. However, there are often multiple potential acquirers with strategically related resources that can create value through synergies and whose estimates of the economic value created from acquiring the target firm are comparable. As a result, the competitive bidding among them to acquire the target firm raises the premium (above the market price of the target) to equal the expected synergistic gains from the acquisition. Thus, when the market for corporate control is perfectly competitive, abnormal returns for acquiring firms are expected to be null (Barney, 1988).

The market for corporate control is imperfect when an acquiring firm possesses unique value-creating resources and capabilities that competing bidders do not possess and when competitors cannot duplicate the synergies and cash flows attributable to these resources (Barney, 1988; Chatterjee, 1986). In such instances, the competitive bidding process raises the premium paid for the target only to the level of the synergistic value to other potential acquirers. The incremental value attributable to the acquiring firm’s unique capabilities is then reflected in the outcomes (e.g., abnormal returns) for the acquiring firm. Analogously, it is also likely that target firms possess unique resources that have complementary and synergistic value, which is realizable when combined with the resources of the acquiring firm. The value of these target firm resources is likely to be reflected in the outcomes (e.g., capital market reaction) for target firms when the merger becomes public knowledge. Situations and contingencies associated with outcomes for acquiring and target firms therefore continue to be of great interest to both practitioners and academics (Capron & Pistre, 2002).

We adopt the resource-based and organizational capabilities perspective, where firms compete by acquiring, integrating, and reconfiguring distinctive resources and capabilities (Ray et al., 2013; Teece et al., 1997; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Building on this perspective, we contend that ES create organizational capabilities that enable firms to engage in M&As by helping them to acquire new resources, integrate them with existing resources, and reconfigure and redeploy resources in new contexts to generate economic value. In this way, the ES capabilities of the acquiring and target firms can create private, uniquely valuable, and inimitable synergistic cash flows. More specifically, we examine value creation through the impact of ES capabilities on the potential risk and return from the M&A across both the short- and long term.

On the one hand, the ES-enabled capability to integrate operations across multiple businesses has the potential to contribute to post-M&A firm performance (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Benitez et al., 2018). This is because an extensive set of business processes managed through ES across a large portion of its business units allows managers to integrate business processes across the enterprise and realize resource synergies (Hedman & Sarker, 2015), facilitating opportunities for unique resource combinations to produce new growth avenues in the newly combined entity. For instance, Tanriverdi and Du (2011) highlighted how EMC’s ES enabled the firm to effectively integrate the business operations of the 50 businesses that it acquired between 2005 and 2010 to become a vendor of comprehensive solutions to large enterprises.

On the other hand, there are many uncertainties regarding the synergy realization. First, the possible disruption stemming from IT integration is a major concern for the capital market in the short term. When an M&A deal is announced, neither the acquirer nor the capital market is fully aware of the extent of the integration complexities. Second, IT resources contribute significantly to firm risk (Dewan & Ren, 2011). Prior studies on the stock market reaction to IT investment announcements echo a similar point, where the stock market reaction ranges from negative, to no effect, to significant positive effects (Hunter, 2003; Dehning et al., 2003; Subramani & Walden, 2001) depending on firm characteristics and the types of IT investment. Thus, although the ES-enabled capabilities of the acquiring and target firms offer potential synergies to the combined entity, there is also a high level of uncertainty and risk in the realization of such synergy. In the following sections, we theorize the impact of ES-enabled capabilities on the stock market reaction (cumulative abnormal return), valuation of the target firm (offer premium), and synergy realization (goodwill impairment and operating performance) in M&As, through the role of ES in risk and return from M&As.

We conceptualize ES-enabled capabilities as varying along two dimensions. The work of Du (2015) highlights that the ability of multibusiness firms to acquire, integrate and redeploy digital resources to create value through M&A is influenced by the level of IT standardization and IT extensiveness. IT standardization refers to the common IT tools and systems implemented to support common organizational activities within the firm (Du, 2015). In contrast, IT extensiveness refers to the functional and organizational scope of IT tools and systems used in a firm to support business activities. Analogously, ES standardization refers to the use of ES modules from the same vendor implemented across different units of an organization to support common organizational activities. In a similar fashion, ES extensiveness refers to the variety of different functional ES modules and the organizational breadth of the implementation of different ES modules. ES standardization and ES extensiveness are seen as contributing to the acquisition readiness of firms (Yetton et al., 2013). In the following, we provide detailed theoretical arguments for each hypothesis and Table 2 summarizes the five hypotheses.

## Acquirer ES Standardization and Capital Market Response

The capital market’s reaction to M&A announcements reflects a forward-looking estimation of the potential value creation or destruction from combining the acquiring and target firms. When the cost of integrating the two entities and the synergy created are uncertain, the capital market takes a conservative stance (Laamanen, 2007). Among all the uncertainties, integrating IT capabilities is one of the most risky challenges (Henningsson & Carlsson, 2011; Tanriverdi & Du, 2011). After the M&A deal is completed, the acquirer has to systematically evaluate the two entities’ IT resources and make detailed integration plans to decide how to integrate different operating models, business processes, IT infrastructure, and business applications. However, at the time of the M&A announcement, neither the acquirer nor the capital market may be fully aware of the extent of the integration complexities. Prior literature has highlighted that the immediate concern of the capital market regarding the M&A announcement is the risk of IT integration disrupting the target’s operations (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2015). We argue that the ES standardization of the acquirer provides information about the acquirer’s IT integration capabilities and generates a positive market reaction.

Firms with higher levels of ES standardization have a greater number of common functional processes across business units managed using the ES modules from the same vendor, reducing technology costs and ensuring uniform service quality within the enterprise (Toppenberg et al., 2015). In comparison, in firms where ES standardization is low, similar business processes are often handled differently within various business units, leading to limitations in data and process integration across units (Ross et al., 2006). In the M&A context, the level of ES standardization across the sites of the acquiring firm has implications for the costs and the complexity of integrating target firms (Yetton et al., 2013).

First, firms with higher levels of ES standardization can reduce the complexity of the technology consolidation involved in combining post-merger operations of acquiring and target firms. ES standardization can reduce integration costs due to the inherent ease of replication of standardized ES modules across business units (Wheelwright et al., 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2007). ES standardization may also facilitate the harmonization of operations of acquiring and target firms with minimal disruptions (Sarazzin & West, 2011). Second, acquirers with greater ES standardization also have experience implementing ES modules in different contexts within the firm and thus have stronger ES implementation capabilities (Venkatesh et al., 2007), through routine refinement and expertise building (Henningsson, 2015). Implementing ES modules in one organizational unit builds expertise that can be redeployed in subsequent implementations, e.g., redeployed in integrating target firms. Hence, we propose that the integration cost reduction effect and the future implementation capabilities provided by the acquirer’s ES standardization should mitigate concerns about the acquirer’s IT integration capability, leading to a positive capital market reaction to the M&A announcement.

In comparison, ES extensiveness, while providing valuecreation potential, can complicate the integration process and impose short-term risks. The wide availability of ES in the acquirer, when not standardized, contains distinct operating models and processes. As a result, the integration with the target’s ES requires more rationalization and reconfiguration and is thus more likely to cause disruptions in the short term. We therefore hypothesize:

H1: The greater the ES standardization of the acquiring firm, the more positive will be the capital market reaction (i.e., CAR) to the M&A announcement for the acquiring firm.

## Acquirer ES Standardization and M&A Premiums

The M&A premium is defined as the acquirer’s payment for the target firm above the target’s market value. M&A premiums are judgments about potential synergy that acquiring firms make based on uncertain and ambiguous information inputs from diverse sources of information. One stream of literature deems the M&A premium a measure of overpayment and suggestive of low-quality decision-making by the acquirer (Sirower, 1997) because payments above the market value of the target end up offsetting the benefits from combining the acquirer and target operations. This literature highlights reasons, including managerial hubris (Hayward & Hambrick, 1997), bidding competition (Shelton, 2000), board interlocks and investment advisors, and resistance to takeover (Beckman & Haunschild, 2002; Haunschild, 1994), to explain the sizable and positive premiums paid by acquirers.

Another stream of literature believes that positive premiums can be explained by the private, uniquely valuable, and inimitable synergistic cash flows from the acquiring and target entities combined (Laamanen, 2007). To support this argument, there must be information asymmetry between the acquirer and the stock market, i.e., the acquirer must value the target firm higher than the market, on average. This occurs when the acquirer has a unique fit with the target, where the value generated exceeds the amount that an average acquirer would expect; and the value of such unique synergy with acquirer is not incorporated in the target’s market value before the acquirer’s bid. We take a similar stance and argue that the premium is explained by the private, uniquely valuable, and inimitable synergistic cash flows attributable to features of the ES of the acquiring and target firms.

First, the due diligence process allows firms to obtain private information from the target firm about the target’s current financial position, future projections, and the valuation of their assets and IT capabilities. Hence, an acquirer with high forecasting and investment decision-making capabilities will excel in selecting a target firm with which it has a unique fit and thus private and unique future cash flows. Prior studies have highlighted the role of ES standardization in improving the firm’s internal information environment and thus management forecast capabilities. ES provide integration of information across business processes, functions, and geographies, resulting in information with enhanced completeness, transparency, quality, and timeliness (Spathis, 2006). We propose that the ES standardization of the acquirer can effectively improve the data transparency and quality and hence enhance management forecast accuracy and investment decision-making.

Second, there is often considerable ambiguity in estimating the costs of integrating the target’s resources into the acquiring firm. For the capital market, the future cash flow generated from this combined entity largely depends on the success of the integration process. We suggest that acquiring firms that have invested in standardized ES modules across a larger variety of functional and geographic areas are likely to have an advantage in the integration process. Not only could ES standardization reduce integration costs by replicating standardized modules in the target firm, but the process of standardization also provides information about the cost of integrating the target. Firms with higher levels of ES standardization are likely to possess the knowledge and expertise involved in realistically estimating the task complexity and effort involved in integrating business operations of target firms. In contrast, firms without this advantage are likely to be misinformed and underestimate integration costs. Since post-merger integration costs are, on average, 14% of acquisition costs (Dean, 2014), underestimates of the integration costs and a consequent overestimation of potential synergy from the merger are likely to lead firms to offer higher premiums for targets, compared to firms with more realistic estimates.

We expect that acquiring firms with greater ES standardization would have a better internal information environment and management forecast capabilities, a betterinformed assessment of the cost of integration, and thus a reduced likelihood of making underinformed or misinformed judgments regarding M&A initiatives. In comparison, although ES extensiveness offers a high level of digitalization across the organization, it does not necessarily lead to better information timeliness and accuracy, as different systems might have different data standards, formats, etc. More importantly, ES extensiveness does not effectively inform the task complexity and effort involved in integrating the business operations of target firms. A firm with high ES extensiveness but low standardization typically implements ES from different vendors in a decentralized manner, making it difficult to estimate the cost of integrating the target processes with those of the acquirer. Ultimately, while the acquirer must pay the shareholders of the target firm enough for them to sell and must pay enough to beat out other potential bidders, the acquirer wants to pay as little as possible for the target firm. Thus, the more informed the acquirer is about ES standardization, the less likely the acquirer is to overpay. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2: The greater the ES standardization of the acquiring firm, the lower the M&A premiums will be.

## Acquirer ES Extensiveness and Long-term Performance

Firms engage in M&As to realize potential synergies from combining target resources with acquirer resources. This synergy is reflected in the long-term performance of the combined acquiring and target firms after the M&A, and can be measured by the goodwill impairment and operating performance of the combined unit. Goodwill is an intangible asset of the acquiring firm; it is recorded in an acquisition where what the acquirer pays for the target is more than the fair value of the target’s separable assets. Goodwill impairment charges happen when the excess premium paid above the value of the target’s separable assets is no longer justified (Goodman et al., 2014). Thus, the magnitude of postacquisition goodwill impairment reflects the expected synergy from the M&A that is not realized (Gu & Lev, 2011; Goodman et al., 2014). In contrast to goodwill impairment, the operating performance of the combined unit reflects the synergies realized from the M&A. The operating performance (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011) of the M&A is the earnings performance the acquirer (i.e., the acquiring and target firms combined) experiences in the years after the M&A. We contend that the ES extensiveness of the acquirer influences the synergies realized from the M&A over the long term and are reflected in the post-M&A goodwill impairment charges and operating performance.

ES extensiveness reflects the levels of the organizational and functional scope of ES (Ranganathan & Brown, 2006). Firms with higher levels of ES extensiveness have a greater variety of business processes managed through different ES modules (greater functional scope). They also have a larger number of business units managing their processes through ES (greater organizational scope) (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). Thus, an acquirer with higher ES extensiveness has a more extensive set of business processes managed through ES across a larger portion of its business units.

Higher ES extensiveness enables the development of crossunit information processing and data integration capabilities (Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005). These capabilities allow firms to establish value-creating resource combinations across the units of the combined firm that enhance synergy (Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005; Nevo & Wade, 2010). The synergy from enhanced coordination of interdependencies across geographically dispersed units enabled by ES is also highlighted in prior studies in the manufacturing context (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Cotteleer & Bendoly et. al, 2006). This involves novel combinations of business process modules and firm resources into resource bundles that create unique opportunities for new products and services and new avenues for growth for the combined firm (Subramani & Venkatraman, 2003; Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005). This suggests that the greater the extensiveness of a firm’s ES, the greater is the expected and realized synergy a firm can generate through resource combinations with a target firm in an M&A. In contrast, although ES standardization reduces the “one-time” cost of integration, long-term synergy realization relies on a steady stream of future cash flow generated from resource combinations and coordination. Therefore, ES standardization is less likely to have a significant impact on long-term synergy realization. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: The greater the ES extensiveness of the acquiring firm, the greater the long-term M&A performance (i.e., lower goodwill impairment and higher operating performance) will be.

## Target ES and Target Outcomes

Prior research on ES investments suggests that these investments can create significant value for firms (Gefen and Ragowsky, 2005; Hitt et al., 2002). Studies examining ES announcements have suggested that market value is better for ES projects that are high in functional scope (number of modules) and the extent of deployment (number of sites) than for those where the investments are narrower (Ranganathan & Brown, 2006). However, studies have also highlighted that the complementary changes to operational processes and organizational structures associated with technology adoption can be disruptive, causing firms to face competitive disadvantages, and are thus potentially risky (Tian & Xu, 2015). Some studies have suggested that IT investments enhance market value for small rather than large firms (Otim et al., 2012) while others have found that IT investment announcements enhance market value only for investments viewed as transformational and in industries where technologies are viewed as strategic (Dehning et al., 2003). Some studies have even found that IT investments reduce market valuations (Subramani & Walden, 2001; Hunter, 2003).

Taken together, these studies provide evidence that the inherent riskiness of complex IT investments such as ES contributes to considerable asymmetries in assessments of firm value between the stock market and managers within the firm. Since markets are likely to adopt a conservative stance in valuing firms, a firm’s market value may not fully represent the true value of the future cash flows created through its IT investments when it is valued as a stand-alone firm (Zollo & Meier, 2008). However, when information regarding the merger becomes public, investors and analysts are likely to recognize the potential for synergies and the lowered costs and complexity of integrating with the acquiring firm, which is attributable to target ES standardization. As a result, the announcement of the acquisition when the identity of the acquiring firm and the expectations for the M&A become public is likely to lead to abnormal market returns for the target firm (Laamanen, 2007).

Further, the difficulty of assessing the value created by merging the target resources with those of the acquirer has significant consequences for the premiums paid for a target firm with significant investments in ES standardization. Acquirers use investment advisors and perform analyses of target firms’ capital investments and details of information systems supporting firm processes as part of the due diligence process (Yetton et al., 2013). Also, a target firm is motivated to provide private information to communicate the value created by their forward-looking ES investments to potential acquirers (Reuer et al., 2012). In particular, they are likely to highlight that target ES standardization may simplify the complexity of process reconfiguration and restructuring in the post-acquisition period and reduce the overall costs of integration (Du, 2015). As a result, before the M&A is announced, the difference between the market’s valuation and the acquirer’s private valuation is likely to be higher in targets with more standardized ES, thus increasing the premium that acquirers are willing to pay. However, as the M&A becomes public, as discussed above, the market is likely to realize the value creation attributable to the target ES standardization that increases the target CAR. Therefore, higher levels of ES standardization of target firms are likely to be reflected in both higher private valuation (higher premiums) and higher market response (higher CAR) for the target firm. We therefore hypothesize:

H4: The greater the ES standardization of the target firm, the more positive will be the capital market reaction (i.e., CAR) to the M&A announcement for the target firm.

H5: The greater the ES standardization of the target firm, the higher the M&A premiums paid to the target will be.

## Data and Empirical Strategy

## Sample Construction

We used data on M&A announcements from the year 2009 to 2018. In our sample, both the acquiring and target firms are public firms headquartered in the United States or Canada. The M&A announcements are extracted from the Securities Data Corporation (SDC) database. Our datasets combine data from the following five sources: (1) The SDC database provides information on M&A, including deal characteristics and offer premium. (2) Compustat provides firm-level control variables. Variables used to calculate capital investment efficiency, goodwill impairment, and operating performance of the firms are also from Compustat. (3) The Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) provides stock price information: namely, the market capitalization of the acquiring and target firms and CAR. (4) The Harte-Hanks CI database (Harte-Hanks) is used to calculate the ES standardization and extensiveness, as well as other IT controls. (5) The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) provides information to calculate industry relatedness.

There are three major samples used in this paper. The first one matches the acquirers in the M&A announcements to all the other data sources mentioned above (i.e., the acquirer sample). The second sample matches the target firms to other data sources (i.e., the target sample). The third sample matches both the acquirers and targets to the other data sources: the unit of analysis in this case is at the acquirer-target firm-pair level (i.e., the combined acquirer and target samples).<sup>2</sup>

In order to construct the acquirer sample, we first extracted all the SDC deal announcements between 2009 and 2018. Second, we removed M&A announcements with the deal form/type of buyback, exchange offer, recapitalization, repurchase, and selftender offer (Shenoy, 2012). Third, we kept all the deals with publicly traded acquirers. Fourth, the SDC acquirers were linked to Harte-Hanks firms and Compustat. We removed the deal if the acquirer’s market-to-book ratio (MB) or return-onequity (ROE) was higher than 1000% or the offer premium was higher than 6000% (Schwert, 2000). We constructed the target sample in a similar way. Whenever there are several bids for the same target (occurring within one year of the first bid), we kept only the first bid (to remove the possible confounding effects from other bids). The combined acquirer and target sample was constructed in a similar fashion.

## Measurements

## Dependent Variables

There are four dependent variables. The first dependent variable, CAR [-2, 2], measures the market reaction to the announcement of the M&A in a five-day window around the announcement of the M&A, where the announcement day is day 0. Here, we used an event study methodology (Brown & Warner, 1980, 1985) to study the forward-looking expectation of the capital market on M&A announcements. The assumption here is that the capital market is efficient i.e., all the publicly available and relevant information on the acquiring and target firms is incorporated and reflected in the stock price. CAR is measured relative to a CRSP valueweighted market model: $\begin{array} { r } { C A R [ - 2 , 2 ] _ { i } = \sum _ { t = - 2 } ^ { t = + 2 } ( R _ { i t } - \alpha _ { i } - } \end{array}$ $\beta _ { i } R _ { m t } )$ , where $R _ { i t }$ is the return to the acquiring or target firm on trading day $t , R _ { m t }$ is the return on the Standard & Poor’s Composite Index on day t. The market model regression parameters, $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\beta _ { i } ,$ are estimated using data from trading day -205 to -6: $R _ { i t } = \alpha _ { i } + \beta _ { i } R _ { m t } + \epsilon _ { i t } , t = - 2 0 5 , \ldots , - 6 .$ . This choice of event window is consistent with previous studies (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Fuller et al., 2002; Hayward, 2002). Given the possibility of other acquisitions and confounding events in this [-205, -6] estimation window, we also used value-weighted or equal-weighted market returns (Fuller et al., 2002; Haleblian & Finkelstein, 1999) as proxies of normal returns $( \alpha _ { i } + \beta _ { i } R _ { m t } )$ . The models with CAR calculated using value- or equal-weighted market returns as expected normal returns are highly consistent with the results we report in this paper.

The second dependent variable is the offer premium. It is measured as the premium of the offer price compared to the target closing stock price four weeks prior to the original announcement date, expressed as a percentage (((offer price – target closing stock price 4 weeks prior) / target closing stock price 4 weeks prior) × 100).

While CAR and the offer premium can be viewed as ex ante (short-term) measures of the synergy expected by the capital market and management, the goodwill impairment losses and the operating performance are ex post (long-term) measures of realized synergy and measure the long-term performance of an acquisition. Goodwill impairment losses are recorded in the three-year period following an acquisition. At the time of acquisition, the difference between the value of an acquired firm’s assets and the acquisition price, i.e., the acquisition premium, is entered into the acquiring firm’s books as “goodwill.” Goodwill impairment charges are accounting adjustments made by firms when a prior acquisitions’ goodwill—the premium paid above the value of the separable assets—is no longer justified (Goodman et al., 2014). The magnitude of the goodwill impairment entry in any year is the amount by which the goodwill on the books needs to be reduced to reflect the lower value of the acquired firm in that year. We used the sum of the absolute value of goodwill impairment<sup>3</sup> that occurred in the three years after an M&A as our third dependent variable, as a measure of the expected synergy from the M&A that was not realized.

The fourth dependent variable is operating performance (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). We used earnings before interest, tax, and depreciation divided by total assets (EBITD/TA) to measure the firm’s operating performance. Specifically, in our model, we needed to measure the operating performance $P _ { i t }$ of the acquirer and target pair i in year t both before and after the M&A took place. $P _ { i t }$ of the combined acquirer and target pair i before the M&A took place was calculated as the sum of the EBITD divided by the sum of total assets of the acquirer and the target; $P _ { i t }$ after the M&A took place is simply the acquirer’s EBITD/TA since the target was absorbed into the acquirer. Using EBITD to measure operating performance is ideal because it is not affected by special items, interest expense, and tax considerations (Barber & Lyon, 1996). In our combined acquirer and target model, we compared the change in $P _ { i t }$ of an actual acquirer and target pair after the M&A took place to the change in a counterfactual pair of firms where no acquisition occurred. If the change in $P _ { i t }$ is significantly higher in the actual acquirer and target pair than it is in the counterfactual acquirer-target pair, it is an indication that the M&A created abnormal operating performance gains.

## Independent Variables

Our independent variables measure the two dimensions of ES: standardization and extensiveness. Consistent with Du (2015), ES standardization refers to the use of common ES modules from the same vendor implemented across different units of an organization; ES extensiveness refers to the variety and the organizational breadth of the implementation of different ES modules. In our analysis, we consider five ES modules: accounting/finance (ACC), human resources (HR), workflow (WORKFLOW), customer relationship management (CRM), and supply chain management (SCM).

For each site, Harte-Hanks surveyed an employee at the site who was directly involved in IT management. The Harte-Hanks database provides information on the status of implementation of these five modules (installed or planned). We coded the module availability as 1 when it was listed as “installed.” Harte-Hanks also provides information on which vendor is used for each of the five modules in each site.

ES standardization is measured by an entropy measure to assess the diversity of the ES module vendors used across the different sites of the firm (Du, 2015). Controlling for the size of the firms and its sites, a higher value of STD suggests that fewer vendors are used across the five ES modules. The largest possible value of STD is zero; it is achieved when for each of the five ES modules, the firm only uses one vendor.

The firm-level module implementation is the proportion of employees at the firm that use the ES module. Based on the PCA results, we took the first principal component (it has an eigenvalue of 2.41 and is the only component with an eigenvalue higher than 1) as our measure of ES extensiveness.<sup>4</sup> ES extensiveness increases when either the functional scope (the firm implements a new ES module) or the organizational scope of ES (the firm implements the modules already in use onto sites that did not install them) is deepened.

## Control Variables

Based on previous studies on M&A and different economic outcomes, we controlled for a variety of factors, including the deal characteristics and accounting performance of the acquirer and target that could potentially confound the relationships of interest in this paper. The detailed definition of control variables is provided in Table 1.

The deal characteristic controls include hostile deal, tender offer, cash financed, toehold, merger of equals, number of competing bids, and the vertical relatedness and horizontal complementarity between the acquirer and target industries. The bargaining hypotheses predict that target managers resist hostile offers to improve the terms of a takeover offer; therefore, hostility is positively related to the offer premium (Schwert, 2000). Prior literature shows that the stock market reacts positively to cash-financed deals (Fuller et al., 2002; Travlos, 1987) because cash offers signal higher levels of certainty on the synergy of the deal. Previous work has shown that the toehold correlates with the offer premium and the target stock market reaction (Gaspar et al., 2005). The presence of competition for the same target firm increases the premium paid (Gaspar et al., 2005). We also controlled for two additional deal characteristics, tender offer and merger of equals, as recognized by previous studies (Officer, 2003; Laamanen, 2007). We also controlled for the vertical relatedness and horizontal complementarity between the acquirer and target industries since related deals are more likely to realize synergy and create a positive market reaction (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011).

In addition to the above deal characteristics, we also controlled for the financial advisor’s reputation. In the stock market reaction model, we controlled for the offer premium in addition to the above deal characteristics. In the acquirer model, we controlled for the acquirer’s financial performance, including return on equity (ROE), market to book ratio (M/B), sales growth, liquidity, debt to equity ratio (D/E) and price to earnings ratio (P/E), size, and relative size of the acquisition. Managers of high-performing firms are more confident in their capabilities to make successful acquisitions and are therefore willing to pay a higher premium (Hayward, 2002). Thus, we control for the ROE of the acquirer. Acquirers with more slack resources can afford to pay a higher premium for the target firm. Thus, we controlled for the liquidity of the acquirer. Following the finance M&A literature, to predict offer premium as well as stock market reaction (Gaspar et al., 2005; Schwert, 2000), we also included market/book (M/B) ratio, sales growth, and D/E and P/E ratio of the acquirer as control variables. Larger acquirers tend to have more financial resources to support M&A transactions (size of the firm). Previous studies show that the relative size of the acquisition affects acquisition performance (Fowler & Schmidt, 1989)—size ratio. Thus, it can influence the acquirer’s willingness to pay and how the stock market views the deal because of this performance effect. Since an acquirer may be willing to pay a higher premium for a target that is more profitable, we also controlled for the target’s earnings per share. We also controlled for the other IT investments of the acquiring firm, including PC and servers per employee, to make sure the effects of ES on the offer premium, market reaction, and long-term performance actually came from ES rather than general IT investments.

In the target model, we controlled for the same deal characteristics listed above: i.e., financial measures including ROE, M/B, sales growth, liquidity, D/E, P/E, size and other IT investments, and target advisor reputation. Better-performing target firms can receive higher premiums and generate positive market reactions (Gaspar et al., 2005). Acquirers who want to boost their growth might prefer targets with higher growth potential and are therefore willing to pay more. Thus, we controlled for target growth potential, measured as M/B ratio and sales growth. Targets with more slack resources might be more attractive to potential acquirers. Larger target firms are more costly to integrate. Therefore, we controlled for the size of the target. Since there are fewer observations in the combined acquirer and target model, we use fewer controls in this model.

## Empirical Strategy

Our dependent variables are only observable when potential acquiring and target firms enter into an M&A transaction. However, the variables of interest—ES standardization and ES extensiveness—may affect the likelihood of a firm entering into an M&A transaction in the first place because of reduced integration costs or high synergy potential from the M&A. In this case, if we only consider M&A deals that actually occurred, our sample represents cases where the net benefit from the M&A is expected to be high. This selfselection issue could bias the relationship between ES standardization and extensiveness and the dependent variables. In order to correct for this potential self-selection bias, we used a Heckman two-step selection model.

To estimate the Heckman two-step selection model, we needed to add at least one additional variable in the first stage that is not included in the second-stage model. Selection CAR is the additional variable added to the first stage. Selection CAR is the cumulative abnormal return of the firm’s stock for the previous two years (Palepu, 1986). It is estimated using the third year prior to the forecast year with a CRSP value-weighted market model. Selection CAR measures the stock market performance of the focal firm, which affects the likelihood of a firm acquiring or being acquired. Firms with high performance tend to acquire and poor performers tend to get acquired (Gaspar et al., 2005).

## Acquirer Models to Test H1, H2, and H3 (Goodwill Impairment)

In general, the offer premium is only disclosed when the M&A deal meets SEC’s materiality standard for public company disclosure.<sup>5</sup> Thus, in the first stage of the acquirer model, we estimated a Probit model of the likelihood of observing a premium and acquirer CAR [-2, 2] in the second stage model. $h _ { i } ^ { * } = X _ { E S , i } \alpha + X _ { c o n t r o l , i } \beta + \varepsilon _ { i } ,$ where $h _ { i } ^ { * }$ is the latent unobservable variable that represents the economic value of acquiring the target firm. $h _ { i }$ is a dummy variable indicating whether the acquirer conducted a material M&A transaction with the target firm: $h _ { i } = 1 i f h _ { i } ^ { * } \ge 0 ; h _ { i } = 0 i f h _ { i } ^ { * } < 0$ . In order to estimate the above Probit model, we expanded the data beyond the firms that entered into an M&A transaction. We constructed the universe of firms—that is, firms that have a chance of acquiring or being acquired from 2009 to 2018. The universe of firms is constructed by matching all the Harte-Hanks firms available to Compustat firms. 4,013 unique firms were matched across ten years from 2009 to 2018. In the first stage, we estimated the likelihood of acquiring a target firm with disclosed offer premium or observable CAR [-2,2] using a Probit model. Explanatory variables include potential acquirer

ES standardization and extensiveness, size of the potential acquirer (logarithm of market capitalization from the previous year), accounting measures, including ROE, M/B, sales growth, liquidity, D/E, P/E from the previous year, and selection CAR.

## Combined Acquirer and Target Model to Test H3 (Operating Performance)

In order to test the impact of ES on operating performance, we needed to construct an appropriate comparison set. The operating performance of the acquirer post-M&A is the performance of the combined acquirer and target unit. Thus, it is not fair to compare it to the pre-M&A operating performance of the acquirer alone. Therefore, we constructed the combined acquirer and target sample at the acquirer-target pair level to test the performance change of the pair, through the following procedures. (1) We first matched both the acquiring and target firms in the M&A deal to the Harte-Hanks database. This process produced 455 deals, where we have information on both the acquirer’s and target’s ES (real deals). (2) We constructed a sample that matched these real deals with a set of synthetic counterfactual M&A deals (counterfactual deals)— i.e., combinations of firms that could have merged but did not.

To achieve this, we matched both the acquirer and target in the real deals to an industry benchmark (another firm in the same industry that was similar pre-M&A). The advantage of using an industry benchmark is that it controls for the differences in characteristics pre-M&A that could lead to different short- and long-term performance differences. (3) The industry benchmark is recognized using the following procedures. Consistent with Barber and Lyon (1996) and Tanriverdi and Uysal (2011), for each acquirer in our real deals, we identified a matched contro firm (counterfactual acquirer) by using the following three criteria: did not undertake any M&A within our sample period, operates in the same three-digit SIC industry as the acquirer, and had a pre-M&A operating performance (EBITD/TA) that was closest to the real acquirer. We followed the same procedure for the target firm. The final sample matched 216 real M&A deals (216 real acquirer-target pairs) to 216 synthetic counterfactual M&A deals (counterfactual acquirer-target pairs).

Using the sample above, we tested the impact of the combined acquirer and target ES standardization and extensiveness on operating performance using a difference-in-differences-indifferences (DDD) method. The treatment is defined as the M&A. In our context, the basic difference-in-differences (DID) setup compares the trajectories of performance in firm pairs that had an M&A (treated) with those that remained independent (control). Besides these two differences, we introduced two additional differences (acquirer and target combined ES standardization and extensiveness) into the estimator—i.e., the DDD approach (e.g., Gruber, 1994; Butler & Cornaggia, 2011). The major explanatory variables are treated (real M&A deal = 1; counterfactual M&A deal = 0), after ( = 1 if it is a year post-M&A,<sup>6</sup> = 0 otherwise), combined acquirer and target ES standardization and extensiveness with a one year lag, and all two- and three-way interactions. The coefficients of interest are treated×after, treated×after×combined acquirer and target ES standardization, and treated×after×combined acquirer and target ES extensiveness. We controlled for pre-M&A combined acquirer and target ROE, sales growth, liquidity, D/E, P/E, and M/B<sup>7</sup> and the sum of market capitalization.

## Target Model to Test H4 and H5

The target model is similar to the acquirer model. The same universe of firms was used for the estimation; the only difference being that the first stage estimated the likelihood of a firm being a target using a Probit model. The variables used for the first stage estimation are the same as in the first stage of the acquirer model.

## Results

## Descriptive Statistics

The acquirer sample contained 403 M&A bids; the combined acquirer and target sample contained 216 real and 216 counterfactual deals; the target sample contained 563 bids. Table 3 shows the descriptive statistics (Panel A: acquirer sample; Panel B: target sample; Panel C: combined acquirer and target sample). The average offer premium of the acquirer sample is 49.96%. The bids, on average, have a much lower cumulative abnormal return (0.004 in Panel A) than the target cumulative abnormal return (0.27 in Panel B). This is consistent with prior research suggesting that, on average, the impact of an M&A on acquirer shareholders’ wealth is negative or not significant whereas the target’s shareholders earn sizeable positive market returns (Bruner, 2002). For the target sample, the offer premium is approximately 42.13%, on average. These deals also show a positive market reaction to the target firm (the average of CAR [-2,2] being 0.27). Overall, the acquirers (targets) in our sample were larger (smaller) than the firms in the universe of firms. Also, acquirers outperformed firms in the universe of firms, while target firms underperformed vis-avis firms in the universe of firms.

## Do Acquirer ES Reduce Offer Premium through Improved Management Forecast Capability?

Accounting literature shows that managements with high forecast capability make better investment decisions, including regarding M&As and capital investments (Goodman et al., 2014). If our argument leading to H2 (ES standardization facilitates realistic estimations of synergy and integration cost, and thus lower premiums) is true, we should observe that acquirers make more-informed M&A decisions when management forecast capability is high. Empirically, this suggests that acquirer ES standardization should influence the relationship between management forecast capabilities and M&A offer premiums. We used capital investment efficiency as a proxy for management forecast capability. Capital investment efficiency was measured using a benchmark investment model of optimal investment (McNichols & Stubben, 2008; Goodman et al., 2014). Capital investment efficiency can be used as a proxy for management forecast capability for the following reasons. First, management forecast capability plays an essential role in making successful capital investment decisions because the investment valuation process relies heavily on the forecast capability. Second, capital expenditures typically reflect purchases of long-term assets that are related to existing operations. Therefore, it is easy for the manager to extend the forecast capability of existing operations to the new capital investments.

Table 5 shows the results. The setup of Models 1-4 is the same as that of the acquirer model, but it controls for capital investment efficiency. Model 2 is the second-stage result of the acquirer model without the acquirer ES variables; Model 4 includes acquirer ES standardization and extensiveness. Model 2 shows that capital investment efficiency has a significantly negative impact on offer premiums. This indicates that when management forecast capability is high (i.e., capital investment efficiency is high), the offer premium is low—an indication of better M&A investment decisions. However, once acquirer ES variables (specifically, standardization) are added into the model (Model 4), capital investment efficiency no longer has a significant impact on offer premiums, indicating that the variation of management forecast capability has been absorbed by and reflected in the acquirer ES standardization. In order to further support the positive relationship between management forecast capability and ES standardization, we also ran a linear firm fixed effect model with capital investment efficiency as the dependent variable (Model 5).<sup>8</sup> This model shows that acquirer ES standardization is significantly positively correlated with capital investment efficiency, i.e., management forecast capability.

This result indicates that acquirer ES extensiveness facilitates synergy realization in the long term, manifested as a lower chance of the expected synergy not being realized. ## Impact of Target ES on the Premium and CAR

Table 8 reports the results of the target model. The Stage 1 results (see Model 1) indicate that target ES standardization increases the likelihood of the firm being acquired. In addition, it is less likely that firms with high sales growth and liquidity and well-performing firms (higher selection CAR) will get acquired. The Stage 2 results (Models 2 and 3) show the impact of target ES on the market reaction to the target firm and on the offer premium after correcting for the selection bias. The results indicate that target ES standardization creates market value for the target firm (p < 0.01 level, Model 2) and increases the offer premium (p < 0.05 level, Model 3). These results support H4 and H5. When target ES standardization increases by one SD (4.69), target CAR increases by 0.073 dollars for every dollar of investment (4.69×0.0156) and the offer premium increases by 32.75% (4.69×6.985). This may mean that the market understands the benefits of target ES extensiveness on M&A and the impact is incorporated in the market price of the target firm as soon as the M&A is announced. Taken together, these results present evidence consistent with research indicating that M&A announcements create market value for the target firm. However, our results provide a more granular perspective, highlighting that this value creation is associated with the IT capabilities attributable to ES standardization and extensiveness.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Enterprise systems are associated with improving the coordination and integration of resources across multiple functional areas and business units in the firm; the provision of real-time, accurate enterprise-wide information for managers; as well as collaboration capabilities and superior analytical tools for decision-making (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Cotteleer & Bendoly, 2006; Liang et al., 2007; Seddon et al., 2010). Similarly, there is a growing body of work examining the impact of IT on M&A (e.g., Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Du, 2015; Cao et al., 2022). However, few empirical studies have examined the broader benefits accruing from ES. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first large-scale empirical examinations of the relationship between the coordination capabilities provided by ES and M&A outcomes. We make the argument that ES are an organizational capability (Teece et al., 1997; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Barua et al., 2004) that influence M&As by helping firms acquire new resources, integrate them with existing resources, and redeploy resources in new contexts to generate economic value.

This study also contributes to M&A research in the strategic management literature, where the role that IT can play in M&As is currently underappreciated. It is therefore not surprising that fewer than 25% of firms involve senior IT executives in deliberations and M&A decision-making processes (Yetton et al., 2013). The M&A literature recognizes that high premiums reflect inadequacies in the decision-making processes of acquiring firms. However, prior research has approached the study of premiums from the perspective of understanding why firms pay premiums in M&As (Hayward & Hambrick, 1997; Beckman & Haunschild, 2002; Laamanen, 2007; Rueur et al., 2012). What is lacking is a theoretical explication of factors that can ameliorate the decision-making inadequacies of acquiring firms and can contribute to reducing instances of M&A premiums that are preventable failures (Edmondson, 2011). Against this backdrop, we make the theoretical argument that ES standardization impacts M&As by reducing the complexity of integrating the target firm into the acquiring firm and by enabling a more accurate estimate of the potential synergy and cost of integrating the target, and that ES extensiveness impacts M&As by helping firms realize the opportunities for synergistic value creation by integrating the resources of the acquiring and target firms.

Our empirical analysis reveals the essential role that the acquirer’s ES play in both ex ante (i.e., acquirer CAR and offer premium) and ex post measures (goodwill impairment and operating performance) of M&A outcomes. ES standardization is highlighted as being critical in short-term value creation because of its capability of accurately estimating potential synergy and the cost of integration and reducing the cost of integrating the target. Our acquirer model suggests that the acquirer’s CAR immediately surrounding the announcement date is positively related to the acquirer’s ES standardization. This suggests that the capital market recognizes the value of the acquirer’s ES standardization, as it can enable the acquirer to reduce the cost and complexity of integrating the target firm. Our results also suggest that acquirers with high levels of ES standardization offer significantly lower premiums. This is because acquirers with high ES standardization are more likely to have realistic estimates of the potential synergy, as well as the cost, complexity, and risks involved in the post-merger integration of the target firm, and thus are less likely to overpay. We also show that enhanced management forecast capability is the mechanism that links the acquirer’s ES standardization to premiums. These results are consistent with Tanriverdi and Uysal’s (2015) study, which shows that the immediate concern regarding IT and M&As from the capital market perspective is the fact that unsuccessful IT M&A integration increases the risk of disruption to the target’s operations and revenue growth and that the negative market reaction can be mitigated if the market has confidence in the prospective integration.

Second, our analysis indicates that ES extensiveness enhances synergy realization and the long-term performance of the M&A. Specifically, when the acquirer has high ES extensiveness, the magnitude of goodwill impairment is significantly reduced. This result indicates that the acquirer’s ES extensiveness facilitates synergy realization over the long term. Additionally, when the combined acquiring and target firm pair has high ES extensiveness, the firm shows significantly higher post-M&A operating performance, compared to the counterfactually constructed firm pair. This provides evidence for the role of ES extensiveness in longterm synergy realization. ES extensiveness in the combined firm enables a rational process for post-merger decisions, such as the identification of redundant resources that can be divested and the recognition of opportunities for resource reconfiguration and resource transfers that can benefit the combined firm.

With respect to target firms, the results suggest that the target’s ES standardization generates a significant positive market reaction to the target firm as well as a higher premium. This suggests that both the market and the acquirer likely recognize the lowered costs and complexity of integrating with the acquiring firm attributable to the target’s ES standardization. In addition, our empirical analysis also shows that the target’s ES extensiveness is positively related to the target’s CAR surrounding the day of the announcement, reflecting the role of the target firm’s ES extensiveness in the synergistic reconfiguration of target firm resources with those of the acquiring firm.

Our results have several important implications for research and practice. First, for acquirers, the standardization of ES enables senior executives to make informed and evidence-based decisions regarding target firm valuation that can dissuade acquirers from overpaying for acquisitions. Second, the extensiveness of the ES enables the long-term synergy realization for acquiring firms that other contenders for targets are unlikely to be able to duplicate. This is reassuring evidence for firms that implement complex, multiyear ES projects. For target firms, our results highlight that targets with standardized ES are more likely to be acquired, reflecting that IT strategies can contribute significantly to the acquisition readiness of target firms. Furthermore, for target firms, which are often smaller, the value realized by shareholders when they are acquired is positively associated with ES standardization and extensiveness. This is an interesting finding, which indicates that even for relatively smaller firms, it is important to invest in creating an information infrastructure using ES and ensure that it is standardized. Standardized and extensive ES allow resources and capabilities to be valued by acquiring firms. More broadly, the results highlight that IT managers need to bridge the gap in the understanding of the value contributions of IT capabilities by senior management (Hedman & Sarker, 2015). A greater appreciation of the role of ES in complex managerial decision-making may help increase the willingness of senior executives to fund and support multiyear ES initiatives to build firm capabilities and to involve the IT group at the early stages of M&A due diligence and integration planning.

Our study opens up a variety of issues for further research. First, only material M&A transactions that significantly affect the acquirer’s operation reveal the premium paid for the deal. In other words, we were only able to observe the premiums paid when they represented major deals for the acquirers. Second, this paper focuses on public acquirers and targets because of data availability. However, prior literature has highlighted some of the differences between acquisitions with public and private targets (Capron & Shen, 2007; Rodrigues & Stegemoller, 2007). Thus, whether our results will hold for small M&As or the acquisitions of private targets is an interesting direction for future research. Third, while studies have highlighted the contribution of a dedicated M&A function (Trichterborn et al.,

2016), we know little about the benefits of similar structural mechanisms and management processes within the IT function for accumulating, managing, and reusing specialized IT and process knowledge in firms. In this vein, highlighting the role of formal mechanisms for articulation and dissemination of technical knowledge gained within the IT function in the integration of complex systems and business processes is an important area for future work. Fourth, in this research we make the argument that ES standardization impacts M&A outcomes by improving the estimates of potential synergy and by reducing the cost and complexity of post-merger integration. However, potential synergy and integration costs are not measured in this study. Future research could operationalize potential synergy and integration costs to examine the mechanisms linking ES standardization to different M&A outcomes. Lastly, our study focuses specifically on two dimensions of ES—i.e., ES standardization and extensiveness. Future studies could examine the impacts of other dimensions of ES as well as the actual use of ES.

In this research, we hypothesized and found that ES standardization impacts short-term outcomes, CAR, and the premium paid, whereas ES extensiveness affects long-term outcomes, goodwill impairment, and operating performance. For the target, ES standardization is also positively related to short-term outcomes, i.e., CAR and the premium paid. However, surprisingly, we also found that the target’s ES extensiveness increases its CAR. This may suggest that M&A announcements reveal how the target firm’s extensiveness can create synergy when combined with the acquirers’ resources. Future research could explore conditions in which the acquirer’s ES standardization impacts different long-term outcomes, and the conditions in which the acquirer’s ES extensiveness impacts short-term outcomes.

In conclusion, our study contributes to the emerging literature (e.g., Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005; Ray et al., 2013; Benitez et al., 2018) on how the coordination capabilities provided by IT in general, and ES in particular, influence economic value creation in M&A initiatives. We highlight two dimensions of ES, standardization and extensiveness, and their distinct roles in M&A value creation. On the one hand, ES standardization may enhance management’s capability to arrive at realistic estimates of the costs, complexity, and risks involved in a post-merger integration, and assist in reducing such costs. As a result, ES standardization provides expeditious benefits for the acquirer in premium negotiation and capital market wealth creation. On the other hand, ES extensiveness, by providing information coordination capabilities across functional areas and business units within large multidivisional firms, can provide important capabilities for resource reconfigurations that can help the acquirer realize synergies from M&A and create competitive advantages over the long term.
