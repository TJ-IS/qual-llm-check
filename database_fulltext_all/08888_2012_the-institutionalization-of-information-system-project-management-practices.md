## a b s t r a c t

Considering that current structures are the result of choices made in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts in the past, we adopt a historical perspective in order to understand how some information systems (IS) project management practices evolved and became norms. Using historical methods, we analyze sources of data spanning 52 years of IS project management (1945–2007) – interviews with IS project managers and academics, IS project management textbooks, curricula, and the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and professional literature – to: (1) determine whether some IS project management practices may now be considered institutionalized, and (2) understand their institutionalization processes over time. Based on this analysis, three groups of IS project management practices may now be considered institutionalized: formal control, external integration, and project risk management.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) project managers are presented with many project management practices deemed essential for their projects. Such practices range from project planning tools, control mechanisms risk management methods, user participation activities, and change management approaches. Professional associations consider several of these practices to have acquired the status of norms. For instance, the Project Management Institute (PMI), which has a membership of several thousand IS professionals, “identi<sup>fi</sup>es the subset of project management body of knowledge [processes, skills tools, and techniques] generally recognized as good practice” (Project Management Institute, 2008, p. 4). By using the expression “generally recognized,” the PMI is referring to the fact that the “knowledge and practices described are ap plicable to most projects most of the time, and there is consensus about their value and usefulness” (PMBoK 2008, p. 4).

It has been suggested that the enactment of some IS project management practices is akin to an institutional behavior. For example, some organizations invariably rely on standardized professional practices in their software development projects (Kirsch, 1997), and some methodologies have become standards (Avgerou, 2000). A practice is said to be institutionalized when its legitimacy is recognized in a given context – an institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld – and when it has acquired the status of a norm or quasi-rule.

Institutional theorists suggest that the structure and enactment of organizational practices that have acquired the status of norms in a given <sup>fi</sup>eld result from choices made in the past in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts (Kieser, 1994). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this means that, prior to becoming institutionalized, a given practice is devised in response to a particular problem faced by the organization on the basis of the technical and economic feasibility of the practice (Tolbert & Zucker, 1996). At this point in time, adoption of the practice appears to be based on ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness motives. Later, when a consensus is reached among the actors of an institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld about the value of a practice and when the practice is considered a norm within the <sup>fi</sup>eld, its enactment is based on legitimacy motives. Along this line of thinking, Kieser (1994) suggests that current organizational structures and practices can only be understood once the historical dimension has been taken into account: the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of current organizational problems and appropriate remedies should be understood at the moment that the problems arose. He further argues that “historical analysis teaches us to interpret existing organizational structures not as determined by laws but as the result of decisions in past choice opportunities” (Kieser, 1994). If we do not want to limit our understanding of organizational phenomena, “we need to take history seriously” (Leblebici & Shah, 2004, p. 353). How? By integrating the concepts and methods of history and organizational theory (Leblebici & Shah, 2004).

Adopting this perspective, the present study identi<sup>fi</sup>es project management practices that have acquired the status of norms in the IS project management profession and seeks to understand their institutionalization process. To this end, we adopted historical methods to review 52 years of IS project management, drawing on many sources of data. Our historical analysis was informed by a model of institutionalization stages (Greenwood et al., 2002; Tolbert & Zucker, 1996), used to examine the content of 321 articles published in 14 journals from 1945 to 2007, 13 books or book chapters, three IS project management textbooks released in several editions from 1982 to 2007, four subsequent versions of the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBoK) Guide from the Project Management Institute (PMI) as well as its preliminary preparatory versions, three versions of the project management methodology of a leading IT consulting group (1985–2005) and 23 computer science/information systems curricula published between 1968 and 2002. Finally, we interviewed 46 IS project managers and scholars from eight countries, whose experience ranged from 3 to 30 years. Based on our analysis of these data, we offer an explanation of how the practices appeared and were diffused.

Our results suggest that three groups of practices – formal control, external integration and project risk management – have reached full institutionalization. The institutionalization processes of these three groups of practices differ slightly. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two groups of practices evolved concurrently. Both groups emerged at the same time – toward the end of the 1950s – and their institutionalization processes were similar. The practices in these groups were introduced in response to managerial problems associated with a major technological jolt: the introduction of computers in organizations. Formal control and external integration practices were introduced to address the problems of projects that went over budget, late system deliveries and unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed users. The complete institutionalization process of these <sup>fi</sup>rst two groups of practices – from inception to a state of full institutionalization – lasted four decades. The third group of institutionalized practices – risk management practices – appeared later, toward the beginning of the 1970s. Our data suggest that these practices were introduced by institutional entrepreneurs, in response to the same problems that had existed for several decades, notwithstanding the use of formal control and external integration practices. The institutionalization process of risk management practices occurred faster than that of the other two groups. We suggest that this may be due to the efforts of institutional entrepreneurs, the existence of multiple diffusion channels – curricula, academic programs, journals, and professional associations – that already existed when risk management was <sup>fi</sup>rst proposed, as well as to environmental events – the Sarbanes–Oxley Act and the Basel agreements – that accelerated the sedimen tation of risk management practices.

The paper begins with an overview of IS project management practices. It then discusses institutionalization as a property variable and process. The historical methods used in the study are then presented, and the institutionalization process of each of the three groups of practices is described. The three process es are then discussed and implications for research and practice are proposed.

## 2. IS project management practices

IS project management involves the use of a number of practices in view of reaching the objectives of on-time and within-budget delivery of a system that meets user requirements. Numerous such practices exist; for instance, Ropponen (1999) surveys over 131 techniques and 37 managerial interventions that can be considered IS project management practices. The range of practices is quite broad, as it varies from project milestones, monitoring procedures and cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses to top management support, user participation and vendor-relationship management.

We reviewed most of our sources of data to arrive at a set of practices that could be considered candidates for institutionalized practices. First, we queried ABI Inform Proquest and Business Source Complete to identify scholarly articles pertaining to IS project management. We then reviewed the references listed in the articles thus identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, which led to the selection of additional sources (Webster & Watson, 2002). We compiled the articles in a table in chronological order. Each article was analyzed and the following information on each was entered in the table: the paper's main topic, the issues it identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the vocabulary used to discuss these issues, and the project management practices proposed or studied in response to the issues. Second, we analyzed all four editions of the PMBoK Guide and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the list of project management practices developed from the literature. Third, we used the interviews with our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of 10 senior expert respondents to validate and further develop the list. Finally we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the practices into coherent groups according to their nature.

This analysis resulted in a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation consisting of four broad categories of practices, presented in Table 1. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category is that of formal control practices. As shown in the table, a large body of research has studied the enactment of IS project management practices from a management control perspective, which de<sup>fi</sup>nes formal control practices as those that are documented and exercised with formal mechanisms (McFarlan, 1981). They help structure the sequence of project tasks and arrive at estimates of the time, money and technical resources needed (King & Wilson, 1967; King, Wittevrongel, & Hezel, 1967; Kirsch, 1997, 2004); they also help project managers make progress assessments, identify disparities, and take corrective action. Formal control practices include mechanisms such as project scope de<sup>fi</sup>nition, work breakdown structure, project milestones, walkthroughs, use of a development methodology, and re view meetings (Cleland & King, 1968; Project Management Institute, 2004).

The second and third categories, internal integration and external integration, are for managing the relationships among the parties involved in an IS project (McFarlan, 1981). They include interpersonal coordination mechanisms, which manage the interdependencies among individuals (such as collocation of staff), and informal control mechanisms (such as social events). Internal integration practices are used to ensure that the project team acts as an integrated unit. They include frequent team meetings, the participation of team members in goal setting, regular status reviews, and team building activities (Baker, 1972; Brooks, 1975). External integration practices include communications and organizational tools that link the project team's work to users, whether they are managers or lower-level users (Kling, 1977; Swanson, 1974). This includes practices such as client participation, the selection of users as team members, user-managed change control processes, and the creation of project steering committees (Barki, Rivard, & Talbot, 2001; Debrabander & Edstrom, 1977; Lucas, 1978a, 1978b; Nolan, 1976; Zmud & Cox, 1979).

The fourth category is that of risk management practices. Although some researchers believe that IS project risk management consists of appropriately enacting formal control, internal integration and/or external integration practices (Barki et al., 2001; McFarlan, 1981), we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of practices that explicitly refer to risk identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, measurement, and monitoring (Microsoft, 2002; Project Management Institute, 2004, 2008). For this reason, a fourth category of practices was deemed appropriate. Risk management practices include techniques and tools for risk identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, risk prioritization, action plan development and risk monitoring.

## 3. Institutionalization: Property and process

Institutionalization is both a property variable and a process (Tolbert & Zucker, 1996). It is a property variable when used to determine whether a practice is institutionalized, i.e. whether it has the following characteristics: it has attained a high degree of resilience (Scott, 2001), it is taken for granted by the members of a given social group. and it corresponds to societal expectations of appropriate behavior and takes on a rule-like status (Tolbert & Zucker. 1996). In this line of thinking, we define institutionalized practices as practices that have come to be considered appropriate – legitimate – in a given context and have acquired the status of norms or have almost become rules in thought and social action. Whether a practice is institutionalized depends on its context. Indeed, a practice can be institutionalized in one context but not in another (Jepperson, 1991). The term “institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld” is used to refer to the context under consideration. The institutional field comprises actors such as professional and trade associations, regulatory groups, and other sources of normative or cognitive in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Scott, 2001), and is formed around a central issue (Hoffman, 2001). The participants in any given institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld share a common understanding of the practices that are appropriate for addressing the core issue: “Reciprocally shared understanding of appropriate practice permits ordered exchanges. Over time, these shared understandings, or collective beliefs, become reinforced by regulatory processes involving state agencies and professional bodies, which normatively and/or coercively press conformity upon constituent communities” (Greenwood et al., 2002, p. 59). Any deviation from these prescriptions results in discomfort for members of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Greenwood et al., 2002).

Institutionalization also refers to the process by which a practice becomes institutionalized. This process is generally characterized as a series of stages marked by events (Lawrence, Winn, & Jennings, 2001). Tolbert and Zucker (1996) propose a dynamic and longitudinal description of the institutionalization process stages, which has been respeci<sup>fi</sup>ed by Greenwood et al. (2002).

The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of institutionalization is preinstitutionalization (Greenwood et al., 2002), also called habitualization (Tolbert & Zucker, 1996), which entails the invention of new practices. The literature suggests two distinct origins for new practices (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006). The <sup>fi</sup>rst is environmental jolts, which can have disruptive effects on organizations (Meyer, 1982). Environmental jolts are as varied as social upheaval, technological disruptions, competitive discontinuities or regulatory changes (Greenwood et al., 2002). These jolts destabilize existing practices (Greenwood et al., 2002). Preinstitutionalization results in the appearance of new structures – or practices – called preinstitutionalized structures (Greenwood et al., 2002) in response to the new problems created by these jolts. Yet these innovations remain very local, since the problems are seen as speci<sup>fi</sup>c. The recent literature has suggested another origin for new practices, that of the institutional entrepreneur (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006; Kisfalvi & Maguire. 2011). Institutional entrepreneurs are actors who recognize problems in a field – such as adverse performance (Greenwood & Suddaby, 2006) – and become champions of the abandonment of “institutionalized practices for alternative ones that eliminate the problem” (Kisfalvi & Maguire, 2011, p. 153).

Preinstitutionalization is followed by a phase of theorization (Greenwood et al., 2002), which refers to the “development and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of abstract categories and the formulation of patterned relationships such as chains of cause and effect” (Strang & Meyer, 1993, p. 492). The third phase is diffusion, which occurs when the new ideas are convincingly presented as more appropriate than existing practices (Greenwood et al., 2002). While Greenwood et al. (2002) suggest that theorization and diffusion are consecutive stages, other authors argue that theorization and diffusion take place during the same stage (Tolbert & Zucker, 1996). Still others suggest that theorization spans the entire institutionalization process (Munir, 2005).

The last stage, sedimentation, is that of full institutionalization. It occurs when the structure, which has now been legitimized, is fully diffused. A certain consensus can be observed with respect to the structure's pragmatic value. At this stage, the structure is completely assimilated by the actors of the institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld, is taken for granted, and is considered the right thing to do. According to Tolbert and Zucker (1996), another sign of sedimentation is the fact that the structure has been perpetuated over a long period of time. This study deals with institutionalization as a property (a list of institutionalized IS proiect management practices is proposed). Yet, it focuses on institutionalization as a process, that is, on how these practices evolved over time.

## 4. Methodology

This study is concerned with institutionalization as both a property and a process, since we are interested in both IS project management practices that are now institutionalized and the process through which they became institutionalized. As any institutional analysis takes place in a wider temporal context than what can be reported by individuals, it is recommended that researchers use archives and historical data in a historical approach (Barley & Tolbert, 1997). A historical approach re<sup>fl</sup>ects cultural circumstances, the ideological postulates underlying the phenomenon under study, and the key roles played by decisionmakers in a given context (Mason, McKenny, & Copeland, 1997). Historical methods are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as being “a systematic body of principles and rules designed to aid effectively in gathering the source-materials of history, appraising them critically, and presenting a synthesis (generally in written form) of the results achieved” (Garraghan, 1946, p. 33) Indeed, the historian's task is basically threefold: to choose reliable sources, read them reliably and assemble them in order to provide reliable narratives (Howell & Prevenier, 2001). Although their study was not conducted in an institutional context, Mason et al. (1997), in a study of the development of a historical tradition in MIS research, propose a structured approach to conducting historical research. We adopted this approach in the present study.

## 4.1. Developing focusing questions

Our research questions are: Which, if any, IS project management practices can be said to be institu tionalized? What were the institutionalization processes for these practices? These questions encompass several speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions that helped determine the domain of enquiry: When did speci<sup>fi</sup>c project man agement practices appear in the <sup>fi</sup>eld? Which problems were they meant to solve? How broadly are they currently diffused throughout the <sup>fi</sup>eld?

## 4.2. Specifying the domain

Identifying institutionalized practices requires specifying the institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld within which they are institutionalized. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned the IS project management institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld as the persons and organizations that share the problem of successfully carrying out IS projects. This includes IS project managers, the professional associations to which they belong and which develop curricula and/or offer certi<sup>fi</sup>cation programs (such as DPMA, ACM, and the PMI), the universities and colleges providing IS project management training, and the large consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms that develop their own IS project management methods. Although we focused on the North American context, we extended our interview base to France, the UK and Australia. With the objective of taking into account all the dimensions mentioned, we gathered evidence that would re<sup>fl</sup>ect the preoccupations of each group.

## 4.3. Gathering evidence

Historians are expected to just give the facts. However, there is no all-encompassing de<sup>fi</sup>nition of what constitutes a historical fact (Shafer, 1980); each fact turns out to be a collection of facts and, in turn, each collection of facts is made up of objects, ideas and values. “Sources are artifacts that have been left by the past” (Howell & Prevenier, 2001, p. 17), they are relics/remains from the past or testimonies (oral or written) left by witnesses, from which historians construct meaning (Howell & Prevenier, 2001). Historical approaches call for the use of several sources of evidence (Mason et al., 1997; Tolbert & Zucker, 1996), including secondary sources such as articles, books and reports, and documents from professional associations. Apart from the actual information they contain, public sources are essential in order to establish an authentic timeline, which is a key methodological tool for analyzing data (Mason et al., 1997). Finally, the historical material is also inexhaustible, and a careful selection must be made (Kieser, 1994).

In this study, we collected and analyzed data from several sources covering a 52-year period (see Table 2) and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the preoccupations of all the stakeholders identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the previous section: journals, IS project management textbooks, computer science/IS curricula, the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBoK) Guide published by the Project Management Institute (PMI), and the IS project management manuals of a large consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm. As suggested by Mason et al. (1997), we supplemented these sources with primary sources of data: interviews conducted with 46 IS project managers whose experience varied from 3 to 30 years. Each source of data was useful in the information it provided on speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of the institutionalization process.

Professional and academic journals were deemed relevant data sources. As shown in Table 2, we included journals from the <sup>fi</sup>elds of management, engineering, and IS. Because the computer <sup>fi</sup>rst appeared in organizations around the end of the 1940s/beginning of the 1950s, our search period started in 1945. We conducted a manual search of all the journal publications prior to 1980. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for this period one author systematically printed and read all the articles related to IS or computers. The texts were read in search of problems identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, solutions proposed, project management and project management practices. An electronic search was not possible because the vocabulary had not yet stabilized at the time, and different expressions were used to refer to the same reality. We also built a list of terms to be used in our electronic searches of the following years. We had to focus on different issues depending on the literature source: we selected all the articles dealing with management issues in IS journals (Communications of the ACM and IBM Systems Journal) and all articles dealing with project management in general in management journals such as Harvard Business Review (HBR). It should be noted that we also read HBR between 1940 and 1945 (the only journal of our selection that existed at the time, see Table 2). but nothing relevant was found and it was decided to start our study in 1945, when we found the <sup>fi</sup>rst data. The data thus collected was coded in MS Excel in chronological order and by author name. For each article we coded: topic, vocabulary used, problem identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and practices suggested to solve the problem. For articles published after 1980 (1980–2007), whenever possible we conducted a systematic (full-text) electronic search (using ProQuest) of the selected journals. We conducted a manual search of those journals for which full-text articles were not available electronically. We completed this electronic search using the references provided in the selected articles in order to ensure that we had not missed any past references (Webster & Watson, 2002). This resulted in 334 documents: 321 articles and 13 books or book chapters (list available upon request). We then coded each article in MS Excel as described above. We stopped searching for more sources for a given practice when the source was in a journal that was not in our list and we already had the practice in our list (we only accepted articles from other journals when they referred to a time when a practice appeared and this information was not found in our sources). Textbooks are important sources of data with regard to the institutionalization of practices since they represent an important means by which institutional pressures are exerted within a <sup>fi</sup>eld. Indeed, DiMaggio and Powell (1983) believe that universities and professional training institutions are important vectors in the formation of norms. Textbooks could thus be considered proxies for what is being taught in universities (see Table 2). We wanted to select textbooks that would meet the following criteria: (1) being widely used in IS and computer science classes, and (2) having existed for a long period of time, with a series of editions in which we would be able to compare content over time. This is how we selected the <sup>fi</sup>rst two of our three IS project management textbooks. Pressman (1982, 1987, 1992, 1997, 2004, 2005) and Sommerville (1982, 1985, 1989, 1992, 1996, 2000, 2004, 2006) are, to our knowledge, the only textbooks to have discussed IS project management over such a long period of time and have been re-issued in new editions. A third, more recent textbook was also selected after systematically analyzing the content of numerous IS project management course outlines published on the websites of universities and colleges. This book, by Schwalbe (2000, 2006, 2007), is extensively used in universities and closely follows published editions of the PMBoK Guide published by the PMI. We coded the data in MS Excel, applying the same approach used for the journals; this is also true for the following documentary sources.

For several years now, professional associations (ACM, IEEE, DPMA now AITP) – often in collaboration with academics – have published curricula (see Table 2) to provide guidance in the training of future IS/ computer science/software engineering professionals. We analyzed 23 of these published curricula from 1964 to the most recent, which appeared in 2003 in the Communications of the AIS (Gorgone et al., 2003).

The four editions of the PMBoK Guide that appeared during the period of 1983–2007 served as another source of data. The PMI plays a particularly important role in the IS project management profession, since a large proportion of its members are IS professionals. The role of the PMI is recognized by several IS-related professional associations. For instance, in 2000 the Association of IS professionals (AIS) recognized the role played by the PMI in developing curricula. Indeed, the 2000 model curriculum (Gorgone et al., 2000) for Masters Students included project management for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time by suggesting widespread use of a course entitled Project and Change Management. In the course description, the curriculum explicitly and directly refers to content in the PMBoK Guide (Gorgone et al., 2000). This was unchanged in the 2006 curriculum (Gorgone, Gray, Stohr, Valacich, & Wigand, 2006). Similarly, software engineering professionals acknowledge the PMI as an important actor in software project management, as suggested by the 2004 SWEBoK: “Project management is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the 2000 Edition of A Guide to the Project Management Body of

Knowledge (PMBoK® Guide 6) published by the PMI and adapted as IEEE Standard 1490–2003, as the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements” (Abran & Moore, 2004).

These sources of data were supplemented with two sets of interviews with 46 respondents selected through purposive sampling (Patton, 2002). First, we deemed it essential to interview project managers with considerable experience and experts who had been active during the 1970s. Second, our goal was to interview project managers with the widest possible range of experience (in terms of years of practice, types of projects and geographical dispersion). The <sup>fi</sup>rst set consisted in 10 unstructured interviews with respondents who had IS project management experience. Most of them had been active in the 1970s. In addition to their project management expertise, the interviewees present a wide range of experience and included: two retired IS project managers with extensive experience in the domain (they had begun working in 1967 and 1973, respectively); six senior IS project managers who were still active (they had begun between 1970 and 1975); and one who had founded his own IS consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rm (now a leader in its <sup>fi</sup>eld, with global reach). We realized that the point of view of these seasoned managers could not be generalized to all IS project managers and decided to meet with project managers with less experience. The second set of interviews was conducted with 36 people: 32 active IS project managers (including the president of a PMI chapter), one project manager who is now responsible for project management methodology at a large IS consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rm, two academics, and another respondent who founded his own IS consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rm (which also has global reach). As shown in Table 3, these respondents represent a wide range of experience and geographical dispersion.

After meeting with the <sup>fi</sup>rst set of respondents, we established a list of candidate project management practices based on these interviews and on the literature. This list was re<sup>fi</sup>ned in later interviews (we asked each respondent if more practices should be added and if some were redundant). The <sup>fi</sup>nal list has 60 practices, and each respondent was asked if the practice can be considered standard today and when it was <sup>fi</sup>rst used. We went back to our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of respondents to complete their answers with the <sup>fi</sup>nal list. The data thus obtained was coded in an MS Excel table (list of practices vs. respondent). We also gathered citations and anecdotes when deemed relevant.

One of the authors collected and coded the data. The coding was validated by both authors and any discrepancy was discussed until we reached a consensus. For example, all selected early HBR articles were printed and systematically reviewed by the second author to ensure that they had been properly coded. All the MS Excel tables were reviewed by both authors. Differences were discussed and resolved.

Overall, our objective in the selection of data sources (Table 2) was to be as thorough as possible and ensure that all the aspects of the history of project management practices would be covered. The information thus gathered in the literature since the 1940s re<sup>fl</sup>ects what researchers found in the <sup>fi</sup>eld in terms of problems, solutions and practices as well as how they theorized about them in all the disciplines related to IS project management: IS, but also software engineering, computer science and management. Curricula and textbooks re<sup>fl</sup>ect what an IS professional has been taught in school, i.e. what we, as a <sup>fi</sup>eld, feel students should know in order to be IS professionals; the PMBok Guide (by the PMI, a professional association that also provides certi<sup>fi</sup>cation) and the project management methodology used by a leading consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm are professional literature and re<sup>fl</sup>ect what practices IS professionals and consultants are expected to use in a project. Finally, the interviews reveal the degree to which practices are diffused today as well as their history. By combining these six sources of data we feel con<sup>fi</sup>dent that we have very broad coverage of the topic that also enables us to triangulate the data and <sup>fi</sup>nd patterns. It was our intention that these different sources (see Table 2) would sometimes be both complementary (e.g. confronting theory and practices) and similar (e.g. testimonies from project managers and literature related to project management practices) in order to triangulate the data and validate patterns.

## 4.4. Critiquing the evidence

Using evidence requires knowledge of (1) external criticism, which determines the authenticity of evidence; (2) internal criticism, which determines the credibility of evidence; (3) the grouping of evidence in relationships of various sorts; (4) the interpretation of evidence in the light of many factors and in the absence of others; and (5) exposition or the communication of evidence to others” (Shafer, 1980, p. 127). Evidence can be compared through corroboration, contradiction and measurement. The quantity of corroboration required to make the author comfortable with his interpretation depends on the types of problems and evidence available (Shafer, 1980). An absence of contradiction should also be noted. The objective of critiquing the evidence is to ensure consistency when data is collected from a variety of sources. In this study, this was achieved through triangulation and by contrasting and comparing data from various sources. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we compared the data from distinct sources by corroborating the content of distinct tables. When discrepancies were found, information from professional sources was given greater weight in our analysis. This step occurred concurrently with the next step, determining patterns.

## 4.5. Determining patterns

Determining patterns involves analyzing and interpreting the data in order to give them meaning. Analysis and synthesis involve comparison, combination and selection (Shafer, 1980): “Analysis is a systematic attempt to learn about a subject or problem by looking at its elements, breaking it into components” (Shafer, 1980, p. 172). In this study, we constructed a list of candidate IS project management practices, determined which practices had become institutionalized, and then portrayed their institutionalization processes.

The iterative process that led to the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation presented in Table 1 also yielded a list of 55 candidate IS project management practices, provided in Table 4.

In order to determine whether a given candidate practice had indeed acquired the status of a norm among practitioners, we presented the 36 respondents from our second set of interviews with the list of 55 practices and asked them to indicate, for each practice, whether it is standard today and how long it has been used. A practice was considered institutionalized when at least 75% of this group stated that it was a standard practice. The resulting list of practices was triangulated by verifying whether a given practice appeared in our documentary sources listed in Table 2. Since our sample size is small, we made the decision to consider a practice institutionalized if it was mentioned as standard 75% of the time and more importantly if it was present in all the professional and academic literature we had gathered. We feel con<sup>fi</sup>dent that using these criteria together makes the items in our list of institutionalized practices as likely as possible.

The institutionalization process for a group of activities was portrayed on the basis of all our data sources. First, all documentary sources were coded (in Excel spreadsheets) in terms of the project management practices mentioned and the emphasis put on a practice. In addition, the number of articles, books and book chapters with one of the 55 project management practices as a key element was used to generate graphs depicting changes over time in the number of publications associated with each group of practices. We used another graphical tool in order to make sense of the data: events (derived from the interviews, articles, books, book chapters, and textbooks) were plotted on a timeline. For instance, using the interviews we plotted each practice according to its year of appearance. Likewise, we plotted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant events such as wars, crises, and salient dates in IS (the founding of IS programs, journals and IS <sup>fi</sup>rms). This chronology allowed us to perceive links between the data sources and <sup>fi</sup>nd patterns: what Langley (1999, p. 695) calls a “data organization device.” Several iterations (using the spreadsheets, the graph and the chronology of events) were needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd periods of time that made sense. Concretely, we tested numerous periods (5-year and 10-year periods); for each of them we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed al the project management practices in relevant periods (specifying when a practice is considered as new and then as a standard by respondents) in a spreadsheet. We eliminated the iterations for which there was no homogeneity in the practices (new/standard) per period. We continued this process until we reached the highest homogeneity in periods and associated practices. This analysis suggested <sup>fi</sup>ve key periods: 1945–1959, 1960–1979, 1980–1989, 1990–1999, and 2000–2007. We then returned to the interview data and coded the project management practices in each period, on the basis of their appearance (<sup>fi</sup>rst mention) and number of mentions. Note that our respondents did not mention the period of 1945–1959 because none of them was active at that time.

## 5. Results

In this section, we begin by presenting the institutionalized project management practices: formal control, external integration and risk management. We then describe their institutionalization processes, starting with a description of the initial context (the 1940s and 1950s).

## 5.1. Institutionalized IS project management practices

As shown in Table 5, 25 of the 55 IS development practices were considered standard practices by over 75% of our 36 interviewees. Statistical differences based on the respondent's current country of professional activity were observed for only one of the 25 activities; preparing a baseline plan was considered a standard practice by 17 out of the 19 North American respondents, by all 9 of the European respondents, but by only 4 of the 8 Australian respondents. Institutionalized practices fell into three of the four initial categories: formal control, external integration, and risk management. None of the practices in the internal integration category was considered standard by our respondents; indeed, the percentage of respondents that considered these practices standard varied from less than 5% (self-directed teams) to a maximum of 41% (team building). Our detailed analysis of institutionalization processes therefore pertains only to the three groups of institutionalized practices. Given the small sample size of respondents and the depth of our interviews with seasoned project managers, we will mostly refer to the content of the interviews in our analysis rather than to the quantitative aspect.

## 5.2. Initial context: the 1940s and 1950s

The <sup>fi</sup>rst computers appeared at the end of the 1940s in the United States (the Mark I was built by IBM in 1944 and the Mark II in 1947, ENIAC was built in 1946 and EDVAC ran its <sup>fi</sup>rst production program in 1952). The early applications of this technological innovation were initially for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c purposes and only gradually became available for business use (Schmidt, 1960). Existing organizational practices therefore changed with the appearance of a technological innovation, the computer, which was gradually used to meet existing calculation needs. These ideas were new and challenged an existing consensus, particularly with respect to calculations that were considered impossible. Practices therefore changed gradually as computers entered organizations.

In 1952, Fairbanks, in the Harvard Business Review, predicted that electronic machines could soon be used in the mechanization of certain accounting transactions (Fairbanks, 1952). The <sup>fi</sup>rst articles on computer use appeared at the end of the 1950s and focused on the cost of computers and their potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Clippinger, 1955). Authors were mostly interested in explaining how much computers cost, how to procure one, and how they could serve organizations. They recommended that a potential purchaser should plan the procurement process and the use of the computer (Kircher, 1956). The issue of project planning or management was not raised at the time, nor was the idea of a project, yet there was a vision of the implementation process (called “preparation” or “installation”): the senior manager, after a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t study, would authorize the purchase, process charts would be prepared for approval, and the programmer would prepare <sup>fl</sup>ow charts and begin writing code (Schmidt, 1958).

The introduction of computers as a tool for business data processing had a jolt-like effect for organizations, in that this technological disruption brought about new problems. As shown in Table 6, which presents some of the key problems that were discussed in the literature of the period, some of the problems were related to the project itself: scheduling, dealing with changing requirements, managing large diverse teams (Hosier, 1961), controlling programming costs (Clippinger, 1955), and meeting schedules (Mann & Williams, 1960). As also shown in Table 6, two types of solutions were then proposed. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type addressed problems related to the project itself, and included control practices such as budgeting, identifying milestones and planning.

## 5.3. Institutionalization of formal control practices

Our data suggest that the preinstitutionalization stage of the institutionalization process for formal control practices began in the early 1960s, followed around 1970 by theorization. Diffusion lasted from the early 1980s to 2000. As of 2000, formal control practices appear to have sedimented (Fig. 1, Table 7).

## 5.3.1. Preinstitutionalization (1960–1969)

It was during the 1960–1969 period that the <sup>fi</sup>rst practices addressing the problems of “computer installation” were proposed. This represents the preinstitutionalization phase, characterized by the appearance of new structures (procedures or practices) generated in response to the new problems that organizations were facing; during this stage, there were only a few adopters of the new structures. Regarding computer installations, Garrity (1963) reports that high-achieving companies were developing project plans, preparing project reports and appraising completed projects. The importance of sound planning and control tools is also emphasized by Dearden (1965). Fiock (1962) claims that <sup>fi</sup>rms do not spend enough time evaluating projects, and that they “should accept the fact that over 80% of the getting ready is selfanalysis and planning” (p. 90). The importance of determining the amount of time needed and assigning responsibilities in order to carry out the “plan” was also stressed (Evans & Hague, 1962, p. 101).

Although the new practices had been devised by some <sup>fi</sup>rms, they were not widely used. One of the interviewees, active during the 1960s, reported that no control practices were being used at the time. Even in the 1970s, the use of control practices was not generalized. The following description made by another respondent corresponds to what the literature describes (Dean, 1968; Garrity, 1963):

“I can state that there was no talk of project management when I began (…) I had to prepare a system of schedules (…). We bought the equipment <sup>fi</sup>rst. (…) I got there, got put on the job, and we tried to make it work. Once we learned how to operate it, we started in on what we needed to accomplish. (…) The computer was working after 6 months (…). There was no course in analysis. How it worked was, we sat down with the user – a very likeable guy – and he said, ‘You're going to develop my computer system, so you take my of<sup>fi</sup>ce.’ So the three of us set up in his of<sup>fi</sup>ce. That's how it went.” (PM6).

In terms of IS curricula, in 1968 the ACM studied the issues surrounding what a program of studies in Computer Science should include. This led to the publication of Curriculum 1968 (Atchison et al., 1968). The document makes no mention of project management.

## 5.3.2. Theorization (1970–1985)

The theorization stage is characterized by the development of abstract categories, the elaboration of cause-and-effect relationships, and the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and legitimation of proposed solutions. We analyzed the content of publications pertaining to control practices to assess the intensity of theorization efforts. As shown in Fig. 2, the number of publications grew from 12 in the 1960s to 32 in the 1970s and to a high point of 39 in the 1980s.

During the 1970s, authors set out to explain the need for planning and control (Gorry & Scott-Morton, 1971; Powers & Dickson, 1973) and demonstrate that problems arise when appropriate techniques are not used (Brooks, 1975). In the early 1970s, authors called for the use of formal control practices such as schedules and cost estimates (Dearden & Nolan, 1973; Hax, 1973; McFarlan, 1973). In the late 1970s and early 1980s, researchers empirically examined the relationship between control practices and project success and/or failure (Benbasat & Vessey, 1980; Schmitt & Kozar, 1978). They were also developing estimation techniques (Boehm, 1981a, 1981b, 1984; Boehm, McClean, & Urfrig, 1975; Putnam, 1978). Finally, another major sign of theorization related to project management in general – one that would have an impact on IS project management – was the publication in 1983 of a special issue of Project Management Quarterly. It was a preliminary version of what would later become the PMBoK Guide, and took the form of a collection of articles (Project Management Institute, 1983).

As Fig. 2 shows, publications follow an inverted U-shaped pattern. The number of occurrences of a given group of practices in the literature of a period merely gives an idea of the theorization effort in that period. After some time, theorization decreases, which does not mean that the practice is abandoned in projects, but rather that the focus of research has changed. Indeed, the practices are still present in textbooks and professional methodologies even after intensive theorization in academic journals decreases.

In 1984, an article in MIS Quarterly de<sup>fi</sup>ned project management as follows: “Project management concerns itself with planning, organizing, directing, and controlling the efforts of an applications development team to bring to life a computer application that is both ef<sup>fi</sup>cient to use and cost effective to build” (Felix & Harrison, 1984, p. 161). IS project management is thus formally associated with management. In the late 1980s, although formal control practices were still a concern to researchers, they became peripheral to their investigations rather than a main object of study. For instance, Mahmood (1987) compared the system development life cycle and prototyping, incorporating control practices in addition to other points of comparison. Other researchers have examined the roles played by several project management practices in project outcomes and listed formal control practices as one of several success factors (Pinto & Slevin, 1987; Zahedi, 1987). This lack of a focus on formal control practices suggests the termination of the theorization stage. This is not to say that it marks the end of research on control practices, as illustrated by several recent studies (Faraj & Sambamurthy, 2006; Kirsch, 2004), but rather that researchers' current focus is not on legitimizing the practices so much as understanding how they are used in organizations.

## 5.3.3. Diffusion (1980–1999)

In the institutionalization process, the diffusion phase represents massive adoption of the structure by adopters, who use information from the actors in their environment (for example, the media, champions and consultants) to promote the structure. The initial heterogeneity of the adopters gradually disappears.

An important indicator of diffusion is the presence of a given practice in textbooks and curricula: a practice that is learned at school often becomes a way of doing things by default. Our data indicate that, prior to 1980, control practices had not yet entered the diffusion stage. Indeed, besides mentioning the idea of budgeting and planning in 1971, succeeding issues of curricula did not explicitly mention formal control practices in a core course before it appeared in 1982 in “IS curriculum recommendations for the 80s" (Nunamaker, Couger, & Davis. 1982). These facts suggest that during this period. formal control practices had not vet entered the diffusion stage, However, starting in the 1980s textbooks did mention control practices. The <sup>fi</sup>rst versions of the Pressman and Sommerville textbooks date from 1982 (Pressman, 1982; Sommerville, 1982) and discuss methods for quantifying the work required to carry out, plan and control project progress. Formal control practices are present in the 1985 version of the IS consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm's project management methodology included in our documentation, indicating that such practices had started to be diffused through consultants. Finally, they were also present in the preliminary and <sup>fi</sup>rst versions of the PMBoK Guide published by the PMI (1986, 1987). These publications were diffused to project managers though certi<sup>fi</sup>cation and training.

Illustrations of diffusion are provided by some of the interviewees who had to acquire their training in formal control practices “on the job” (PM6). Our interview data indicate that formal control practices started to be widely diffused to practitioners in the 1980s. About the critical path, PM1 says “It started to be used in the 70s, but then we would only look at what was done or not, and we would catch up. Today, after two weeks of project we can tell that at the end of the project we will be two weeks late; we were not able to do that in the 70s.” For PM1, cost control appeared in the end of the 70s, and list of activities around 1985: “At the end of the 70s, we started to develop forms, to de<sup>fi</sup>ne what is the objective of the project, who is the client, what are their expectations, what is the project we are going to do, evaluation, development, cost; allocation budget, purchase budget […] the objective was to be able to <sup>fi</sup>nd the money to develop project; this was the idea: legitimize ourselves […] we put a formal project management methodology in place around 1980 […] We started doing activity reports at the end of the 70s, but we would not confront them with the project. It took time before we started to actually do progress reports, in 1985.”

Among the 16 respondents who spoke about the 1980–1989 period, most were using kick-off meetings, progress reports and project time management during this period. Even the practices that appeared during this decade (status meetings, scope de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, project charters, etc.) were used by the majority of project managers working in the period: GP12 about kick-off meetings “We do this systematically, unless it is a project with very few people, but if there are more than 10 people… if there are two or three people on the project it is just called differently!” Some practices may have existed for some time before being recognized; for instance GP10 mentions that although he did scope management in the 70s and in the 80s he only really started formally managing the project scope in 1994. In the following period, 1990–1999, all of the practices that today are considered institutionalized were being used by virtually all our respondents.

Some actors in the institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld played a key role during this period. Many of the interviewees mentioned that new practices were introduced by consultants. The PMI played an ever greater role in this domain: “When I became a member of the PMI in 1995, we were about 200 members. Now, in 2005, we'll be 2000 members by the end of the year. That's incredible growth. That's the [local chapter] PMI, and it's the same thing for PMPs. In 1995, we were about 80% engineers and architects – the rest was everybody else. Today, 80% of members are from IT, the technology sectors” (PM5). As part of its certi<sup>fi</sup>cation activities (PMP), the PMI ensures the diffusion of the project management practices identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the PMBoK Guide (Project Management Institute, 2004), which includes formal control practices in <sup>fi</sup>ve of its nine knowledge areas (i.e. integration, scope, time, cost, quality).

The context may also have been instrumental to the widespread diffusion of project management practices that occurred in the 1990s, a decade marked by the fears surrounding the arrival of the year 2000. Systems had to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed before December 31, 1999, and there would be no question of missing the deadline (PM 27). “With the coming of the new millennium, with Y2K, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, they [people] adopted a real project approach, because the deadline couldn't be changed. The history of project management in IT was a disaster, and now, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, most projects were delivered on time” (PM 5). Another seasoned project manager remarks: “In the 1990s came the Y2K bug. What this did is that all the money people had was put there. We started managing with deliverables. What this implies is: Is this going to work or not? We had all the management tools available. […] Fundamentally, people started focusing more on actually delivering” (PM 6).

## 5.3.4. Sedimentation (2000–)

Practices are said to have reached the sedimentation phase when they have been taken for granted by most of the actors of an institutional <sup>fi</sup>eld for a long period of time. The practices that can now be considered institutionalized are listed in Table 5. Indeed, control practices are still at the core of curricula, textbooks and the PMBoK Guide. To illustrate the extent to which these practices are now taken for granted, it is interesting to note that project managers, when asked about the actual use of each of the formal control practices in their current projects, were generally answering “always” or “of course,” without even going into detail. Several complained about these practices consuming most of their time.

## 5.4. Institutionalization of external integration practices

The preinstitutionalization stage of external integration practices began at the same time as that of control practices, and their institutionalization process followed a similar path (Fig. 1 and Table 7).

## 5.4.1. Preinstitutionalization (1960–1969)

In addition to issues related to scheduling and budgeting, the new computer installations faced a second set of problems: their acceptance – or lack thereof – by users. As shown in Table 6, one type of problem was related to the fact that often the new system did not meet user expectations (Diebold, 1964) or did not ful<sup>fi</sup>ll user requirements (Ackoff, 1967; Dearden, 1965). Several users also feared losing their jobs because of the new system or because they lacked the competencies that these systems required (Mann & Williams, 1960), and user resistance followed (Fiock, 1962). Solutions proposed in response to resistance included: top management support (Cox & Good, 1967; Dearden, 1965; Garrity, 1963; Mann & Williams, 1960) and operating management involvement (Cox & Good, 1967; Dearden, 1965; Garrity, 1963; Mann & Williams, 1960). Yet adoption of these practices was limited. Indeed, none of the practices is mentioned in the curricula, which means that they were probably not yet being diffused at the education level.

While some of the external integration practices appeared in the 1970–1975 period, such practices were mentioned by only one of the respondents interviewed on their experience prior to 1969.

## 5.4.2. Theorization (1970–1985)

Major theorization efforts were made regarding external integration practices in the period 1970–1985, with user involvement and change management accounting for most of the publications on external integration. In the early 1970s, several authors associated failed IS implementations with lack of top management support or poor user participation/involvement (Igersheim & Swanson, 1974; Powers & Dickson, 1973). These ideas then received progressively more attention, and were associated with training and change management (Schewe, 1976), excessive focus on technical problems without dealing with behavioral problems (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977), and poor understanding of user needs (Carlson, Grace, & Sutton, 1977).

Early on, cases cited in the literature tied top management support and user involvement to project success (Davis, 1974; Radnor, Rubenstein, & Tansik, 1970; Swanson, 1974). Starting in the mid-1970s, many researchers conducted large-scale <sup>fi</sup>eld studies that helped demonstrate that management support (Lucas, 1978a, 1978b; Morgan & Nolan, 1977; Swanson, 1974), change management, and user involvement (Davis, 1974; Ginzberg, 1981a, 1981b; Lucas, 1974; Lucas, 1978a, 1978b; Swanson, 1974) were important antecedents of project success. These studies re<sup>fl</sup>ect theorization efforts to legitimize the new practices. Fig. 2 shows changes in the number of publications by 10-year period and illustrates how the number of publications on external integration practices grew from 1970 to the 1990s. While the subject of user participation, user involvement, change management and top management support continued to garner the attention of researchers (Barki & Hartwick, 1994; Lawrence & Low, 1993), the focus turned to clarifying the constructs and understanding the antecedents rather than searching for pragmatic legitimacy.

## 5.4.3. Diffusion (1980–1999)

The presence of a practice in textbooks and curricula is an indication of diffusion, which is the massive adoption of a structure. In the curricula, external integration practices <sup>fi</sup>rst appeared in the 1980s (Nunamaker et al., 1982) and have continued through to today (Couger et al., 1995: Davis. Couger. Feinstein, Gorgone & Longenecker Jr, 1997; Gorgone et al., 2003; Gorgone et al., 2000; Gorgone et al., 2006; The Joint Task Force on Computing Curricula, 2001; Turner, 1991). It is worth noting that the textbooks studied did not mention these practices before 2000. In contrast, by 1988 all the practices were being mentioned in the IS project management methodology we examined. This suggests that consultants had begun diffusing these practices. Indeed, the preliminary version of the PMBoK Guide as well as its subsequent versions mentions the importance of communication, even outside the team (Project Management Institute, 1986, 1987, 2000, 2004), whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rst publication of the standards that would lead to the PMBoK Guide merely refers to communication inside the team (Project Management Institute, 1983). Our analysis of the changes in practices reported by our interviewees shows that all external integration practices that are now considered institutionalized were widely diffused in the 1980s and 1990s.

## 5.4.4. Sedimentation (2000–)

Most of the project managers we interviewed indicated that the external integration practices listed in Table 5 were now considered standards. About user participation, respondents report that it is a practice that is always followed “because it is very linked with customer satisfaction” (GP12), “because there has to be someone who of<sup>fi</sup>cially represents users, they are very involved” (GP11), “I would say that [the use of this practice] may have stopped, before 86, 87 users were always involved […] it came back!”(GP6). A few testimonies illustrate how these practices were then taken for granted. Regarding top management support, two project managers (PM3 and PM6) mention that no large project can even be considered if the vice-president of the division in question is not involved; when asked about user involvement, PM11 comments that “there are always representatives of users involved in the project.” This quote is representative of many of the answers related to this particular practice.

## 5.5. Institutionalization of risk management practices

The risk management institutionalization process commenced later than and differed from that of control and external integration practices. First, risk management practices appeared in the 1970s, a full decade after the beginning of the institutionalization process for control and external integration practices (Figs. 1 and 2). Second, our data suggest that the problems that risk management practices aimed to solve were not caused by a new disruptive jolt. Rather, these practices were put forth by actors who acknowledged the inability of the sole use of formal control and external integration practices for successful information systems projects. Third, the pace of institutionalization of risk management practices was more rapid than that of the other two sets of practices.

## 5.5.1. Preinstitutionalization and early theorization (1970–1979)

In the 1970s, the issue of IS project failures – projects that went over budget, systems that were delivered late and that not meet users' needs – became paramount. The situation was very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult: an enormous number of “broken promises [had] been created in project management” (McFarlan, 1973, p. 139). McFarlan alludes to risk as he tries to understand why formal control practices were insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient: “I do not wish to get involved here in the controversy about the absolute impact of these tools and techniques. Instead, I wish to identify certain critical dimensions that limit their effectiveness in any particular setting. The criterion I have used to identify these dimensions is risk” (McFarlan, 1973, p. 140). He further suggests that “the farther a project moves out on any of these dimensions [technological newness, project size, application complexity and difference in management style between the Data Processing Department and the rest of the organization], the greater will be the inherent risk of project's failure, and the less effective will be the contribution of any formal planning and control” (McFarlan, 1973). In 1978, Alter and Ginzberg (1978) report that IS developments, which consume a significant amount of organizational resources, do not vield the expected benefits. They argue that the control proiect management practices in use – such as PERT or CPM – assumed “a far more certain world than often exists in practice” (p. 23). They propose a risk management approach in which system developers identify the key risk factors in the development process and devise strategies for coping with such uncertainties.

The recommendations made by McFarlan and by Alter and Ginzberg do not address new problems created by jolts. Rather, they address enduring problems, which the authors argue have not been solved by the mere use of extant project management practices. In both cases, the authors refer to a new practice — that of a contingency approach to project management, where the most appropriate practice depends on the level and nature of the project risk. We suggest that these actors played the role of institutional entrepreneurs, who recognized the problems that existed in the <sup>fi</sup>eld proposed alternative practices. These authors also theorized about the practice they proposed, in that they develop and specify abstract categories and formulate cause-and-effect relationships. For this reason, we suggest that the phase where risk management practices appeared, the preinstitutionalization phase, was also one of theorization.

In terms of the IS curricula, during this period seven curricula (or extensions to previous curricula) were published (Ashenhurst, 1972; Austing, Barnes, Bonnette, Engel, & Stokes, 1979; Austing, Barnes, & Engel, 1977; Couger, 1973; Igersheim & Swanson, 1974; McFarlan, Nolan, & Shaw, 1973; McKenney & Tonge, 1971), and none of them mentioned risk management as a topic of study. At the time, no textbook on IS project management had yet been published.

## 5.5.2. Theorization (1980–1999)

In the early 1980s, more effort was being made by institutional entrepreneurs to develop cause-andeffect relationships between project risk management and software project success.

For instance, McFarlan (1981) proposed a framework that operationalized the ideas he had put forth nearly a decade earlier (McFarlan, 1973). In line with the argument that the systematic use of control practices in all projects could not solve the enduring problems of projects that were late, over budget and did not deliver the expected value, the framework proposed project risk measures and risk management strategies that consist in selecting, from among existing control and external integration practices, those that were best suited to a project's risk pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Boehm (1988), who was a recognized expert in software project management also became an institutional entrepreneur in terms of project risk management, and proposed and extensively diffused a method for measuring the risk of a software project and a “spiral model of software development” that explicitly accounts for the project's risk pro<sup>fi</sup>le.

Several publications followed on risk measurement and on the relationships between risk and project outcome (Barki, Rivard, & Talbot, 1993; Barki et al., 2001; Keil, Cule, Lyytinen, & Schmidt, 1998; Nidumolu, 1995; Ropponen & Lyytinen, 1997). Although research on risk management has continued throughout the 2000– period, it has not sought to develop abstract categories and cause-and-effect relationships, as in the theorization phase. Rather, authors have re<sup>fi</sup>ned models in order to apply them to new IS endeavors (Bernard, Rivard, & Aubert, 2004; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003).

## 5.5.3. Diffusion (1990–1999)

On the basis of our data, risk management was first mentioned in the 1989 Sommerville manual and in the 1992 Pressman manual, where it was covered by a small section in one chapter. In the 1990s the IS curricula repeatedly recommend risk management as a teaching topic for undergraduate and graduate programs (Couger et al., 1995; Davis, Couger, Feinstein, Gorgone, & Longenecker, 1997; Gorgone et al., 2003; The Joint Task Force on Computing Curricula, 2001). Interestingly, the preliminary versions of the PMBoK Guide (Project Management Institute, 1983, 1986) did not mention risk management, but in the <sup>fi</sup>rst of<sup>fi</sup>cial version, risk management became one of the eight dimensions of the PMI's framework (Project Management Institute, 1987, 1996, 2000, 2004). During the diffusion phase, the initial heterogeneity of adopters gradually disappears. This is supported by our interview data. Indeed, for the 1980–1989 period, 2 out of 16 respondents (12.5%) mentioned risk management as an existing practice, but the situation had changed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by the 1990s, with 21 out of 33 of the project managers (63.6%) reporting risk management as an existing practice.

## 5.5.4. Sedimentation (2000–)

Practically all of the interviewees indicated that risk management was an established practice as of the early 2000s. The diffusion stage for risk management appears to have been 10 years shorter than that for control and external integration. This widespread and rapid diffusion is striking: as several seasoned interviewees, GP2 remarks “I heard about this very recently.” External forces may have played a role. Our data suggest that environmental forces, such as international agreements, may have played a role in certain sectors. For instance, PM3's comment on the appearance of IS risk management in his organization is revealing:

“It has been a good change. Today we have a senior vice-president in charge of managing risk. This is since 2001. What made this necessary in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector was the Basel agreements, which required <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to comply with international standards. So it had to be done. But beyond those standards, we have developed ways to manage risk. We categorized risks with the PMI, with its nine areas of project management competencies. It started to be examined, taken more seriously, after 2000.” (PM3)

One respondent (PM12), also from banking, mentioned the Basel agreements as a driving force behind the enthusiasm for risk management. He also mentioned that this trend was due to other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant events, particularly enactment of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act: “I would say that it [risk management] has become more important over time. There are tools for risk analysis. We have seen more and more of this kind of thing since Enron and the like.”

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Adopting historical methods, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three groups of IS project management practices that can now be considered institutionalized and described their institutionalization processes. Two groups of practices – formal control and external integration – appeared at the same time and evolved concurrently, following similar patterns. They took four decades (from 1960 to 2000) to reach full institutionalization. The third group – risk management practices – emerged more than a decade later, in the mid-1970s. Yet their institutionalization process was more rapid, since they reached full institutionalization at approximately the same time as the other two groups.

Using the model of institutionalization stages proposed by Greenwood et al. (2002), we were attentive to identifying the point of origin of the institutionalized information systems project management practices. First, there is no doubt that the introduction of computers was a dramatic technological disruption, a jolt, that called for the invention of practices to manage the process of “installation” – control practices – and to alleviate the negative consequences of this installation, such as unmet user needs and user resistance – external integration practices. This phenomenon in which jolts play a role as triggers for the emergence of practices has been amply discussed in the institutional theory literature (Greenwood, Oliver, Sahlin, & Suddaby, 2008; Greenwood et al., 2002; Meyer, 1982). Second, our data suggest that risk management practices did not appear as a response to new problems created by a jolt — technological or otherwise. Rather, they were proposed by institutional entrepreneurs in response to the failure of the “taken for granted” application of control practices to solve the enduring problem of projects that were late, were over budget and/or did not deliver value. In terms of the role played by jolts, we suggest that the effect of a jolt may last for decades, and that new practices may emerge to compensate for extant practices that have failed to solve the problems caused by a jolt that occurred long ago.

Our data suggest that while environmental jolts may be at the origin of new practices, other important environmental events may play the role of catalysts in the institutionalization process. We give the example of three such events that played the role of catalysts in the institutionalization of IS project management practices. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is threat of the Y2K bug. As expressed by some of the project managers we interviewed, since late system delivery was out of the question due to the immovable deadline of year 2000, major efforts were invested in developing the formal control aspects of IS project management, and this accelerated its diffusion process. The second event is Basel agreements, which required that <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>rms pay careful attention to both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and operational risks, appear to have accelerated the diffusion of project risk management practices within the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector. The third event is the 2002 Sarbanes–Oxley Act, which required that US publicly-traded corporations adopt a recognized control framework to conduct internal control assessments. The main control frameworks adopted by corporations included risk assessment as a major component (Damianides, 2005).

The institutionalization process we observed for risk management practices differed slightly from the model proposed by Greenwood et al. (2002) with respect to the phases at which theorization took place. While we observed that, in the case of formal control practices and external integration practices, theorization occurred – as suggested by Greenwood et al. (2002) – after preinstitutionalization, our data suggest that for risk management practices the preinstitutionalization stage also involved theorization. Theorization is said to entail two activities. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of an “organizational failing” to which an innovation (here a practice) represents a solution (Tolbert & Zucker, 1996). The second is justi-<sup>fi</sup>cation of the new practice. This corresponds to the interventions of McFarlan (1973) and Alter and Ginzberg (1978), who were not referring to new problems but to enduring ones and to the failures of current practices to address these enduring problems. Our analysis leads us to conjecture that when this occurs, the preinstitutionalization phase is accompanied by theorization efforts, as the proponents of the new practices – institutional entrepreneurs – must explain the failure of existing practices in order to justify the new practices they propose.

What does the presence of institutionalized practices tell us about the profession of IS project management today? In recent literature, there is a growing awareness of the importance of professions, institutions that experienced deep changes in the last 30 years (Muzio, Brock, & Suddaby, 2010). Professions “have assumed leading roles in the creation and tending of institutions” (Scott, 2008, p. 219). Professional identities are more and more framed around logics of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and commerce (Brint, 1994). Muzio et al.

(2010, p. 1) state that “while we understand that professions are both key mechanisms for, and primary targets of institutional change, the precise role of professions and professional service <sup>fi</sup>rms in processes of institutional change remains under-theorized.” Our study <sup>fi</sup>lls part of this gap by explaining concretely how, through the institutionalization process, IS project managers are now institutionalized into using these practices, which many take for granted.

This “taken for grantedness” of IS project management practices raises the issue of mindfulness – or lack thereof – when they are enacted by IS project managers. Indeed, extant IS project management studies share the underlying assumption that managers are rational actors who make decisions in order to maximize ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness (Avgerou, 2000). In this view, IS project management is a set of “management controls needed to impose discipline and coordinate action to ensure goals are met” (Nidumolu & Subramani, 2003, p. 159). Notwithstanding the contributions of this stream of research, some paradoxical results must be acknowledged. First, despite the abundance and sophistication of the project management practices available to project managers, the failure rate of IS projects continues to be high. Indeed, the literature of the past 30 years shows that the problem of IS projects not delivering the intended systems or being late and over budget has been an enduring issue (Barki et al., 1993, 2001; Brooks, 1975; McFarlan, 1981). Second, several studies have led to contradictions, or “results that run counter to expectations based in the theory guiding the research” (Robey & Boudreau, 1999, p. 170). For instance, in a survey of 70 IS project managers, Bernier and Rivard (1990) tested a model of IS project management under uncertainty that was based on contingency theory. The model hypothesized that for a project to be successful, a <sup>fi</sup>t must exist between project management practices (the degree to which the project structure is organic, the degree of personalization of the coordination mode, and the intensity of boundary-spanning activities) and the sources of project uncertainty (task complexity, application complexity, user availability, and organizational climate). None of the hypotheses embedded in the model was supported by the study data. The authors commented that habitualized action might in part explain the results obtained. Similarly, Barki, Rivard, & Talbot (1994), and Nidumolu (1996) found that IS project managers did not react as hypothesized in their theoretical models based on contingency theory. In short, these studies found that IS project leaders had “a rather narrow view of a software development project, not devoting due attention to several of its . . . dimensions, or not reacting adequately to certain situations” (Barki et al., 1994, p. 14).

Although contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be elucidated by improved research designs (Carmines & Zeller, 1979), it has also been suggested that researchers consider adopting theoretical logics that accommodate contradiction (Robey & Boudreau, 1999, p. 172). Adopting the perspective of institutional theory allows us to challenge the assumption of IS project managers as ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-seeking decision makers and suggest that they may more closely resemble institutional actors who, in order to gain and maintain their legitimacy, unquestioningly adopt social norms (Tolbert & Zucker, 1983). In this view, IS project managers unquestioningly adopt project management practices that, over the years, have been institutionalized into norms, rather than analyzing the characteristics of a given project before deciding which management practices to use. This enactment of institutionalized practices may minimize personal risk by providing ready justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for less than successful choices, since it confers legitimacy on actors. Indeed, using practices that are considered norms legitimates the project manager; even if a project is considered a failure (over budget, late, and not meeting user needs), having used well-recognized project management practices could be invoked as a defense. Although enactment of an institutional practice is a source of legitimacy, it has been argued that when a practice has reached the status of a norm, it may occur that “rather than being the logically coherent, philosophically consistent, and cost-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient patterns of action that the pioneers of methodological movement envisaged, [institutionalized practices] provide, at best, systematic compromises of the con<sup>fl</sup>icting aspects of the systems development process, and often misleading reassurance of doing so” (Avgerou, 2000, p. 239). At the organizational level, research has found legitimacy-based compliance with institutional pressures to be negatively related to <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>tability (Barreto & Baden-Fuller. 2o06). At the IS proiect level, we coniecture that this may be detrimental to project success. This assumption does not contradict the fundamental bases of institutional theory. In their seminal article “Institutionalized Organization: Formal Organizations as Myth and Ceremony,” Meyer and Rowan (1977) focussed on organizational forms and proposed that “independent of their productive efficiency, organizations which exist in highly elaborated institutional environments and succeed in becoming isomorphic with these environments gain the legitimacy and resources needed to survive” (p. 352). The quest for survival does not necessarily come together with optimal results. Regarding this absence of focus on actual outcomes, Scott (2001) remarked that: [Meyer & Rowan] “shifted the focus from organizational goals to the structural and procedural aspects of organizations. The structural vocabulary of modern organizations – their emphasis on formality, offices, specialized functions, rules, records, routines – was seen to be guided by and to reflect prescriptions conveyed wider rationalized environment. These structures signal rationality, irrespective of their effects on outcomes” (Scott, 2001, p. 152). Following Meyer and Rowan, could we go as far as to consider institutionalized IS project management practices as myths and ceremonies? This new perspective on IS project management practices, which could contribute to explaining the high failure rate of IS implementations, would be worth exploring in future research.

An alternate interpretation could be made of our data, as one might question whether the mere presence of practices in text – articles, textbooks and “bodies of knowledge” – is infallible evidence of their actual use by project managers. In fact, it would be possible that what is in the texts does not correspond to actual practice. This is why we relied on multiple data sources, which included interviews with project managers, curricula and a project management method that had been in use for several years in a large consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm. Notwithstanding these methodological precautions, one could argue that the epistemological issue of the distinction between rhetoric and practice, that is, of the gap between what people say and what they do, remains.<sup>2</sup> This would mean that professional associations such as the PMI, universities and colleges that train future IS project managers and project managers themselves consider the practices as institutions, that is, as “practices applicable to most projects most of the time, and there is consensus about their value and usefulness” (PMBoK 2008, p. 4). However, this would also mean that IS project managers do not actually enact the institutionalized practices. If this was the case, would it undermine our claim that control practices, external integration practices and risk management practices are institutionalized? Our answer is no. If IS project managers do not actually enact the practices they claim they do use, this would mean that they adopt an avoidance strategy, which has been widely acknowledged as a response to institutionalized pressures (Oliver, 1991). Avoidance can take the form of concealment, where that actor disguises “nonconformity behind a façade of acquiescence” (Oliver, 1991, p. 154) by engaging in “window dressing; ritualism; ceremonial pretence; or symbolic acceptance” (Oliver, 1991, p. 155). This would be the case of an IS project manager who develops precise project plans or conducts detailed risk management evaluations, without actually using them over the course of the project.

Our study opens a number of avenues for future research. Based on the conjecture that the enactment of institutionalized practices for the sole purpose of gaining or preserving legitimacy may be detrimental to project outcomes, and may even resemble myths and ceremonies, one research avenue would be to examine the effect of the adoption of institutionalized practices on the outcome of IS projects (negative, positive or neutral outcomes). Second, the issue of whether IS project managers actual enact the institutionalized practices or merely claim that they do so would be worth investigating further. This could be done by adopting research methods closer to those of ethnography, in order to provide the researcher a better grasp of actual behavior. Third, based on the suggestion made by institutional theorists that professional <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly important in the process of professionalization – i.e. Cooper and Robson (2006) when they argue that professional <sup>fi</sup>rms play an important role in the institutionalization process – one potential research avenue would be to examine the role of large professional IS <sup>fi</sup>rms in the institutionalization of IS project practices.

Some limitations need to be acknowledged. The <sup>fi</sup>rst concerns the institutional lens that we used to develop our historical account. We are aware that researchers using another perspective might have arrived at a different process or explanation. The second limitation concerns the presentation of our results, but we had to be pragmatic in our choice of time periods within which to depict the evolution of practices. The third limitation is our omission of internal integration practices from our analysis. We recognize that it would have been interesting to analyze the evolution of these practices and attempt to understand why they did not become institutionalized, but we felt that this analysis, interesting in itself, would have taken us too far from our primary focus on institutionalized practices

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study makes a number of contributions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is empirical, inasmuch as we identify and describe the professional practices that have reached the status of norms for IS project managers, a key profession in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Moreover, we provide an explanation of how these practices originated, and we depict their evolution over time. It has been argued that organization science was witnessing a tendency among scholars to become “ahistoric,” which placed the domain at risk of building a utilitarian science focused strictly on utility at the expense of comprehensive explanations (Goldman, 1994, p. 621). We contend that IS research resembles organization science in this area, as the study of IS project management practices usually discounts the past. By undertaking this study, which adopts historical methods, our goal was to examine the current situation in terms of its historical development in order to deepen our understanding of the phenomenon, in the hope that this atypical approach to investigating IS project management practices would provide a new perspective. The second contribution is theoretical, inasmuch as our analysis of IS project management practices leads us to suggest that evnets that occur in the environment are not only the source of new practices, they can also act as catalysts in their diffusion/sedimentation processes.
