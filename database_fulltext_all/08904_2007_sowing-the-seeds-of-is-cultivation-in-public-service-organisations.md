## Abstract

This paper aims to highlight the relevance of a cultivation approach with the goal of exploring the concrete implications it may have for public administrations (PA) involved in projects of organisational change. We suggest that adopting an approach to change that reflects the cultivation perspective is an unavoidable choice for PA, much more so than it is for the corporate world. The claim is that public-sector organisations design and implement organisational solutions that find it hard to move away from the ‘maintenance’ logic of legacy systems. Compared with the rational perspective, which is geared entirely to establishing optimal relations between means and ends, the cultivation approach enables us to make valuable advances at the interpretive level. We argue that the value of the processual and incremental perspective can be useful in creating a more realistic and less illusory reconstruction of the relationship between technological change and organisational change. In this paper, we discuss how combining policy studies with ICT social studies can help empower the cultivation logic, originating new tools for analysing and evaluating e-government results.

## Introduction

CTs have significant potential to improve public administration (PA), even though these technologies invariably pose a challenge to the status quo and put a strain on existing social structures (Landsbergen and Wolken, 2001). The advent of the Internet has given the use of ICTs more than just a new name – e-government – and a higher profile. It has also accelerated the diffusion of e-government applications worldwide (United Nations, 2003; OECD, 2004).

e-Government can be seen to encompass project of technology change that is occurring globally, but particularly in developed economies (Heeks, 2005). As such, we might expect it to reflect some issues and findings consistent with those of Organisation and IS studies. Therefore, we should not be surprised if one of the themes that has always been the focus of reflection in these disciplines – or the way in which the technologies are put tangibly to use – is forcefully re-proposed also in the current e-government debate. ‘Is a strategy of ‘‘big bang’’ change in large and complex organisations wise, or indeed even achievable?’ is the opening question of a recent paper by McNulty and Ferlie (2004) appearing in Organisation Studies. Drawing on a study of the implementation of business process reengineering (BPR) in a UK National Health Service hospital, the authors affirm that the presence of such hurdles takes on ‘wider relevance to many current efforts of change designed to ‘‘transform’’ public service organisations, which go well beyond reengineering’ (ibid, p. 1406). The paper by McNulty and Ferlie concludes by saying that ‘sedimented’ rather than ‘transformational’ form of change (in which former organisational methods retain resilience) is a realistic way to implement the change through the appropriate use of technology.

The assumption that even the most precise and detailed programmes of change cannot foresee and solve all the problems arising in the actual process of setting up an IT project is a familiar topic in social studies of ICT (Ciborra, 1997, 1998; Avgerou et al., 2004). The studies that adopt this approach are extremely diffident towards determinism and technocratic thinking. They deny the relevance of conventional models that are based on recommendations such as: identifying goals, adopting calculated decisions, sticking to the plans, etc. on handling organisational change. Ciborra (1998: 9) qualified BPR as an ‘(y) incredible melange of propaganda, ideology and elements of method’. In particular, he was highly sceptical about the so-called ‘critical success factors’ that so many writers on management have been enamoured of. One-directional conceptual models dominate not only in business school teaching, but also in daily practice, both in companies and in the public sector (Cutler, 2004).

Social studies of ICT offer us an approach to change that suggests many tactics instead of a purely top-down strategy, which is known as ‘cultivation’ (Dahlbom and Mathiassen, 1993; Dahlbom and Janlert, 1996). Construction and cultivation represent two opposite versions of systems thinking: rational organisations are designed, but natural organisations evolve. ‘Construction is a radical belief in our power to, once and for all, shape the world in accordance with our rationally founded goals. Cultivation is a conservative belief in the power of natural systems to withstand our effort at design, either by disarming them or by ruining them by breakdown’ (Dahlbom and Janlert, 1996: 6). The organism metaphor (i.e. organisation as a natural system) helps us to clearly and immediately understand that organisation is not a machine, but a living thing, just like the plants that grow in our garden: cultivating would mean developing tactics of interaction with such an organism (Ciborra, 2002: 64).

For several years, a panoply of literature has proposed cultivation as an approach to the development and evolution of IT infrastructures in sustaining global enterprises (Ciborra and Hanseth, 1998; Hanseth, 2004; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2004). But relatively little has been researched about the specifics of the cultivation approach in public service organisations and it is this knowledge gap that this paper seeks to address.

In this paper we suggest that a cultivation approach is an unavoidable choice for PA, much more so than it is for the corporate world. Our assumption is that public sector organisations implement organisational solutions that find it hard to move away from the logic of the ‘maintenance’ of legacy (technological) systems. However, this is not due to incompetence, inefficiency or lack of effort, but to the existence of those restrictions that the public function has always put in front of their citizens.

We believe that an analytical approach incorporating the social studies of Information and Communication Technologies will enable us to widen our lens and to further our knowledge of e-government. As the incorporation of technology into government inherently is a political process (West, 2005) our reflection will draw on some contributions from policy studies. In this paper we will use the term egovernment in the sense proposed by Lenk (2002: 87): ‘(y) e-government is about supporting all types of business and deliberation processes within the different branches of government, as well as among them and their environment’.

This paper aims to highlight the relevance of cultivation approach with the goal of exploring the concrete implications it may have for PA involved in projects of organisational change. After the introduction, the second section describes some salient aspects of the e-government experience in the Italian landscape. Current experience reveals that the rhetoric of managerialism has been, and remains, very significant in initiatives developed by public service organisations. The section following briefly retraces the steps of the academic e-government debate. Based on an exploratory case of e-government presented and discussed in the next section, we show how combining policy studies and ICT social studies can contribute to empowering the cultivation logic, originating new tools for analysing and evaluating the implementation results. The section after that discusses the implications of the proposed multidisciplinary perspective: unlike radical change, the cultivation approach augments the capacity of the public-sector organisation to learn through experience and to connect problems and solutions in a new way. The conclusions reveal the limits of our paper, but also the compelling perspectives opened up on both the analytical level and that of concrete implementation.

## The Italian e-government scenario

During the 1990s, most developed countries initiated the widespread adoption of ‘information society’ policies (Avgerou, 2001) accompanied by large investments in technology at all government levels. This technology has greater capabilities and is more diffused throughout government agencies (Kraemer and King, 2003). E-government is perceived as the latest trend in a set of marketdriven reforms initiated by many governments since the early 1980s (Navarra and Cornford, 2005; West, 2005).

The plans promoted by the different national governments revolved around the creation of global frameworks enabling the diverse administrations to interconnect and channel a growing number of front-office services to citizens and companies, based on a logic that – tendentiously – would have enabled the establishing of what is called a one-stop service. The sizes and features adopted by the programmes carried out in the diverse countries lead us to affirm that the governments’ action pointed towards the implementation of vast information infrastructures – that is ‘a highly complex and extensive physical network of interconnected modules of communication technology’ (Hanseth et al., 1996) – entailing the harnessing of substantial resources. In Italy, for example, the past seven years have seen the launch of numerous projects by the individual central bodies (typically, ministries), as well as ambitious computerisation initiatives at the local level (CNIPA, 2005a). Lombardy has recently promoted a largescale regional health service plan, based on a microchip service card for both the citizens and the health workers, which calls for an arterial network of services for general practitioners, hospitals and health professionals. Most of the plans were conceived when the so-called new economy was at the height of expansion, precisely when the term ‘egovernment’ was coined.

The current (2006) Italian scenario is prevalently one of light and shadow. The IT projects launched since 2001 have enabled the public sector to partly catch up with other major European countries. Indeed, connectivity has expanded significantly in the space of just a few years, in terms of both inter-administration connection and bandwidth (CNI-PA, 2005a). The effort needed to implement organisational change (the real challenge for e-government) has been left, however, to the individual administrations. In a country like Italy, overwhelmed with small local authorities and agencies (only 5% of Italy’s 8100 municipalities have more than 20,000 residents) and numerous competing jurisdictions, administrative fragmentation is one of the strongest reasons for implementing e-services; at the same time, this fragmentation is also one of the constraints that most influences the self-same possibility of creating integrated solutions and services useful to the recipients, whether these be citizens, companies or other PA.

Current experience (far from consolidated) reveals clearly that the rhetoric of managerialism has been, and remains, very significant in e-government initiatives. This widespread trend is also found in most western governments (Metcalfe, 2004). There is an assumption that the managers are capable of exploiting technology in coherence with the goals and are able to steer the organisational change as they please. The belief is that the choice and use of the ‘right’ tools (i.e. the capabilities offered by IT artefacts and infrastructures), together with adequate professional resources, will necessarily generate ‘good’ solutions and, therefore, results that cannot be other than ‘positive’ for the performance of the relevant administrative bodies.

The valuation criteria used by the Italian government to select e-government projects eligible for co-financing (out of a total of 400 presented) provide insights worthy of reflection. ## An exemplifying case: selection criteria for e-government projects

In 2002, the Italian government published its first invitation to local and regional authorities to submit their egovernment projects for selection and co-financing. The selection process identified two basic goals: the first was that of promoting the transformation of local PA services into on-line services; the second centred on the creation of regional infrastructure services for the interconnectivity of all the local authorities in the region. To determine the eligibility of the projects, the Italian Ministry for Innovation and Technology (MIT) developed an evaluation table based on a system of indicators divided into five main classes, each one assigned its own relative weight (Table 1). The table is a kind of scorecard and is designed to be used by both the public bodies proposing the projects and by the experts responsible for evaluating them.

The projects selected for co-financing were the result of a nation-wide selection process. The authorities responsible for the selection took into account and evaluated positively the following features:

\- ‘objective’ parameters relating to the public body making the proposal (including former experience in similar projects, the present level of computerisation, experience in the field and the number and category of e-services already in use);

\- plans based on the use of project management methodologies and the conduct of work programmes, together with the use of instruments for the analysis of user requirements;

\- the presence of adequate technological and professional resources in the administrative body and in any private partners (if involved in the project);

\- the extent of the services to be created and their cost in relation to the extent of the pool of users; and

\- references to ICT standards and to the use of open technologies, with a view to promoting the extension of the same services to other administrations and to redeploying the experience gained.

The indicators chosen and the relative weight given to each seem to respond to two well-established questions (Ginsberg, 1979: 528) concerning selection and resource allocation decisions:

i Does the project respect the minimum requirements expected?

ii Does the project represent the ‘best’ use of resources among the available (and potential) uses?

The aim of the first question was to ensure that the contents of the proposals met the general requirements outlined in the plan, as well as those of the accompanying technical specifications. While the second question emphasises the control of the sustainability of the projects, with a view to facilitating the creation of services that are accepted and used appropriately. In both cases the evaluation gives weight to measurable parameters so as to facilitate a comparison between different proposals.

A further confirmation of the adoption of a technical/ rational perspective by the Ministry for Innovation emerges in the enabling role attributed to open technologies and to standards. For example, the XML language and the SOAP protocol are indicated as suitable technologies to design, structure and carry information via the Internet. The inclusion of these requirements is tied to a dual objective: to guarantee the exchange of data even between heterogeneous technical platforms and to facilitate interoperability. However, the same documents make no mention of the organisational problems that often represent real obstacles to the realisation of effective interaction between local administrations. As Landsbergen and Wolken (2001: 206) noted: ‘interoperability is more than ‘‘digital plumbing’’ (y). Fundamentally, interoperability is people talking and sharing information. Sharing information reduces the ‘‘paperwork burden’’ on the citizen, streamlines work processes, and enriches the formulation, implementation and evaluation of policy’.

The Italian plan conceptualises the role of information technologies in terms of an ‘enabling instrument’ for the creation of e-government. We believe that if the intention was to facilitate service innovation at the local level, attention should have extended beyond solely project selection. Conversely, in terms of controlling those activities (in the context of the work plan) aimed at determining user-satisfaction levels, the official documents refer only to the implementation and use phases. Thus, the e-government projects have been perceived as ‘one-shot’ initiatives rather than as recursive ones (‘on-going’, as Kling and Lamb (1999) put it).

In the case in question, the integration of the grid with the indicators inspired by the socio-technical perspective (Robey and Markus, 1984; Sorrentino and Virili, 2003), and the political perspective (Bellamy and Taylor, 1996) would not only not have overlooked the importance of the rational approach but contributed to placing it in a more realistic context. Thus, we could conclude that the relative ease with which – thanks to the spread of Internet technologies – it is possible to provide new e-services has lead the Italian Ministry of Innovation and Technology to undervalue the complexity inherent processes of technological innovation.

Today, three years after the public announcement of the co-financing selection process, the data ensuing from the monitoring of the 134 approved projects (which involved a significant number of local authorities: 19 regions, 95 provinces, 3574 municipalities, or unions of municipalities, and 218 mountain communities) are showing an average 13-month delay in the work plans. The projects (except for the three projects that failed to launch) should all have reached their present stage of completion in August 2004. In addition, under assessment, 26 projects showed a 35% lower rate of advancement than envisaged (CNIPA, 2005b).

Neither does the situation improve in the implementation of what Jones and Irani (2003) define as the ‘mandatory solutions due to legislation, where there is no option but to proceed’. For example, less than half of Italy’s central PA have attained an adequate level of project advancement in terms of the computerised correspondence register (the system whereby each document sent and received by the administration is automatically registered with information enabling its identification). Despite the fact that the deadline for compliance, laid down in a ministerial directive issued as long ago as 2002, was 1 January 2004.

Which raises the following question: given that the practical success of the models inspired by management practice is anything but a given in public service organisation, how can we explain the assumed superiority and attractiveness of these models on the ideological and cultural front? The first strong reason is that management practices from the business community are considered to be superior to those of the public sector (Bourgault and Savoie, 2000). The managerial prescriptions, according to their supporters, have the merit of forcing bureaucrats to become managers, to look ahead towards the effects of their actions, instead of always looking backwards to the conformity of their statutory acts. The second reason for the success of rational methods has been further strengthened by the development of ICT. The diffusion of DBMS, of the fourth-generation languages and of application packages offering multi-dimensional analysis and control systems, make the decisional techniques, which yesterday seemed overly complex due to the high number of variables involved or the quantity of data to explore, appear more realistic and manageable. According to Cutler (2004), the public sector and the business world share a strong belief in management efficacy. Even when failure is frequent ‘it has not shifted the dominant view that the road to ‘‘success’’ is managerial’ (ibid, p. 209).

## Rhetoric of administration vs rhetoric of realpolitik

The international e-government debate has developed mainly in the past five years, even though we can trace its origins to a few decades earlier. In terms of the public service organisations, the academic reflexion traversing the whole of the twentieth century can be seen as a counterposition between two reorganisation rhetorics (March and Olsen, 1983): on the one side, there is the view (generally optimistic) of the scenarios envisaged thanks to the hefty use of technology at all levels of PA (‘rhetoric of administration’); on the other, there is the opposing view (generally diffident, even pessimistic, compared with the first type), which translates reorganisation effort into a series of conflicting relationships, where opposing interests (in the form of the relevant social groups) face each other with outcomes that are, more often than not, uncertain and unpredictable (‘rhetoric of realpolitik’). Following, we will briefly retrace the steps of the e-government debate, citing some contributions by way of example. Our attempt to summarise will also serve to demonstrate how the dichotomous views described by March and Olsen more than 20 years ago can also be found in the current egovernment literature.

The studies that share the rhetoric of administration claim that e-government is both possible and necessary. Internet technologies are given a central role in the studies, which sustain that these have the potential to help achieve important objectives such as: transparency within public sector organisations (Westerback, 2000; McIvor et al., 2004), better communication between public sector and citizens, increased democracy. Technologies (and more markedly, the internet) are attributed with the power to heighten the transparency of internal PA processes. This heightened transparency also infuses the relationship with the citizens who interact with the administrations (ibid). Many scholars focus on the research of optimal relations between structure, technology and environment. The researchers who adopt this approach propose tactics and techniques aimed at optimising the project’s success rate and minimising the risk of failure (McIvor et al., 2004: 29). Many studies, for example, emphasise the need to adopt a structured design approach: ‘In the move of e-government, process analysis and optimisation of administrative procedures are key prerequisites for successful organisational and technical re-structuring of municipal administration’ (Becker et al., 2004). Other studies propose rational criteria for monitoring, evaluating and benchmarking e-government development (Kunstelj and Vintar, 2004). Those studies inspired by administrative orthodoxy are mainly prescriptive in their orientation and place the accent on efficiency and control aspects. They promise offices and agencies that could be eliminated, costs that could be reduced. Failures are interpreted as being the result of dissonances and inertia (Davenport and Horton, 2004). The idea shared by the evolutionist models of e-government is the continuity present in the processes of social change. This is why e-government is interpreted in line with the stage models (e.g. Gil-Garcia and Martinez-Moyano (2005); Layne and Lee (2001), where the next stage is characterised by increasing levels of complexity (technological, informative, communicativey). Aside from the peculiarities inherent each proposal, the evolutionary models share a claim of generality, accompanied by a strong one-directional character.

The contributions that adopt the assumptions of the rhetoric of realpolitik dispute the presumed neutrality of the technology and underscore the impossibility of uncritically transposing the same criteria adopted for corporate ICT systems to the public sector (for examples, see Kraemer and King, 2003; Traunmueller and Wimmer, 2003). Researchers meritoriously highlight the importance of aspects such as intentionality, the goals of the actors and the interests at stake in the e-government initiatives. They underscore the fact that each goal serves a particular interest. For instance, according to an empirical research study (McNeal et al., 2003: 53): ‘(y) e-government implementation is driven by legislative professionalism and, to a lesser extent, state professional networks, rather than citizen demand’.

The literature shows how, in complex and loosely coupled social systems like PA, technological change often sparks unintended consequences (Orlikowski and Hofman, 1997; United Nations, 2003) and contradictory effects (Becker et al., 2004). ‘e-government is anything but the objective’ say McLoughlin and Badham, 2005: 20). The overly optimistic techno-centric viewpoint is questioned. Scholars who adhere to this line of thinking assume that egovernment cannot be entirely planned and ‘applied’ (Andersen, 2004). The ideal linear trajectory that should lead from the design to the use of the new solutions is believed impracticable, due to aspects such as cultural barriers (‘silo cultures’, according to McLoughlin and Badham, 2005), and a ‘mismatch’ with the context in which e-government systems are deployed (Heeks, 2005).

The interpretive approach of March and Olsen is woven with an ongoing leaning that falls between the denouncement of the constraints of the dominant theories of managerial imprint and the search for a new approach to the study of social phenomenon. These counter the two orthodox rhetorics (‘administration’ and realpolitik’) with the decisional model known as the garbage can. These authors say that the problems, the solutions and the actors in the decisional processes and the opportunities for choice follow one another inside the organisations without an established order, just like garbage thrown into the bin, that is, in a totally random manner. According to this view, e-government would become on a par with other reorganisations, ‘collections of solutions looking for problems, ideologies looking for soapboxes, pet projects looking for supporters, and people looking for jobs, reputations, or entertainment’ (March and Olsen, 1983: 286). Indubitably, compared with the two orthodox rhetorics and aside from the fascination that this approach may spark, in prescriptive terms, the garbage can model is neither appropriate nor direct. We need to find a more satisfactory analysis framework, one capable of offering useful insights at the time of implementation.

To do this, we will analyse another e-government experience in the Italian landscape. In contrast to the earlier case discussed, we propose an exploratory study, the goal of which is to develop pertinent hypotheses and propositions for further inquiry (Yin, 1994). This study has to be considered a prelude to further research (Tellis, 1997). The study draws on documents such as announcements, papers appearing in the professional press, information freely available on the corporate website, etc.

## The case of Milan’s urban transport company

ATM SpA is an Italian joint-stock company wholly owned by the City of Milan, which manages the public transport service for an area encompassing Milan city and 85 provincial municipalities, the whole populated by roughly 3 million residents. Each year, about 3000 vehicles (buses, trams, trolleybuses and underground trains) carry 600 million passengers across 138 million kilometres. In 1995, the company decided to harmonise the fare system in Milan city, Milan province and, potentially, the whole of Lombardy. This led to the creation of a system (the ‘SBME’ or Magnetic and Electronic Ticket System), one of the most challenging projects faced by ATM in recent years and one that is going to have an enormous impact on customers’ travelling habits. The system entails the gradual replacement of the current form of travel documents with magnetic tickets and contact-less electronic cards, which will provide access to an integrated network (transport and parking) shared by ATM and two railway companies, the Ferrovie Nord (a private company) and Trenitalia (a stateowned company).

The new fare system is designed for easier and more transparent use and carries the added advantage of reducing the number of unpaid tickets. The card has the same features as a credit card and is thus easy to handle and store. Further, the fact that it is an information system makes it possible to collect and process data and produce statistics on the use of the system, the behaviours of certain customer categories, the use of the lines and so forth. The card will also be a useful tool for customers as it will ensure a smoother and more punctual service schedule; for example, the decision to enhance some lines rather than others will no longer be based on statistical, but real data.

Compared with the previous system, passengers will be required to validate their electronic pass card each time they use the service. In line with the gradual distribution of the electronic supports, the number of new ‘electronic barriers’ at entrances and exits will increase until these completely replace the current turnstiles. It will be possible to recharge the electronic pass card with the chosen type of season ticket, using the automatic machines located in the underground stations and at ATM outlets, etc.

ATM will continue to progressively install the new electronic-validating machines in buses, trolleybuses and trams. Used on vehicles that have not yet been upgraded to this system, the card is a valid document for presenting ‘on demand’. When taking the underground, magnetic ticket holders will now need to validate their ticket when both entering and exiting the platform by inserting it into the slot provided, where it is read by system and then returned, in tandem with the simultaneous opening of the barrier.

Until the system has been completely implemented, holders of paper documents will continue to use these in the same way as they have done to date. Indeed, the transition phase will see over-ground vehicles installed with machines capable of validating both paper and magnetic tickets, until these are gradually replaced with the new ones.

It is fairly unique to see such a complex initiative – in terms of technical implementation and the repercussions on the diverse stakeholder categories – launched as a standalone project, fully independent of the IS division. Initially, the project was mandated to ATM’s internal engineering department, due to its ‘systems project’ connotation. The IS department was not brought in until the technical specifications study had already been completed. This need arose while work was in progress because each functional component, ticket machines or turnstiles, had an IP address. When it entered the work group, the IS division first had a consulting role, which then grew in importance as application integration needs arose: it was necessary to connect the new system with the legacy software applications, first and foremost, the SAP R/3 ERP system run on the Windows platform. Integration with the other subsystems and the expansion of the corporate network redflagged the security issue. Up till then, each dedicated network constituted an autonomous and independent system without any kind of external connection interface. The infrastructure created as a result of the convergence of the existing networks (inside and outside the company) is a ‘system of systems’ of vital importance to ATM. The SMBE is slated to become fully operational by end-2006.

## Case commentary

Any decision can be described (without undue force) as the result of the application of design plans, as the outcome of political exchanges between actors or as the random product of the meeting of problems, solutions, actors and decision-making). This paper does not seek to establish the superiority of one framework over another, but to understand which interpretive key is the most satisfactory.

Interpreting the case of ATM in line with the first approach (or administrative rhetoric), suggests that the SBME system has been implemented in a fairly unusual way compared with the technocratic top-down approach, that is, one that focuses on purely technical problems. The project was developed over time using an unusual approach and this probably triggered effects that were not envisaged. Before taking its present shape, the work plan underwent many rethinks, which means that the specifics were never ‘frozen’.

The goals kept changing as technological opportunities arose and the social contest evolved. Ten years ago the possibility of using IT artefacts by members of the public was much rarer than it is today. Electronic payment systems (credit cards and prepaid cards), cell phones, Internet and call centres have spread rapidly among the broad segments of the ATM-user population. Higher levels of computer literacy and know-how among consumers have supported the launch of new self-service machines (e.g. recharging of travel passes, use of a microchip-enabled cards, credit card payment of bills y), which the first project had not taken into account because the technologies available at the time had not yet been fully developed. The use of self-service technology to buy tickets and travel passes and to top-up electronic cards was eased also by the introduction of the euro, which brought new denominations of banknotes and coins.

If ATM had proposed the new system with the ambitions goals of the current SBME, the City of Milan and the Regione Lombardia (financer of the initiative) would have found it hard to understand the value and would probably have judged it impracticable or unjustified. The reason for the materialisation of the tangible support of these agencies can be traced also to the fact that ATM has become more visible in the eyes of public opinion, given that the themes of mobility and traffic have taken the lead in the political agendas of the major urban areas. The official website of ATM (www.atm-mi.it) states that ‘making public transport more attractive means contributing to the well being of all citizens’. In addition, we can read ‘The subject of environmental quality is central to the process of drawing up ATM’s policies’. The company has been able to redefine its field of action thanks to the SBME system, also as a result of the growing environmental awareness infusing public opinion.

Realpolitik rhetoric helps to pinpoint the probable conflicts that emerge between competing interests in times of decision, for example, when the project has changed reach, scope and integration and has extended across diverse corporate divisions. Another key moment coincides with the participation of the two railway companies in the project. The new system raised strong public interest when the first papers appeared in the local and national press. The SBME was the object of an interrogation by Milan’s town council and the topic for discussion in the forums of the RCM (the Milanese Civic Network). However, the data available do not enable us to go beyond a case reading. For the same reasons, we cannot apply the garbage can approach. But we can say that the interpretive keys proposed are not necessarily mutually exclusive: as shown by the ATM case, the concrete reality almost always presents articulated and hybrid situations. Therefore, it is reasonable to think that the ‘rhetoric of administration’ is interwoven with the ‘rhetoric of realpolitik’, in an interaction that involves formal structures, power relations, ‘anticipated, emergent and opportunity-based’ changes (Orlikowski and Hofman, 1997).

The ATM case also shows how ‘building large infrastructures takes time’ (Ciborra and Hanseth, 1998). The IT division has taken on the role of the ‘cultivator’ of a ‘corporate information infrastructure’ (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2004), which has developed also by following spontaneous and unexpected dynamics. The installed base has muscled into the game, creating constraints for both functional and technical developments. No type of change has been unilateral, computed in full autonomy: the reciprocity that distinguishes the organisational action (like any other type of social action) has ensured that the (new) field of action has become reality because ATM found in the environment, that is, in other organisational processes and in other actors, the legitimation to pursue its stated goals (Thompson, 1967).

## A multi-disciplinary perspective

That the sense of what is happening in public service organisations is revealed by the processual flows and not by the rules or formal plans is not a new idea. This was formulated for the first time by a political scientist, Arthur Bentley, as far back as 1908, in a book entitled The Process of Government: a Study of Social Pressure.

In this section, we will refer to the contributions of academics who, in contraposition to the rational and normative approach of public policies, propose an alternative view (known as policy inquiry, Lindblom, 1990). This attempt warrants attention because its striking similarities with the cultivation approach enable us to reciprocally empower the e-government analysis toolbox. Embracing the policy inquiry interpretation adds a political dimension to the relational dimension found in the cultivation concept. According to Lindblom (1959), public decisions are the result of a mutual adjustment between ‘partisan’ actors, each one of whom conducts a simplified and partial analysis of the problem, sometimes neglecting important aspects, in order to concentrate exclusively on the marginal variations introduced by the solutions proposed. It also means that a public programme (e.g. an e-government project) is seen as a process without a solution of continuity, in which unfolds ‘an uninterrupted chain of decisions’ (Regonini, 2001: 327). Rationality is not guaranteed a priori to the calculation, but is instead triggered by the interaction. The processual character attributed to these decisions prompts two other indications, highly useful for their touch points with the themes of cultivation: the centrality of implementation and the need to adopt an incremental approach.

## Implementation as the moment of truth

Viewing public policies as processes primarily means sustaining that their identity is revealed only when the statements of principle (e.g. in the form of national plans) translate into concrete actions. ‘Unlike a symphony that exists independently from its composer, thanks to a number of strict conventions based on sound (as we know, Beethoven was deaf), public policies have no significance on their own, except in the way these are implemented’ (Regonini, 2001: 251, translation ours).

Sustaining that the organisation cannot be de-merged from the process that has led to its implementation overturns the step-by-step heuristics on which the rational perspective is based. This is because the problem contours cannot be established once and for all at the beginning of the process, but change continuously, step-by-step, along the road to activation (‘implementation is evolution’). Majone and Wildavsky (1979) make an interesting observation when they recognise that also the rational perspective envisages the possibility of implementation failure due to the unfeasibility of the original plan. Nevertheless, these same authors point out that the rational perspective does not admit to the fact that many restrictions (probably, the larger part) remain hidden in the design phase, only to show up once implementation is under way. Further, the conditions of feasibility continue to change over time. The actions, in their turn, simultaneously change both the resources and the goals. Nor should the difficulty in defining the goals justify abandoning the research into new techniques for improved requirement analysis and the envisaged reassessment processes. The goal is not to arrive at certainties, but to identify those possibilities capable of enduring longer than the alternative hypotheses: a theory of a ‘medium-range’ kind.

## Incremental approach

All projects must start by recognising that the problems and expected solutions are rooted in complex social interactions, in inevitable political conflicts, in uncertain conjecture and not only in terms of the future, but also the past. According to this perspective, ‘muddling through’ or incrementalism, is both the best descriptive model of governmental policy making and often the most appropriate strategy to follow (Lindblom, 1958, 1959; Braybrooke and Lindblom, 1963). This view is coherent with the idea of organising compared with that of organisation; comparing the idea of cultivation with that of design (Ciborra and Hanseth, 1998). Governance becomes less a matter of engineering than of gardening (March and Olsen, 1983).

The contributions from policy studies bolster the validity of the cultivation approach. The processual view, on the one side, re-affirms the continuity between design and implementation, while, on the other, factors in the rare possibility of huge shifts or clamorous fractures in the PA environment. The incremental approach fosters two points of interest. On the descriptive front, because it points to the need to continue the research beyond the rhetoric of innovation, the momentous turning point, to focus on the conditions and methods with which concrete practices have been changed. On the prescriptive front, incrementalism proposes taking the status quo constraint to an aware level.

The incremental approach supports an apparently minimalist arrangement, but one that is far more prepared to take into account the variety and complexity of social phenomena. In public sector organisations – as opposed to what happens in the corporate world – the process leading to a specific result is just as important as the result itself. It is not enough to take yourself along to the public agency to demonstrate your living existence. However, this apparent paradox does not conceal bureaucratic arrogance nor an intention to punish, but rather a grid of operating rules and mechanisms that ensure the continuity, correctness and legality of the action. Naturally, all this involves unavoidable restrictions. It means that ‘how’ an entity has arrived at a specific decision or action is important not only for the directly interested parties, but also for other people. Moreover, the sequence and structuring of the operating phases are established by specific rules that define the roles and responsibilities of the different actors. We must be able to reconstruct the procedures that lead to a specific action also in future years. The need to comply with such dictates makes it even harder to implement organisational reengineering (even when supported by information and communication technologies) in a radical and frequent way. Change can only be incremental and is much closer to cultivation than to (re)construction.

The interpretive key offered by policy studies enables us to highlight that the information infrastructures are continuously in the pull of two gravitational fields: on the one side, the weight of the existing, anchored to the past by thickly tangled roots (e.g. procedures, standards, operating practices, technology platforms, etc.); on the other, the perception that the change is needed and legitimate. The two perspectives are destined to run after each other in the search for a reciprocal alignment that is anyway precarious. The incremental approach underscores the importance of the fact that processes developed over time retain strong ties with the recent past. Cultivation, in that it is a ‘softer, less disruptive approach compared to more radical methods for organisational change’ (Bergqvist and Dahlberg, 1998) lessens the risk that the actors lose their usual reference points and affirms the importance of reciprocal adaptation as a primary form of coordination.

Lindblom (1959) qualifies the incremental method with the adjective ‘disjointed’ to underscore the fact that this approach considers coherence neither an achievable nor desired goal. Public-sector processes connecting the project design phase to that of project implementation are normally lengthy, contorted and full of contingencies. Egovernment services (think of what is known as the ‘onestop business counter’) suffer significant hurdles in getting the administrations called to interact in the same procedural sphere to sit down around the same table: local health authorities, environmental protection agencies, emergency services, etc). In these situations, incrementalism is disjointed to the extent to which each actor, despite ‘doing their bit’ (e.g. disclosing their information files), reduces this contribution to the basic minimum.

## Discussion

The consequences, both on the descriptive and the normative level, ensuing from an approach to e-government that adopts the principles of cultivation are worthy of specific attention in future research projects. Below, we will merely outline the most promising implications.

Firstly, on the descriptive front, incrementalism downsizes the aura of exceptionality with which the PA love to surround their initiatives. Applied to the analysis of e-government, the whole means switching our focus from the prior definition of the problems to be addressed and the viable technical solutions, to the actions undertaken and the logics that characterise these. Where approach is incremental, ‘successive incremental comparisons help to make analysis feasible in the absence of adequate theory’ (Lindblom, 1959: 303). In the valuation phase, however, the (unavoidable) shifts away from the initial plan are seen not so much as failures, distortions or deficiencies, as much as significant policy aspects that express the many rationalities present and their interaction.

Compared with the rational perspective, which is geared completely to foreseeing the impacts of the public policies and to establishing precise relations between means and ends, the cultivation approach enables us to take a substantial step forward on the interpretive level. In adopting a rational perspective, once the gap between the initial goals and the effective results is ascertained, the knowledge of the cause of these shifts not only fails to improve, but no significant advances are made on the cognitive level. Above all, if we concentrate exclusively on the gap between expectations and outcomes, we cannot appreciate what has effectively happened; it then becomes impossible to accumulate any useful knowledge of the conditions of feasibility and reproducibility inherent the technological decisions. And when an organisation does not assert its power to connect problems and solutions in a new way (i.e. ‘does not learn’), the public action suffers an additional source of waste (in terms of the under-used potential of the available means). In this context, the cultivation approach, if practiced systematically, can heighten the overall efficiency of organisational change initiatives. The analysis of the deviations paves the way for orienting and focusing on future action.

In terms of the normative aspects, we can affirm that it is hard to commit the error of underestimating the importance of pre-existing restrictions (whether these be regulatory provisions, operating standards or legacy information systems) when taking an incremental approach, like that underpinning cultivation. Cultivation also reflects a risk-aversion attitude that, as we know, assumes critical relevance in orienting public-sector activities (Willcocks, 1994). Kraemer and King (2003: 13) affirm that: ‘Government organisations (y) are driven by political institutional forces that are not and cannot be subjected to destructive changes without severe consequences for their constituents’. Ciborra (2004: 13) invites us not to be deceived by appearances: even a goal that is unanimously (and uncritically) believed to carry benefits can, in certain circumstances, become a two-edged blade. For example, integration among intergovernmental information systems pursued in a pushy and indiscriminate way can lead to substantial risks (defined ‘important and somewhat paradoxical’). According to Ciborra (2004): ‘Technical integration may bring with it a number of side effects and unexpected consequences, for example interference between different standards or an infrastructure that is welltuned but too rigid’. Nor should we underestimate the fact that ‘any transition to a more integrated and coordinated way of working adds new demands for planning, management, design, operations, and resource allocation’ (Werthmuller et al., 2005: 24). This is why a cultivation-oriented approach may be advisable. Moreover, ‘government’s business is public business. This means that errors can be costly in social terms. An innovation ‘gone wrong’ risks not only dollars, but also the credibility of an agency and its leadership with legislators, executive officials, and the public. It is not surprising, then, that government tends to rely on the ‘tried and true’ (Dawes et al., 2004).

Regonini (2001) opines that the ‘conservative’ nature of the incremental approach, which admits only slight deviations and favours the reversibility of decisions, it not always a positive value, given that this assigns an implicit advantage to the status quo. The same stance is taken by a recent contribution dedicated to information infrastructures, in which Cordella (2006) suggests giving priority to a policy that revolves more around the dynamic (rather than static) dimension of the infrastructures (infrastructures as ‘phenomena in action’). In this case, we can advance the hypothesis that also the cultivation approach takes on a more experimental character, which we could call here, paraphrasing Lindblom, ‘disjointed cultivation’.

## Conclusions and limitations

Our purpose in this paper has been to highlight and explore the relevance of cultivation to the public sector. We sustain that the need to account for cultivation is a perspective that must be recognised and which should become embedded in e-government agenda. Using ideas also originating in policy studies, we have shown that the value of the processual and incremental perspective can be useful in creating a more realistic and disillusioned reconstruction of the relationship between technological change and organisational change. The picture opens up compelling perspectives not only on the analytical level, but also on that of the concrete intervention on organisation settings.

The change in PA is conservative in the sense that it usually involves small adjustments to the status quo. It is an ongoing journey made up of fine-tuning and progressive modifications with its own rhythms, moments of stasis, retreats and recoveries. The convergence between ICT studies and policy studies enables us to widen our lens because we need to understand much more how political processes influence organisational choices in the public sector. West (2005: 167) observes that, up to now egovernment has been characterised by a ‘non partisan, technocratic reform that would improve public sector performance and democracy’. Going forward, this neutrality will tend to disappear. Technology requires up-front investments and the relative scarcity or abundance of budget support makes a huge difference to the ability of government to innovate (ibid, p. 11). Nevertheless, egovernment is bound to take on the nature shared by all public policies, which – according to who sustain these – can transmit a ‘conservative message (such as cost savings and improved efficiency) or a liberal message (closing the digital divide or providing more universal access)’ (ibid, p. 170).

A number of limitations found in this paper demand more in-depth reflection. Above all, we do not claim that our account is applicable in every case, or in every organisational context, nor do we deny that certain PA decisions are the bearers of truly innovative logics. We also realise that the examples cited are not sufficient to demonstrate the theory advanced (i.e. disjointed incrementalism as a suitable approach to cultivate the information infrastructures in action), nevertheless we believe that these can provide indications on the factors that lead us to propose it, as well as the conceptual bases that support it.

Further, we point out that the decision to proceed – in line with the cultivation approach instead of a radical way (construction) – is not always practical or efficacious. When thresholds exist below which a service is judged unsuccessful or an unattained goal (which happens in many IT projects), the inability to obtain a result, even by only a small margin, translates into a huge waste of resources. The final outcome of an information system development project depends on many factors, including the commitment of resources. Unlike other fields, where a dilution of the commitment and resources leads to a proportional reduction in the results (such as the construction of a building where, by spending less, smaller but still usable rooms can be obtained), the absence of some functions in the design of an IT artefact can render the whole system completely unusable. When gradualness leads to a postponement of some steps, albeit not especially important, which open and close the doors to specific applications, gradualness can be transformed into a harmful element from the economic standpoint. Clearly, also in this case, the nature of the project is the dominant factor in choosing both the implementation methods and the timeframe.

In particular, we have revealed how some outlooks (among which, that adopting purely rational principles) help to bolster the dominant rhetoric and, therefore, to broaden the gap between interpretation and reality. In concluding, and once again drawing on the contribution from Ciborra, we believe that the change in the public sector channelled through e-government projects slots better into the organisation caring category than that of organisation building.
