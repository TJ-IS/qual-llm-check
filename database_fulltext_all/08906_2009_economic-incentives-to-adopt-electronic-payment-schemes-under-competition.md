# Economic incentives to adopt electronic payment schemes under competition<sup>☆</sup>

Vidyanand Choudhary ⁎, Rajeev K. Tyagi <sup>1</sup>

Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine, Irvine, CA 92697, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 17 March 2008 Received in revised form 3 October 2008 Accepted 19 October 2008 Available online 5 November 2008

Keywords: Electronic payment systems Credit cards Price competition Duopoly

## a b s t r a c t

With advances in information and other technologies, newer forms of electronic payment schemes such as Google Checkout, NetPay, WebMoney, etc., have emerged over the last decade. These payment schemes are cheaper for sellers as compared to traditional payment schemes such as credit cards. Further, the convenience of credit cards over these alternative payment schemes is not valued the same by all consumers. Inspite of the cost differences between these payment schemes and the consumer heterogeneity in their relative valuations, sellers typically charge the same price from consumers irrespective of the payment scheme. We develop an economic model to examine a seller's incentive to offer discounts to consumers to encourage the use of alternative payment schemes. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that while a monopolistic seller does <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to offer a discount, offers of such discounts are almost never optimal when two verticallydifferentiated sellers compete with each other. Overall, we demonstrate that sellers' desire to not intensify price competition may partly explain why they do not encourage use of newer and cheaper alternative payment schemes by offering discounts to consumers.

## 1. Introduction

Credit cards have become a popular form of payment by consumers. For example, according to the 2004 Survey of Consumer Finances conducted by the Federal Reserve, 74.9% of families in the United States possess at least one credit card. This popularity and wide adoption by consumers is not surprising because credit cards provide consumers many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts — convenience, rewards such as frequent <sup>fl</sup>yer miles, and credit for a time period, etc. However, from the seller's perspective, this payment scheme is not inexpensive and imposes a cost since the seller has to pay a discount fee to its bank, and also incur interest expense since there is a time lag between a sale and the receipt of funds from the seller's bank. For example, in percentage terms, credit card companies charge about 2 to 6% of the transaction amount from the seller, which can be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost for sellers who operate on thin percentage margins, and which can become signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in absolute dollars for big-ticket purchases.

With advances in information and other technologies, alternative forms of payment that are less costly to sellers have been introduced during the past 10–15 years. Some examples of such cheaper alternative payment schemes are CyberCash, Digicash, First Virtual, Millicent, MPay, NetBill, NetPay, and PayWord. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Google Checkout is free for merchants for transaction volume up to 10 times the merchant's Adwords spending, and costs about 2% of the transaction amount above that;<sup>2</sup> NetPay currently offers payments with transaction costs as low as 1 cent or 1% of the transaction amount [10]; and WebMoney charges as little at 0.8% of the transaction amount.<sup>3</sup>

Given the difference in cost to the sellers between credit cards and alternative payment schemes, one would expect sellers to offer discount to consumers if they used these cheaper alternative payment schemes. Such discounts would incentivize consumers to adopt and use these alternative payment schemes, thus saving sellers the costs associated with credit cards. In addition, these alternative payment schemes may be valued less by the buyers than credit cards because of credit versus prepaid/debit nature of these two types of payment schemes, and limited acceptance of these newer payment schemes by sellers. Thus, the above-mentioned discount for using the alternative payment schemes could play another important bene<sup>fi</sup>cial role for sellers. It could allow the sellers to price discriminate better: consumers using the higher valued credit card payment scheme would pay the posted price, while those using the lower valued alternative payment scheme would pay a lower price — posted price minus a discount. Overall, one would expect sellers to offer discounts for the use of alternative payment schemes to: (i) incentivize consumers to switch to this lower-cost (to seller) payment scheme; and (ii) engage in price discrimination on the demand side.

However, in practice, such discounts from sellers are not common. We observe that when alternative payments schemes are offered by a seller, they generally charge consumers the same price.<sup>4</sup>

One might argue that most consumers would not change their payment schemes based on discounts offered by sellers, and that is the reason sellers do not try to offer discounts to encourage use of alternative payment schemes. However, prior research has shown that consumers do react to discounts in this regard. For example, in a consumer survey, Ingene and Levy [17] found that just a 2% discount for a hundred dollar purchase was suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for about a quarter of all respondents to switch from credit card to cash, and be willing to even switch sellers to avail of such discounts. Amromin et al. [1] <sup>fi</sup>nd that toll road users are very sensitive to the relative costs in choosing between cash and electronic payments. Humphrey et al. [16] use Norwegian data on retail purchases to show that consumer choice between paper payments and electronic payments is quite sensitive to the relative prices of these payment schemes to consumers. Bolt et al. [4] show consumer sensitivity to relative prices of electronic and paper payment schemes in data on Point-of-Sale and bill payment transactions from Netherlands. Borzekowski et al. [5] use debit card Point-of-Sale data from US and <sup>fi</sup>nd that a 1.8% increase in the cost of use of a payment method reduced its use by 18%. Overall, consumers have shown reasonably high sensitivity to relative costs of various payment schemes in their choice of a payment scheme.

The goal of this paper is to develop an economic model to examine a seller's incentive to offer discounts to encourage use of alternative payment schemes by consumers. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that while a monopolistic seller does <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to offer a discount, such discounts are almost never optimal when two vertically-differentiated sellers compete with each other. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if competing <sup>fi</sup>rms that accept payment through credit card and alternative payment schemes offer discounts to consumers to encourage a switch to alternative payment schemes, the price competition between the sellers can become unpro<sup>fi</sup>tably intense. Thus, we demonstrate that sellers' desire to not intensify price competition may partly explain why they do not offer customer discounts to encourage the use of newer and cheaper alternative payment schemes.

Of course, there could be other reasons why <sup>fi</sup>rms choose to not offer such discounts to encourage adoption and use of alternative payment schemes. For example, it could be that sellers believe that some consumers may not like that other consumers can pay a lower effective price by using alternative payments, or that sellers believe that consumers spend more when they pay using credit card [6,13]. We view our economic explanation as complementary to and not competing with these other possible explanations. We also acknowledge that sellers may offer such discounts for reasons outside of our model. For example, Google offers discounts to consumers for their first Google-Checkout transaction. Such discounts for new customers are usually given to cover the initial setup cost of customers and to induce customer trial under uncertainty [21]. Some other factors that we do not model which may incentivize <sup>fi</sup>rms to offer temporary customer discounts include change in consumers' tastes and seller's inventory.

Our work falls in the area of second-degree price discrimination where <sup>fi</sup>rms offer multiple options to consumers, who then self select an option [19,20]. For example, Dewan et al. [12] examine a <sup>fi</sup>rm's incentive to add customized products to its standard product when technology enables such customization; Bhargava and Choudhary [3] show that a monopolist's decision on whether to offer multiple options depends on the nature of heterogeneity in consumer valuations; Cheng and Dogan [7] show how the Internet's effect on lowering couponing costs and improving knowledge of consumer preferences can affect a monopolist's optimal use of second-degree price discrimination through couponing.

Choudhary et al. [8] show that although the use of <sup>fi</sup>rst-degree price discrimination by competing <sup>fi</sup>rms can be an equilibrium, it makes both <sup>fi</sup>rms worse off. Shaffer and Zhang [25,26] show that price discrimination can result in lower pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for competing <sup>fi</sup>rms in settings where <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to identify and charge different prices for the same product to different consumers. In contrast to these papers, <sup>fi</sup>rms in our setup do not have the ability to engage in <sup>fi</sup>rst-degree price discrimination (i.e., sellers do not know how much a given consumer values credit-card convenience), and consumers self-select whether they use credit card or alternative payment system (seconddegree price discrimination).

We also point out that prior research has examined many other issues related to alternative payment systems. For example, several papers have examined the adoption of smart-card payment systems by consumers and sellers by using models such as Technology Acceptance Model, Perceived Characteristics of Innovation Model, and Roger's Diffusion of Innovation Model [22,28]. Zhang and Li [30] examine how product and seller characteristics, such as seller size and reputation and product price, in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' choice of payment methods. See-To et al. [24] develop an economic model of two-sided network externality from the perspective of micro-payment system provider to examine the optimal prices for consumers and merchants using the system. Clemons, Croson and Weber [9] point out how lack of proper incentives for channel members and concerns with security and fraud limit the adoption of Mondex and other electronic cash systems. For the case of M-payment, researchers have also examined additional adoption hurdles such as interoperability across service providers [29]. Finally, there is also work on engineering and design aspects of alternative payment schemes [14,15].

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 examines a monopoly seller's incentive to offer consumer discount for using alternative payment schemes. Section 3 examines these incentives when there are two competing sellers. Section 5 concludes with some implications and directions for future research.

## 2. Monopoly seller

Following standard models of vertical differentiation [18,20,27], we consider consumers that are heterogenous in their tastes for product quality. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if a seller offers a product of exogenously given quality q, then a consumer of type θ values it at θq, where consumer heterogeneity is captured by parameter θ, which is assumed to be distributed uniformly between 0 and 1. For simplicity, we normalize q=1 and assume the marginal cost of the product for the seller is zero.

We use the terms traditional payment system (TPS) to represent traditional payment systems such as credit cards, and alternative payment systems (APS) to represent alternative payment systems such as e-payments or m-payments. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conceptualize TPS as payment schemes that have ubiquitous acceptance by sellers and buyers, that have been around for a long time, that are trusted by buyers and sellers, and are highly reliable. APS are newer forms of payment schemes that have limited acceptance by sellers and buyers, that may not be as trustworthy for buyers and sellers, and that may have less reliability in consumers' minds. Also, while a consumer may be able to do all routine transactions using a TPS alone, it is sometimes not possible to do so with an APS alone.<sup>5</sup>

On the demand side, we model consumers as getting more value from TPS than APS. We normalize consumer utility from using APS to zero, and the additional bene<sup>fi</sup>t arising from using TPS as θk, where kb1. This phrasing of the formulation in terms of the difference in bene<sup>fi</sup>ts a consumer gets from TPS and APS can also be seen as capturing the difference in costs a consumer incurs in using TPS and $\mathsf { A P S } . ^ { 6 }$ Our formulation also captures the idea that consumers who have higher marginal valuations of product quality are also likely to place a higher value on extra bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using TPS. This could happen because people who value a higher-quality product more are likely those with higher income and those same people are also likely to value extra convenience more. One way to look at TPS is to view it as another desirable product offered by a seller, and consumers who value higher quality products are likely to value this desirable product highly also. This assumption about the correlation between consumer valuations of quality and convenience is common in prior literature on models of product quality [11,23].

On the cost side, APS is modeled as cheaper than TPS for the seller. For example, as discussed in the Introduction section, Google Checkout is free for merchants for transaction volume up to 10 times the merchant's Ad-words spending; NetPay currently offers payments with transaction costs as low as 1 cent or 1% of the transaction amount; and WebMoney charges as little at 0.8% of the transaction amount. On the other hand, credit cards cost the seller about 2% to 6% of the transaction amount. Overall, we normalize the seller's cost to zero if a consumer uses APS, and as cp if the consumer uses TPS to make a payment in the amount of $p .$ Thus, $c p$ should be considered as the cost difference between TPS and APS rather than the absolute cost of using TPS. Further, this formulation of seller's cost of offering a payment scheme as a percentage of its price captures the fact that suppliers of these payment schemes, e.g., credit card companies, charge sellers a certain percentage (up to 6%) of the transaction amount. ## 3. Two competing sellers

We now consider a situation where there are two competing vertically-differentiated sellers, A and B. As before, we normalize the quality of A's product to 1 and denote B's product quality as $\scriptstyle x \in ( 0 , 1 )$ Thus, (1−x) is a measure of how “differentiated” the seller's offerings are in consumers' minds. Then, the consumer of type θ values the product/service from seller A as θ, and that from seller B as θx. As in the monopoly case, the marginal cost of the product is zero. Also, as before, the consumer of type θ values the convenience<sup>7</sup> of TPS (over APS) as θk, and the seller incurs a transaction cost cp if it lets a consumer use TPS to make a payment of p.

In this setup, the following cases arise: (i) neither seller offers discount; (ii) only seller A offers discount; (iii) only seller B offers discount; and (iv) both sellers offer discount. We next derive the sellers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in each of these scenarios, and determine their optimal strategy.

Comparing these pro<sup>fi</sup>t expressions to those in the case when neither seller offers discount,<sup>8</sup> we get the following result.

Lemma 1. If seller B does not offer a discount, then seller A does not find it optimal to offer a discount.

The proof is in the Appendix. Intuitively, if seller A offers a discount for APS, then it becomes more attractive to some consumers who have lower valuation of TPS and who were earlier buying from seller B. This then elicits price retaliation from seller B, which tries to keep these consumers from switching by lowering its price, which, in turn, makes seller A also lower its price. The net result is that although seller A is able to increase its market share by offering discounts, the intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed price competition on prices lowers both sellers' prices,<sup>9</sup> reducing seller A's net pro<sup>fi</sup>t. ## 5. Concluding remarks

Newer forms of electronic payment schemes such as Google Checkout, NetPay, WebMoney, etc., are relatively cheaper for sellers as compared to traditional payment schemes such as credit cards. Also, all consumers do not value the convenience of credit cards over these alternative payment schemes the same. In this paper, we developed an economic model to examine why inspite of these cost differences between these payment schemes and the consumer heterogeneity on their relative valuations, sellers typically charge the same price from consumers irrespective of the payment scheme. We showed that a monopolistic seller <sup>fi</sup>nds it optimal to offer a discount to encourage use of alternative payment schemes; however, competing sellers rarely <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to offer such discounts. Overall, we demonstrate that sellers' desire to not intensify price competition may partly explain why they don't encourage use of newer and cheaper alternative payment schemes by offering discounts to consumers.

We generated these insights using an analytical model which can be extended in a number of directions in future research. For example, future research can allow for a larger number of <sup>fi</sup>rms and examine the effect of number of <sup>fi</sup>rms. Future research can also model general distributions of consumer heterogeneity. Finally, we conceptualized the convenience afforded by payment schemes as an additional service provided by the seller, and hence modeled higher valuation consumers as placing greater value on this convenience. Future research can model an alternative conceptualization of the value of payment schemes as increasing in the price paid by a consumer. That is, while we modeled extra bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a payment scheme as a function of consumer “type,” future research can also model it as a function of consumer price.

From a broader perspective, we note that most of the newer payment schemes have struggled to obtain wide consumer adoption. One natural way to encourage wider adoption would be to offer discounts to consumers for using these payment schemes. We have shown that although the sellers save on their costs by use of these newer payment schemes, competitive considerations may eliminate their incentives to pass on a portion of these savings to consumers. Thus sponsors of these newer payment schemes may need to recognize this limitation, and develop business models that provide direct incentives to consumers for adopting their payment schemes.
