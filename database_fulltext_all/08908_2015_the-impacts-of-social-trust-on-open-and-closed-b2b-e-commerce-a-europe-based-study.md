# The impacts of social trust on open and closed B2B e-commerce: A Europe-based study

## A B S T R A C T

The increasing popularity of open standards in B2B e-commerce demands more research on generalized trust. This study investigates how a country’s social trust affects the use of open and closed B2B ecommerce. The analysis based on data from 27 European and related countries shows that social trust in a country promotes the use of open B2B e-commerce, but it impedes the use of closed B2B e-commerce. In addition, social trust negatively moderates the relationship between firm-level IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce. Our results highlight the important role of social trust in B2B e-commerce, especially in open e-commerce.

## 1. Introduction

Nowadays, the competitive unit in many industries has changed from an individual firm to a whole supply chain [38]. Managing resources effectively across partner firms has become paramount for the success of a supply chain. The role of information technology (IT) in improving supply chains by facilitating inter-organizational transactions and collaboration has been emphasized in the literature [8,58]. Studies show that firm ability in conducting transaction and collaboration with business partners over electronic channels (i.e., B2B e-commerce) can have a strategic impact on firm performance [64,66].

The main IT application used in the context of inter-organizational exchange is inter-organizational systems (IOS), which can be categorized into two types: open standards-based IOS (open IOS) and closed standards-based IOS (closed IOS) [55,72]. If a standard is developed and available only to a closed set of firms that requires a private communication platform, it is considered to be a closed standard (e.g., a proprietary system between a manufacturing firm and its suppliers). On the contrary, open standard is available to the public and uses public communication platforms and software (e.g., RosettaNet and ebXML) [56,72]. Nowadays, with the fast development of open standards (e.g., RossetaNet in the electronics industry), firms are increasingly deploying open IOS [2].

Trust has been identified as a key factor in promoting B2B ecommerce [21,30,37,40,61,62,71]. For instance, Zaheer and Venkatraman [71] investigate the electronic linkages in the insurance industry and find that insurance agencies’ trust in a carrier increases their business toward the carrier over electronic channels. Hart and Saunders [21] find that suppliers’ trust in customers increases the suppliers’ use of electronic data interchange (EDI) for diverse functions (e.g., inventory advice and order status report). Klein et al. [30] report that mutual trust between trading partners increases IT customization for supply chain coordination and improves strategic information sharing about capacity planning, production schedule, marketing strategies etc.

However, prior studies have more often focused on the trust between particular trading partners in B2B e-commerce, which is one type of particularized trust. Two types of trust have been distinguished in research: particularized trust and generalized trust [48,49]. Particularized trust can be thought of as a characteristic of an interpersonal relationship. It occurs in dyads and refers to a particular person. Generalized trust is the faith that we have in people in general (including, e.g., neighbors, fellow citizens, and even foreigners). As a process-based trust, particularized trust develops over time through interactions between the two parties in a relationship [75]. Conversely, generalized trust is a belief that people have about others and often arises from one’s institutional and cultural environment [49].

Although prior studies have offered valuable insights on the role of trust on B2B e-commerce based on closed IOS (closed B2B ecommerce hereafter), their findings may not be applicable in B2B e-commerce based on open IOS (open B2B e-commerce hereafter) due to the significant differences between closed IOS and open IOS. Based on a comprehensive literature review on IOS research, Robey et al. [58, p. 508] state that ‘‘research results generated from the first wave of IOS research on EDI are not particularly insightful for understanding the next generations of IOS based on open standards’’. This statement can also be applied to the trust research in B2B e-commerce. In fact, for closed B2B e-commerce that involves a stable group of firms, studying particularized trust between partners is appropriate. However, for open B2B e-commerce that involves a huge number of firms with dynamic membership, focusing on the role of generalized trust among firms, rather than particularized trust, may be more likely to augment our understandings on open B2B e-commerce.

Therefore, our study focuses on generalized trust. More specifically, our study examines how social trust of a country affects firms’ use of open and closed B2B e-commerce. In this paper, we define a country’s social trust as the faith that people of the country have in other people in general (i.e., social trust represents the country-level aggregation of the individual-level generalized trust) [31,48]. We contribute to the research by showing the important role of social trust in B2B e-commerce, especially in open ecommerce. With the increasing popularity of open B2B e-commerce in the business world [58,72], more research on open B2B e-commerce is urgently needed to provide insights regarding the use and management of open B2B e-commerce [55]. Our study helps to fulfill this research gap in the literature and practical needs from the business world.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. First, we review the literature that discusses particularized and generalized trust and that investigates open and closed B2B e-commerce. Drawing on research on social trust [39,67], inter-organizational governance [29,50], and collective action [35,46], we develop a theoretical framework to link social trust in a country to B2B e-commerce. Our framework is then tested using a large-scale dataset from 27 European and related countries. We conclude our paper with a discussion on implications and potential extensions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Open and closed B2B e-commerce

Numerous benefits of open standards have been noted in the literature. These include operational cost savings, supply chain flexibility, and inter-organizational knowledge creation [19,45,46]. Studies typically posit that open standards-based IOS will become the next generation of IOS because they are far superior to traditional closed IOS due to their openness/ubiquity and advanced functions. For instance, open standards are more likely to facilitate cross-industry coordination because they are more flexible and possess more easy-to-learn formats, and they do not suffer from partner-specific customization [72]. They also allow many-tomany electronic connectivity relationships and herald a much richer and more flexible form of business process connectivity across enterprises [45].

One of the highlighted advantages of open standards is that, unlike closed standards, open standards enable firms to connect to more trading partners and give firms the flexibility to shift from one partner to another with a low level of switching costs [20,72]. For instance, Gosain et al. [20] suggest that new technologies such as Web services and XML-based data interchange help overcome the inflexibility of conventional EDI and closed standards. They do not require the excessive investment costs associated with traditional EDI that often rule out the possibility of connecting with diverse partners. Similarly, Zhu et al. [73] point out that, compared with closed standards, open standards generally have a broader trading partner base; and unlike closed standards, with which electronic connections are established only with existing partners, open standards enable indexing and searching for unknown buyers and suppliers.

On the other hand, the benefits of closed B2B e-commerce have also been widely documented in the literature. For instance, it is suggested that the use of closed IOS may lead to a partnership relationship and often create value for both trading parties [1,13]. In addition, firms engaged in closed B2B e-commerce with their partners often gain competitive advantages over those that do not use closed IOS [47,64]. In fact, the bilateral investments in relationspecific closed B2B e-commerce can be viewed as an alternative governance mode that acts as a substitute for managerial hierarchy and vertical financial ownership [29]. With this commitment, both trading parties are often more willing to invest in the relationship, which in turn generate more value and lead to competitive advantages.

## 2.2. Generalized trust

Important differences exist between particularized trust and generalized trust [49,52]. As proposed by Luhmann [43], particularized trust is based on the emotional bond between individuals and is more characteristic of primary and small group relationships; and generalized trust is more characteristic of abstract relationships where trust is related to the functioning of bureaucratic systems (e.g., legal, political and economic systems). In other words, particularized trust occurs in dyads and refers to a particular person. Generalized trust is the faith that we have in people in general, including people we have never met. For a given person, even though her particularized trust could vary greatly from relationship to relationship, her generalized trust is likely to remain relatively constant. A similar distinction of trust has been made by several other researchers [3,51,56].

Researchers have provided different explanations for the sources of generalized trust [48,52]. One school of thought claims that good institutions may create and maintain incentives for behaving trustworthily and thus result in generalized trust [41,59]. The reason is that appropriate institutions in a country are more likely to detect and sanction betrayal so that people may not behave opportunistically. As a result, there is a rational basis for trusting other people. That is, good institutions may improve generalized trust by diminishing the risk associated with trusting other people. In particular, scholars have highlighted the important role of institutional fairness and evenhandedness in supporting generalized trust, especially in socially or ethnically diverse society [48]. For instance, it is suggested that democratic institutions may encourage generalized trust because democracy usually assigns the same rights and duties to all members of the society [42].

Another stream of research suggests that generalized trust is ultimately based on cultural values and moral norms, as argued especially by Uslaner [65]. Unlike the institutional explanation that generalized trust depends on institutional environments and personal experience, the cultural explanation states that generalized trust is determined by cultural values and norms such as optimism, certain religious values (e.g., values embodied in

Protestantism), and egalitarianism, which are learned early in life through socialization processes and are largely stable [16,48]. Based on the value they learn through socialization, people may develop a generalized belief whether people in general can be trusted (i.e., generalized trust).

Empirical studies have provided some evidences for the above two explanations on the sources of generalized trust. For the institutional explanation, several studies find that a range of institution-related variables (e.g., quality of government, institutional performance, a universal welfare state, and economic freedom) are positively associated with generalized trust, though these studies may suffer an endogeneity problem because the positive relationships might reflect the effects of generalized trust, not the reverse [48].

For the cultural explanation, Uslaner [65] shows the causal effects of levels of optimism, Protestant religious traditions, and income inequality (as an indicator of egalitarian value orientation) on generalized trust. Uslaner’s main findings with respect to these three variables have largely been confirmed by other studies [5,14]. In addition, the influence of individualism/collectivism culture on generalized trust has also been documented in the research [18,67]. Recently, Dinesen [16] compares the institutional explanation and the cultural explanation using migration as a natural experiment and finds that both institutional and cultural factors have significant impacts on generalized trust. By and large, empirical research suggests that generalized trust appears to have both cultural and institutional foundations.

Generalized trust can also be linked to Hofstede’s culture framework. For instance, in the recent book, Hofstede et al. [24] state that in the collectivist society, the personal relationship prevails over the task and should be established first, whereas in the individualist society, the task is supposed to prevail over any personal relationships. It implies that particularized trust is more important than generalized trust for business relationship in the collectivist society. They also suggest that citizens of strong uncertainty-avoidance countries are less inclined to trust their country’s politicians, civil servants, and the legal system. Such a low level of trust may lead to a low level of generalized trust as well in these countries. In addition, Hofstede et al. also notice that ‘‘being very careful about trusting people’’ is one key item of Inglehart’s [26] value dimension of ‘‘well-being versus survival’’, which in turn is highly associated with individualism and masculinity.

As mentioned above, social trust is a country-level construct and represents the overall generalized trust of people in a country [31,48]. As the primary form of social capital, social trust has been suggested to lead to numerous positive outcomes [48]. For instance, social trust may contribute to economic growth because a high level of social trust reduces unnecessary opportunism costs, facilitates impersonal transactions, and encourages innovation and investments [31]. Social trust may also lead to good government, such as low corruption and better government performance [32,65]. Furthermore, social trust may help solve the dilemma of collective action that causes huge welfare loss because social trust leads people to cooperate in collective action [63]. In this study, we focus the effect of social trust on two types of e-commerce—open and closed B2B e-commerce.

## 3. Hypotheses

While there is an agreement in the literature that social trust can lead to positive outcomes, such as mutual cooperation, economic growth and effective democracy by mitigating the behavioral uncertainty involved in human interaction [17,31], it is still not well understood how social trust influences the adoption of different B2B e-commerce. Based on Yamagishi’s [67,68] ‘‘emancipation theory of trust’’, the current study proposes that high social trust impedes the use of closed B2B e-commerce but promotes the use of open B2B e-commerce.

The main idea of Yamagishi’s [67,68] emancipation theory of trust is that people tend to form committed relations to deal with social uncertainty (i.e., the risk of being exploited in social interaction); however, people with high generalized trust tend to form committed relations less frequently than would people with low trust when facing social uncertainty. By committed relations, Yamagishi [68] means that two partners in a relation refuse temptation of more profitable offers from outside and stay in their relationship with the same partner.

More specifically, Yamagishi suggests that social uncertainty is present almost every day. To avoid being cheated while maintaining interaction with others in order to obtain needed psychological and material resources, people form committed relationships with specific partners because mutual commitment can reduce the risks of opportunistic behaviors [36]. This is because various bonds are developed in committed relations, ranging from mutual emotional attachments to relation-specific assets which disappear once the relationship is dissolved. Such bonds can be seen as ‘‘hostages’’ as they can be mobilized to provide deterrence against unilateral defection. In addition, people often know more about the partner’s intentions and have more mutual control over each other in close and stable relationships, making it more likely for an actor to induce the partner to take a certain course of action [69].

Closed B2B e-commerce can be viewed as a type of committed relationships. This is because closed B2B e-commerce requires trading partners to make relationship-specific investments in closed IOS. As mentioned above, closed B2B e-commerce refers to e-commerce based on a standard that is developed and available only to a closed set of firms and requires a private communication platform. In other words, all investments in closed IOS are usually relationship-specific and thus indicate a kind of commitment. Actually, it is because the commitment involved in closed B2B e-commerce, closed B2B e-commerce can have the similar functions of managerial hierarchy and vertical financial ownership [29].

However, if in a country where people believe other are trustworthy and there are not much social uncertainty, such committed relationships may become unnecessary [56,67]. The main function of a committed relationship is to deter the opportunistic behaviors of transaction partners. If in some countries people are trustful, there may be few reasons for people to develop committed relationships in order to protect themselves from opportunistic behaviors. In fact, prior studies have shown that, instead of sticking to existing transaction partners, firms are more willing to initiate exchange with new partners in countries high in social trust [39,67].

Given firms in countries with high generalized trust have less motivation to commit to a particular relationship and closed B2B e-commerce represents one type of committed relationships, it follows that high social trust may deter firms from using closed B2B e-commerce. Accordingly, we propose:

Hypothesis 1a. The greater the social trust in a country in which a firm operates, the less likely the firm will use closed B2B e-commerce.

Open B2B e-commerce is a different story. While investments in closed B2B e-commerce are usually relationship-specific and cannot be recovered if firms shift to other trading partners, it is not the case for open B2B e-commerce [10,72]. Recall that one of the key advantages of open standards is that firms can switch their trading partners easily and that this is due to the low assetspecificity involved in open standards [20,72]. This characteristic of open standards will benefit firms in high-trust countries. In such countries, managers often have a high level of trust of other firms and are willing to transact with new trading partners due to this high level of trust [31,39,44]. With the aid of open standards, managers in high-trust countries are also more likely to find better trading partners in markets due to the reduction in communication and transaction costs [72]. As a result, firms in high-trust countries should be more likely to adopt open B2B e-commerce.

Furthermore, firms’ adoption of open standards may be regarded as collective action [46]. According to this perspective, the diffusion of open standards creates a collective action dilemma. This occurs when firms deem that they are individually best served in delaying their own adoption of a technology until a large enough number of other firms have actually adopted. By delaying, each firm believes that it will benefit more from the network effects of open standards (i.e., the more adopters of an open standard, the more potential partners a firm can connect with) [46,72]. This situation can become problematic, because as more and more firms delay their adoption, the more time it takes for significant network effects to materialize which then further delays adoption—a vicious circle.

Social trust may work as a mechanism to solve the collective action dilemma. Social trust can stimulate people to cooperate in collective action. People are often conditional cooperators, that is, people will tend to cooperate in collective action if they expect others will also cooperate [63]. Typically, people with high generalized trust will assess others’ trustworthiness as being quite high and thus expect others will cooperate. Therefore, people with high generalized trust are more inclined to cooperate when faced with collective action dilemmas [63]. In the case of open B2B e-commerce adoption, high social trust may encourage firms to adopt early (a cooperative behavior) because they expect that others will also adopt later. Consequently, it is reasonable to assume that social trust may help promote the diffusion of open B2B e-commerce.

Hypothesis 1b. The greater the social trust in a country in which a firm operates, the more likely the firm will use open B2B e-commerce.

Above discussion suggests that social trust has direct influences on the use of open and closed B2B e-commerce. As a contextual factor, social trust may also affect B2B e-commerce indirectly by moderating the relationship between firm-level antecedents and B2B e-commerce. In this study, we investigate the relationship between a firm’s IT experience and the use of open/closed B2B e-commerce and how social trust moderates these relationships.

As the deployment of B2B e-commerce, either based on open standards or closed standards, requires IT expertise and skills, it is expected that a firm with more IT experience should be more likely to have the ability to deploy and use B2B e-commerce than a firm with less IT experience [58]. For instance, it is found that prior related IT experience are positively associated with EDI adoption [6] and prior IT experience are antecedents for organizational adoption of new IT innovation [28]. Therefore, we propose that a firm’s IT experience should facilitate the use of both open and closed B2B e-commerce.

Hypothesis 2a. A firm’s IT experience is positively associated with the use of closed B2B e-commerce.

Hypothesis 2b. A firm’s IT experience is positively associated with the use of open B2B e-commerce.

The association between IT experience and B2B e-commerce may be moderated by the social trust of a country. In the case of closed B2B e-commerce, as we suggested above, firms in countries with high social trust are less willing to engage in closed B2B e-commerce because they do not want to commit to particular trading partners [39,67]. This low willingness in closed B2B e-commerce should mitigate the positive association between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce. In other words, even if firms in high social trust countries have the IT experience and ability to deploy closed B2B e-commerce, they may not actually do it because they are not willing to. Therefore, we suggest that the positive association between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce will become weaker in countries high in social trust.

Hypothesis 3a. The positive effect of IT experience on closed B2B e-commerce becomes weaker when the social trust of a country is higher.

The influence of social trust on open B2B e-commerce is different. As discussed above, firms in country with high social trust are more willing to engage in open B2B e-commerce because open B2B e-commerce provides better trading partners to them [72] and they also have the tendency to do so [31,39]. As a result, in countries with high social trust, firms are more likely to deploy open B2B e-commerce if they have the IT experience and ability to do so. On the contrary, in countries with low social trust, the willingness to deploy open B2B e-commerce is low. This low willingness should mitigate the positive relationship between IT experience and open B2B e-commerce. Therefore, we propose that the positive association between IT experience and open B2B e-commerce are stronger in countries high in social trust.

Hypothesis 3b. The positive effect of IT experience on open B2B e-commerce becomes stronger when the social trust of a country is higher.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data and variables

Our data sources include the World Values Survey (worldva luessurvey.org) and e-Business Watch (ebusiness-watch.org). The data for social trust were taken from the World Values Survey (wave 4, 1999–2004), which is the most comprehensive and wideranging survey of human values ever undertaken. One question asked in this survey is, ‘‘Generally speaking, would you say that most people can be trusted, or that you can’t be too careful in dealing with people?’’ As commonly did in prior research (e.g., [3,31]), we measured social trust as the percentage of respondents in a country replying ‘‘most people can be trusted’’ (after deleting the ‘‘don’t know’’ responses). Research has shown that the reliability of responses to this trust question in the World Values Survey is remarkably high [12]; and the value of social trust is correlated well across countries with several indicators of trustworthiness, such as the proportion of wallets returned intact in wallet-dropping experiments and the prevalence of corruption and of violent crime [48].

The data for firm use of open/closed B2B e-commerce and IT experience were taken from e-Business Watch, which is developed and managed by the European Commission. E-Business Watch collects data relating to enterprise use of ICT and e-business in European Union member countries and candidate countries by means of representative surveys. In 2006, e-Business Watch interviewed decision makers from 14,065 enterprises in 10 sectors across 29 European and related countries about their use of e-business and related issues (our final sample contains 10.455 firms from 27 countries after removing missing data). Interviews were carried out in March and April 2006 using computer-aided telephone interview technology. The sample drawn was a random sample of companies from the respective sector population with the objective of fulfilling strata with respect to business size (see Table 1 for sample characteristics). The decision maker in the enterprise targeted by the survey was normally the person responsible for the IT within the company, typically the IT manager. Alternatively, particularly in small enterprises without a separate IT unit, the managing director or owner was interviewed. Data from e-Business Watch have been used in previous studies [34].

The measurement for firm use of open B2B e-commerce is based on one question in the e-Business Watch survey: ‘‘Do you use XMLbased standards such as ebXML, RosettaNet, UBL for the exchange of electronic data with buyers or suppliers?’’ Respondents can choose yes (1) or no (0) or don’t know (missing data), according to their own situations. The measurement for firm use of closed B2B e-commerce is based on another question in the survey: ‘‘Do you use closed standards agreed upon between you and your business partners for the exchange of electronic data with buyers or suppliers?’’ Again, respondents can choose yes (1) or no (0) or don’t know. Our measures of open and closed B2B e-commerce are similar to those in prior IT adoption/use studies [7,74].

IT experience is measured here by three questions asking whether a firm uses ERP, CRM (customer relationship management) systems, and KM (knowledge management) systems. Because B2B e-commerce is usually an enterprise-wide initiative, we believe that a firm’s experience in implementing other enterprise-wide IT systems should be most relevant to the adoption of B2B e-commerce. Therefore, we measure a firm’s IT experience as its experience in deploying other enterprise-wide IT systems such as ERP, CRM and KM systems [22,33]. Respondents can choose yes (1) or no (0) or don’t know (missing data) for each question. Firms using these IT applications should have better IT experience than firms that do not use them. The value of IT experience is set as the sum of the three questions.

We also controlled the effect of firm sizes by dividing firms into four categories: micro (1–9 employees), small (10–49 employees), middle (50–249 employees), and large size (250+ employees). Three dummy variables were used for the size effect. In addition, we used 9 dummy variables for the 10 industries in the survey to control for the effect of industry, given that research suggests that firm size and industrial factors may influence the use of e-commerce [11,73]. Furthermore, a firm may be already a user of closed B2B e-commerce when it considers the adoption of open B2B e-commerce (or vice versa). The experience in one type of e-commerce may influence the adoption of the other type of e-commerce. To control this influence, we added firm use of closed (open) B2B e-commerce as a control variable when conducting analysis on firm use of open (closed) B2B e-commerce.

At the country level, similar to other studies investigating the effects of country-level factors on IT-related phenomena [9,15], this study controlled the effect of the economic development level of a country, using the gross national income (GNI) at purchasing power parity (PPP) per capita provided by the World Bank. We also controlled the IT development level of a country using the number of Internet hosts per one million people from the World Bank, as firms in IT-developed countries can get more help from external IT services vendors for conducting e-commerce.

## 4.2. A multi-level model

Our data are hierarchically organized with firms nested within countries. Traditionally, there are two OLS approaches for analyzing data involving more than one level [23]. First, we can disaggregate the data such that each firm is assigned a score representing the country within which it is nested. The problem with this solution is that multiple firms are in the same country and are thus exposed to similar stimuli within the country. As a result, one cannot satisfy the independence of observations assumption [57]. The second major approach is to aggregate firms and to investigate relationships at the country level of analysis. The disadvantage of this approach is that potentially meaningful firm level variance in the outcome measure is ignored.

To test the hypotheses outlined above, we used a multi-level model that simultaneously estimates firm-level and country-level effects [57]. Our approach overcomes the disadvantages of the previous two approaches because we can model explicitly both within and between country variance as well as investigate the influence of countries on firm-level outcomes [23]. Because information at both the firm level and the country level was used to determine the open and closed B2B e-commerce usage, our approach should obtain better estimates than traditional OLS approaches. As the use of open (closed) e-commerce is 0/1 variables, we used a Bernoulli distribution with logit link function for the estimations. ## 4.3. Results

The statistical package HLM 6 was used for analyzing the multilevel models. All country-level independent variables were standardized in order to ease the interpretation of analysis results [57]. As our level-1 model is a logit model, it was not meaningful to compare the variances at different levels or to compare the variances between baseline model and full model. Therefore, instead of conducting a series of HLM analyses to examine additional variance explained by newly added variables at different levels, we only did one HLM analysis for open B2B e-commerce and another one for closed B2B e-commerce.

The main analytical results are shown in Fig. 1, with a sample size of 10,455 at firm level and 27 at country level. We find that there is a negative relationship between social trust and closed B2B e-commerce $( \beta = - 0 . 2 3 ; ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This finding supports Hypothesis 1a, namely, that high social trust will prevent firms from using closed B2B e-commerce. Hypothesis 1b is also supported in that we find that social trust is positively associated with the use of open B2B e-commerce $( \beta = 0 . 3 4 ; ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ suggesting high social trust may promote the diffusion of open B2B e-commerce.

At the firm level, the results show that there are positive associations between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce $( \beta = 0 . 5 3 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ as well as between IT experience and open B2B e-commerce $( \beta = 0 . 7 2 ; ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which support Hypothesis 2a and Hypothesis 2b. Finally, there are also cross-level effects. We find that social trust negatively moderates the relationship between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce $( \beta = - 0 . 1 3 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting Hypothesis 3a that the positive effect of IT experience on closed B2B e-commerce becomes weaker when the social trust of a country is higher. The moderating role of social trust on the relationship between IT experienced and open B2B e-commerce, however, is not significant, suggesting Hypothesis 3b is not supported.

Regarding control variables, the results suggest that large firms are more likely to use open and closed B2B e-commerce than small firms. This result is consistent with previous finding that large firms are more likely to use B2B e-commerce [2,11]. The influence of industry is also significant. For instance, firms in the telecommunications industry and in the hospital activities industry are more likely to use open and closed B2B e-commerce than firms in other industries. In terms of country-level control variables, we find that the number of Internet hosts is positively associated with the use of closed B2B e-commerce $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , implying that the IT-intensive environment may promote firms to use closed B2B e-commerce.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Findings

We find that there is a negative relationship between social trust in a country and firm use of closed B2B e-commerce in that country. This result is consistent with the proposition in prior research that people in high-trust environments are less likely to form committed transaction relationships [39,69]. In closed B2B e-commerce, trading partners usually need to commit to each other and make relationship-specific investments [29,62]. These committed investments cannot be recovered if the transaction relationship does not continue. Because firms in high social trust countries are unwilling to make such committed investments required for developing closed B2B e-commerce, they should be less likely to use closed B2B e-commerce than would firms in low-trust countries.

On the other hand, there is a positive relationship between social trust and the use of open B2B e-commerce. This may be due to open B2B e-commerce being particularly well-suited to firms in high-trust countries in that it allows them to more easily transact with new partners with open standards [39,72]. Furthermore, the diffusion of open B2B e-commerce can be viewed as a type of collective action, which involves a dilemma in that no firm is willing to adopt it early [46]. Social trust may work as a mechanism to solve this dilemma because it can lead people to cooperate in collective action [35,67]. If a firm trusts that other firms will adopt open standards later, it may decide to adopt open standards early [35,63]. As a result, high social trust may help promote the diffusion of open standards in a country.

In addition, the social trust of a country may also set boundaries for the influence of firm-level factors on B2B commerce. In this study, we find that a firm’s IT experience facilitates the use of both open and closed B2B e-commerce, probably because the deployment of B2B e-commerce requires necessary IT skills and expertise that come from experience. However, we also find that the positive association between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce becomes weaker when the social trust of a country is higher. This may be due to firm’s unwillingness to commit in countries with high social trust. In such countries, firms are usually unwilling to commit to particular trading partners and engage in closed B2B e-commerce. Therefore, even though a firm in such countries has the necessary IT experience and ability to deploy closed B2B e-commerce, it may not do that due to its unwillingness in closed B2B e-commerce.

## 5.2. Research and practice implications

Prior studies have investigated how particularized trust between trading partners affects the use of closed B2B e-commerce. Though important and insightful, these studies have not noticed the role of generalized trust in the use of B2B e-commerce, especially in open B2B e-commerce. In fact, firm-level antecedents are usually highlighted in the research of B2B e-commerce and related technologies (i.e., IOS), whereas factors beyond firm-level, especially national cultural and institutional factors, are often under-investigated [58]. On the other hand, research has shown that cultural and institutional factors can play important role in the adoption of new IT [54,60]. For instance, it is found that cultural dimensions of individualism/collectivism and uncertainty avoidance can influence the ease and speed of IT adoption [25]. Therefore, we suggest that researchers may need to go beyond firm-level and pay more attention to national cultural and institutional factors when studying B2B e-commerce, especially open e-commerce.

The strategic importance of closed B2B e-commerce to firm performance has been highlighted in the literature, and it is suggested that firms may gain competitive advantages by conducting closed B2B e-commerce with their partners [30,47,64]. However, we find that high social trust in a country may impede firms from using closed B2B e-commerce. Our findings suggest that social trust, which usually leads to favorable results such as economic growth and adoption of online shopping [17,31,44], may have some unexpected outcomes. That is, high social trust may reduce a firm’s willingness to conduct closed B2B e-commerce with its trading partners. If closed B2B e-commerce has strategic significance on firm performance [30,64], high social trust in a country may be harmful to firm strategic performance.

On the other hand, high social trust promotes firms to use open B2B e-commerce. By mitigating the uncertainty involved in transactions, social trust enables firms to find the best trading partners in markets with the aid of open standards. Since research has shown that social trust is positively associated with economic growth [4,31], it is suggested that open B2B e-commerce may be more important to the growth of the whole economy than closed B2B e-commerce, though the latter is strategically important to particular firms.

Our study also provides implications for policy makers. The advantages of open B2B e-commerce, such as cost saving and flexibility, have been widely noted in the literature [19,46]. As a new generation of IOS standards, open standards are suggested to facilitate supply chain coordination and improve supply chain performance [10,72]. However, e-commerce based on closed IOS (e.g., customized EDI) is still prevalent despite the claimed advantages of open B2B e-commerce [70]. Our study implies that the low social trust of a country may be an inhibitor for the diffusion of open B2B e-commerce. Due to the positive influence of social trust on economic growth in general and on open B2B e-commerce in particular, a country’s policy makers may consider taking action to improve the social trust in their country. Given well-functioning institutions are the main sources of social trust [41,59], policy makers may improve their countries’ social trust by modifying some elements of the institutions.

The study also sends important messages to managers. One of the primary benefits that firms can gain from open B2B e-commerce is the network effects of open IOS—the wider diffusion of open IOS, the higher value of open IOS to its users [72]. However, in different countries, the benefits from network effects may be different. For instance, in countries with high social trust, firms are more willing to transact with new partners through open standards. As a result, a firm may gain a lot from the network effects of open B2B e-commerce. In this case, firms should consider the network effects when making the decision on the adoption of e-commerce. To contrast, the benefits from network effects in lowtrust countries will not be as high as that in high-trust countries. In low-trust countries, firms are usually unwilling to transact with new partners; instead, they stick with existing partners. Consequently, even though many other firms have adopted open IOS, firms sticking with their existing partners will not benefit from the network effects of open IOS. Accordingly, in low-trust countries, the network effects of open B2B e-commerce should not be considered a critical factor in firms’ decisions on the choice of different types of e-commerce.

## 5.3. Limitations

This study has three limitations that may be addressed in future research. One limitation is that we only have information about whether a firm has used open or closed B2B e-commerce, but not the details on how the firm uses them. B2B e-commerce may vary in use pattern [64]. Future research may expand our study by investigating the use of B2B e-commerce in detail. Further, due to the important role of trust in e-commerce, this study only focuses on the effect of social trust on B2B e-commerce. As research has suggested that national cultural and institutional factors are under-investigated in B2B e-commerce [58], future research may investigate how other national cultural and institutional factors affect B2B e-commerce and expand e-commerce research at the macro level. Finally, this study only investigates the effect of IT experience on B2B e-commerce and the cross-level effect of social trust on this relationship. Future research may take more firm-level antecedents into account and examine more cross-level effects.

## 6. Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature by investigating the direct and indirect effects of social trust on the use of B2B e-commerce. We distinguish between open and closed B2B e-commerce and suggest that social trust will have different effects on these two types of e-commerce. In addition, we also suggest that social trust will moderate the relationship between IT experience and B2B e-commerce. The analysis results show that social trust increases the use of open B2B e-commerce, but decreases the use of closed B2B e-commerce. Also, the social trust at the country level negatively moderates the association between IT experience and closed B2B e-commerce at the firm level. The finding of a negative effect of social trust on closed B2B e-commerce is interesting because it is contrary to the intuitive insight that trust should promote e-commerce. Our study reminds researchers that more attention should be paid to different types of trust as they may lead to unexpected results. Furthermore, research has suggested that open B2B e-commerce is the future direction of e-commerce due to its various advantages. Our study implies that in countries with low social trust, migration to open B2B e-commerce might be more difficult than expected.
