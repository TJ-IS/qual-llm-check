# Market Reaction to the Announcement of an Information Technology Investment: Evidence from Indonesia

Singgih Wijayana<sup>\*</sup> singgihw@ugm.ac.id and Didi Achjari

Department of Accounting Gadjah Mada University

Tel: (+62 274) 54 85 10 | Mobile Phone +6281391117864 | Fax: (+62 274) 56 32 12

ABSTRACT: This study examines the market’s reaction around and subsequent to the announcement of an Information Technology Investment (ITI). The Scholes-Williams’ and Dimson’s betas, a more accurate, and longer time-window are employed to capture market reactions in an environment of thin trading on the Indonesian capital market from 2001 to 2016. The results show that Indonesian investors need time to recognise the signals of either an increase or decrease in value from an ITI. This study also finds that greater market reactions are characterised by firms in the banking industry, smaller sized firms, lower competitive position firms, first-time IT adopter firms, and enterprise resource planning investment firms.

Keywords: Event Study, Adjusted Beta, Information Technology Investment, Market Value, Post Announcement Drift, Indonesian Bank Industry.

## 1. Introduction

Prior studies suggest that investors react to Information Technology Investment (ITI) announcements by publicly listed firms (Agrawal, Kishore, and Rao, 2006; Bharadwaj, Keil, and Mähring, 2009; Dehning, Richardson, and Stratopoulos, 2005; Muhanna and Stoel, 2010). The previous literature has also documented that ITI presents a positive signal for potential increases in firms’ wealth (Beccalli, 2007; Chen et al., 2006; Dehning et al., 2005; Lee, Xiang, and Kim, 2011), as shown by the investors’ reactions to the announcements of an ITI (Agrawal et al., 2006; Chen and Chang, 2010; Dobija et al., 2012; Dos Santos, Peffers, and Mauer, 1993).<sup>1</sup> Nevertheless, Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014), who examined market reactions to the announcement of an ITI in Indonesia, found no immediate reactions around the announcement of the ITI.

The theory of bounded rationality suggests that people try to make rational decisions, but their ability to do so is often limited by inadequate knowledge, ability, time, and other factors (e.g. Kahneman, 2003; Simon, 1955). In the context of asset pricing, the theoretical and empirical literature suggests that financial markets are not always informationally efficient and therefore investors continue to buy a security long after the initial, rational, reactions to the information released should have ended (Berrada, 2009; Lei, 2010; Liang, Yang, and Cai, 2017a; Liang et al., 2017b). Investors’ uncertainty about fundamentals tends to increase the stock’s volatility, suggesting that the investors’ ‘learning risk’ causes the stock’s volatility to increase

(e.g. Berrada, 2009; Lei, 2010; Veronesi, 1999). Drawing from this bounded rationality literature, we expect that investors have a limited ability to process the information (an ITI announcement). They have a limited ability to recognise the impact of an ITI on a firm’s performance, leading to uncertainty about whether the ITI will positively or negatively affect the firm’s asset price. Correspondingly, the result of no market reaction is likely to be related to the precise time that the market reacts to the ITI’s announcement. Analyses of the market’s reactions after the announcement of an ITI are more likely to capture the effect of this ITI’s information on the market (Barua and Mani, 2018; Ferguson, Finn, and Hall, 2005).<sup>2</sup>

Furthermore, the theory of contrast effects suggests that investors often interpret information by contrasting it with what was previously observed (Hartzmark and Shue, 2018).<sup>3</sup> The impact of an ITI on the firm’s market value can be positive or negative, according to the type of ITI (Beccalli, 2007). Given that the value of an initially observed signal inversely biases the investors’ perception of the subsequent signal (Hartzmark and Shue, 2018), we expect a positive or negative market reaction subsequent to an ITI announcement. Positive stock price reactions are cancelled out by negative ones (Oh, Gallivan, and Kim, 2006; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015). Correspondingly, this study investigates whether the post ITI announcement’s drift and positive/ negative market reactions capture the usefulness of ITI’s information.

This study also examines five factors that explain the variations in the market’s reactions

##

to ITI announcements. First, financial (banking) firms adopt IT more aggressively than nonfinancial sector firms (e.g. Beccalli, 2007; Dehning, Richardson, and Zmud, 2003; Dos Santos et al., 1993; Lapavitsas and Dos Santos, 2008). Correspondingly, ITI announcements by firms in the banking industry are likely to result in a greater market reaction, relative to those in other industries. Second, larger firms disclose more items more frequently than smaller firms do; therefore, firm size is negatively associated with the market’s reaction (e.g. Atiase, 1985; Bamber, 1987; Chen and Sami, 2008). Third, ITIs are a must for firms to allow them to maintain their competitive advantage. Firms with a higher revenue or sales turnover will invest in IT, leading to greater market reactions, relative to those with lower competitive positions. Fourth, first-time IT adopter firms are responded to by investors more than the later IT adopters, because the earliest ITI announcement has a greater surprise effect, and thus, leads to greater market reactions (Chen and Sami, 2008; Lev and Zarowin, 1999). Lastly, an ITI in enterprise resource planning (ERP) can increase firm efficiency and firm accessibility for all parties (Atul, 2000; Chen et al., 2006). An ITI in ERP is also fundamental in terms of providing basic needs before a firm can invest in other types of IT effectively. Consequently, an ITI in ERP is likely to be valued more by investors, relative to other types of investment.

To investigate market reactions to ITI announcements and the factors explaining the variation in the market’s reactions, this study employs a market model with three alternative betas: 1) traditional, 2) Scholes-Williams (Scholes and Williams, 1977), and 3) Dimson (Dimson, 1979). The first one is used to ensure that this study uses the same method employed by Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014), by applying it to this study’s dataset. The adjusted betas of Scholes-Williams and Dimson are employed in the context of the Indonesian trading capital market. Traditional cumulative abnormal returns $( C A R _ { M m } )$ , Scholes-Williams $( C A R _ { S c h } )$ , and

Dimson $( C A R _ { D i m } )$ are calculated using the cross-sectional average daily abnormal return from 1 day before and after (CAR<sub>3</sub>) and 2 days up to sixty days after (CAR<sub>59</sub>) the ITI’s announcement. $C A R _ { 3 }$ is employed to ensure that this study uses the same event window as Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014). $C A R _ { 5 9 }$ is employed to anticipate investors’ delayed response in recognising the impact of an ITI on a firm’s value. This study also applies the division of positive and negative abnormal returns to deal with write-offs that potentially lead to failing to capture the market’s reactions to ITI announcements (Oh et al., 2006; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015). This study uses z-tests to examine whether market reactions exist and t-tests to compare split-samples, based on the factors that explain variations in the market’s reactions.

This study finds no significant market reactions around ITIs announcements for all the alternative methods of calculating $C A R _ { 3 }$ . Significant market reactions subsequent to ITIs announcements are found under $C A R _ { 5 9 S c h }$ and $C A R _ { 5 9 D i m } .$ , supporting this study’s prediction of detecting market reactions under longer event windows. These results also indicate that the use of an adjusted beta in $C A R _ { 5 9 S c h }$ and $C A R _ { 5 9 D i m }$ captures the abnormal returns better than the use of the adjusted beta in the traditional market model $( C A R _ { 5 9 M m } )$ . For the division of positive and negative abnormal returns, the z-test results show that positive (negative) reactions are significantly greater (smaller) than zero at 1% level of significance for both $C A R _ { 3 }$ and $C A R s g ,$ supporting this study’s first hypothesis. These results present a further explanation why Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014) found no market reaction around ITI announcements. They might be able to capture the market’s reaction if they divide the positive and negative abnormal returns. The results of examining the factors influencing the variations in the market’s reaction show that greater market reactions are characterised by firms in the banking industry, smaller firms, lower competitive position firms, first-time IT adopter firms, and ERP investment firms.

Prior studies indicate that the links between ITI and firm performance remain inconclusive (Khallaf, 2012).<sup>4</sup> By adding empirical evidence of an examination of the market’s reaction both around and subsequent to an ITI’s announcement on the Indonesian capital market, this study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, the use of the Indonesian capital market, characterised by its thin trading, adds empirical contributions beyond the previous studies that focused on a non-thin market.<sup>5</sup> While most prior studies use a non-adjusted beta, this study employs Scholes-William’s and Dimson’s betas to detect abnormal returns. Second, in contrast to the prior studies that focused on the market’s reaction around an ITI announcement (e.g. Achjari and Wahyuningtyas, 2014; Agrawal et al., 2006; Benco and Prather, 2008; Dehning et al., 2003; Hayes, Hunton, and Reck, 2000; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015), this study focuses on the market reactions both around and subsequent to an ITI announcement. It presents empirical evidence with a new perspective of a longer period event window. Third, the use of positive and negative abnormal returns in the analyses complement prior studies that concern the potential write-off between positive and negative abnormal returns (e.g. Oh et al., 2006; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015). Lastly, the use of the market perspective captures the firms’ performance, which may not be seen with accounting-based measures. The intangible benefits obtained from ITI are captured and reflected in firms’ market values. This study shows that the intangible benefits of market value are higher for banking firms, smaller firms, lower competitive position firms, first-time IT adopter firms, and ERP investment firms.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 presents the theoretical background and hypotheses development. Section 3 demonstrates how the data, sample, and research methodology are used to investigate the hypotheses. Section 4 presents the results of the hypotheses testing. Section 5 presents the summary and conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

## 2.1 Market reactions to ITI announcements

The theoretical background for the information content is derived from the early seminal studies into information content (Ball and Brown, 1968; Beaver, 1968; Fama et al., 1969). Ball and Brown (1968) and Beaver (1968) suggest that the usefulness of the information contained in financial reports can be assessed by analysing the changes in share prices around earnings announcements. Any variability in the security’s price or volume, in response to new information in the financial report, is evidence that the information is useful for investors (Ball and Brown, 1968; Beaver, 1968; Kothari, 2001; Lev, 1989).<sup>6</sup> With regard to the association between capital investments and future equity returns, Xing (2008), based on an empirical investigation of a firm’s value effect through the Q theory, suggests that firms with high book-to-market ratios will invest less than firms with low book-to-market ratios. Similarly, from the perspective of the catering theory, Polk and Sapienza (2009) empirically show how stock market mispricing influences an individual firm’s investment decisions. In particular, it is suggested that capital investment is negatively correlated with future equity returns (Polk and Sapienza, 2009; Xing, 2008).

Wide-ranging evidence shows that the theory of bounded rationality is important and incorporating bounded rationality in economic models has successfully described economic behaviour (Conlisk, 1996). It suggests that decision makers should be viewed as boundedly rational (Kahneman, 2003; Simon, 1955). Investors continue to buy a security long after the first information released because the financial markets are not always informationally efficient (Berrada, 2009; Lei, 2010; Liang et al., 2017a; Liang et al., 2017b). The literature of incomplete information suggests that ‘learning risk’ is generated when investors update their beliefs (Lei, 2010). The price for the security may continue to rise as investors may not believe that they have enough time to make decisions. Investors’ uncertainty about the fundamentals tends to increase stock volatility, suggesting that the ‘learning risk’ causes the stock volatility to increase (Veronesi, 1999). On the basis of this bounded rationality, we expect that investors have a limited ability to process the information (an ITI announcement), and thus, it takes some time for investors to recognise the impact of an ITI on a firm’s value.

The prior literature shows a delayed price response to the new information’s release (e.g. Bernard and Thomas, 1989). The stock’s price does not immediately fully reflect the implications of current earnings for future earnings (e.g. Ball and Brown, 1968; Beaver, 1968; Bernard and Thomas, 1990). This post-earnings announcement drift literature suggests that the drift in the market’s reactions remains for several weeks (Livnat and Mendenhall, 2006), or up to 13 weeks (Foster, Olsen, and Shevlin, 1984) or 60 days (Bernard and Thomas, 1989; Liang, 2003) subsequent to the earnings announcement. The delay in the price’s response to new information may occur because investors fail to assimilate the newly available information (e.g. Bernard and Thomas, 1989). This reasoning is applied to ITI announcements where the direct impact of an ITI on a firm’s value cannot be ensured by traders or investors (Dehning et al.,

##

2005). The literature suggests that an ITI leads to either better firm performance (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Kim, Xiang, and Lee, 2009; Lee et al., 2011), a productivity paradox<sup>7</sup> (Badescu and Garcés-Ayerbe, 2009; Dehning and Richardson, 2002; Dos Santos and Peffers, 1995; Osei-Bryson and Ko, 2004) or mixed results (Beccalli, 2007; Ho, Wu, and Xu, 2011). In the setting of an ITI announcement, investors do not know whether the ITI will positively or negatively affec firm performance. In a financial market, the contrast effects literature suggests investors perceive new news as more impressive if the prior news was bad and less impressive if the prior news was good (Hartzmark and Shue, 2018). The value of a previously observed signal inversely biases the perception of the following signal. Consequently, a positive or negative market reaction around the ITI’s announcement and a negative or positive market reaction subsequent to the ITI’s announcement can be expected.

Indeed, a number of studies have documented that an ITI has a positive impact on a firm’s products, services, internal processes, and performance (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Kim et efficiency and productivity. In the case of an ITI in ERP, integrating the data into a central storage and application can increase a firm’s accessibility for all parties and thus increase the firm’s efficiency (Atul, 2000; Chen et al., 2006). Quick decisions can be made by providing a financial analysis, on-time sale’s reporting, inventory, and production reports through ERP (Atul, 2000). Thus, a firm’s investment in information technology may indicate an increase in the firm’s future cash flow and performance. This potential future benefit from the ITI is valued by investors, as indicated by greater market values for ITI firms, compared to those non-ITI firms

##

(Henderson et al., 2010; Muhanna and Stoel, 2010).

Stock market reactions to ITI announcements have also been investigated across various countries. For instance, Ferguson et al. (2005) investigated stock market reactions to e-commerce projects in Australia. They found that Australian markets appear to react positively to ITI/ecommerce project announcements. IT failure is reacted negatively by the market, as indicated by a drop in the average cumulative abnormal return (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). Positive reactions to IT outsourcing, R&D, and ITI announcements are found in the capital markets of Poland (Dobija et al., 2012), the USA (Dehning et al., 2005; Ehie and Olibe, 2010; Muhanna and Stoel, 2010), Japan (Nagaoka, 2006) and Europe (Hall and Oriani, 2006). However, in the context of Indonesia, prior studies have found no reaction to ITI announcements. Hendratmoko and Achjari (2008) and Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014) found no significant market reaction around the announcement of an ITI.

The ITI literature has documented several explanations as to why ITIs do not significantly affect firms’ performance or the market. First, external factors such as the economic and industry conditions contribute to the firms’ productivity (Dehning and Richardson, 2002; Dehning et al., 2005). When the external and industry factors are considered, the market’s reaction towards ITI information is explained more by the external and industry variables, rather than the firms’ performance. Second, prior studies also argue that increasing a firm’s performance, as a result of an ITI, may not always be followed by a response from the market (Dos Santos and Peffers, 1995; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015), for example when market conditions are unfavourable or firms are in financial distress (Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2009).

The third explanation is related to the time when the market’s reaction towards an ITI’s

##

announcement is determined. When making an ITI, there could be delays in the actual implementation, due to labour shortages, inadequate technical skills, poor planning or technological unfeasibility (Dehning et al., 2005). Following the post-earnings announcement drift literature, which also suggests that the price response to new information is delayed due to the investors’ failure to quickly assimilate the new information (e.g. Bernard and Thomas, 1989), it is expected that the market’s reaction to an ITI will occur subsequent to its announcement. Lastly, the impact of an ITI on market value can be positive or negative, according to the type of ITI (Beccalli, 2007). Given that market reactions are determined using cross-sectional averages of daily abnormal returns, the use of positive stock price reactions may nullify the negative ones (Oh et al., 2006; Roztocki and Weistroffer, 2015). This decreases the likelihood of capturing precise market reactions due to ITI announcements. Following this positive and negative division literature, this study expects to capture market reactions to an ITI when the testing is based on separated positive and negative abnormal returns. Correspondingly, the first hypothesis is presented as follows:

$H _ { I } ;$ : There are market reactions to the announcements of ITIs.

## 2.2 Factors explaining market reactions to ITI announcements

## 2.2.1 Industry

Prior studies have documented inconclusive results about the association between market reactions to ITIs in the financial and non-financial industries. Early studies of the market’s reaction to an ITI’s announcement propose that the ITI should be more important for service firms than for other types of firms (Dos Santos et al., 1993). The stock market’s response to ITI announcements in the financial and non-financial sectors is explained by innovative and non-

innovative ITIs (Dos Santos et al., 1993), the strategic role of an ITI (Dehning et al., 2003), contextual factors such as size, leverage, and time period (Im, Dow, and Grover, 2001) and efficiency (Beasley, Bradford, and Dehning, 2009). Interestingly, the type of industry is found to be a factor that explains ITI failures, as Bharadwaj et al. (2009) found that the market penalises manufacturing firms that incur failures slightly more than it does for service firms.

The impact of an ITI on banks’ performance is an important issue, because an ITI constitutes a substantial component of their costs, and has a strong influence on a bank’s strategy and operations (Beccalli, 2007). In addition, in the banking industry, the impact of different types of ITI (services, hardware, and software) on the banks’ performance is heterogeneous. Investments in IT services from external providers, for such things as consulting, implementation, training and education, and support services positively influence their profits and efficiency, while the acquisition of hardware and software reduces the banks’ performance (Beccalli, 2007). Given this unclear evidence of the impact of an ITI on banks’ performance (Beccalli, 2007), there still remains some doubt as to whether ITIs affect firms’ market values.

The different nature of business between banks and non-banking firms leads to an investigation of whether ITI announcements by banking and non-banking firms are valued differently by investors. In line with past studies (e.g. Achjari and Wahyuningtyas, 2014; Beasley et al., 2009; Dehning et al., 2003; Dos Santos et al., 1993; Ehie and Olibe, 2010), this study proposes that the market’s reaction to an ITI’s announcement, is greater for firms in the banking industry than for those in non-banking industries, for the following reasons.

First, given that the literature suggests that financial sector firms adopt IT more aggressively than non-financial sector firms $\mathrm { d o } ^ { 8 } ,$ , the market’s reactions to ITI announcements tend to be greater for banking firms, relative to non-banking firms. Second, an ITI is a must in the banking industry, to allow it to maintain its competitive advantage. As a consequence, an ITI by a bank is valued more by investors, relative to other industries. In this case, investors are likely to react more to a bank’s ITI announcement, relative to a non-bank’s one. Thus, in line with prior studies, which suggest that market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for the finance industry firms, rather than the non-financial industry firms; it is expected that market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for banking firms than for non-banking firms. Thus, the hypothesis is presented as follows:

$H _ { 2 a \cdot }$ Market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for banking industry firms than for nonbanking industry firms.

## 2.2.2 Firm Size

Contextual factors such as size, leverage, and time period explain the stock market’s response to ITI announcements (Im et al., 2001). Smaller firms are likely to disclose less information to the public than larger firms are (Atiase, 1985; Bamber, 1987). The prior literature suggests that the market responds more to information from smaller firms than to that of larger firms (Hora, Tondkar, and McEwen, 2004). The authors argue that less information (characterised by smaller firms) has greater information value on the market than more information (characterised by larger firms). This is because the information disclosed by small firms is worth more than that disclosed by large firms (Hora et al., 2004). Larger firms disclose more items, more frequently, than smaller firms do; therefore, firm size is negatively associated with the market’s reaction (e.g. Chen and Sami, 2008). The information released by firms that less frequently disclose information is worth more than the ones that disclose more frequently. The former leads to greater surprises and attention from the market’s participants than the latter. Correspondingly, smaller firms are likely to announce ITI information less often than larger firms. Once smaller firms invest and announce their investment, it comes as a greater surprise and attracts the attention of investors to see how this new ITI will affect the performance or productivity of the sector. Consequently, these ITI announcements have greater information content to trigger larger market reactions.

$H _ { 2 b } .$ Market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for smaller firms than for larger firms.

## 2.2.3 Competitive Position

Prior studies suggest that ITI plays an important role in managing firms strategically, for example in the hotel industry (e.g. Bilgihan et al., 2011) or in the US lodging industry (Bilgihan and Wang, 2016). The timely deployment of new IT applications increases the efficiency and opportunities for services, meeting customers’ expectations, improved control, more effective marketing strategies, and expanded business opportunities (Bilgihan et al., 2011; Piccoli and Ives, 2005). The dynamic capabilities literature suggests that firms renew their superior capabilities and leverage their current asset positions to enable them to maintain competitiveness (Piccoli and Ives, 2005; Teece, Pisano, and Shuen, 1997), so firms can successfully do this through an ITI, to respond to shifts in the business environment. To maintain their competitive positions, firms tend to invest in IT more aggressively, particularly in the current turbulent global environment (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006).

Prior studies indicate that ITI positively associates with company performance, for instance sales (Sircar, Turnbow, and Bordoloi, 2000), and as a source of competitive advantage (Breznik, 2012). Porter and Millar (1985) argue that IT contributes to competitive advantage in two ways: enhancing differentiation and lowering price. Companies invest in IT, so they can create more cost effective products and provide better value than their competitors. Therefore, by using IT, a company can sell its products more easily than its competitors and outperforms them.

From the market’s perspective, the strategic role of an ITI can explain the variation in the stock market’s response to ITI announcements (Dehning et al., 2003). Prior theoretical and empirical literature on capital investment and stock returns suggest that an effective ITI increases a firm’s value (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Kim et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2011). Given this positive between capital investment and equity returns, firms with higher competitive positions probably have higher firm value and consequently tend to experience greater market reactions to their ITIs.

H<sub>2c</sub>: Market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for higher competitive position firms than for lower competitive position firms.

## 2.2.4 First-time adopter

In the real world, a company may frequently invest in IT. For instance, one particular company implemented customer relationship management (CRM) and ERP in its first and second years, consecutively. Both implementations are considered to be the first investment in each year. ITIs across the period can also be similar. The first implementation of a particular IT may cause a great surprise for the market, while subsequent investments in the same technology may not get much attention from investors. Therefore, the investors may respond differently to the first and subsequent implementations of a particular technology. They may expect that the company will benefit more from the first implementation.

##

This study predicts that first-time IT adopter firms are likely to be valued by investors more than late IT adopters. The underlying argument is that if firms rarely invest in IT, once they invest and announce it, it comes as a greater surprise and attracts the attention of investors to see how these investments will affect the performance or productivity of the sector. Consequently, these ITI announcements have greater information content to generate a market reaction. The announcement of first-time IT adopter firms will have a greater surprise effect, and therefore, lead to greater market reactions (Lev and Zarowin, 1999). They also benefit by being a pioneer, which can bring competitive advantages for them (Bilgihan and Wang, 2016; Piccoli and Ives, 2005; Teece et al., 1997). Consequently, investors tend to value first-time IT adopter firms more than the later ones.

H<sub>2d</sub>: Market reactions to ITI announcements are greater for first-time IT adopter firms than for later IT adopter firms.

## 2.2.5 The types of technology

The impact of ITI on market value can be positive or negative, depending on the type of ITI (Beccalli, 2007). ITI can vary across companies, from very simple technology and low cost implementations, such as internet access in an office, to very complex, lengthy, and expensive processes, such as with ERP’s implem entation. Indeed, the literature suggests that the information technology’s capabilities relate to firms’ competitive advantage (Bhatt and Grover, 2005). Prior studies suggest that an ITI in ERP can increase a firm’s accessibility for all parties and increase the firm’s efficiency (Atul, 2000; Chen et al., 2006). The use of ERP leads to a quick decision from a financial analysis, on-time sale’s reporting, inventory, and production reports (Atul, 2000). For example, a study in Taiwan finds that small and medium enterprises benefit from ERP’s implementation (Yang and Su, 2009).

##

From the capital market’s perspective, Benco and Prather (2008) present evidence of the market’s reaction to announcements of investments in ERP systems. An investment in ERP requires greater funding and effort than in other types of IT. It requires coordination and integration of the business’s processes amongst the business’s functions to make it a successful investment. The differences in ITIs may lead to different firm performance and market reactions. The variations in the market’s reactions to announcements of investments in ERP systems are expected, because an investment in ERP is deemed to be high risk, but the benefits are tremendous (Mehrjerdi, 2010). Therefore, the announcement of $\mathrm { E R P s }$ implementation may be responded to positively by investors, due to the future benefits of ERP. Based on these reasons, this study examines whether an ITI in ERP leads to greater market reactions, relative to those for non-ERP investments.

$H _ { 2 e } .$ Market reactions to ITI announcements are better for ERP investment firms than for non-ERP investment firms.

## 3. Sample, data, and research methodology

The sample data comprise Indonesian firms listed on the Indonesia Stock Exchange (IDX). Data from 2001 to 2016 were collected; 2001 was selected because many firms were expected to make ITIs after recovering from Indonesia’s 1998 crisis. The period of analysis finishes in 2016 due to this being the most recent year that data are available. The sample to investigate $H _ { I }$ and $H _ { 2 }$ is selected based on the following criteria: 1) the firms were listed on the IDX during the period 2001-2016; 2) the firms’ ITI information is available, so the event date and window can be determined; 3) the firms’ security price is available to determine the market reactions around and subsequent to the announcement of the $\operatorname { I T I } ;$ 4) firm-specific data are available; and 5) the date of the ITI’s announcement (the event date) by the firms is not potentially confounded by

other information or events. Table 1 presents the outcome of the sample’s selection criteria and the number of observations employed to examine both $H _ { I }$ and $H _ { 2 }$ .

This research mainly uses Bloomberg’s and Compustat Global’s databases and websites to source the data for this study. Firm-specific data such as firm size and the industry sectors are collected from these databases. Google Search, Yahoo! ## 3.1 Market reaction to ITI information

The market’s reaction towards the information’s release is investigated by using event study methodology. It is common for an event study to extend the window for the period by more than one day. Thompson et al. (1995) noted that the precise identification of an event window is important, for at least three reasons (Bowman, 1983): 1) the power of the tests is highly sensitive to the precision of the event window’s identification; 2) misidentification of the event window is likely to reduce the ability to observe security price movements; and 3) the

precision of the date effectively controls the potential problems presented by confounding events. The argument to support the extension of the length of time is related to the uncertainty in identifying exactly when the information released is responded to by the market’s participants. Following prior studies, the market’s reactions around (e.g. Rees, 1995) and post-ITI announcement’s drift (e.g. Bernard and Thomas, 1989; Foster et al., 1984) are calculated by accumulating the daily abnormal return from day 1 to day+1 and day+2 to day+60, respectively.<sup>9</sup> These CAR are expressed as (Equation 1):

$C A R _ { j 3 , 5 9 } = \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { 3 , 5 9 } A R _ { j t }$ …….……… (1)

Where $C A R _ { j 3 , 5 9 }$ is the CAR for j securities for a period of 3 and 59 days duration; j is the number of securities; and $A R _ { j t }$ is the average daily abnormal returns for j securities for period t.

A firm’s abnormal return $( A R _ { j t } )$ is the difference between the firm’s actual return $( R _ { j t } )$ and its expected return $( E ( R _ { j t } ) ) ^ { 1 0 }$ during the event window. To calculate the actual returns, this study uses simple returns, which are defined as the difference between the price for security j for period $t \left( P _ { j t } \right)$ relative to the price for security j for period $t - l \left( P _ { j t - l } \right)$ . There must be more than 100 days of security price data during the estimation period. To prevent the event from influencing the normal performance model’s parameter estimates (MacKinlay, 1997), returns from the period of 10 days before the event window are excluded.

This study uses adjusted beta methods to calculate the expected returns, as presented in Equation 3 (Scholes and Williams, 1977) and Equation 4 (Dimson, 1979). Infrequent trading activities lead to non-synchronous trading, and therefore, the unadjusted beta is likely to result in biased beta estimates. Beta, measuring volatility, is represented by covariance security’s returns, relative to the market’s returns in the same period. The beta becomes biased if the period t of security returns and market returns are not at the same time (non-synchronous). Measured returns often deviate from true returns because reported closing processes typically represent trades prior to the actual close of the trading day (Scholes and Williams, 1977). If a security is not traded for a certain period, the price of the security used to calculate beta is taken from the last period’s price (last time traded), and this results in bias in

the beta estimates. To correct this bias, the lag and lead market returns’ models, based on Scholes and Williams’ (1977) or Dimson’s (1979) methods, are applied.<sup>12</sup>

The Scholes-Williams’ beta $( \beta _ { j } )$ is calculated as

$$
\beta_ {j} = \frac {\left(\beta_ {j} ^ {- n} + \dots + \beta_ {j} ^ {- 1} + \beta_ {j} ^ {0} + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ 1} + \dots + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ n}\right)}{\left(1 + 2 . \rho_ {1} + 2 . \rho_ {2} + \dots + 2 . \rho_ {n}\right)}
$$

where $\beta _ { j }$ is adjusted beta for security j; $\beta _ { j } ^ { - n } , \beta _ { j } ^ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { j } ^ { + n }$ is the estimate of the parameter, derived from the following regressions between the observed security’s return $( R _ { j , t } )$ and the market index’s return $( R _ { M t } )$ , with n lags, coincident and n leads, respectively:

$$
R _ {j, t} = \alpha_ {j} + \beta_ {j} ^ {- n} R _ {M t - n} + \varepsilon_ {j, t}; R _ {j, t} = \alpha_ {j} + \beta_ {j} ^ {0} R _ {M t} + \varepsilon_ {j, t} \text { and } R _ {j, t} = \alpha_ {j} + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ n} R _ {M t + n} + \varepsilon_ {j, t}.
$$

$\rho _ { n }$ is the first-order serial correlation coefficient of the market return’s proxy, derived from the estimate of the parameter from the following regressions: $R _ { M t } = \alpha _ { j } + \rho _ { 1 } R _ { M t - 1 } + \varepsilon _ { j , t }$ ;

$$
R _ {M t} = \alpha_ {j} + \rho_ {2} R _ {M t - 2} + \varepsilon_ {j, t} \text {   and   } R _ {M t} = \alpha_ {j} + \rho_ {n} R _ {M t - n} + \varepsilon_ {j, t}.
$$

Dimson’s beta $( \beta _ { j } )$ is calculated as

$$
\beta_ {j} = \beta_ {j} ^ {- n} + \dots + \beta_ {j} ^ {- 1} + \beta_ {j} ^ {0} + \beta_ {i} ^ {+ 1} + \dots + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ n}\tag{4}
$$

where $\beta _ { j } ^ { - n } , \beta _ { j } ^ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { j } ^ { + n }$ is the estimate of the parameter, derived from the following multiple regression between the observed security’s return and the market index’s return with n lags, coincident and n leads:

$$
R _ {j, t} = \alpha_ {j} +... + \beta_ {j} ^ {- n} R _ {M t - n} + \beta_ {j} ^ {- 1} R _ {M t - 1} +... + \beta_ {j} ^ {0} R _ {M t} + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ 1} R _ {M t + 1} +... + \beta_ {j} ^ {+ n} R _ {M, t + n} + \varepsilon_ {j, t}
$$

Where $R _ { j , t }$ is the return of security j for the period t; $R _ { M t - n }$ is the market’s returns for the period lag t-n and $R _ { M t + n }$ is the market’s returns for the period lead t+n.

To examine H<sub>1</sub>, a calculation of the cross-sectional average CAR around and subsequent to the announcement of the ITI must be made (Binder, 1998; Campbell, Lo, and MacKinlay, 1997; Kothari and Warner, 2004; MacKinlay, 1997).<sup>13</sup> Following the event study literature (e.g. Kothari and Warner, 2004), the average CAR for a sample of j securities of cross-listed firms is calculated by using the cross-sectional mean cumulative abnormal returns.

A z-test is employed to investigate whether the CARs in the around and post-ITI announcement period are significantly different from zero. To ensure that this study’s results equate to those of Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014), this study examines market reactions around ITI announcements using the same method they used in their study (traditional market model beta) and apply it in this study’s dataset. This study also applies adjusted betas by Scholes-Williams/Dimson in the event study, to ensure that appropriate methods are used in Indonesia’s thin market. This study considers the positive or negative effects of ITI on the firm’s value. Analyses by dividing positive and negative abnormal returns are performed to present further insights. This sign, or the direction of the surprise effect from the announcement indicates that ITI leads to either better firm performance (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Kim et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2011) or a productivity paradox (Badescu and Garcés-Ayerbe, 2009; Osei-Bryson and Ko, 2004).

## 3.2 Factors explaining the post-ITI announcement drift

Further analyses are performed to investigate the factors explaining the market’s reaction to ITI announcements. The magnitude of the market’s reaction can be different, depending on the type of industry, size of the firm, adoption time (first-time adopter), competitive position, and the type of technology. To address these issues, this study splits and compares the sample based on those factors. The t-tests are performed to provide thorough analyses of the market’s reaction to the announcement of an ITI. Given that the average size of the banking firms is greater than nonbanking firms (Table 8 ‘Descriptive Statistics’), the endogeneity issue may be significant. To address this issue, this study matches the size of the banking firms with the non-banking firms, and then compares them to show whether the impact of the banking industry is significant.

As in the previous section, the market’s reactions are measured by CAR during 3 days around (CAR<sub>3</sub>) and 59 days (CAR<sub>59</sub>) after the ITI’s announcement, using traditional, Scholes-Williams’ and Dimson’s betas. To support H<sub>2</sub>, the average market reactions to banking firms, large-sized firms, higher competitive position firms, first-time adopter firms, and ERP firms must be significantly greater than non-banking firms, smaller sized firms, lower competitive position firms, non-first-time adopter firms, and non-ERP firms, respectively.

## 4.2 Results of H<sub>2</sub> test

In this section, the market’s reactions are explained by industry, size, competitive position, first-time IT adopter firms and the types of technology (H<sub>2</sub>). We split the sample based on these factors and employed t-tests to investigate how significantly they affect market announcements, this study’s t-test results are presented, without (CAR) and with absolute values of the abnormal return (Cumulative Average Absolute-Abnormal Return (CAAR)). The use of absolute values removes directionality from the return measures and permits the aggregation of returns with negative and positive abnormal returns (e.g. Meek, 1991; Olibe, 2001), allowing this study to compare the difference in the magnitude of the market’s reactions based on industry, size, competitive position, first-time adopter and the types of technology.

## 4.2.2 Firm Size

Prior studies suggest that the market responds more to information from smaller firms than to that of larger firms, because the information disclosed by small firms is worth more than that disclosed by large firms (Hora et al., 2004). To examine the impact of size, this study splits the sample into above and below average, based on firms’ total assets. This study documents that 51 firms are above average and the remaining 126 firms are below average. Independent sample ttests are employed to investigate whether a greater size is likely to have a smaller reaction, relative to those of a smaller size.

## 4.2.4 First-time adopter

The current study predicts that first-time adopters will be more valued by investors than late IT adopters. The underlying argument is that the announcements by first-time IT adopter firms supply a greater surprise effect, and therefore, lead to greater market reactions (Lev and Zarowin, 1999). They also benefit as pioneers, which can bring competitive advantages for them (Bilgihan and Wang, 2016; Piccoli and Ives, 2005; Teece et al., 1997). Consequently, investors tend to value first-time IT adopter firms more than the later ones. To test the hypothesis, the earliest ITI announcement date is selected. This study identifies a first-time adopter firm for every announced investment. For example, across the 16 years, firms invest in IT several times, for the different types of investment, such as ERP, CRM, SCM, e-commerce, e-money, B2B, and B2C. This study categorises a particular firm as a first-time adopter when the announcement date is before the other firms, during this study’s sample period. Ninety-four out of 179 firms are identified as first-time adopters. An independent sample t-test is employed to investigate whether the earlier adoption time is associated with greater market reactions.

Table 10 Panel A presents the results of the t-test’s mean comparison between first-time IT adopters and late IT adopters for both the market’s reactions around $( C A R _ { 3 M m } , C A R _ { 3 S c h } ,$ and $C A R _ { 3 D i m } )$ and subsequent to an ITI’s announcement $( C A R _ { 5 9 M m } , C A R _ { 5 9 S c h } ,$ and $C A R _ { 5 9 D i m } )$ . ## 4.2.5 The types of technology

A quick decision can be made through ERP by providing an inventory and production reports, on-time sale’s reporting, and financial analysis (Atul, 2000). An ITI in ERP requires greater funding and effort than the other types of ITIs. It requires the coordination and integration of a business’s process amongst the business’s functions to make it a successful investment. On the basis of these reasons, this study divides the sample into ERP and non-ERP types of ITIs. ## 4.3 Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses are performed to check the robustness of the findings. They are related to: 1) the use of $C A R s ;$ 2) the use of one and two lags-leads of calculating betas; and 3) the use of alternative event windows for the post-ITI announcement drift. The results of using CAR<sub>5</sub> are similar to those in the main analyses. Overall, CAR<sub>5</sub> are identified as being analysed separately. When one and two lags-leads of calculating betas are used to analyse the market reaction subsequent to ITI, the results are similar to those in the main analyses (three lags-leads), but weaker in terms of having smaller z-value and significant at the 10% level. The results of using alternative event windows<sup>17</sup> subsequent to an ITI announcement show that alternative 1 (day+2 to day+10) has the weakest market reactions. Strongest market reactions (greatest different from zero) are found during the alternative 3 (day+30 to day+60). These results can be interpreted as an indication that after the event date’s market reactions, delayed market reactions are also found. On average, the reactions are robustly captured during day+30 to day+60. The results from these sensitivity analyses are untabulated but available upon request

## 5. Summary and conclusion

While most of the prior studies investigate the market’s reactions around an ITI’s announcement, this study examines the market’s reactions both around and subsequent to the announcement of an ITI. Both the traditional beta (the same method as Achjari and Wahyuningtyas (2014)) and the adjusted beta of Scholes-Williams’ and Dimson’s betas

##

(considered as appropriate for Indonesia’s thin capital market) are employed. This study finds that the results suggest that the CAR subsequent to the announcements of ITIs are significantly different from zero (supporting H<sub>1</sub>). Detailed analyses indicate that the effects of ITIs on the firms’ market values are better captured by using the adjusted betas than the traditional beta. By considering a separation between the positive and negative abnormal returns, investors capture the signals of either an increase or decrease in value from an ITI. This evidence adds further insights into how the market’s reactions both around and subsequent to an ITIs’ announcement are identified, given that prior studies and this current study fail to identify abnormal returns around ITI announcements with no separation between positive and negative abnormal returns.

Further analyses of the factors that explain the variations in the market’s reaction to ITI announcements can be summarised as follows. Banking firms have greater market reactions than non-banking firms. Banks, as part of the financial services industry, tend to be more aggressive with their ITIs. These aggressive investments lead to better performance and thus they are valued more by the market. This study finds that smaller firms create more of a market reaction than larger firms. This result supports the argument that the information disclosed by small firms is worth more than that from large firms, because larger firms are more likely to disclose more information than smaller firms. Contrary to this study’s prediction, the results show that higher competitive position firms have smaller market reactions than lower competitive position firms. or technological unfeasibility (Dehning et al., 2005) may prevent investors from reacting to higher competitive position firms. This study finds that first-time IT adopter firms produce greater market reactions relative to the later adopters (Lev and Zarowin, 1999). They supply a greater surprise and benefit as a pioneer, which brings competitive advantages for them (Bilgihan and Wang, 2016; Piccoli and Ives, 2005; Teece et al., 1997). Lastly, this study finds that the announcement of $\mathrm { E R P } ^ { \prime }$ s implementation by firms receives a positive response from investors because of the future benefits of ERP.

To conclude, this study finds strong evidence to support both $H _ { I }$ and four factors mentioned in H<sub>2</sub>. The empirical results present deep insights beyond the prior studies’ mixed results, which suggested no market reactions (Achjari and Wahyuningtyas, 2014; Dos Santos et al., 1993) or in contrast showed definite market reactions (Agrawal et al., 2006; Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Dehning et al., 2005; Muhanna and Stoel, 2010) around an ITI’s announcement. This study shows how the market captures the potential wealth increase or decrease of a firm’s ITI. Both around and subsequent to the ITI’s announcement cause the market to react and the variation in those reactions can be explained by the variations created by the firm being in the banking industry, its size, a first-time IT adopter and the type of technology. The results of sensitivity analyses indicate that the use of CAR<sub>5</sub>, one and two lags-leads to calculate beta and different event windows lead to similar conclusions as the main analyses

## Author biography

Singgih Wijayana (singgihw@ugm.ac.id) is a senior lecturer and the Head of Accounting Undergraduate Program at the Faculty of Economics and Business, Universitas Gadjah Mada (UGM). He is a member of the Indonesian Accounting Standards Boards responsible to set accounting standards in Indonesia. He serves as a member of the audit committee for PT TWC Borobudur, Prambanan, and Ratu Boko. He earned his bachelor and master’s degree in accounting from UGM. His Ph.D. degree was obtained in 2012 from the Monash University, majoring in accounting and finance. His research interest is cross-country analyses of financial reporting quality and capital market research. His research paper has been published in the tier 1 journals-International Review of Financial Analysis and Management International Review. Recently, his paper has been accepted for publication at The International Journal of Accounting. He has actively presented his research paper in international forums, such as the American Accounting Association (AAA), Southern African Accounting Association (SAAA), Taiwan Accounting Association (TAA) and Accounting and Finance Association of Australia and New Zealand (AFANZ). He has been invited as Guest Speaker in a number of international forums, including at Kwansei Gakuin University in Hyogo and Aoyama Gakuin University Tokyo. He received a number of awards, including Best Paper Award at the SAAA and TAA conferences. He was an awardee of Deloitte-IAAER Scholar Program 2013-2015, DIKTI-Academic Mobility 2015 and Fulbright Scholar Program 2017/2018. He was a visiting scholar at the University of Sydney in 2015, 2016, 2018 and at the University of Dayton-Ohio U.S. during 1 September 2017 - 27 February 2018.

Didi Achjari (didi\_a@ugm.ac.id) is a professor at the Faculty of Economics and Business, Universitas Gadjah Mada. His degrees are from the Universitas Gadjah Mada (S.E.), the University of New South Wales (M.Com.) and the Curtin University of Technology (DBA). His current research interests are on information technology, accounting information system, internet adoption/diffusion, electronic business and the roles of IT/IS in business strategy. His articles have appeared in several peer-reviewed scholarly journals such as Telecommunications Policy, Asian Journal of Business and Accounting, Allied Journal of Business Research, Journal of Indonesian Economy and Business, The Indonesian Journal of Accounting Research and Gadjah Mada International Journal of Business. He also authored many articles published in numerous international conference proceedings.
