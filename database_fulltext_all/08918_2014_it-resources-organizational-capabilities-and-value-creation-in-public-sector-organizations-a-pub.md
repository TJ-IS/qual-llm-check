## Abstract

What value does information technology (IT) create in governments and how does it do so? While business value of IT has been extensively studied in the information systems <sup>fi</sup>eld, this has not been the case for public value. This is in part due to a lack of theoretical bases for investigating IT value in the public sector. To address this issue, we present a conceptual model on the mechanism by which IT resources contribute to value creation in the publicsector organizations. We propose that the relationship between IT resources and organizational performance in governments is mediated by organizational capabilities and develop a theoretical model that delineates the paths from IT resources to organizational performance, drawing upon public-value management theory. This theory asserts that public managers, on behalf of the public, should actively strive to generate greater public value, as managers in the private sector seek to achieve greater private business value. On the basis of the review of public-value management literature, we suggest that the following <sup>fi</sup>ve organizational capabilities mediate the relationship between IT resources and public value – public service delivery capability, public engagement capability, co-production capability, resource-building capability, and public-sector innovation capability. We argue that IT resources in public organizations can enable public managers to advance public-value frontiers by cultivating these <sup>fi</sup>ve organizational capabilities and to overcome con<sup>fl</sup>icts among competing values. Thus, taxpayers might ask – Does this enormous amount of spending in IT create enough suf<sup>fi</sup>cient value to the public that justi<sup>fi</sup>es the investments?

It has been one of the primary research focuses in the information systems (IS) literature for the last couple of decades to <sup>fi</sup>nd out whether or not IT investments can lead an organization to generate greater value and achieve sustainable competitive advantage (Brynjolfsson, 1993, Melville et al., 2004, Kohli and Grover, 2008). Numerous IS researchers to date have reported a broad range of empirical evidence that IT investments are positively associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in terms of productivity, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, and market value (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996, Bharadwaj et al., 1999, Anderson et al., 2006).

However, the IS literature has not paid much attention to value creation by IT resources in the public-sector domain. As our literature review in the section ‘Public-sector IT-value research’ shows, only a minuscule number of studies on IT value in the government sector have been published in major academic journals in IS and public administration. We attribute this dearth of research to the absence of established theoretical bases. To the best of our knowledge, the theoretical models for existing IT-value research such as the production function model (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996, Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996, Dewan and Min, 1997) or the resourcebased view (RBV) (e.g., Sambamurthy et al., 2003, Wade and Hulland, 2004) do not take into consideration idiosyncratic characteristics and peculiarities of the public-sector organizations. Such characteristics that distinguish governments from business <sup>fi</sup>rms include the absence of pro<sup>fi</sup>t-seeking motive and competitive environments, plurality and trade-off in values (Molina and Spicer, 2004, Nabatchi, 2011, Cordella and Bonina, 2012), political or bureaucratic nature of public organizations (Cordella, 2007, Yildiz, 2007), and diversity in stakeholders who in<sup>fl</sup>uence public administration and demand accountability of public of<sup>fi</sup>cials (Newcomer and Caudle, 1991). Theoretical models and empirical studies in IS incorporate these factors. Bozeman and Bretschneider (1986) and Bretschneider (1990) point out that the IS literature in general does not fully account for these unique factors of public organizations such as political environments and bureaucratic nature in organizations. Therefore, the theoretical models developed for IT value in the private sector are not completely suitable for investigating IT value in the public sector.

A related question inspired by the IS literature is how investments in IT contribute to organizational performance. This question leads IS researchers to shift their attention from the direct impact of IT on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance to the mechanisms through which IT contribute to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Piccoli and Ives, 2005). In this research stream, a number of relatively recent studies take a process-view approach, in which IT resources, which consist of IT assets and capabilities (Wade and Hulland, 2004), do not lead to greater <sup>fi</sup>rm performance directly but do so through intermediate organizational capabilities such as those in manufacturing, marketing, customer service, or supply chain management (Ray et al., 2005, Banker et al., 2006, Rai et al., 2006). These studies assert that the effective use, management, and leverage of IT resources in fostering organizational capabilities can lead to value creation (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). The section ‘IT resources, organizational capabilities, and organizational performance’ reviews prior IS studies with this process view. On the basis of this process view, the present study aims to identify the processes through which IT resources contribute to value creation in the public-sector organizations. Taking an interdisciplinary approach that combines the IS and the public administration literature, we propose that the relationship between IT resources and organizational performance in the public sector is mediated by organizational capabilities (Figure 1).

In this study, drawing upon public-value management theory from the public administration literature (Moore, 1995, Alford and O’Flynn, 2009, Moore and Benington, 2011), we develop a theoretical model that explains the rela tionship between IT resources and value creation in the public-sector organizations. This theory asserts that public managers, on behalf of the public, should actively strive to generate greater public value rather than passively following the dictates of elected of<sup>fi</sup>cials, as managers in the private sector seek to achieve greater private value (Moore, 1995). Our review of public-value management literature suggests that the following <sup>fi</sup>ve key organizational capabilities are paramount to public-value creation – public service delivery capability, public engagement capability, co-production capability, resource acquisition capability, and public-sector innovation capability (Moore, 1995, Stoker, 2006, Alford and Hughes, 2008, Alford and O’Flynn, 2009). In addition, as for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms seek to maximize long-term shareholder values as an ultimate goal to achieve, we put forth that governments aim at advancing a public-value frontier by reducing con<sup>fl</sup>icts among competing values (Public-value frontier).

## Public-sector IT-value research

In order to examine the status quo of public-sector IT-value research, we conducted a comprehensive literature search for scholarly journal articles in a broad range of <sup>fi</sup>elds, spanning from IS to economics, public administration, and political sciences.

First, in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, we searched articles in MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Research, a journal published from January 1990 to December 2012. We searched titles, abstracts, and keywords that contain any of the following terms: government, public organization, public sector, and public employee. The initial search yielded only 13 articles. Thus, we expanded the scope of the literature search to the leading IS journals identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Lowry et al. (2004), Rainer and Miller (2005) and Association for Information Systems Senior Scho lars’ Basket of Journals.<sup>1</sup> Table A1 in Appendix presents the list of these journals. We excluded journals targeting practitioners, such as Harvard Business Review and Sloan Management Review. We also excluded commentary, editorial articles, and book reviews. This expanded search generated 139 articles.

We searched for research conducted in the public administration <sup>fi</sup>eld by choosing the top 10 journals in the public administration category in terms of the 5-year impact factor from Thomson-Reuters’ Journal Citation Report (JCR) 2011 Social Science Edition. Papers published from January 1999 to December 2012 were searched using the keywords computer, digital, online, Internet, technology, information system, electronic government, e-government, and open government. Again, we excluded commentary and editorial articles. We also referred to JCR 2009 to select top journals in political sciences and economics. For each discipline, we select the top 10 journals by the 5-year impact factors. In addition to these top 10 journals, we also included public economics and industrial organization journals within the top 100 economics journals. The same timeframe and the same set of keywords with public administration were employed for the literature search. In total, 68 articles were found in the non-IS journals.

Among these 207 studies, we <sup>fi</sup>rst selected IT-value studies based on the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of Kohli and Grover (2008), which states that an IT-value study have two components: (i) IT variable, IT management variable, or manifestation, and (ii) endogenous variable with IT economic impact. Applying these criteria, we discovered 12 articles that are quali<sup>fi</sup>ed for IT-value research in the public sector and have been published in mainstream research journals between 1990 and 2012, as shown in Table 1(A). Seven studies are published in IS journals, and the remaining <sup>fi</sup>ve are published in non-IS journals. In addition, we conducted a keyword search with the terms value, performance, efficiency, and effective in two journals focusing on government IT, Government Information Quarterly and Information Polity, but found no studies quali<sup>fi</sup>ed for IT-value research.

On the basis of the production-function framework, Lehr and Lichtenberg (1998) discover positive IT productivity in US federal agencies. Moreover, with the production–function framework, Lee and Perry (2002) <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive impact of IT assets in US state governments on state gross domestic product (GDP). Mukhopadhyay et al. (1997) discover an improvement in labor productivity in the toll collection process after the adoption of a new IT application in the Pennsylvania Turnpike. Unlike Lehr and Lichtenberg (1998) and Lee and Perry (2002), however, their study considers their setting (the Pennsylvania Turnpike) just as a context for <sup>fi</sup>nding the process-level effect in IT value and does not explicitly aim at examining IT business value in public organi zations. In addition, in nine papers in Table 1(A), IT value is not the main focus, but only part of their research scope. For example, in a case study of the Singapore Trade Development Board, Teo et al. (1997) report that the new electronic data interchange (EDI) system, which interconnects traders with trading and custom agencies, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved the effectiveness of trading declaration processes and the satisfaction of traders. While their study also covers many other aspects of the EDI systems, such as changes in organizational structures and processes, their main focus is not on performance improvement.

Given that the public-sector organizations pursue a more diverse set of values than for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms, we relaxed the second condition of Kohli and Grover (2008) and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed prior studies on the impact of IT on non-economic value such as trust in government, transparency, satisfaction, or con<sup>fi</sup>- dence in government. However, as listed in Table 1(B), even this broader search only generated eight studies, three of which are published in IS journals.

In sum, the literature search reveals that between 1990 and 2012, only 139 papers published in mainstream IS journals are more or less related to a topic of government IT, and only 11 papers explicitly investigate the impact of IT on economic or non-economic outcomes. In other words, the IS community has largely overlooked the public sector, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the economy (35 and 45% of the GDP in the United States and the European Union, respectively, in 2007) (Tsouhlou and Mylonakis 2011). Kraemer and Dedrick (1997) call for more research efforts in the public-sector IT, but it appears that their call has not been embraced enthusiastically by IS scholars. Moreover, we have discovered a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dearth of studies on IT value in the public sector, compared with a cornucopia of IT-value articles in the private-sector business setting. In their review article, Melville et al. (2004) review and synthesize 198 IT-value studies in business settings that are published in IS journals. On the other hand, our journal search demonstrates that not only IS, but also public administration researchers have paid scant attention to IT value in the public-sector organizations. In addition, as shown in Table 1 and pointed out by Danziger and Andersen (2002), there are few quantitative empirical studies on the topic of public-sector IT value.

Why is there a lack of research efforts that focus on understanding the contribution of IT to value creation in the public sector? As most IS researchers are af<sup>fi</sup>liated with business schools, they simply may not have enough background knowledge or strong interests in looking at government IT. However, we identify two more fundamental challenges that may have impeded the research. First, it is not as straightforward to de<sup>fi</sup>ne value or performance in governments as it is in commercial organizations. There are numerous ways to measure organizational performance in the business setting, such as sales revenues, productivity, market share, pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and customer satisfaction. Whereas the public administration literature offers some performance measures for individual government service areas such as education (standard test results, dropout rates) or public safety (crime occurrence and clearance rates), no consensus performance measure for governments has been established in prior literature. Therefore, it is still challenging to measure organization-wide performance in government because conventional market performance measures such as sales, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, and market value do not capture the governments’ primary objectives. Furthermore, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and measure other ‘non-market’ values, such as transparency, democracy, and rule of law.

## IT resources, organizational capabilities, and organizational performance

The literature on IT value and strategic IT management has three related, but distinct, views on the relationship between IT resources, organizational capabilities, and performance – the complementarity view, the process view, and the con<sup>fi</sup>guration view (Table 2). In the complementarity view, IT assets and capabilities contribute to organizational performance in conjunction with other organizational capabilities. This perspective maintains that IT resources are complement to other ordinary organizational processes such as human resource management and customer service. To put it differently, when accompanied by development in organizational capabilities, IT resources are associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm performance to a greater extent. For example, Bharadwaj et al. (2007) demonstrate that coordination capabilities in manufacturing, marketing, and supply chain moderate the impact of integrated IS capability (the degree to which IS provides integrated data and process integration) on manufacturing performance.

The second perspective – the process view – stresses that IT resources are associated with organizational performance indirectly via organizational capabilities. The major thrust of this view is that IT resources facilitate the development of superior organizational capabilities such as those in marketing, operation, or supply chain management. These capabilities in turn positively affect <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and become a source of sustained competitive advantages, as illustrated in Figure 1. For example, Rai et al. (2006) show that IT infrastructure integration with suppliers and customers (IT resources) drives supply chain integration (organizational capability), which subsequently contributes to greater <sup>fi</sup>rm performance with respect to operational excellence, customer relationships, and revenue growth. Banker et al. (2006) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the adoption of plant IS such as resource planning systems and EDI applications contribute to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance by improving manufacturing capabilities in terms of just-in-time manufacturing and customer/supplier participation programs. In a multi-business context, Tanriverdi (2005) <sup>fi</sup>nds that IT relatedness across business units, which refers to a shared, standardized IT infrastructure or IT management process, increases <sup>fi</sup>rm performance by improving cross-unit knowledge management capability.

The third, emerging perspective – the con<sup>fi</sup>guration view – asserts that the relationships among IT resources, organizational capabilities, surrounding environment, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance are not as simple as the complementarity and the process views would suggest. Rather, an organization’s competitive advantages and performance are rendered by a dynamic, complex mutual interdependence among IT resources, dynamic capabilities, and environmental turbulence (El Sawy et al., 2010). This perspective suggests that organizations strive to understand the holistic effect of a con<sup>fi</sup>guration of resources, capabilities, and environmental conditions rather than aiming at understanding the net effect of individual resources or capabilities in isolation.

In this research, we select the process view as the theoretical model for this study over both the complementarity view and the con<sup>fi</sup>guration view, since the objectives of this study go beyond the input–output relationships and intend to theorize how IT resources (input) affect public value (output) through certain organizational capabilities (process). We believe that the process view can provide implications for the public-sector managers as to how they can utilize IT resources for organizational capability development and in turn create value in their organizations. Andersen et al. (2010) also put forth that IT has a key impact on organizational capabilities in governmental organizations. Kraemer and King (2003) argue that IT by itself does not directly cause changes or improvement in public administration. Rather, as Fountain (2001) and Cordella and Iannacci (2010) posit, technologies are enablers or carriers of the public-sector reforms initiated by the public-sector managers. With the process view, we aim to theorize the organizational mechanisms by which IT assets and capabilities create public value.

## Public-value management theory and public organization capabilities

## What is public-value management?

In this study, we adopt the public-value management theory as a theoretical basis for our model. The basic tenet underlying this theory is that public of<sup>fi</sup>cials strive to maximize public value, just as business managers seek to maximize private business value (Moore, 1995). Public value broadly refers to the value created by government through services, laws, regulation, and other actions (Kelley et al., 2002). This theory emphasizes that rather than passively following the dictates of elected of<sup>fi</sup>cials and meeting the given responsibilities of public service provision, public managers need to use their ingenuity, creativity, and expertise (Williams and Shearer, 2011) and play an active role in exploring new opportunities in public-value creation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Moore (1995) suggests that public managers can create greater public value by proactively engaging in: (1) increasing the quantity or quality of public activities per resource expended; (2) reducing costs used to achieve current levels of production; (3) making public organizations better able to identify and respond to citizens aspirations; (4) enhancing the fairness with which publicsector organization operate; and (5) increasing their continuing capacity to respond. In other words, to generate greater public value, public managers need to become not a simple technician or a passive producer, but a strategist and an entrepreneur with a clear strategic mission and vision, as do the private sector managers (Moore, 1995, Alford and Hughes, 2008).

Interestingly, the public-value literature promotes entrepreneurship in the public-sector organizations, because like forpro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms, governments are subject to increasingly dynamic and uncertain environments (Moore and Khagram, 2004, Swilling, 2011). Social, political, and economic circumstances surrounding governments continue to evolve; technologies advance rapidly and sometimes disrupt business and society at large; and risks and threats to the public such as terrorism, outbreaks of pandemic diseases, or global warming continue to emerge. As a result, citizens’ needs, goals, and expectations for public organizations are by no means constant. The publicvalue literature emphasizes that public managers, who have extensive expertise and in-depth knowledge in public service delivery, need to play a more proactive role in sensing changing environments, responding to new needs for legitimate government intervention and public services, and innovating public management for greater public value.

It should be noted, however, that public-value management is not free from criticisms, as summarized and addressed by Alford and O’Flynn (2009). Major criticisms include vagueness in what public-value means, confusion as to whether the theory is empirically testable, and an inappropriate emphasis on political roles of public managers (Rhodes and Wanna 2007, 2009). As the theory is still emerging and developing, consensus on the conception of public value is yet to occur. Alford and O’Flynn (2009) clarify that public-value theory is both a descriptive theory and a normative prescription. It intends to explain what public managers actually do and to stipulate what they should do. Finally, according to Alford and O’Flynn (2009), the prescription that public of<sup>fi</sup>cials need to be active in the political arena bothers some scholars who believe that the clear distinction between politics and public administration is sacred. However, Moore (1995) and Alford and O’Flynn (2009) defend that politicians are the <sup>fi</sup>nal arbiters in leading policy development and formulating public service delivery, and the role of public managers should not be more than advisors or counselors to elected of<sup>fi</sup>cials with expertise in public management.

## Two research streams in public-value management research

According to Davis and West (2009), there are two major research streams in the public-value management literature. In the institutional perspective, researchers seek to <sup>fi</sup>nd out what public value is, how it is de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and what constitutes public value. In the generative perspective, scholars discuss and propose normative frameworks for appropriate behaviors of public managers who would like to secure greater public value.

The literature in the institutional perspective consistently highlights that public value is far more multi-faceted than private value, which in the business context usually refers to monetary pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Public value not only includes direct, tangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from public services such as education or public welfare that accrue to individual clients (Alford, 2002), but also comprises other broader, tangible values such as trust in governments, fairness, and national pride, which are valued collectively by the public as a whole (Moore, 1995, Alford and Hughes, 2008, Alford and O’Flynn, 2009). While for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms may also pursue other objectives such as sustainability, customer satisfaction, or safety, these objectives in most cases morph to the ultimate goal, which is to create sustaining economic value for shareholders. In contrast, Davis and West (2009) argue that ‘the public interest cannot be reduced to singular, economic measures. The values that inhere in the public realm in both government processes and outputs are irreducibly plural’ (604).

Thus, the institutional perspective primarily examines what constitutes public value. For example, Beck Jørgensen and Bozeman (2007) attempt to construct a constellation of public values that classi<sup>fi</sup>es 72 value concepts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by their review of public administration literature. This includes not only economic values such as productivity and effectiveness, but also non-economic values such as sustainability, responsiveness, or accountability. Kelley et al. (2002) argue that public values consist of three components – objective outcomes of public services, service qualities perceived by citizens, and trust in governments. Nabatchi (2011) proposes a comprehensive set of public-value universe that consists of four dimensions – political, legal, organizational, and market value (Table 3).

It should be noted, however, that there is no absolute, universally accepted conception of public value (Alford and Hughes, 2008, Alford and O’Flynn, 2009). What is publicly valued depends on the needs and desires of the public as well as on social and environmental circumstances with which the public and public managers deal. Stoker (2006) states that ‘public value is more than a summation of the individual preferences of the users or producers of public services. The judgment of public value is collectively built through deliberation involving elected and appointed government of<sup>fi</sup>cials and key stakeholders’ (42). This literature thus argues that democratic processes and active roles of public managers are essential for true public values to be uncovered, a point that leads to the generative perspective.

The generative perspective points out that in increasingly uncertain, unstable environments, public managers need to play a key role in discovering what truly amounts to public value. The public-value management stresses that government of<sup>fi</sup>cials need to consciously engage in political discussions and actively participate in discourses with politicians, citizens, not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, and businesses to discover how to advance public value (Hui and Hayllar, 2010). With this backdrop, the generative perspective attempts to provide normative prescriptions and guidelines for public managers. Smith (2004) states that ‘public of<sup>fi</sup>cials must engage political authority, collaborate with each other within and across institutional boundaries, manage ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively, and engage with communities and users of services, and re<sup>fl</sup>ectively develop their own sense of vocation and public duty’ (69–70). Stoker (2006) asserts that public of<sup>fi</sup>cials need to recognize and seek input of a wide range of stakeholders in an open-minded approach and to equip with an adaptable and learning-based approach to the challenge of public service delivery. Hence, what public-value management and RBV have in common is that both emphasize the importance of organizational capabilities in value creation.

## Public-sector organizational capabilities

On the basis of the generative perspective we explained above, we present <sup>fi</sup>ve key organizational capabilities that moderate the relationship between IT resources and public value. Here, we do not intend to offer a complete, exhaustive set of capabilities in public organizations. Rather, we propose that it is these <sup>fi</sup>ve capabilities for which IT resources can play an enabling and facilitating role in eventually advancing public value.

## Public service delivery capability

One of the primary ways to accomplish greater public value is to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of public service production. In other words, public value is enhanced when a government improves its public service delivery capability by improving the quality or increasing the quantity of public services, given the available resources. Moore (1995) states that ‘It is not enough to say that public managers create results that are valued; they must be able to show that the results are worth the cost of private consumption and unrestrained liberty forgone in producing the desirable results. Only then can we be sure that some public value has been created’ (57). Similarly, Alford and Hughes (2008) argue that ‘although the term public value draws our attention to results or outcomes, it does not ignore inputs and processes … creating public value will mean maximizing within a constraint, that is, seeking the greatest possible bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the public within the available monetary or legal resources’ (3). Hence, the public-value management demands that public organizations have an ability to deliver the maximum possible outcome of public services with as limited resources given by the public as possible.

## Public engagement capability

The public-value management argues that engagement of a large number of citizens and stakeholders in policy decision and service delivery is crucial. Stoker (2006) notes that ‘Politicians and of<sup>fi</sup>cials have a particular legitimacy given the government is elected, but there are other valid claims to legitimacy from among others, including business partners, neighborhood leaders, those with knowledge about services as professionals or users, and those in a position of oversight or regulators … The public value management paradigm relies on a stakeholder conception of legitimacy in its governance arrangements. The fundamental idea is that for a decision to be legitimate or for a judgment to be made, it is necessary to have all the stakeholders involved’ (Stoker, 2006: 47). As stated in the section ‘Two research streams in public-value management research,’ there is no de<sup>fi</sup>nite group of public values that are universally appreciated in every society, and whether a particular value is treasured or not depends upon the needs and preferences of the public. For governments to create public values that are truly endorsed by the public, ordinary citizens need to actively inform politicians and public mangers of their needs and demands for their governments. This is why the public value literature emphasizes that it is the citizens responsibility to actively engage in policymaking and public service delivery (Benington, 2011; Bogason and Musso, 2006).

The public management theory argues that because of the increasing size and complexity in public administration, decisions on policies and public services need to be made not by governments alone but via deliberation and negotiations among the governments, individual citizens, and other stakeholders that are potentially affected by the decisions. Simply put, without participation and consent of the citizen, whatever outcome public policies or services generate, the public would not value the outcomes, and public value would not be created. Therefore, the public-sector organizations require an ability to seek participation of a broad range of necessary stakeholders in every step of policy formulation and implementation.

## Co-production capability

The public-value literature suggests that in an increasingly dynamic environment, provision of public services by a single organization may not be able to keep up with changing needs and aspirations of the public. Broussine (2003) also states that ‘in order to solve complex problems, public leaders have to be able to initiate concerted action not only within their own organizations but among a set of stakeholders with different and competing interests’ (175). The literature argues that one single public organization alone does not possess all the necessary resources and capabilities to tackle challenging public issues. Co-production is not limited within the public sector; many public organizations collaborate with privatesector business organizations to deliver quality public services (Donahue and Zeckhauser, 2011). This is illustrated by recently emerging public management paradigms such as collaborative public management (Alford and Hughes, 2008), public–private partnership (Klijn and Teisman, 2003, Ruuska and Teigland, 2009), and hybrid governance, in which the lines between the public, the not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and the for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t sectors are being blurred (Klitgaard and Treverton, 2004). For collaborative public management to be successful, public organizations need to have an ability to marshal all the necessary resources from participating partners, to align their competing interests, and to coordinate their efforts and activities toward advancement in public value.

## Resource acquisition capability

As we discussed in the preceding section, the public-value management theory emphasizes an entrepreneurial capability of government managers and asserts that public managers play a key, if not leading, role in policy exploration and formulation. This is because public managers have more <sup>fi</sup>rsthand knowledge and deep ground-level experiences on public services and operations, putting them in a better position than politicians to assess and respond to changing public needs, surrounding circumstances, and emerging challenges. Simply put, they can act as entrepreneurs and strategists better than politicians can.

To effectively perform an entrepreneurial role in public-value creation, public-sector managers need a resource acquisition capability, which is the ability to garner resources necessary from resource providers for their initiatives. The resource providers consist of politicians, taxpayers, higher-level governments, media, interest groups, and non-governmental organizations (NGO). Just as business entrepreneurs have to raise capital and other resources from investors for their business endeavors, so do government of<sup>fi</sup>cials need to secure suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources from what Moore (1995) calls the authorizing environment.

It is the public manager’s job to proactively pursue and build adequate resources needed to initiate and sustain their public-value efforts by championing and demonstrating the causes to the authorizing environment (Moore, 1995, Smith, 2004, Stoker, 2006). Resources include not only tax revenues but also legal grounds, legitimacy, consent, and political support from the authorizing environment. Suf<sup>fi</sup>cient monetary resources alone do not guarantee successful value creation in the public sector. For example, without consensus and consent from citizens and political supports from legislatures, public of<sup>fi</sup>cials would not be able to ensure the ongoing, stable operation of public services for sustained public value. Therefore, to be truly entrepreneurial, the public-sector managers need to be politically savvy.

## Public-sector innovation capability

Innovation in the public sector could be perceived as an oxymoron (Borins, 2002). This perception originates from the absence of competitive pressures and pro<sup>fi</sup>t motives and the presence of bureaucratic public managers and outdated administrative structure. However, the public-value research recognizes the importance of innovation in public management and <sup>fi</sup>nds that public managers, who possess deep knowledge and expertise in administration, can play a pivotal role in initiating and leading innovation (Borins, 2002). Moore (1995) states that ‘It is not enough that managers simply maintain the continuity of their organizations, or even that the organizations become ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in current tasks. It is also important that the enterprise be adaptable to new purposes and that it be innovative and experimental’ (Moore, 1995: 55). Thus, the literature stresses that governments be vigilant, understand changing circumstances, and sense emerging needs and aspirations of various stakeholders. The governments need to become <sup>fl</sup>exible and agile and to cope with emerging challenges in an innovative way (Dunleavy et al., 2006).

## The theoretical model

In this section, we explicate our theoretical model for publicsector IT value, which is built upon the process view that the relationship between IT resources and performance is mediated by organizational capabilities (IT resources, organizational capabilities, and organizational performance). We begin this section by discussing key IT resources in public organizations. The section ‘Public-value frontier’ de<sup>fi</sup>nes a public-value frontier, which is the focal performance outcome of our model. The section ‘Paths from IT resources, to organizational capabilities, to public-value frontier’ lays out the paths between IT resources, organizational capabilities (Public-sector organizational capabilities), and public-value frontiers.

## Key IT resources in the public-sector organizations

Following Wade and Hulland (2004), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT resources as both IT assets and IT capabilities. IT assets refer to anything tangible or intangible related to IT that can be used in organizational processes for creating, producing, and offering products and services. IT assets may include hardware, software, network infrastructure, information assets, or human resources in IT functions. IT capabilities refer to repeatable patterns of actions in the use of IT assets. Bharadwaj (2000) de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT capability as a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ‘ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources in combination or co-present with other resources and capabilities’ (171). Pavlou and El Sawy (2006) explain that IT capabilities have three key dimensions – the acquisition of IT assets, deployment of IT assets through tight IT–business relationships, and leveraging of IT assets in formulating business strategies.

Among many IT assets and capabilities, we focus on the <sup>fi</sup>ve key IT resources (Figure 2) that we believe play a crucial role in shaping organizational capabilities in government agencies.

We selected these IT resource constructs from our review of IT-value and capability literature and public-value theory literature. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that one IT resource – public information dissemination – is unique to the public-sector domain and thus has not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the prior IT research. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of each IT resource is presented in Table 4. In the subsections to follow, we explicate how these public-sector IT resources cultivate organizational capabilities for public value.

## Digitized administrative process

Digitized administrative processes refer to a set of IS and infrastructures that digitize and automate administrative tasks and transactional processing (assets) and the ability to develop and implement such systems (capabilities). The public-sector organizations heavily rely on integrated enterprise systems and databases to automate a range of business processes that used to be handled by paperwork-based manual processes.

The digitization in administrative processes can take place in two areas – internal business processes and external service delivery to citizens (Moon, 2002, Henriksen and Damsgaard, 2007, Lim and Tang, 2008). As an example of digitization in internal processes, North Carolina Department of Transportation adopted a tablet-PC-based mobile inspection system for state transportation infrastructures including highways and local roads (NASCIO, 2009). The system automates outdated manual inspection processes for more than 21,000 bridges, culverts, and signs and enables an inspector to transmit assessment information immediately from a remote inspection site to the central database, a task for which it would have taken several days to complete in the old process. As an example of external service delivery, automation in citizen-facing processes has enabled citizens to conduct a range of activities via the Internet such as tax <sup>fi</sup>lings, license application and renewals, or voter registration (Beynon-Davies and Williams, 2003, Norris and Moon, 2005; Thomas and Strieb, 2005). Instead of physically visiting an agency of<sup>fi</sup>ce in-person, the citizens can now use government services anytime, anywhere, saving both the service users’ time and efforts and the agency’s resources that would otherwise have been incurred at the interpersonal service channel.

## Public intelligence analytics

Public intelligence analytics refer to both technological tools (assets) to gather information from the public and the environment and to the ability to analyze and derive knowledge and insights from the information (capabilities). Intelligence includes information on the public’s needs for government services and the demographic, social, and economic environment that surrounds the public. Governments generate, collect, and possess a large amount of information regarding policymaking, public service delivery, and various aspects of the public. Public intelligence analytics offer governments a means to uncover valuable knowledge and insights from the sheer amount of information resources in the public sector. Such intelligence generated by information analytics can be used strategically in policy for mulation and implementation and deployment of other publicsector resources.

For example, a number of law enforcement agencies adopt business intelligence systems along with data warehouses and data mining tools. Digitized crime data and state-of-theart analytic tools bring new intelligence to law enforcement agents, who used to rely mostly on their own knowledge and experience in targeting offenders and solving crimes. By analyzing and evaluating incident patterns and histories, the agencies are now able to conduct more proactive, predictive patrols, and focused deployment of law enforcement personnel, targeting speci<sup>fi</sup>c criminal groups or neighborhoods. Predictive policing, facilitated by the public intelligence analytics, enables law enforcement to identify what are known as ‘hotspots,’ geographic areas where crime is most predicted and also prevalent (Government Technology, 2013). Governments are using similar technologies in public welfare programs and tax collection to detect fraudulent or illegitimate bene<sup>fi</sup>t claims and to identify unpaid tax revenues, respectively (NASCIO, 2006; Digital Communities, 2010a).

## Inter-organizational system integration

Inter-organizational system integration includes integrated systems and communication technologies (assets) that enable to exchange information and intelligence with external organi zations and the ability to implement and integrate the systems with the external organizations (capabilities). External organizations include not only peer government agencies, but also for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms and not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations. Such interorganizational system integration is warranted because in contemporary public administration that is faced with evolving, complex public needs, a single government agency cannot effectively manage public services by itself. Instead, the government needs to collaborate closely with external organizations, a process that demands seamless information exchange and sharing.

For example, in response to the residents’ demand for effective emergency response services that a small-scale municipality is struggling to meet by itself, Waukesha County in Wisconsin established the Waukesha County Communication Center (WCC), a county-wide emergency call and dispatch headquarters (Schultz and Tuma, 2007). This project was driven by the collaboration of the local municipal agencies, including police and <sup>fi</sup>re departments within Waukesha County. Among the objectives was to improve the quality of public safety services throughout the county, which had been fragmented by municipal boundaries. By consolidating the call center systems of small local governments into one entity and integrating the county-wide dispatch functions that used to be operated separately by individual municipalities, WCC could achieve economies of scale in operation and staf<sup>fi</sup>ng and proactively adopt advanced technologies such as wireless 911 systems or computer-aided dispatch tools.

## Online public interactive interfaces

Online public interactive interfaces refer to online interactive venues such as Websites, social media, and mobile channels (assets) and the ability to use these channels to interact with citizens and other stakeholders in a bilateral manner and on an ongoing basis (capabilities). Interactive implies two-way communication between governments and citizens instead of unilateral information transfer from the former to the latter. Through online public interactive interfaces, a public organization is able to not only deliver its messages to the public but also to listen to the voices and concerns of as many citizens as possible on a real-time basis and utilize them in policymaking and implementation.

Recently, several governments are using Web 2.0 technologies and social media tools to solicit citizens’ ideas and input for government administration (Lee and Kwak, 2012). For example, in 2008, the Of<sup>fi</sup>ce of the President-Elect Obama set up Citizen’s Brie<sup>fi</sup>ng Book site, collecting policy recommendations from ordinary citizens for the new administration. State and local governments such as Chicago and Manor, Texas (Digital Communities, 2010b, Government Technology, 2012a), are also operating similar crowd-sourcing platforms where residents can submit suggestions and evaluate ideas submitted by other citizens. Furthermore, social media tools enabled governments such as New York City and Chicago to launch participatory budgeting initiatives, in which citizens are invited to participate and vote for how and where tax revenues are used (Yes Magazine, 2010; The New York Times, 2012). Thus, these online public interactive interfaces provide the governments with the ability to assess what the public wants and to engage in deliberation with citizens (Hui and Hayllar, 2010).

## Public information dissemination

Public information dissemination refers to infrastructures and channels through which the public can access government information repositories (assets) and the ability to publish government information assets in a manner that is easily accessible and comprehensible to the public (capabilities). Similar to the for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms, the public-sector organizations own a large swath of information that is either internally generated or gathered from the public. But in contrast to the private sector, the information resources in the public sector belong to the public, and public organizations are increasingly required by the public’s demands, executive mandates, or various laws to make the growing amount of information assets available to the public.

In order to meet such demands, many governments recently began to spearhead open-data initiatives by publish ing a large set of government information to the public via online channels (Rodríguez Bolívar et al., 2007, Lee and Kwak, 2012). These practices allow ordinary citizens, especially those without expert knowledge on administration, to evaluate how public managers perform in delivering public services, enriching openness and accessibility to the administration. Information resources such as performance records that have been accumulated in the enterprise systems can be an effective tool in such an activity. For instance, US states such as Oregon and Virginia distribute a wide range of performance information on their executive agencies. Virginia operates Virginia Per forms (http://vaperforms.virginia.gov/), in which they regu larly distribute several hundreds of performance indicators from all state agencies, such as the number of individuals served by state health-care agencies, the amount of time to process applications or permits, or satisfaction scores of the state functions.

## Public-value frontier

As we discussed in the section ‘Public-sector IT-value research,’ an important challenge for IS researchers investigating public value of IT has been that governments aim at ful<sup>fi</sup>lling plural, competing values concurrently, such as economic growth, social welfare, and rule of law, which do not combine into one single value such as sales or pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Public organizations have to deal with a more diverse group of stakeholders – citizens, taxpayers, local businesses, politicians, media, and so forth – than do forpro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms, and each group has diverse interests and demands. This poses another important challenge – the values governments pursue con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one another, an effect that the public-value researchers call value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts (Molina and Spicer, 2004, Nabatchi, 2011).

In many government activities, one value is achieved at the expense of another. There are two ways in which such a valuebased con<sup>fl</sup>ict occurs. First, one type of value directly con<sup>fl</sup>icts with another. For example, ensuring public safety or national security may require sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce to some extent in individual liberty and privacy. Income redistribution for equity via progressive tax rates or public welfare programs may not be on par with fairness, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, or economic development. Democratic process or rule of law, which is sacred in developed countries, is inherently and intentionally slow and expensive to maintain. Second, one kind of value negatively affects another indirectly by limiting resources available to the latter. As the recent <sup>fi</sup>scal crises in the public sector vividly illustrate, governments do not have an unlimited amount of resources; in fact, government budgets have become tighter each passing year. Thus, investing in one government service area comes at the expense of others. If a government is to improve the quality of education system, for instance, it has to reduce spending on other services such as public welfare or transportation.

Value-based con<sup>fl</sup>ict highlights one of the key differences between business – and public-sector organizations. Caudle (1991) note that ‘Markets signals and pro<sup>fi</sup>t guide the private sector. In contrast, public-sector organizations face multiple goals, many of which are intangible or in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one another’ (172). For instance, an airline company may strike a balance between passenger safety and short-term pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Ultimately, however, both of them converge to long-term shareholder value and sustainable competitive advantages, since overlooking the safety will jeopardize the existence of the company. On the other hand, as a similar example, a public transit agency (such as subway or bus) may continue to exist and operate even with serious passenger safety issues, since many of the customers would have no alternative but to use the service of the public agency and the existence of the agency is chartered by laws and statutes that are hard to be amended.

Recently, management scholars have proposed that even commercial organizations should embrace multiple goals that are above and beyond market-oriented goals. Porter and Kramer (2011) urge that for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms pursue ‘shared value,’ which accounts for both private business pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and societal values for surrounding communities and society at large. Shared value is not at odds with business value in their concept. They state that ‘social harms or weaknesses frequently create internal costs for <sup>fi</sup>rms – such as wasted energy or raw materials, costly accidents ... And addressing societal harms and constraints does not necessarily raise costs for <sup>fi</sup>rms, because they can innovate through using new technologies, operating methods, and management approaches’ (65). In other words, from their perspective, pursuit of shared value aims at ensuring continued, long-term value creation for shareholders, an objective that is not of interest in the public sector.

Given limited resources available to the public-sector organizations, it is a constant struggle for them to balance competing interests and achieve a trade-off among con<sup>fl</sup>icting values. Therefore, we propose that just as it is the ultimate goal for business <sup>fi</sup>rms to generate maximum, sustained shareholder value, the paramount objective of the publicsector organizations is to advance its public-value frontier (Figure 3), which we de<sup>fi</sup>ne as the set of maximum multiple public values that is achievable given available resources and the constraints among certain values. In our conceptual model, we propose that IT-enabled organizational capabilities can relax and overcome value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts in the public sector and thus move upward the public-value frontier to further northeast in Figure 3.

## IT resources and public service delivery capability

IT resources – in particular, digitized administrative processes and public intelligence analytics– facilitate the public service delivery capability by either reducing the amount of inputs or costs that are necessary to produce outputs, increasing the quantity of outputs or the quality of outcomes, or both. This effect in turn alleviates a value-based con<sup>fl</sup>ict between outputs and inputs. An improvement in the delivery capability lessens both types of con<sup>fl</sup>icts we have mentioned above – a con<sup>fl</sup>ict within one government service and ones in which two or more policy areas compete for limited resources. Alleviation of the latter is possible by saving the investment of resources in one government function, which can be used for other competing functions.

With respect to the <sup>fi</sup>rst type of value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts, in the case of North Carolina’s infrastructure inspection system introduced above (Digitized administrative process), there is a value-based con<sup>fl</sup>ict between transportation safety (outcome) and expenditures in infrastructure construction and maintenance (input). Given the limited amount of input resources that are available to the agency, it had to make a trade-off between how safe the infrastructure should be and how much to invest in it. The digitized administrative processes that automate and streamline the inspection process relax the need for trade-off by both enhancing the safety and reducing the use of tax revenues. With public intelligence analytics, law enforcement agencies can relax a tension among public safety, cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and individual liberty. Improving public safety (outcome) requires not only substantial investments in manpower and equipment (inputs) but sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in liberty of individual citizens (costs) that may result from strict enforcement. Predictive and targeted policing enabled by the public intelligence analytics improves the delivery capability of law enforcement by both saving necessary resources used in law enforcement and reducing the possibility of encroaching on the liberty and rights of innocent citizens, leading to an expansion of the public-value frontier along public safety, costs, and liberty.

Regarding the second type of con<sup>fl</sup>icts, resources saved by digitized administrative processes and public intelligence analytics can be used in other public services, investments that would otherwise be forgone. For instance, cost savings in the infrastructure maintenance made possible by the digitized inspection process can be directed toward new investments in transportation infrastructure or other public service areas such as education or public welfare. Savings in manpower and expenditures in public safety from deployment of public intelligence analytics can be used in other valuable public safety efforts such as improvement in community outreach programs and effective rehabilitation of criminal offenders.

There are many other ways that the public service delivery capabilities fueled by IT resources mitigate value-based con <sup>fl</sup>icts. Democracy (outcome) is one of the treasured values in modern developed nations, but maintaining democratic processes such as election, voter registration, and <sup>fi</sup>nancing to political campaigns requires a substantial use of tax revenues and human resources (inputs). Digitized administrative processes in voter registration and election administration can improve the delivery capability by reducing the required amount of resources in maintaining such processes and thereby expanding the public-value frontier along ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and democratic value dimensions (Government Technology, 2012b). Public welfare by income redistribution is one of the areas in which value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts are severe and politically charged. Ensuring equity and safety nets for the underprivileged not only is costly for governments, but also competes with interests of overall economic development and growth, posing opportunity costs. Again, public intelligence gathering and analytic capabilities in public welfare provision and fraud detection (NASCIO, 2007) can curb both expenses and wasteful spending in public welfare programs, leading to an expansion in the public-value frontier. On the basis of the arguments above, we propose the following proposition.

Proposition 1: Digitized administrative processes and public intelligence analytics increase the public service delivery capability of public-sector organizations, which in turn advances public-value frontiers.

## IT resources and public engagement capability

The preceding section explains that participation of the public in policymaking and implementation is paramount to publicvalue creation. In many cases, however, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to lure various stakeholders to engage in decision making. Ordinary citizens tend to be busy and preoccupied with their everyday personal lives. Getting involved in governmental decision making in such ways as attending meetings, writing formal feedback, and responding to surveys may require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time commitment (Ho, 2002). In this respect, online public interactive interfaces provide an alternative venue for participation from and dialog with as many relevant stakeholders as possible. Stoker (2006) argues that ‘new information and communication technologies offer a range of further opportunities to get people’s participation in ways that are <sup>fl</sup>exible, attractive to them, and not too time-consuming’ (48).

A challenge to public managers is that broad, sustained engagement of relevant stakeholders is a slow and complex process for both the governments and the participants. On one hand, as discussed above, having citizens participate in decision making, deliberation, and negotiation promotes transparency and democratic values. On the other hand, this participatory process, which involves a variety of participants with varying interests and points of view, could be time consuming and slow. For example, a budget appropriation process would be much faster if budget decisions are made by only a small number of elected representatives and public of<sup>fi</sup>cials rather than by a large number of citizens, interest groups, and other relevant stakeholders. In the course of slowmoving processes, governments would lose responsiveness, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, the public engagement capability nurtured by online public interactive interfaces such as online forums, social media, and other tools can both mobilize a wide a range of engagement and simplify the participation processes simultaneously, thus advancing the public-value frontier along several competing values such as participation, responsiveness, and trust. This point leads to the following proposition.

Proposition 2: Online public interactive interfaces increase the public engagement capability of public-sector organizations, which in turn advances public-value frontiers.

## IT resources and co-production capability

As we have stated earlier, the environmental landscape and the public needs that the public-sector faces are profoundly dynamic and continuously evolving, creating mounting challenges for a single government agency to cope with alone. This requires governments to collaborate and co-produce public services with other public organizations and/or NGO (such as for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms or voluntary organizations). Such cross-organizational collaboration aims to pool and leverage resources, expertise, or capabilities that each co-production participant has (Donahue and Zeckhauser, 2011). For instance, Luna-Reyes et al. (2007) chronicle a case of New York State Bureau of Housing Services (BHS), which provides homeless support services in a partnership with local governments and not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations. These local organizations manage shelters and assistance programs and directly interact with the homeless, while the BHS provides funding to the local organizations and oversees their programs and facilities. This partnership emerges because the local organizations lack <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources and need assistance from the state and local governments, while the state government does not have direct, hands-on knowledge of local needs in the community (Agranoff, 2004).

As inter-organizational system integration is a key ingredi ent in integration and cooperation between suppliers, producers, and customers in the private sector (Barua et al., 2004), public organizations can utilize integration to cooperate with peer agencies or private-sector organizations. In the BHS case above, the State of New York built the Homeless Information Management System to integrate and share information resources with partnering agencies and organizations. It includes information on the homeless, their medical or criminal histories, the facilities, and the programs. In the past, this information had been fragmented throughout the various state agencies and the local organizations. In this initiative, the sharing of knowledge and technical expertise among the participating organizations is pivotal, and inter-organizational knowledge exchange and collaborative tools facilitate a broad range of knowledge sharing (Majchrzak et al., 2000).

This co-production capability enabled by inter-organizational system integration is instrumental in advancing public-value frontiers. For example, emergency response operations, which aim to ensure public safety and individual well-being, are costly for governments to offer. It is particularly the case for small municipalities, since the emergency operations require a large amount of <sup>fi</sup>xed-cost investments in infrastructure, equipment, and personnel. Hence, it entails a trade-off between cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and public safety. However, as illustrated by the WCC case (Inter-organizational system integration), pooling the emergency operation infrastructures and co-producing the 911 services across neighboring municipalities improve cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the emergency operations and improved citizens’ well-being at the same time. The New York BHS case also demonstrates how IT-driven knowledge exchange and collaboration across the organizational boundaries enable the

State of New York to advance a frontier of competing public values, including responsiveness, public health, and cost-saving dimensions.

In summary, we propose the following proposition for a link from inter-organizational system integration to publicvalue frontiers mediated by the co-production capability.

Proposition 3: Inter-organizational system integration increases the co-production capability of public-sector organizations, which in turn advances public-value frontiers.

## IT resources and resource acquisition capability

IT resources enable the resource acquisition capability in two ways. First, public intelligence analytics help governments build awareness of social and economic environments and make sense of the citizens’ desires, aspirations, and expectations of the governments. Public organizations can utilize IT resources in gathering intelligence from the public and the environment, and subsequently, they use the intelligence to make the case for necessary government actions to resource providers (the authorizing environment, Moore, 1995), such as politicians, taxpayers, higher-level governments, and interest groups. For example, Baltimore, Maryland, periodically collects a variety of citywide data such as crime statistics, vacant buildings, and homeless residents and publishes that data on its Open Baltimore site (http://data.baltimorecity .gov/). By collecting and making this data available to the public, the city government aims to build awareness and consensus among the citizens and galvanize monetary and political resources from the elected representatives that are needed for government activities in public safety, housing development, and human services.

Second, the public-sector organizations can utilize public information dissemination to demonstrate that they are capable producers of public services. There may be a widespread perception around the authorizing environment that public agencies are inef<sup>fi</sup>cient, bureaucratic, and unresponsive organizations. Leveraging their performance information, governments can reverse such a perception by conducting public information campaigns (Weiss and Tschirhart, 1994) or what Moore (1995) calls ‘public sector marketing’ or ‘strategic communication’ (185). These aim at convincing the authorizing environment of importance, legitimacy, and value of public services and policy decisions. As in Virginia and Oregon (Public information dissemination), by publicizing information resources on performance and effectiveness in administration and delivery of public services, the publicsector organizations help taxpayers and elected politicians understand how well they perform and why the authorizing environment needs to support and provide resources and legitimacy for continued, effective operations.

The resource acquisition capability fostered by public intelligence analytics and information dissemination enables public organizations to ease trade-offs between competing values in several ways. This capability helps relax a con<sup>fl</sup>ict between responsiveness and <sup>fl</sup>exibility on one hand and transparency and accountability on the other (Beck Jørgensen and Bozeman, 2007). It is a daunting task for governments to <sup>fi</sup>gure out dynamic interests and desires of many different citizens and stakeholders and to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll these diverse aspirations in a speedy, responsive manner. However, it is even more challenging to devise and deliver public services that serve various public needs in a transparent, democratic manner. To do so, public managers need to interact with a variety of citizens, interest groups, and elected representatives and to guide deliberation, negotiation, and compromise, a process that is expensive and time consuming and undermines <sup>fl</sup>exibility and responsiveness. Clear, easy-to-understand information on what a government can do, has done, and should do can help streamline this process. Therefore, the resource acquisition capability facilitated by public intelligence ana lytics and information dissemination enables public organizations to quickly marshal necessary monetary, legal, and political resources from resource holders, without losing <sup>fl</sup>exibility and transparency. Thus, we present the following proposition.

Proposition 4: Public intelligence analytics and information dissemination increase the resource acquisition capability of public-sector organizations, which in turn advances public-value frontiers.

## IT resources and public-sector innovation capability

IT resources can cultivate the public-sector innovation capability by enabling the public-sector organizations to create a whole new sort of public services (Hartley, 2011) that they would not have been able to offer without IT. Governments can utilize public intelligence analytics to discover needs and opportunities for new innovative public services. For example, the public intelligence analytics are indispensable components in the Illinois National Electronic Disease Surveillance System (I-NEDSS) (NASCIO, 2005b). This system aims at identifying and tracking outbreaks of infectious diseases that might escalate to a statewide emergency. It connects local health-care providers and state and federal agencies such as Center for Disease Control and Prevention. This initiative is a response to the continuous emergence of new types of pandemic diseases such as H1N1 and the increasing threat of biological terroristic attacks. This innovative public service (the real-time detection and response system for outbreaks) could not be operated without intelligence gathering and analytic systems.

In addition to innovation within the public sector, governments can spark innovation in the private-sector organizations with public information dissemination. As part of the open-government project, several governments make available to the public a variety of information on administration and local environment that they have accumulated. Citizens or private-sector organizations can utilize the government data to develop innovative services or products that the governments have not been able to create (NextGov, 2013). For example, US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) has led the Health Data Consortium (http://www .healthdataconsortium.org). Here, the HHS collects and publishes a variety of healthcare-related data from the federal government and the health-care sectors. The health-care data disseminated by the HHS enables private-sector entrepreneurs to develop innovative health-care products and services. The city of San Francisco also publishes the data of its transit systems and restaurant health inspections over its Open Data Website (https://data.sfgov.org/), with which individual IT professionals develop various mobile applications for transit users and tourists. Washington DC has inspired similar efforts in making its parking and transit information public (Government Technology, 2011). Thus, private-sector innovation has been made possible by both the open government data and the public’s creativity and market intelligence that government of<sup>fi</sup>cials may lack. In short, by opening up the data, the public-sector organizations can tap into the ‘wisdom of crowds’ to bolster innovation.

The literature on public-value management asserts that innovation should not be a term that only appears in the private sector. Public organizations also need to continuously innovate themselves in order to navigate a turbulent environment and to keep delivering values to the public. The innovation capability of public organizations supported by public intelligence analytics and information dissemination advances public-value frontiers in a number of ways. Without innovating itself, the public-sector organization could not become responsive and <sup>fl</sup>exible in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the dynamic demands of the public and dealing with social and economic environments that are increasingly more uncertain and dynamic. At the same time, by utilizing IT resources, the publicsector organizations can overcome its limitations in terms of necessary knowledge, monetary resources, and organizational agility, an effort that would otherwise be expensive and time consuming to achieve without IT. This leads us to the following proposition.

Proposition 5: Public intelligence analytics and information dissemination increase the public-sector innovation capability of public-sector organizations, which in turn advances public-value frontiers.

## Conclusion

In this study, we develop a theoretical model for creating public value through IT by blending two distant research streams – strategic IT management from the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and public-value theory from the <sup>fi</sup>eld of public administration. We adopt the IT resources, internal organizational capabilities, and organizational performance process framework (Figure 1), and from the public-value management literature we identify the key government organizational capabilities that drive public-value creation (Figure 2). The proposed theoretical model suggests that IT resources enable public organizations (i) to deliver public services with limited resources; (ii) to draw engagement from a broad range of stakeholders; (iii) to initiate and lead coordination and co-production across organizational boundaries; (iv) to galvanize the necessary resources for their strategic initiatives from resource holders; and (v) to bolster innovation within or outside of the publicsector organizations. We further propose that these digitized organizational capabilities advance public-value frontiers so that the governments can increase public values by overcoming value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

This research offers IT-value researchers with ample guidance and directions for future research in government IT value. First, researchers can resort to our conceptual model with the public-value management in developing their theoretical discussions and hypotheses. As we stated above, the current theoretical basis for IT business value, such as RBV or dynamic capability, does not explicitly consider several unique factors in the public-sector organizations. For example, the extant literature is inadequate to explain how IT assets and capabilities contribute to performance improvement and value creation in governments, which are not subject of competitive forces and rent-seeking shareholders. Our theoretical framework, based on the public-value theory, helps researchers to understand that IT can be an enabler for public managers who are motivated to move a public-value frontier forward. Furthermore, the existing IS literature has not paid much attention to the political nature of public organizations, which are required to ful<sup>fi</sup>l multiple objectives that are often con-<sup>fl</sup>icting with each other and meet diverse needs of various stakeholders. Our conceptualization of organizational capabilities and public-value frontiers will guide future researchers in exploring more dynamic and nuanced aspects of public-sector IT value.

Second, IS researchers can develop new measures that operationalize our constructs of public-sector IT resources, organizational capability and public values. By doing so, IS researchers will be able to contribute not only to the IS literature but also to the public management discipline, in which the public-value theory is still an emerging and nascent paradigm. Referring to the public-value literature, particularly the institutional stream of public-value studies (Alford and Hughes, 2008, Alford and O’Flynn, 2009, Davis and West, 2009, Nabatch, 2011), future studies can measure a broader range of government performance than that of business organizations and investigate the impact of IT resources on such performance indicators. In the government setting, researchers may go beyond conventional economic performance measures that the literature has so far examined for IT value such as cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or productivity and explore a more variety of non-economic, societal values such as transparency, equality, and trust in government (Table 3).

Further, it would be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to the IS literature if one can demonstrate that IT can relax a trade-off between two or more competing value measures and thereby push the public-value frontier forward, an approach that to the best of our knowledge, few IS researchers have adopted (Figure 3). IS scholars can also expand the horizon of IS research by further studying and developing measures for public-sector organizational capabilities that previous IS literature has paid scant attention to. For example, the resource acquisition capability can be measured by how widely and frequently public of<sup>fi</sup>cials interact with the authorizing environment to make their case for their strategic initiatives. The co-production capability can be operalizationalized by the variety and richness of collaboration and cooperation with peer government agencies, not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, or business <sup>fi</sup>rms.

This study also provides public-sector IT managers with meaningful implications. Public-sector IT organizations need to develop and deploy IT assets and capabilities in a way to cultivate the <sup>fi</sup>ve organizational capabilities in Figure 2. It is the job of IT managers in public organizations to make sure that investments and utilization of public-sector IT resources and management of IT organization are fully aligned with organizational-wide strategic goals and efforts toward public value. As we emphasize throughout the paper, to create greater public value, public-sector managers, including IT managers and professionals in governments, need to have strategic perspectives, entrepreneurial insights, and agility and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, all of which are necessary to effectively deal with mounting challenges in the public sector. Without such abilities, bureau cratic, unresponsive IT organizations could play a hampering, not enabling, role in the efforts of other public managers to advance a public-value frontier.

This research is not free from limitation. While we have made conscious efforts to present as comprehensive a set of IT resources and organizational capabilities in public organizations as possible, there might be other key constructs of organizational capabilities or IT resources for public-value creation that we missed. In other words, our list of IT resources and organizational capabilities in Figure 2 is by no means exhaustive. Building upon our framework, future research will offer a more complete nomological network in IT and public value and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the relationship between IT resources, capabilities, and public value with empirical evidence.

The size of the public-sector organizations has been growing signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly for the last several decades, and so has their investment in IT. Nonetheless, it seems that the IT-value research has ignored this important segment of the economy. We maintain that without a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient body of knowledge accumulated in the IS literature on the performance implications of government IT, the IS community would not be able to effectively inform and interact with the public-sector managers as our audience. Recognizing the need for a new theoretical underpinning for IT-value research in the government sector, our research aims to offer theoretical framework that can bolster and provide guidance for future government IT-value research. We hope that more research efforts on IT and public value underpinned by our theoretical foundation will have a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on other disciplines including public policy and administration or political science, a goal that Straub (2010) calls ‘Himalaya #3’ in his MISQ editorial comment.

## About the Authors

Min-Seok Pang has a Ph.D. in business administration from the Stephen M. Ross School of Business at University of Michigan as well as an M.S. in management and a B.S. in industrial engineering from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology. He is currently an Assistant

Professor of Management Information Systems at Fox School of Business, Temple University. His research has been published in journals such as Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Decision Support Systems, and International Journal of Electronic Commerce. His research interests include IT business value, IT governance, Electronic Governments, and Online and Network Services.

Gwanhoo Lee received the B.S. and M.S. degrees in Industrial Engineering from Seoul National University and the Ph.D. degree in information systems from the University of Minnesota. He is currently an Associate Professor of Information Technology and the Director of Center for IT and the Global Economy in the Kogod School of Business at the American University, Washington DC. His current research interests include complexity and agility issues in information systems development, global teams, complex adaptive systems, social media, open innovation, digital government, and information privacy. He has published his research in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, Communications of the ACM, European Journal of Information Systems, Information & Management, and Government Information Quarterly. He has received grants from IBM Center for the Business of Government. He is an advisory fellow to Samsung Economic Research Institute.

William H DeLone received the B.S. degree in mathematics from Villanova University, the M.S. degree in industrial administration from Carnegie-Mellon University, and the Ph.D. degree in computer information systems from the University of California Los Angeles. He is currently a Professor of Information Technology and Executive Director of the Center for IT and the Global Economy in the Kogod School of Business at the American University, Washington DC. His current research interests include the assessment of information systems’ effectiveness and value, e-government, and public value and the management of global software development. His research has been published in Information Systems Research, Management Information Systems Quarterly, The Journal of Management Information Systems, The Journal of the Association of Information Systems, The European Journal of Information Systems, and IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering.
