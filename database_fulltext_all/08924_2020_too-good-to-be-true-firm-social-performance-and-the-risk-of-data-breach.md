# Too Good to Be True: Firm Social Performance and the Risk of Data Breach

John D’Arcy,<sup>a</sup> Idris Adjerid,<sup>b</sup> Corey M. Angst,<sup>c</sup> Ante Glavas

Abstract. In this paper, we draw from research in the information systems security and management fields to theorize that a firm’s social performance, as measured by its engagement in socially responsible (or irresponsible) activities (i.e., corporate social performance (CSP)), affects its likelihood of being subject to computer attacks that result in data breaches. Drawing from stakeholder theory and positioning employees and external hackers as key stakeholders of the firm with respect to information security, we propose a set of hypotheses that elaborate relationships between aspects of a firm’s CSP and the likelihood of experiencing a data breach. To test our hypotheses, we compiled a unique data set that consists of publicly available data on firms’ data breach incidents, external assessments of their CSP, and other firm-specific factors. Our contribution is an intriguing and previously unknown account of CSP as it relates to information security. Paradoxically, our results suggest that firms that are noted to have poor CSP records (i.e., CSP concerns) are no more likely to experience a data breach, although a positive CSP record (i.e., CSP strengths) in areas that are peripheral to core firm activities (e.g., philanthropy, recycling programs) results in an elevated likelihood of breach. Delving into this latter finding, our results suggest that firms that simultaneously have peripheral CSP strengths along with high CSP concerns in other areas are at increased risk of breach. The increased likelihood of breach for firms with seemingly disingenuous CSP records suggests that per ceived “greenwashing” efforts that attempt to mask poor social performance make firms attractive targets for security exploitation.

## Introduction

Nearly 5,000 data records are either lost, stolen, or left exposed online somewhere around the globe every minute—that is, roughly 7.2 million records per day (Gemalto 2018). Indeed, data breaches<sup>1</sup> are a serious concern for organizations of all sizes and across different industries with associated costs that include technical repair, determining how the breach occurred, offering free credit services to victims, litigation expenses, and various regulatory fees (Goel and Shawky 2009). Research also indicates that many firms experience lost revenue and decreased stock prices, at least in the short term, following the announcement of a data breach (Campbell et al. 2003, Wang et al. 2013). Perhaps most harmful to a firm’s long term viability is the reputational damage that can accrue from a breach should the firm not employ an effective postbreach response strategy (Goode et al. 2017).

Given the importance of the issue, scholars are working to identify factors that could impact the likelihood of a firm experiencing a data breach. Information systems (IS) research indicates that firms enactment of various technical and nontechnical security controls, as well as the information technology (IT) infrastructure and governance of the firm, are all significant factors in this regard (e.g., Kwon and Johnson 2013, 2014; Sen and Borle 2015; Wang et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2017; Pang and Tanriverdi 2017). Yet, as data breaches become more frequent and severe, it is evident that the IS/IT environment of the firm alone does not provide a complete understanding of data breach risk. In this vein, IS research has begun to identify a wider range of environmental and organizational factors that contribute to data breaches. The limited work in this realm shows that, for example, a firm’s age, size, and entrepreneurial mindset, along with cybercrime regulations, all influence the likelihood of breach (Png et al. 2008, Sen and Borle 2015, Angst et al. 2017, Hui et al. 2017, McLeod and Dolezel 2018). More broadly, this work points to a complex set of factors that drive data breach risk and the potential for gaining deeper insight into this phenomenon by expanding the line of reasoning beyond just the IS/IT environment of the firm.

To this end, we consider the link between a firm’s social performance and the likelihood of data breach. Although financial motives are at the heart of many data breaches, there is evidence that additional, noneconomic motives contribute to some firms being breached while others are spared. The opening excerpt illustrates one such motive: a sense of justice for what hackers<sup>2</sup> perceive as irresponsible business practices. Likewise, both industry and academic research identifies instances in which hackers targeted firms because of company policies or stances on social issues (e.g., AT&T 2015, Benjamin et al. 2016). More extensive data from Verizon show that “hacktivist” groups were responsible for more than half of all data breaches during a one-year period, which prompted the report’s authors to state, “It’s not just about money any more. It’s a big change in our adversaries. . . . [T]he target selection by these [cyber activist] groups didn’t follow the logical lines of who has money and/or valuable information” (Verizon 2012, p. 47). IS research also provides evidence that internal hacking incidents are more likely when employees perceive their own moral and social values as incongruent with those of their employers (Son 2011, Kim and Han 2019). Connecting firm social performance with data breach risk is also important because, as was recently noted by Kolbel et al. (¨ 2017), the socially responsible (or irresponsible) actions of a firm are closely linked with its risk-mitigation strategies, and in this digital era, there are few business risks as consequential as a data breach (Collins 2018).

To expand on these ideas and explicate a theoretical link between social performance and the likelihood of breach, we draw from management research on the social activities of corporations, which has crystallized into the field of corporate social responsibility (CSR). In this paper, we focus on firms’ enactment of CSR by invoking the concept of corporate social performance (CSP). CSP<sup>3</sup> is defined as an organization’s configuration of principles, policies, practices, programs, and observable outcomes relating to its social responsibilities and relationships with stakeholders (Wood 1991). Although it is intuitive to expect that a strong CSP record is universally applauded by stakeholders, emerging literature suggests ambiguity in the relationship between CSP and outcomes and finds that socially responsible actions can be both rewarding and harmful for firms (Godfrey et al. 2009, Luo et al. 2012, Flammer 2013, Minor 2015). Underlying this ambiguity is that stakeholders are known to scrutinize CSP and, in so doing, infer the motives behind firms’ social activities (Briscoe and Gupta 2016, Donia et al. 2019).

Taking cues from this work, we draw on stakeholder theory and argue that CSP affects the behavior and motives of both internal and external stakeholders in ways that impact the risk of data breach Pivotal to our theorizing is that we focus on deliberate breaches, which are caused by the intentional actions of the parties who perpetrate the breach, as opposed to data breaches resulting from inadvertent employee mistakes, for example. We propose a set of hypotheses about the impact that firms’ CSP strengths and CSP concerns have on the likelihood of experiencing a data breach. Following prior research (e.g., Mattingly and Berman 2006, Bermiss et al. 2014, Perrault and Quinn 2018), CSP strengths capture firms’ activities related to fair treatment of stakeholders and imageenhancing social expectations (e.g., diversity initiatives, philanthropy, pollution-prevention programs), whereas CSP concerns capture activities that are viewed as socially irresponsible, controversial, dangerous, and/or illegitimate (e.g., poor worker conditions in the supply chain, fines or civil penalties related to the safety of products and services, unfair treatment of employees, environmental harm).

A unique aspect of our theorizing is that we draw from emerging evidence that shows that socially responsible actions can be both rewarding and harmful for firms and conjecture that certain CSP strengths can be a detriment to information security. In particular, our study is informed by critiques of firms’ CSP efforts as often being disingenuous and as strategic attempts at portraying an image of social responsibility by investing in CSP areas that are highly visible yet tangential to core business operations (e.g., philanthropy, recycling programs); we call these investments peripheral CSP strengths. Furthering our focus on firms’ seemingly disingenuous attempts at social responsibility, we draw on the concept of “greenwashing,” which has been used to refer to firm investments in superficial social and environmental programs that attempt to mask more serious, negative impacts of the firm’s operations on its stakeholders and society at large. Extending the greenwashing concept to the domain of information security, we hypothesize that investments in peripheral CSP strengths increase the likelihood of breach, particularly when the firm simultaneously exhibits significant CSP concerns.

We operationalize CSP strengths and CSP concerns with data from the Kinder, Lydenberg, and Domini (KLD) database, a multidimensional method for measuring firms’ CSP that is widely used in the management literature and generally considered the most comprehensive data available (e.g, Waddock and Graves 1997, Flammer 2013, Hubbard et al. 2017, Perrault and Quinn 2018). We combine our measurement of CSP with publicly available information on data breaches and other firm observables (e.g., assets, capital expenditure, etc.), and leveraging between- and within-firm variation in CSP strengths and concerns, we use econometric methods to disentangle the effects of CSP on the likelihood of data breach. Our results provide an intriguing and previously unknown account of CSP as it relates to information security. Paradoxically, our results suggest that firms that have been identified as having CSP concerns are no more likely to experience a data breach, although, consistent with the notion that peripheral CSP strengths can beget increased risk of breach from employees and external hackers, we found a positive relationship between the likelihood of breach and firms known to have peripheral CSP strengths. Delving into this relationship and in line with evidence that stakeholders can detect and will respond negatively to firms’ attempts to mask poor social performance, we find that firms that simultaneously have peripheral CSP strengths and high CSP concerns are at increased risk of breach. This result suggests that perceived greenwashing efforts make firms attractive targets for security exploitation. We find consistent results using a battery of robustness checks, including falsification tests, alternate measurement and estimation approaches, and sample construction.

Our results contribute to the literature in several ways. Prior IS security research is largely focused on aspects of the IS/IT environment (e.g., security-related policies, procedures, and technical controls) as the sole drivers of information security performance. Although technology infrastructure is surely important, our work suggests that firms’ society-facing activities can also be key determinants of data breaches. In a broader sense, our work establishes a connection between the CSP and IS domains, which, as we later discuss, is an area of growing importance (e.g., IT can legitimize a firm’s social performance through increased information transparency). We also contribute to the budding literature on IS security that utilizes secondary data sources to measure constructs of interest and analyzes these data over extended time periods using econometric methods. Such analyses enable a better assessment of the causal factors that contribute to data breaches and complement several existing IS security studies that employ cross-sectional designs with perceptual measures. Our study is also one of the few to examine how a management activity such as social responsibility (or irresponsibility) might impact data breaches. Past research highlights diverging stakeholder reactions to firms’ seemingly positive CSP but is uncertain about the source of this divergence. Our work informs this tension by empirically assessing differential impacts for firms with peripheral forms of CSP and simultaneous high levels of CSP concerns. The results provide a cautionary tale for organizations regarding their promotion of CSP, particularly with respect to information security: Firms need to be wary of excessively promoting CSP activities that are peripheral in nature, especially when the firm simultaneously has meaningful CSP concerns.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. First, we briefly review the IS security and CSP literature streams that are foundational to our study This is followed by a discussion that theorizes how CSP can influence the likelihood of data breaches emanating from both internal and external sources and subsequent hypotheses that link CSP strengths and concerns to data breaches. We then discuss our data and the methodological approach used to test our hypotheses. We conclude with a discussion that highlights the contributions of our work and implications for research and practice.

## Literature Review

## Predictors of Data Breaches and IS Security Performance

Research on organizational information security effectiveness and the likelihood of data breaches in particular provides a contextual starting point for our study. This work indicates that various technical and nontechnical security controls reduce the likelihood of data breaches and other security incidents (Straub 1990, Spears and Barki 2010, Kankanhalli et al. 2003, Kwon and Johnson 2013). Notably, these studies employ cross-sectional survey designs with self-report and perceptual measures for their constructs of interest (e.g., self-reported number of breaches, perceived security effectiveness). An advancement in this research stream, which we also employ, is the use of secondary data sources to measure the study constructs over extended time periods. In this manner, Kwon and Johnson (2014) find that hospitals’ proactive investments in technical security controls (e.g., encryption, firewalls, intrusion detection systems) influenced subsequent data breach occurrences. These same authors also find a link between hospitals’ meeting certification requirements for security of electronic health records and reduced data breach risk (Kwon and Johnson 2018). Angst et al. (2017) also study data breaches in hospitals and find that increased security investment is associated with fewer breaches, but the reduction is not immediate and depends on the extent to which the security investments are integrated into organizational practices.

Other studies in nonhospital contexts find that centralized IT governance structures and the modernization of IT systems are associated with fewer breaches (Liu et al. 2017, Pang and Tanriverdi 2017). Ransbotham and Mitra (2009) analyze a proprietary database of attack data and find that aspects of the IS environment, such as the volume and intensity of network activity, influence whether firms are victimized by targeted versus random attacks. In another study with proprietary data, Wang et al. (2015) study insiders’ unauthorized access attempts toward the IT applications of a large banking institution and find that those applications with higher business value, lower strength of controls, higher visibility and accessibility, and fewer data protection mechanisms are more frequently targeted.

Research has also identified a limited number of environmental and organizational factors that contribute to data breaches. For instance, the aforementioned Angst et al. (2017) study finds that hospital data breaches are predicted by the age and size of the hospital, whether it has an entrepreneurial mind-set, and whether it belongs to a hospital network. Some of these same organizational factors are also predictive of hospital data breaches in a study by McLeod and Dolezel (2018). Studies have also linked certain economic indicators (e.g., gross domestic product) and legislation against cybercrime to a reduction in data breaches and other forms of attack (Png et al. 2008, Sen and Borle 2015, Hui et al. 2017).

The preceding studies provide important contributions to our understanding of data breaches and suggest avenues for thwarting such incidents, yet it is clear, based on the continued proliferation of data breaches, that our knowledge of the phenomenon remains incomplete. Accounting for factors beyond just the IS/IT environment of the firm and the limited corpus of non-IS/IT variables included in prior studies may help explain and predict the likelihood of data breaches. As noted at the outset of this article, there is some evidence that noneconomic motives contribute to data breaches, for both employees who engage in insider breaches and hackers who target firms from the outside. Clearly, financial gain is a strong motivator for breaches emanating from both of these sources, but noneconomic reasons may still play a nontrivial role in an attacker’s motives. To this end, we consider a firm’s CSP and argue that it affects the behavior and motives of both employees and external hackers in ways that impact data breach risk. Before explicating the underlying rationale for a linkage between CSP and data breaches, we next describe the CSP concept and its associated research in greater detail.

## The Socially Responsible Activities of Firms and Stakeholder Responses

CSR is a general concept that emphasizes firms’ responsibilities beyond the sole pursuit of shareholder economic interests to include their broader responsi bilities to society and the welfare of multiple stakeholders (e.g., customers, employees, community members, the environment; Wood 1991, Barnett 2007). As research on the social activities of corporations has grown, the literature in this area has become diverse and vast with various definitions, phrases, and operational izations relating to CSR (Aguinis and Glavas 2012). Although CSR has been used as an empirical measure in several studies (e.g., Yoon et al. 2006, Luo et al. 2012, King and McDonnell 2015), many argue that the more precise term, CSP, should be used because it captures observable processes and outcomes of social responsibility (Wood 1991, Ruf et al. 1993, Perrault and Quinn 2018). That is, CSP is a specific operationalization of CSR that places emphasis on social outcomes and performance (i.e., a firm’s enactment of CSR; Barnett 2007). Beyond directly indicating a firm’s social performance, CSP signals a firm’s larger motives and values with respect to social issues and the treatment of its stakeholders. Accordingly, CSP has been used to indicate a firm’s prosocial (or lack thereof) orientation to a variety of internal and external stakeholder groups (David et al. 2007, Bermiss et al. 2014).

The explicit operationalization that CSP affords provides another important distinction between CSR and CSP. The operationalization suggests that social activities can result in both strengths and weaknesses (sometimes simultaneously) and that these are potentially distinct, nonoverlapping constructs—not opposite ends of a continuum (Mattingly and Berman 2006, Mattingly 2017). Thus, firms can engage in social actions that are viewed as strengths by some stake holders and as weaknesses (or concerns) by others. Consistent with this line of reasoning, we use CSP as assessed via KLD data,<sup>4</sup> but we treat CSP strengths and concerns as unique constructs (Mattingly and Berman 2006).

In terms of stakeholder responses to CSP, studies that have assessed the linkage between CSP and aspects of firm performance, including both individual- and firm-level outcomes, suggest that stakeholder attributions of CSP may be complex. On the one hand, studies have found positive CSP records to be associated with improved financial performance (Godfrey et al. 2009, Flammer 2013), corporate reputation (Brammer and Pavelin 2006), consumer attitudes about the firm (Brown and Dacin 1997), and relationships with employees (Bauman and Skitka 2012, Rupp and Mallory 2015, Donia et al. 2019).

On the other hand, broader reviews of the CSP literature yield inconsistent and often contradictory results, particularly when it comes to CSP strengths (Orlitzky et al. 2003, Margolis et al. 2009, Peloza 2009). For example, Peloza (2009) reviews 128 empirical studies that link aspects of CSP strengths to firm financial performance and finds that 59% report a positive relationship, 27% report a mixed or neutral relationship, and 14% report a negative relationship. Aguinis and Glavas (2012) argue that this equivocality may be due, in part, to the aggregation of CSP strengths into overly broad constructs. They contend that if CSP strengths are unpacked—as we do—this can shed light on the positive and negative effects of firms’ socially responsible actions. From the stakeholder perspective, different facets of CSP strengths can be perceived both positively and negatively simultaneously, but when aggregated, these differences might be averaged out and thus lost.

Expanding on this point, the equivocality of the CSP findings may also be due to social responsibility being a topic that is highly scrutinized and contested by stakeholders. With increasing information transparency, along with public distrust of business in general and skepticism toward firms’ espoused CSP “successes” in particular (Yoon et al. 2006, Edelman 2015),<sup>5</sup> many stakeholders are moving beyond simple evaluations of CSP to make judgments of whether a firm is actually “walking its talk” when it comes to social responsibility. In this sense, research has shown that firms that espouse strong CSP records can attract increased stakeholder attention and even cynicism and, hence, be punished by them (e.g., lower stock price, targeted by activists, reduced firm reputation, weakened employee morale) on the grounds of their CSP being deemed self-serving, misleading, or otherwise misaligned with stakeholder expectations (Baron and Diermeier 2007, Baron 2009, King and McDonnell 2015, Briscoe and Gupta 2016, Marquis et al. 2016).

A related issue is that as firms improve their CSP, stakeholder expectations can likewise increase, which can be harmful to firms. In this vein, Luo et al. (2012) find that positive CSP is a liability in that firms with stellar environmental records were more likely to be highlighted in media reports and that the tone of the reports was no better than that of firms with poor environmental records. In contrast, Flammer (2013) finds evidence of CSP as a competitive resource in that eco-friendly events (positive press releases) are responsible for upticks in stock price, although eco-harmful events (negative press releases) result in downturns. Supporting the complex nature of stakeholder reactions to CSP, however, Flammer (2013) also finds that the positive returns from eco-friendly events diminish over time.

Collectively, the extant literature suggests that CSP is subject to significant stakeholder scrutiny, which can result in outcomes that are both rewarding and harmful for firms. Our theorizing integrates such perspectives, as we describe next, and considers CSP a salient issue for the specific types of internal and external stakeholders we consider in our study context.

## Theoretical Framing and Hypotheses Development

## CSP Impacts on Data Breaches: Employees and External Hackers as Relevant Stakeholders

Stakeholder theory proposes that the better the firm manages its relationships with entities impacted by its actions, the more successful it will be over time (Freeman 1984, Laplume et al. 2008). The theory addresses the fundamental questions of who (or what groups) are stakeholders of the firm and, thus, to whom (or what groups) management should pay attention (Donaldson and Preston 1995). In the seminal work in this area by Freeman (1984), stakeholders are broadly defined as “any group or individual who can affect or be affected by the achievement of the organization’s objectives” (p. 46). Freeman (1984) articulates stakeholders in terms of those that are internal to the firm, such as owners, customers, employees, and suppliers, and those that are external, such as activist groups, consumer advocates, the media, competitors, and government. A more succinct conceptualization of stakeholders considers them to be individuals or groups that have some power over firms, such as by influencing the firm’s economic well-being, reputation, and/or long-term survival (Mitchell et al. 1997).

Engaging in socially responsible behavior is one of the main avenues for firms to build and maintain trusting stakeholder relationships (Wood 1991, Laplume et al. 2008). Because CSP is directly related to stakeholder expectations with regard to social issues and, thus, serves as a stakeholder management device, much of the research on CSP has used stakeholder theory as a basis (Laplume et al. 2008, Godfrey et al. 2009, Perrault and Quinn 2018). We do the same and focus on two key stakeholder groups—employees and external hackers—to help illuminate the link between CSP and data breaches.

Employees. Employees are an important internal stakeholder group that is pertinent to the discussion of CSP and information security. With the growing number of data breach incidents, it has become apparent that the actions of employees are often a root cause regardless of whether the breach is deliberately perpetrated by parties internal or external to the organization (Gemalto 2018). In terms of CSP, research shows that employees’ perceived congruence with their firms social posture affects their workplace morale and contributes to several behavioral outcomes, including harmful, counterproductive, compliant, and extrarole behaviors (Hansen et al. 2011, Aguinis and Glavas 2012, Bauman and Skitka 2012, Rupp and Mallory 2015, Donia et al. 2019). Because it has been shown that CSP seeps into all aspects of the employment relationship, it follows that employees’ views of their firms with respect to CSP influence their security-related behaviors, thus impacting the risk of data breach.

The question of whether CSP influences securityrelated behavior has a basis in the behavioral IS security literature, which shows that employees can be either an asset or a detriment to information security efforts depending on how they feel about their employer (Hsu et al. 2015, Cram et al. 2019). The influence of CSP may be direct, such that employees incorporate CSP in deciding whether to engage in internal hacking activities, such as fraud or stealing sensitive corporate data. In this way, insider breaches can be prompted by the firm’s failure to satisfy employee expectations about CSP. In saying this, we recognize that financial gain is likely a leading motive for internal hacking activities, but we also contend that any discord that employees perceive toward their firms’ CSP tips the scales toward internal hacking or perhaps serves as an alternate motive altogether. It is intuitively appealing that the social posture of the firm and how it treats its stakeholders (as captured by CSP) would be a salient issue for the types of employees who are apt to engage in deliberate hacking activities because these individuals often have a “beef” with and/ or are skeptical of their employers and perhaps corporations in general.

CSP may also influence the risk of data breach by affecting whether employees enact security guidelines and/or adhere to information security policies (ISPs). Nonadherence to ISPs increases the chances of both internal and external breaches, as shown by an analysis of data breaches that occurred during 2005–2011 (Ayyagari 2012). Within this analysis are breaches in which employees failed to follow password policies, thus allowing an external hacker to access company data on a weakly protected system, and breaches in which personnel failed to install critical software patches, thus facilitating an external hack in which sensitive company data were stolen. In other breaches, employees engaged in more benign activities, such as leaving a file on a desk when there was a “clear desk policy” or failing to log off a personal computer that facilitated an insider breach perpetrated by a coworker. Adhering to ISPs takes cognitive effort and provides no inherent benefit to employees; hence, it is unlikely that employees expend the effort, which is often to serve the greater good of the organization, if they do not feel that the firm is meeting its societal obligations or putting forth a good-faith effort in this regard.

External Hackers. Given the significant financial and reputational damages from data breaches, many of which are perpetrated by external hackers, it is clear that this group yields significant power over firms in today’s business environment. In fact, the World Economic Forum ranked cyber breach as the third highest global business risk in terms of likelihood and impact (Collins 2018). Conceptualizing external hackers as stakeholders, therefore, fits the description of stakeholders as individuals or groups who influence the firm’s economic well-being, reputation, and/ or long-term survival (Mitchell et al. 1997). External hackers are a particularly relevant stakeholder group when it comes to matters of information security, and as previously noted and elaborated as follows, elements of the hacking community have interest in the social activities of corporations. Similar to the role of activists in the literature on CSP (Wood 1991, King and McDonnell 2015, Briscoe and Gupta 2016), we consider external hackers as a type of activist stakeholder group that can hold firms accountable for their CSP. Our contention is that CSP matters to this stakeholder group—particularly because many external hackers are skeptical of and harbor negative feelings toward business in general—and any discord that external hackers feel toward firms’ CSP can influence the likelihood that a firm is targeted for attack relative to another comparable firm (e.g., in terms of potential financial gains) without such considerations.

As with employees who may use CSP as a motive toward internal hacking or noncompliant security behavior that facilitates a breach, external hackers may use CSP as a noneconomic selection criterion that increases the firm’s risk of data breach. Of note is that although we expect the likelihood of breach from the two stakeholder groups to be impacted by CSP, the attack vectors used by each group likely differ. Em ployees can respond to CSP through activities such as internal fraud and stealing sensitive information, whereas external hackers can do so by means of malware and other forms of electronic entry (hacks) from the outside. We later provide empirical support showing that both employees and external hackers respond to CSP in ways that increase data breach risk.

The idea that external hackers target firms based on CSP has a basis in the literature, although a comprehensive, theoretically grounded investigation of the phenomenon is lacking. Accounts of hacking incidents and interviews with “black hat” hackers (i.e., those who willfully attack computer systems for nefarious purposes) indicate that some view themselves as “righters of wrongs” and thus target organizations that do not fulfill their social expectations (Rogers 2001, Young et al. 2007, Gandhi et al. 2011). For example, in their study of online hacker forums, Benjamin et al. (2016) find recruitment advertisements for hacktivist campaigns that targeted companies accused of causing significant environmental damage. Literature also suggests that hackers rationalize their illegal behavior on moral grounds and thus may target firms because they believe that they are providing a service for the greater good (Young et al. 2007, Xu et al. 2013). Media coverage also provides evidence that social issues prompt many attacks from external hackers (Arthur 2015, Bergal 2017). This evidence coincides with a survey of 127 self-identified black hat hackers at the BlackHat USA conference, wherein approximately 30% of the respondents chose the option of social consciousness or moral compass as their primary motive for hacking, and only 18% chose financial gain (Newman 2014). Notably, the current evidence that supports CSP and related concepts as drivers of external hacking is generally limited to firms’ poor social performance (i.e., CSP concerns), whereas we extend this work to include the premise that firms’ attempts to bolster their social performance (i.e., CSP strengths) can also be a driver of external hacking.

## Hypotheses That Link CSP to Data Breaches

Our proposed connection between CSP and data breaches is built on the assumption that employees and external hackers are aware of the underlying factors that comprise CSP. Supporting this assumption is the public nature of the inputs to the KLD ratings that we use for our conceptualizations of CSP strengths and concerns (which we further describe in the “Research Methodology” section). Research indicates that investors, analysts, and executives at both focal and competing firms are aware of the indicators of CSP (Waddock and Graves 1997, Bermiss et al. 2014, Minor 2015), as are prospective employees (Turban and Greening 1997) and activist groups (King and McDonnell 2015). A logical assumption is that employees and external hackers would likewise be aware of such information.

Building on this assumption, we first consider employees as internal stakeholders that evaluate their firms’ CSP concerns. Deficiencies in CSP (e.g., poor employee relations, product safety concerns, involvement in an environmental controversy) can negatively influence employees’ attitudes and behaviors and thus contribute to an unfavorable image of the firm (Hansen et al. 2011). This is a critical issue in terms of information security because workplace disgruntlement is a major cause of malicious insider breaches (Cummings et al. 2012, Liang et al. 2016). Drawing on stakeholder theory’s prediction that stakeholders may bring action against a firm when it fails to meet their duty-of-care expectations (Freeman 1984), employees may be swayed toward an insider breach (e.g., fraud, stealing sensitive data) when their firm is known to have CSP concerns because this paints the firm as a poor corporate citizen that disregards the needs of its stakeholders and society at large. Moreover, CSP concerns can negatively impact employees’ willingness to judiciously follow ISPs (e.g., using strong passwords, encrypting sensitive data, ignoring phishing emails) as well as engage in proactive security behavior such as voicing concerns about ineffective security controls (Hsu et al. 2015). Each of these factors makes firms more susceptible to data breaches.

In terms of external stakeholders, a mechanism by which CSP concerns should influence the likelihood of a data breach is through external hackers acting as disciplinarians that target firms with poor CSP records. Again, drawing on the stakeholder theory prediction that firms may be punished by stakeholders when failing to meet duty-of-care expectations, external hackers may view CSP concerns as indicative of the firm failing to meet its societal obligations through poor treatment of its stakeholders, thereby elevating the risk that a firm is targeted. This reasoning is consistent with evidence that activist groups target firms that do not live up to ethical or social standards (King and McDonnell 2015) and the previously described accounts of hackers that target firms based on similar rationale. Hence, we predict the following:

Hypothesis 1. CSP concerns are positively related to data breaches.

Turning to CSP strengths, a simplistic application of stakeholder theory in our study context is that CSP strengths improve stakeholder relationships with specific influences on employees and external hackers that are opposite to those described for Hypothesis 1, resulting in reduced risk of breach. We submit, however, that such consideration of CSP strengths is an oversimplification. In making this assertion, we draw on the previously described tension in the literature, which shows that CSP strengths can be both rewarding and harmful for firms. Delving into the nuanced aspects of CSP strengths provides insight into this tension and offers a basis for why certain CSP efforts increase the likelihood of breach.

Scholars have categorized socially responsible actions into those that are peripheral to the firm and those that are embedded in its strategy, routines, and core operations (Laszlo and Zhexembayeva 2011, Aguinis and Glavas 2013). Embedded CSP is indicated to stakeholders based on a firm’s commitment to social responsibility throughout the entire organization and particularly the core aspects of the business. That is, embedded CSP is represented in a firm’s products and services and also in areas such as corporate governance structures, the treatment of employees, policies and practices in regard to diversity, and human rights issues/fair labor practices throughout the supply chain. In contrast, peripheral CSP actions are not synchronized to the core business activities and operations of the firm. This latter form of CSP often makes up the bulk of firms’ CSP posture. For example, the literature provides evidence that philanthropy and other community relations activities are firms most common forms of social responsibility (Peloza and Shang 2011, Minor 2015). Although highly visible, these efforts are generally not resource intensive and often employ a limited set of practices, such as cause-related marketing campaigns with slogans related to social responsibility (Chen et al. 2014, Nyilasy et al. 2014). Charitable giving and volunteer programs, for instance, are often administered by a few employees in a particular department or specialized unit. Likewise, environmental programs (e.g., recycling programs) are often not central to the firm’s core business operations, employ limited resources, and are often highly publicized on company promotional materials.

Because peripheral CSP is highly visible yet not integrated into a firm’s daily practices and routines, promotion of this form of CSP has been attacked by some stakeholders for being primarily motivated by profit and image enhancement as opposed to sincere social and environmental concerns (i.e., CSP “window dressing”; Barnett 2007, Aguinis and Glavas 2013). The reality of peripheral CSP is that the practices are not bad, nor are they always strategically pursued. Even if strategically pursued, engaging in peripheral CSP presumably does more for a cause than abstaining from such activities. One expectation is that the average stakeholder takes peripheral CSP strengths at face value and reacts positively, or peripheral CSP strengths may induce no response from stakeholders who have become a bit wary of their authenticity. But, for the stakeholders under consideration in our study (who, again, are apt to harbor negative feelings toward business and/or specific firms), peripheral CSP strengths are likely at odds with their established views of the firm and thus may trigger a more significant backlash.

Academic literature helps support this viewpoint. Namely, the corporate reputation literature suggests that self or external promotion results in notoriety and celebrity-like attention (Rindova et al. 2006, Pfarrer et al. 2010), which can be problematic for peripheral CSP. As described earlier, firms that espouse CSP successes are often held to a higher standard and so endure increased stakeholder scrutiny and even cynicism toward their CSP. For particularly skeptical stakeholders, firms’ promotion of peripheral CSP strengths can be viewed as insincere and perhaps misleading, which can produce negative outcomes (e.g., lower stock price, targeting by activists, reduced reputation, weakened employee morale; Baron and Diermeier 2007, Baron 2009, King and McDonnell 2015, Briscoe and Gupta 2016, Marquis et al. 2016). Related to this point, stakeholders are known to be skeptical of CSP information that is self-disclosed by the firm (Kolbel et al. ¨ 2017), which is typically how information on peripheral CSP strengths is distributed (e.g., company websites, standard and annual CSP reports).

We posit that the enhanced skepticism and cyni cism attached to peripheral CSP permeates to the employees and external hackers we consider in this study, thereby supporting a positive relationship between peripheral CSP strengths and data breaches To the extent that these stakeholders view peripheral CSP strengths as indicative of the firm putting itself on a pedestal and portraying a “holier than thou” image, they may be skeptical of peripheral CSP strengths and thus target these firms for an attack that results in a data breach. This assertion is consistent with the tenets of stakeholder theory in that stakeholder skepticism toward the firm or its actions may prompt stakeholder backlash (Freeman 1984). Disgruntled employees, for example, might become even more positively disposed toward internal hacking in firms that promote peripheral CSP strengths because these employees may feel that their employers are not worthy of social praise.<sup>6</sup> Employees (particularly those who place strong importance on social responsibility) may also feel disengaged from the firm because of its perceived superficial attempts at CSP, thereby prompting ISP noncompliance, which increases the likelihood of breaches. For external hackers, just as they use CSP concerns as a noneconomic selection criterion to target firms with poor social performance, we posit that they target firms that promote peripheral CSP because this form of social responsibility is tangential to the firm and often viewed with suspicion, particularly based on our conjecture that external hackers are naturally skeptical. External hackers may feel that the firm can and should do more when it comes to CSP despite the fact that it is doing something. Likely contributing to this perception is that peripheral CSP efforts are highly visible to external stakeholders, whereas the firm’s potentially more substantive CSP efforts may be less evident to these outside groups. The preceding discussion leads to the following hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 2. Peripheral CSP strengths are positively related to data breaches.

The impact of peripheral CSP strengths is further complicated by the reality that some firms are known to have CSP concerns in conjunction with their strengths (Strike et al. 2006, Perrault and Quinn 2018). In fact, a persistent critique of firms’ CSP efforts is that they involve strategic promotion of CSP strengths (often peripheral ones) in an effort to mask or divert stakeholder attention from their significant CSP concerns—a practice known as greenwashing (Lyon and Maxwell 2011, Marquis et al. 2016). Academic literature suggests the prevalence of greenwashing. For example, empirical studies have shown that firms tend to engage in peripheral CSP (e.g., community relations, philanthropy) in times when they perceive negative stakeholder reactions for poor CSP records (David et al. 2007, Lin et al. 2011, Kim 2014). Likewise, in a comprehensive review of CSP studies, it was found that firms with documented CSP weaknesses would often neglect the weaknesses and instead engage in peripheral CSP activities that could be perceived as strengths in order to improve public perception and mitigate stakeholder backlash (Perrault and Quinn 2018). The media are also replete with stories of greenwashing. A well-known case is that of British Petroleum (BP), which had marketed itself as environmentally responsible based on its investments in alternative energy (even touting BP as standing for “Beyond Petroleum”), only to have it discovered that the company was engaging in extremely irresponsible environmental practices, some of which came to light during the 2010 Gulf of Mexico oil spill. Another example is Walmart, which invests heavily in charitable causes and environmental programs but has been criticized for providing low wages and neglecting investments in employees’ physical and psychological working environment.

Research suggests that stakeholders can detect and will respond negatively to perceived greenwashing attempts. For example, firms’ promotion of peripheral CSP strengths has been linked to increased employee skepticism and backlash (e.g., weakened morale, counterproductive behavior) in firms that are poor social performers (Donia and Sirsly 2016, Donia et al. 2019). There is also evidence that activist groups target firms that claim to be leaders in social and environmental initiatives yet have poor CSP records (Lyon and Maxwell 2011, King and McDonnell 2015).

Stakeholder theory supports the notion that firms get punished for such disingenuous attempts at CSP because this weakens stakeholder relationships and constitutes a failure to meet their expectations regarding social responsibility. Based on this reasoning, we expect that the employee and external hacker stakeholder groups under consideration in our study react negatively to peripheral CSP strengths when coupled with high CSP concerns. Because CSP concerns are generally not self-disclosed by the firm, they have strong credibility (Kolbel et al.¨ 2017), which may confirm the previously described suspicions from our stakeholders (Hypothesis 2) regarding the disingenuous motives behind peripheral CSP strengths. For employees, perceptions that the firm is misleading stakeholders by creating a facade of CSP should weaken the employee–firm relationship, particularly for those already skeptical employees who are apt to engage in deliberate hacking activities. Again, this weakened employee–firm relationship should contribute both directly (i.e., insider breach) and indirectly (i.e., ISP noncompliance) to an increased likelihood of breaches. For external hackers, they likewise should respond negatively to firms’ perceived disingenuous attempts at social responsibility, which are more explicit when peripheral CSP strengths exist alongside high CSP concerns, and thus target these firms for an attack. The rationale is that external hackers do not consider these firms to be walking their talk when it comes to CSP and perhaps deliberately attempting to deceive their stakeholders, thereby prompting backlash. Hence, we offer the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. Peripheral CSP strengths have a stronger positive relationship with data breaches for firms with high CSP concerns as compared with firms with low CSP concerns.

## Research Methodology Sample and Data Construction

We constructed a panel data set that started with all firms listed in the KLD database with CSP ratings from years 2005–2010.<sup>8</sup> Within this data set (henceforth KLD05-10 data set) of approximately 3,000 firms per year during our study time frame,<sup>9</sup> we next identified all firms reported to have a data breach during this period. We did this by accessing breach data from the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC) website (privacyrights.org). The PRC website provides a listing of publicly announced data breaches with its information sourced from government agencies and news media websites; it is the largest and most extensive public data set of its type (Edwards et al. 2016). The PRC website contained 391 breaches among 243 different firms over the years 2005–2010, but some of the firms that were breached were not-for-profits, nongovernment organizations, or privately held, so we elimi nated these breaches because of key information that was missing and/or unavailable in either the KLD database or our other data sources. The reduced set consisted of 190 firms with 240 breaches that matched firms in our initial data set (i.e., KLD05-10). Within this data set, we then created a matched sample<sup>10</sup> by identifying a breached and nonbreached firm that came from the same four-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code and were similar in size (on average, the firms were within 6.7% of each other in terms of total assets and 16.5% in terms of sales). One firm with two breaches was removed because there was not a similar firm to match it. Thus, our final data set consisted of 378 firms, of which 189 experienced one or more breaches (238 breaches total). The remaining firms in the KLD05-10 data set were removed from this portion of the analysis. Notably, no single firm in our data set experienced more than one breach per year, which is consistent with other studies of breaches over similar time frames (Angst et al. 2017).

For each firm-year, we created a dummy variable coded as one if the firm experienced a breach and zero otherwise. Consistent with our focus on deliberate breaches and for purposes of our analysis, we had to distinguish between breaches that were malicious and those that were nonmalicious (e.g., inadvertent employee mistakes). We labeled the malicious breaches as MalBreach, and they were distinguished as follows: the PRC website provides its own categorization of each breach (see Table 1, “Type”); however, two of the authors—both with experience in IS security research and the study of data breaches in particular— manually coded each of the 238 breaches as either malicious or nonmalicious based on the text within the breach descriptions (see Online Appendix A for examples). There were no disagreements between the two authors regarding the classification. The only debatable point was the treatment of the “Unknown” type, which we coded as nonmalicious to be conservative. The resulting breakdown was 71 malicious (i.e., our MalBreach variable) and 167 nonmalicious breaches, as further detailed in Table 1. In terms of our matched sample, whereas 50% of firms in the sample had a breach during the time frame of our analysis, 18% of the firms had a malicious breach during the same period.

As noted earlier, we drew on the KLD database for ratings of firms’ CSP. The KLD database includes ratings of CSP strengths and concerns for a wide range of publicly traded firms using more than 70 binary indicators that cover seven major CSP dimensions:

community, corporate governance, diversity, employee relations, environment, human rights, and product. There are few sources of independent evaluations of social activity, but KLD (now known as MSCI) has been shown to have several advantages over others (Ruf et al. 1993, Mattingly and Berman 2006) and has essentially become the largest CSP data available (Chatterji and Toffel 2010, Mattingly 2017) and the de facto standard in CSP research (Mattingly and Berman 2006, Hull and Rothenberg 2008, Choi and Wang 2009, Hart and Sharfman 2015). For a detailed discussion of the methodology through which KLD data are collected and verified, please see Graves and Waddock (1994). In short, on an annual basis, KLD evaluates its universe of firms on the criteria listed in Online Appendix B. The evaluations are performed each year at the same time in order to reduce the likelihood of seasonal effects. KLD uses its research staff to evaluate the companies on objective rules, and any disagreements between staff are resolved by a third party. The objective assessments are based on information from public disclosures, company and government documents, financial statements, academic publications, database searches, press releases, and interviews with corporate managers, regulators, and activist groups. Finally, the ratings are sent to each firm to provide managers with the opportunity to discuss discrepancies.

In terms of scoring, each year KLD assigns each firm a one if a particular CSP strength or concern exists and zero otherwise. For example, as described by Bermiss et al. (2014), a firm that gives more than 1.5% of its net earnings to charity receives a value of one for the charitable giving strength indicator (within the community dimension), whereas a firm whose actions have adversely affected the quality of life, tax base, or property values of its community receives a one for the negative economic impact concern indicator (also within the community dimension). KLD does not provide an index or cumulative score; hence, following the bulk of CSP studies and the norm of recent work (Strike et al. 2006, Bermiss et al. 2014, Mattingly 2017), we measured CSP strengths and concerns by summing the total number of strengths and concerns, respectively, across the relevant CSP dimensions. For CSP concerns (CSPConcerns), our measure is the summation of the concern indicators across the seven CSP dimensions. We also include total CSP strengths (CSPStrengths), which is the summation of the strength indicators across the seven CSP dimensions as a way to assess the overall effect of CSP investments and as a control in our model specifications.

In order to test Hypotheses 2 and 3, which focus specifically on peripheral CSP strengths (PeripheralStrengths), we operationalized this form of CSP by summing the strength variables that are specific to the community and environment dimensions of KLD. We chose these two dimensions for the following reasons: of the seven CSP dimensions in the KLD database, community and environment are best representative of peripheral CSP, whereas the other five dimensions could be considered embedded CSP, based on the categorization of Aguinis and Glavas (2013). Specifically, the KLD measure of community strengths is largely based on firms’ philanthropic efforts, which are peripheral to the firm and not tied to its daily practices and routines (Peloza 2009). Likewise, environmental practices, as defined by KLD, are usually peripheral. For example, research suggests that when firms strategically attempt to appease stakeholders on CSP, they often implement environmental practices, such as recycling programs, while not integrating CSP practices throughout the entire organization (Ramus and Montiel 2005).

We also included a measure of embedded CSP strengths (EmbeddedStrengths) in order to conceptually distinguish peripheral from embedded forms of CSP and to enable a cleaner test of the influence of peripheral CSP strengths in our empirical analyses. Based on the categorization of Aguinis and Glavas (2013), the KLD dimensions of product, employee relations, diversity, corporate governance, and human rights fall under embedded CSP practices, so we measured embedded CSP strengths as the sum of the strength indicators for these five dimensions. To elaborate, the products that a firm produces (e.g., General Electric produces eco-friendly lighting products) are obviously a direct representation of its commitment to CSP as a core principle. Embedding CSP internally in a firm’s daily practices is also seen through the policies and practices in regard to employees and diversity (Rupp and Mallory 2015) as well as the governance structures of the firm and whether they support CSP (Marquis and Qian 2014). Finally, and probably most indicative of embedded CSP practices, is the human rights dimension of KLD.

The indicators in this dimension are largely related to a firm’s labor practices throughout its supply chain, thereby touching on the core operations of the firm (Matten and Moon 2008).

We collected additional data commonly used in firm-level CSP and IS studies to control for variation in firm characteristics, particularly those related to size and resources. For each firm-year observation, we obtained from Compustat the stockholder equity of the parent company (Equity), retained earnings (Earnings), net income (Income), cash flow (Cash), and total liabilities (Liabilities). We also reasoned that a firm’s public visibility would influence its likelihood of being a target of attack (particularly for external hackers) and thus control for this in our analysis. For these data, we queried the Thomson Reuters News Archive (TRNA) database and extracted a count of the number of times each firm in our data set (identified by its stock ticker) was mentioned in a press release in a given year. To validate this measure, we performed two additional comparisons. First, we conducted the same search with the Factiva news database, and although there was more missing data (454 missing values across 2,268 firm-years), the correlation between the TRNA and Factiva results was 0.84. Second, we conducted spot checks using Google News searches for stock tickers in a given year, and the results were again consistent. We used TRNA because it had the fewest missing data points (84 missing values across 2,268 firm-years); hence, with this source, we captured the number of press mentions (PressMentions) that each firm received as a measure of public visibility. ## Estimation Approach

We estimate the relationships in our model using econometric models for panel data. Specifically, we estimate an ordinary least squares (OLS) panel fixed effects model including both firm and time fixed effects and robust standard errors. We estimate our main specification using a linear probability model. We opted for a linear probability model as our main estimation approach for a few reasons. First, this model offers interpretable results, which are typically consistent with estimates from nonlinear models (Angrist and Pischke 2008). Second, we wish to avoid the potential of biased estimates from nonlinear models with fixed effects (fixed effects are key to identifying robust estimates of CSP in our analysis). Third, nonlinear fixed effects models are estimated using a conditional maximum likelihood estimation and thus only leverage variation in the outcome across time in our data. This precludes a significant portion of our data from being included in our main analysis (e.g., firms that did not experience a data breach).

This specification is intended to estimate the impact of CSP concerns on malicious breaches (Hypothesis 1). MalBreach indicates whether firm i suffered a malicious data breach at time $t ,$ and CSPConcerns is the number of CSP concerns for firm i in time t. We follow prior literature by including total CSP strengths (CSPStrengths) as a control to address potential omitted variable bias (Ioannou and Serafeim 2015). More so, estimating the effect of total CSP strengths may hint at the directionality of subsequent analysis focused on Hypotheses 2 and 3 (see model (2)). The coefficients of the variables in model (1) $( \beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 } )$ represent the impact of total CSP concerns and total CSP strengths on the relative risk of a data breach. We also include a vector of controls focused on relevant firm characteristics, as described earlier. These measures help control for any time-varying differences in performance between firms that may make some firms differentially more likely to suffer a data breach and also more likely to invest in CSP initiatives. To the extent that information security investments reflect discretionary or ancillary spending by firms, these measures capture cash that firms have on hand to invest in information security. Interestingly, this suggests that the effect of better financial performance is ambiguous. Better-performing firms might be more appealing targets for hackers but could also have more substantial infor mation security investments. The vector of controls also includes PressMentions for firm i in time t to help address selection concerns associated with firms that suffer data breaches $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , firms high in CSP strengths or concerns may also be more prominently featured in the press, thus making them more likely to suffer breaches).

We include firm and time (year) fixed effects (represented by $\theta _ { i }$ and $\lambda _ { t } ,$ respectively); $\mu _ { i t }$ is the error term. Firm fixed effects allow us to control for time-invariant factors that could simultaneously drive engagement in CSP practices and data breaches. For instance, it may be that firms in a particular geographic region or industry have more CSP concerns but also have weaker processes and lack extensive IT infrastructures, thus making them simultaneously more susceptible to breaches. Absent firm fixed effects, this can explain why CSP concerns could result in increased data breaches. Because these factors remain constant over time, our model accounts for the potential bias from them. Time fixed effects allow us to control for time trends in our data and any shocks that impact all firms in a given year; for example, any new legislation that applies nationally would be captured by our time fixed effects.

We estimate a second main model intended to more directly evaluate Hypotheses 2 and 3. This model is similar to model (1) but separates the effects of peripheral and embedded CSP strengths by breaking CSPStrengths into the number of peripheral and embedded strengths for firm i at time t (PeripheralStrengths and EmbeddedStrengths , respectively). We also evaluate whether the effect of peripheral CSP strengths differs for firms with significant CSP concerns by interacting PeripheralStrengths with an indicator for whether a firm i has an above-the-median number of concerns in year t (HighConcerns ). Similar to model (1), we include a control for total CSP concerns (i.e., to control for omitted variable bias) as well as other firm-level controls and firm and year fixed effects.

## Main Results

In Table 3, we report estimates from our main analysis, with MalBreach serving as the dependent variable in each model. We first assess the variance inflation factor (VIF) of our covariates to ensure that multicollinearity is not an issue for our estimation. We find an average VIF of 1.9 across all models, and the highest VIF score for any variable is 3.3—both of which are below the recommended maximum of 10 (Kennedy 1992). We next evaluate the impacts of CSP concerns and CSP strengths using our first model specification (model (1)) that includes firm and year fixed effects and controls that account for various firm factors that may bias our estimation (columns (1)–(3)) Surprisingly, as shown in columns (1) and (3), we find no support for CSP concerns influencing the probability of a breach (Hypothesis 1 not supported). Although this initial model does not differentiate between peripheral and embedded CSP strengths, we find evidence of a positive effect of total CSP strengths on data breach risk. Specifically, estimating the impact of CSP strengths in our fixed effects model with controls (column (2)) reveals a positive and significant influence $( \beta _ { C S P S t r e n g t h s } = 0 . 0 0 \hat { 9 } , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). Estimating both CSP strengths and CSP concerns in the same model (column (3)) confirms our result: one additional CSP strength results in a 0.09% increase in the probability of a data breach. Notably, the average firm in our data set has between two and three CSP strengths. These initial results hint at some of the positive effects of CSP strengths on data breach risk that we previously hypothesized and highlight the importance of delving into the nuances of the impact of CSP strengths. Accordingly, we next explore the role of peripheral CSP strengths in these initial findings (Hypothesis 2) as well as whether high levels of CSP concerns moderate these effects (Hypothesis 3).

Leveraging the second model specification (model (2)) that separates the impact of peripheral and embedded CSP strengths and includes all controls and firm and time fixed effects, we estimate the influence of peripheral CSP strengths and find that it is positive and marginally significant $( \beta _ { P e r i p h e r a l S t r e n g t h s } = 0 . \bar { 0 } 1 8 , p <$ 0.10, column (4)). As described previously, we also include a measure of embedded CSP strengths to better differentiate the effects of peripheral CSP strengths on the likelihood of breach. Supporting the distinctiveness of the two forms of CSP strengths, the influence of embedded CSP strengths does not have a significant impact on the probability of breach $( \beta _ { E m b e d d e d S t r e n g t h s } = 0 . 0 0 8 , p > 0 . \bar { 1 0 } _ { }$ , column (5)).<sup>11</sup> These results in combination suggest that the observed positive effect of total CSP strengths is largely attributed to peripheral CSP strengths. To test Hypothesis $^ { 3 , }$ , we evaluate the effect of peripheral CSP strengths on the probability of breach while parsing for whether the firm has CSP concerns above or below the median level for firms in our sample. Presumably, employees and external hackers view peripheral CSP strengths as more disingenuous when firms are simultaneously plagued by high CSP concerns, and our results confirm this conjecture (Hypothesis 3 supported). Specifically, we find that the positive effect of peripheral CSP strengths on the probability of data breach is more salient when firms simultaneously have high levels of CSP concerns (column (6)). In contrast, when CSP concerns are low, peripheral CSP investments have a small and nonsignificant effect on the risk of data breach (column (7)). ## Robustness Tests

Although our main results are encouraging and provide support for our hypotheses, some concerns arise with analyses using secondary data. Next, we address concerns about biased estimates resulting from endogenous pursuit of CSP investments, spurious correlation, model estimation, and data-selection issues. The results of these robustness tests are reported in Table 4.

Endogeneity of CSP. A common concern with this type of analysis is that firms select into different levels of CSP strengths and concerns. This introduces endogeneity concerns and the possibility that other factors correlated with both CSP levels and data breaches are biasing our results. In particular, a salient concern is simultaneity or reverse causality. Relative to our main results, it may be that firms suffering from reputational issues are more likely to be breached and also more likely to pursue CSP strengths to repair their image. In effect, it might be true that data breaches are driving firms to pursue CSP strengths and not the other way around. We investigate these concerns using lead variables for CSP strengths that effectively capture the impact of changes in CSP before they actually happen. As shown in column (1) of Table 4, we find that the effects of CSP strengths lead variables going back three years are generally nonsignificant, and when significant (one-year lead), the effect is actually negative. That is, if a pretrend exists, it is trending toward lower probability of a data breach for firms prior to pursuing CSP strengths (opposite our main effect). We also estimate a similar analysis focusing on the pretrend for the subcategories of CSP strength types as well as CSP concerns focusing on year leads to diminish loss of sample size. Similar to the main analysis, we find a lack of significant coefficients on lead variables for CSP concerns as well as peripheral and embedded CSP strengths (column (2) of Table 4).

An additional concern is that unobserved firm factors are driving our results. A typical concern in this regard is unobserved differences between “good” and “bad” firms (e.g., quality of processes, management, organization, etc.) and their potential impacts on our estimation. As we noted previously, our fixed effects modeling approach and our controls address some of these concerns (e.g., it is fine if quality differs between firms; as long as firm quality does not shift dramatically during our time period, it would not bias our results). Moreover, our results shield us somewhat from this concern. Specifically, we find that firms that exhibit CSP strengths, in an overall sense, are actually more likely to incur the negative outcome of a data breach. Prior literature suggests that CSP strengths are a proxy for higher quality of firm management, not lower (Waddock and Graves 1997). Thus, if this concern persists in our models, our results may actually be conservative.

Nonetheless, we supplement our main analysis with falsification tests to evaluate whether unobserved factors or spurious correlations are driving our results. Specifically, in our data set, we observe various types of breaches (see Table 1); some are malicious and intentional (i.e., those that make up our MalBreach variable), and others are accidental and, if purposeful (e.g., theft of a laptop), are more likely crimes of opportunity as opposed to truly motivated actions against the firm. If our results are driven by a correlation of CSP capabilities and other unobserved factors (e.g., lower-quality processes, bad information security management), then we may expect our effects to persist for accidental and other nonmalicious breaches. To test for this, we focus on the impacts of CSP on the stolen/lost media and accidental disclosure categories of breaches in our data set. As shown in columns (3) and (4) of Table 4, we find that CSP strengths have no significant effect on the probability of these breaches occurring, which suggests that our main estimation results are not spurious or driven by some unobserved factors related to firm processes or management.

We also examine the impact of peripheral CSP strengths and their interaction with CSP concerns while parsing for malicious and nonmalicious breaches. This allows us to further break down the effect of CSP strengths in terms of the types of breaches impacted. We previously conjectured that although the mechanisms may differ, CSP impacts the likelihood of breach from both employees and external hackers. However, we also clarified that the types of breaches are likely to differ. We find support for this conjecture in our data, with the vast majority (>90%) of internal malicious breaches coming as a result of employee fraud, whereas the majority (again >90%) of external breaches perpetrated through malware and hacks. With this context in mind, we break down our analysis and separate internal and external malicious breaches. We find that the combination of peripheral CSP strengths and high CSP concerns increases the probability of both internal and external malicious breaches (columns (5) and (6) of Table 4). This suggests that the effect of seemingly disingenuous CSP is doubly harmful because it can impact the motives of both internal and external stakeholders toward breach. Evaluating the impact of CSP on nonmalicious breaches acts as a further falsification test for our evaluation of Hypotheses 2 and 3. Importantly, we do not observe a significant effect of any form of CSP on either internal or external nonmalicious breaches (columns (7) and (8) of Table 4), suggesting that these dynamics are impacting willful compromise of the firm’s data and not from spurious correlation with, for example, unobserved firm quality.

Another possible concern is that increases in CSP strengths are related to more advanced IT capabilities in a firm over time. If this is the case, it may be that firms with CSP strengths are also investing in more IT capabilities and are thus more susceptible to data breaches over time. To our knowledge, there is no comprehensive resource capturing IT capabilities for the broad set of firms that are included in our analysis. Thus, we rely on a proxy measure, Tobin’s q, which has been shown to correlate with firm IT investments (Tam 1998, Bharadwaj et al. 1999). Tobin’s q is defined as the capital market value of the firm divided by the replacement value of its assets (Montgomery and Wernerfelt 1988). Unlike many other firm performance measures, Tobin’s q is forward looking (which is useful because IT investments are known to have substantial learning curves), risk adjusted, and less susceptible to changes in accounting practices. Furthermore, Tobin’s q accounts for IT’s many intangible benefits (e.g., improved product quality, customer service, and decision making). We present the results with Tobin’s q in Table 5. As shown, the results for total CSP strengths are consistent (column (1)) with our main analysis, as are those for the interaction of peripheral CSP strengths and high CSP concerns (column (2)). We extend this analysis with a control for firm capital expenditures because IT investments are included in these financial measures. We again find consistent results for total CSP strengths (column (3)), suggesting that time-varying changes in IT investment are not confounding our findings. We also evaluate this concern for our second model (model (2)) with the controls for Tobin’s q and capital expenditure added, and the results for the interaction of peripheral CSP strengths and high CSP concerns are once again consistent (column (4)).

Another potential concern is whether there is a differential effect of CSP strengths based on the industry in which the firm operates. For instance, CSP strengths may be capturing differences between firms in terms of their attractiveness to internal and external hackers. This could be true, for example, if firms with access to large volumes of consumer personal information or financial data were more “consumer facing” and thus invested more in CSP strengths. This concern would be partially addressed by the firm fixed effects because no firms in our data set switched industries during our study time frame. We further address this concern by creating a binary measure to indicate whether a firm is in an industry with recognized financially attractive assets for hackers. For this measure, firms in the financial (SIC codes between 60 and 67), retail (SIC codes between 52 and 59), and healthcare (SIC code 80) sectors are coded as one because industry reports (e.g., Verizon 2012, 2018; Gemalto 2018) have consistently shown that firms in these industries are some of the highest value targets for hackers; firms in all other industries are coded as zero. In columns (5)–(8) of Table 5, we parse our data by firms in industries with strong financial motives for hackers and firms in other industries (using the aforementioned SIC codes). As shown, peripheral CSP strengths result in an increase in the risk of data breach for firms without clear financial motives for hackers (column (6)) and a nonsignificant effect for firms with clearer financial motives (column (5)). Examining the interaction of peripheral CSP strengths and high CSP concerns (columns (7) and (8)), we find comparable statistically significant effects for firms with both strong financial and nonfinancial incentives for hackers.<sup>12</sup> Collectively, these results suggest that the effect of CSP strengths on risk of data breach is not driven by firms with financial incentives for hackers.

CSP Measurement and Comparison Groups. We measured our CSP variables using the sum of the various KLD indicators for each variable, which is a common approach used in the literature. However, a potential drawback is that the CSP dimensions in the KLD database have differing numbers of indicators for their respective strengths and concerns. For example, in our data, firms could achieve a maximum of four community strengths and a maximum of five environmental strengths. This raises the concern that our results are driven by the scale differences between the various types of strengths and concerns that make up a firm’s CSP activities. A counterargument to this concern is that the scale issues are relevant and should be incorporated into the measure (based on the prior literature) because certain types of CSP take more forms than others. Nonetheless, to address this concern, we created alternate measures of CSP that use each CSP strength or concern normalized by the number of indicators of strength or concern in that dimension. Specifically, the value for k dimensions of CSP strengths is measured as the average of n binary items that are under the umbrella of that dimen sion k: $\begin{array} { r } { C _ { k } = \sum _ { 1 } ^ { n } i _ { n } / n . } \end{array}$ . To create the firm-level total CSP strengths, we sum these values across k dimensions of CSP strengths $( \Sigma _ { k } C _ { k } )$ . For example, a firm that has three CSP strengths in the community dimension would be assigned a value of 0.75 (three strengths achieved/four total strengths in that dimension). This value for community strengths would be summed with other similarly defined values for other dimensions of CSP strengths. We use a similar approach to normalize our measures of peripheral and embedded CSP strengths and also CSP concerns. Although this measure helps address some of the scale issues, we acknowledge per our prior discussion that the sum of the individual measures may be more appropriate in other respects (i.e., because each aspect of a dimension is a distinct and standalone component of CSP). Nonetheless, using this revised measure, we reevaluate our hypotheses and find highly consistent results (see Table 6). In column (1), we find that total CSP strengths increase the risk of data breaches, whereas CSP concerns do not. In columns (2) and (3), we continue to observe that peripheral CSP strengths seem to drive this effect. In columns (4)–(6), we again see that the impact of peripheral CSP strengths is driven by firms that also have high CSP concerns.

Another potential issue with our estimation is that we evaluate the impacts of CSP strengths and concerns by comparing firms that suffer malicious breaches against those that suffer either a nonmalicious breach or do not suffer a breach at all. To address this concern, we also estimate a model by restricting our data to only the firms that suffered a malicious breach and their corresponding nonbreached firms. We also estimate this model with and without our control variables (columns (7) and (8) of Table 6). We find that our results are not only robust when using this subset of our sample but also are substantially larger and still significant; an additional CSP strength resulted in a 2.4% increase in the probability of a malicious data breach in our full model.

## Capturing Overall CSP Posture

The conclusions of our analysis thus far suggest that CSP has a nuanced impact on data breaches with a notable and positive impact on the likelihood of breach when firms make investments in CSP activities that are peripheral to their core business activities and operations. The combination of firms investing in peripheral (rather than embedded) CSP strengths when they already have notable CSP concerns seems to be particularly harmful with respect to data breach risk. This result points to a potentially broader issue of how to capture legitimate or genuine CSP posture when evaluating its impact on various firm outcomes with implications that likely extend beyond data breaches to other firm performance measures. Of course, there are numerous ways to reconceive the measure of CSP, and a formal construction and rigorous evaluation of such a measure is outside the scope of this paper. However, we suggest one potential alternate approach to measuring a firm’s overall CSP posture that incorporates both its CSP strengths and concerns and attempts to account for whether a firm engages in disingenuous CSP investments. We view this as a particularly worthy endeavor for the context of our study (i.e., deliberate internal and external hacking activity that leads to breaches) given the skeptical nature of the stakeholders we consider.

Specifically, we construct a measure of CSP that first takes the net of CSPStrengths and CSPConcerns and introduces a penalty for less genuine CSP investments measured as the interaction of CSPConcerns and PeripheralStrengths. We also include δ as a multiplier for this term that allows us to modulate how large of a penalty less genuine CSP has on a firm’s overall CSP posture. We call this measure Overall CSP Posture and formally define it as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Overall CSP Posture} \\ = \big (C S P S t r e n g t h s - C S P C o n c e r n s \big) \\ \quad - \delta \times (C S P C o n c e r n s \times P e r i p h e r a l S t r e n g t h s). \end{array}
$$

We assess the impact of our measure using our extended sample (described in Online Appendix C) because this analysis affords us additional statistical power, and the results are highly consistent with our matched sample. In conducting this analysis, we also vary the value of our multiplier δ to capture differences in terms of how much of a penalty less genuine CSP investments have on our measurement of firms overall CSP posture. In Table 7, we show results for the effects of CSP when using our revised measure. Here we observe the effect that the conventional wisdom around CSP would suggest in that there is a negative effect of CSP on the probability of breach. We find this result consistently when we use a multiplier that is two or higher (see columns (2)–(4)). This suggestive analysis implies that discounting measures of CSP posture for less genuine investments in CSP may be an important area of further exploration in terms of accurately measuring CSP impacts on firm outcomes.

Additional Robustness Checks. We include several additional robustness checks to address other potential concerns with our analysis. First, we evaluate the robustness of our results to nonlinear estimation approaches (e.g. logit, hazard) and find consistent results. We also replicate our analysis using a larger sample without the matching approaches used in the main analysis. We find consistent and statistically significant support for each of our hypotheses using the extended sample. These results help alleviate the concern that our results are an artifact of our sample construction and may not persist if we include a broader set of firms. We also estimate the Firth logit to address concerns around the rare nature of data breach events and conduct an instrumental variable analysis. Details of these additional robustness checks are in Online Appendix C.

## Discussion and Conclusions

It has been reported that data breaches are now a daily occurrence in the United States (Riepenhoff and Wagner 2014). Considering this alarming statistic, there is little wonder why corporate leaders are concerned and invest heavily in information security infrastructures. What these leaders may not realize is that certain actions they are taking in the social responsibility space may, in fact, be placing a proverbial target on their backs. In this study, we set out to explore whether a link exists between firms’ CSP and their likelihood of experiencing deliberately perpetrated data breaches. Our theorizing presumes that CSP practices affect the behavior and motives of both internal and external stakeholders in ways that impact the risk of data breach. This inquiry was motivated in part by the focus of IS security research on aspects of the IS/IT environment as the sole drivers of information se curity performance as well as management research that points to diverging stakeholder reactions to firms’ seemingly positive CSP. There is also evidence that the hacking community is not homogeneous, and at least some hackers from both internal and external sources appear to be motivated by what they dislike as opposed to solely financial gain. We put forth the theoretical angle that peripheral CSP strengths can be a detriment to information security, particularly when firms have simultaneous high levels of CSP concerns, and we empirically supported this outcome by employing a variety of statistical tests.

We first predicted that firms with more CSP concerns would be more likely to experience a malicious breach. The rationale for this relationship is that both employees and external hackers target firms with weaker CSP records on the grounds that these firms negatively affect their stakeholders and society at large. Surprisingly, our results did not support this prediction. Although there may be several explanations for this finding, a potential explanation comes from our conjecture that the types of individuals who engage in deliberate hacking activity (e.g., disgruntled current and former employees, hacking groups) are likely to have unfavorable views of firms in the first place (i.e., corporations are “evil”). Considering this viewpoint, it may be that these unique stakeholders are so skeptical of business that a poor CSP record only confirms their existing beliefs and thus does not prompt action. That is, CSP concerns provide cognitive consistency (as opposed to creating cognitive dissonance) in terms of how these stakeholders view firms’ relationships with society; hence, CSP concerns do not even “raise their eyebrows” except in cases where firms also promote certain CSP strengths.

On this matter, it appears that these stakeholders scrutinize and react negatively to firms’ promotion of peripheral CSP practices and perhaps view this form of CSP as superficial attempts at social responsibility that are strategically employed for impression management. Returning to our earlier point, it may be that peripheral CSP strengths create cognitive dissonance such that the skeptical stakeholders who are the subject of our inquiry perceive that firms’ espoused good deeds in the areas of community relations and the environment are not commensurate with their expectations for business, thus prompting these stakeholders to be cynical of such activities (i.e., the holier-than-thou argument). Further supporting this assertion is our finding that peripheral CSP strengths are associated with increased risk of breach when firms have higher levels of CSP concerns. In these instances, it appears that employees and external hackers perceive disingenuous intent behind peripheral CSP in that these firm efforts are used to mask poor social performance, which serves to motivate a data breach.

Collectively, our results provide a cautionary tale for organizations with regard to their promotion of CSP as it pertains to information security. Employees and external hackers appear to be able to sniff out CSP activities that attempt to give the appearance of social responsibility without infusing substantive CSP throughout the entire organization. As such, firms need to be wary of excessively promoting CSP activities that are peripheral in nature (e.g., philanthropy, volunteering, recycling programs) because they run the risk of drawing undue scrutiny of those internal and external stakeholders who are key to information security. Even more so, firms need to be wary of promoting peripheral CSP strengths when they have an otherwise poor CSP record. Firms pursuit of legitimacy and image-enhancement gains through promotion of their peripheral CSP strengths could come at the expense of increased information security risk.

Our study has several additional implications and some limitations. First, we contribute to IS security research that has begun to identify a broader set of organizational and environmental factors, beyond just the internal IS/IT environment of the firm, that contribute to data breaches. Our focus on noneconomic drivers of data breaches provides a platform for future theory-building efforts that extend the current criminological (e.g., deterrence theory; Straub 1990, Png et al. 2008) and management perspectives (e.g., institutional theory, organizational learning theory; Kwon and Johnson 2014, Angst et al. 2017) that underlie extant firm-level IS security studies. Further, we contribute to the budding literature on IS security that utilizes secondary data sources to measure constructs of interest and analyzes these data over extended time periods using econometric methods. Such analyses enable a better assessment of the causal factors that contribute to data breaches and complement several existing IS security studies that employ cross-sectional designs with perceptual measures.

Our study also contributes to the literature streams on corporate reputation management and CSP and ties them to the IS domain. Within the corporate reputation literature, it is recognized that institutional pressures place demands on organizations to exhibit greater accountability and transparency. One manifestation of this pressure is the promotion of peripheral CSP practices, which are put forth to convey a positive public impression and gain legitimacy from stakeholders. Although peripheral CSP may be beneficial from a legitimacy perspective, our results point to the negative implications of this strategy for organizational information security. Our results also suggest the role of a new type of activist in the cyber realm—the hacker—that holds firms accountable for their CSP practices. In terms of the literature on CSP, we address scholarly critiques regarding the simplistic measurement of CSP concepts (e.g., Aguinis and Glavas 2012) by isolating CSP into its peripheral form and considering unique outcomes associated with peripheral CSP strengths. Our empirical evidence of the differential effects of peripheral and embedded CSP strengths on the risk of data breach also suggests that current models that attempt to link CSP activities to firm outcomes may be oversimplified, and we provide the information security context to illustrate this point. More broadly, our results align with the scholarly view of CSP as being complex and not something that is inherently good for firms (Wood 1991, Laplume et al. 2008). We also provide a methodological contribution with a unique measure of firms’ overall CSP posture that penalizes firms for greenwashing. In doing so, we advance current understanding of the negative implications of greenwashing to include deliberately perpetrated data breaches. As noted earlier, we believe that our new CSP measure is suited to the unique stakeholders we consider in this study and also has potential applicability beyond the IS security realm and can help explain a wide variety of firm outcomes.

Additionally, this study is one of the first to empirically investigate concepts from the CSP and IS security fields in an integrated manner, which we believe is an area of increasing importance. As firms become more digital and social responsibility efforts continue to garner stakeholder attention, scholars and practitioners need to better understand the relationship between IT infrastructure and CSP. For example, IT solutions could help legitimize a firm’s CSP by providing increased information transparency (perhaps throughout its entire supply chain) and, in so doing, combat the skepticism that many stakeholders have toward CSP.<sup>13</sup> More broadly, leading tech firms have often touted social good as a driving force behind their technology initiatives. For example, Google famously had the motto of “Don’t be evil,” which it subsequently edited to “Do the right thing.” Our study potentially highlights the complicated impacts that these tech company initiatives have on their relationships with relevant internal and external stakeholders. We hope that the current study stimulates future research in these areas and continues to answer the question of why CSP matters to IS.

In terms of limitations of our study, ours is similar to recent firm-level IS security studies in that we relied on public announcements of data breaches, and thus we cannot determine the extent to which firms may have experienced a hacking incident and thwarted it or perhaps failed to disclose a breach. Regarding the failure to disclose, firms presumably wish to hide breaches for fear of negative repercussions; however, most publicly traded firms are subject to a host of regulatory requirements (dating back to the onset of our study period) that mandate public disclosure of any data breach. Hence, we are confident that failure to disclose a breach is not a serious concern in our data set. In terms of hacking incidents that were thwarted, this is not a concern for our study because we are specifically focused on attacks that resulted in a data breach. Nonetheless, obtaining primary attack data would require a contractual agreement with participating firms and thus is extremely rare in IS security research (for exceptions, see Ransbotham and Mitra 2009, Wang et al. 2015). Hence, we are limited to our secondary data source for breach information, which we believe is an appropriate measure. Our breach data are also limited to U.S. firms, and the KLD data are largely based on rated firms’ U.S. subsidiaries rather than their worldwide operations. Hence, our study is U.S.-centric, yet it is possible that internal and external hacking behaviors are culturally distinct, which is an area for future research. We also attempted to validate our conjecture that CSP is a unique motivator of deliberate breaches, beyond the presumed influence of expected financial gains, by parsing the data by firms in industries with strong financial motives for hackers and firms in other industries. However, we recognize that our industry-splitting approach is only a proxy for hackers financial incentives. Future research should test our models with a more direct measure of financial incentives for hackers to better distinguish CSP as a unique driver of data breach risk. Finally, although KLD data are the most widely used in firm-level CSP studies and generally considered the most comprehensive data of their kind, they have their limitations (see Hart and Sharfman 2015). Future research could test our models with alternate CSP data sources and/ or potentially different operationalizations of our CSP variables.

In conclusion, this study was motivated by our desire to better understand why some firms are targets for data breaches beyond the known influence of perpetrators’ expected financial gain. We believe that our findings are significant for research and practice and encourage others to investigate related questions in the hopes of curtailing the rapid growth of unlawful cyberattacks. In particular, we focused on CSP as the driver, but future research could look at whether factors such as values and political or religious beliefs espoused by senior leaders or boards of directors might affect the likelihood of data breaches. The key takeaway from our study is that promotion of peripheral CSP activities can be a detriment to information security particularly if firms espouse such activities while having otherwise poor CSP records. What was once accepted as meaningful CSP activity may no longer appease certain stakeholders. And, in this era of increased information transparency and greater expectations of the firm’s role in society, engaging in only peripheral actions may result in stakeholder backlash. Firms need to be cautious about promoting their CSP activities unless they can defend their actions as embedded in core practices and as authentically motivated.
