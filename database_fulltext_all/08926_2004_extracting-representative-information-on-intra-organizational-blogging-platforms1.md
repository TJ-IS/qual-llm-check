# EXTRACTING REPRESENTATIVE INFORMATION ON INTRA-ORGANIZATIONAL BLOGGING PLATFORMS<sup>1</sup>

Xunhua Guo, Qiang Wei, and Guoqing Chen

Research Center for Contemporary Management, School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084 CHINA {guoxh@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn} {weiq@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn} {chengq@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

Jin Zhang School of Business, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872 CHINA {zhangjin@rbs.ruc.edu.cn}

Dandan Qiao Research Center for Contemporary Management, School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084 CHINA {qiaodd.12@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

Content generated on intra-organizational blogging platforms may help managers understand the emerging ideas, issues, and opportunities of their companies, whereas the difficulty is how to go beyond the overload of information and obtain an overall view. This paper proposes a system framework for extracting representative information from intra-organizational blogging platforms, as well as the REPSET (REPresentative SET) method, which serves as the core component of the extraction system. Drawing from a novel clustering technique, REPSET is designed to identify a small set of items that largely represent the diversified content of a huge information base. Building on REPSET, an extraction system enables managers to locate representative articles that may serve as starting points for comprehensively understanding the hot topics, prevailing thoughts, and emerging opinions among employees. Empirical evaluations are conducted based on the massive database accumulated on an internal blogging platform at a large telecommunications company. The results from data experiments and user evaluations demonstrate that REPSET and the extraction system upon which it is based can provide outstanding performance, in comparison with benchmark methods.

Keywords: Intra-organizational blogging platforms, representative information extraction, enterprise social media, business intelligence, design science

## Introduction

As one of the typical social media applications, blogs have been prevailing and serving as a substantial source of information production on the Internet since the beginning of this century (Nardi et al. 2004). Recently, blogs have also been introduced into corporate contexts along with other social media technologies such as wikis and group-messaging, creating a new form of collaborative environment (Bente and Karla 2009; Lu et al. 2015; McAfee 2006). It is widely believed that enterprise blogging platforms would help build a flexible intra-organizational networking stage that may effectively facilitate knowledge sharing and support emergent innovations (Li et al. 2015; McAfee 2006; Suh et al. 2011).

Many companies are investing heavily in internal blogging platforms and encouraging their employees to maintain blogs (Chui et al. 2012; Liao et al. 2011; Luo et al. 2015).

When employees have accepted an internal blogging platform and are regularly posting their thoughts and writings on it, information accumulated on such a crowd-based platform is potentially useful for managers to support their managerial work. Existing research has revealed that blog posts often reflect real feelings of the authors (Nardi et al. 2004) and can be considered as a reliable source of information (Aggarwal et al. 2012). By reading internal blogs, decision makers will be able to leverage the shared knowledge originally distributed across the organization (Li et al. 2015; Lu et al. 2015) and react to emerging business opportunities or changing organizational conditions in a more timely fashion.

The difficulty, however, lies in the huge amount of data generated on such a platform. An internal blogging system typically produces thousands of posts every week. It is almost impossible for a decision maker to read or even browse all of the articles. The explosion of content creates a challenge that, while access to knowledge and information is facilitated, causes quality signals to become so faint and diffused that cognitive overload occurs (Bawden and Robinson 2009). In other words, one critical obstacle to the gold mine of valuable blog content is how to look beyond the overload of information and obtain a clear view of the diversified content on the platform. Specifically, to exploit the potential value of internal blogging platforms, decision makers face a fundamental question: Which articles will they read, given the large amount of articles generated every day, in order to better understand the prevailing thoughts and emerging opinions among employees?

The information needs of managers in organizational contexts are notably different from those in public blogging contexts. On public blogs, users are usually concerned about the content that fits with their own interest, as well as widely discussed topics (e.g., top rated or most commented). In contrast, in organizational contexts, to facilitate the managerial use of blog content, managers would prefer to be more aware of diverse voices as well as emerging ideas and opinions that might potentially be meaningful to the organization, no matter how popular they are or whether they fit with the managers’ personal interest. In this sense, the quality of representativeness of the extracted information is critical.

Literally, representativeness can be defined as the degree to which a small set of items reflect the diverse content of a large information base. A simple example for the need of representativeness is that an employee posts an article that implicitly conveys a novel idea for improving customer service, but few other people read and discuss the idea. In this case, the article will not be recommended by a popularitybased system. It will also not be recommended to a manager by a preference-based system if it does not fit with the manager’s pre-stored preferences. However, the idea might prove to be innovative if it is extracted and exposed to the manager. Another example is that an employee posts an article expressing some negative feelings. Using popularity-based mechanisms, the article will not be recommended to the manager unless it is widely discussed, but the manager may find it useful to notice the existence of such articles at an early stage. In other words, suppose that there is a box containing 100 balls, among which 99 are red (common articles) and 1 is green (the special article). Now we want to pick out 10 balls from the box to represent the population in the box. A random sampling or a popularity-based extraction is likely to result in 10 red balls. However, if a method can pick out 9 red balls and 1 green ball, this result can be deemed as more “representative” because it informs us that there are green balls existing as minority in the box.

Since existing methods are mostly designed for mining public blogs, they usually focus on popularity and personalization, whereas representativeness is rarely considered. For intraorganizational contexts, it is meaningful to develop a novel approach that is centered on representativeness. Therefore, the objective of this study is to propose a system framework capable of extracting a small number of articles that can largely represent the diversified content generated on an organizational blogging platform. To achieve this goal, we develop REPSET (REPresentative SET), a business intelligence (BI) method serving as the core component of the extraction system. Featuring a novel clustering technique, REPSET is designed for effectively identifying a small set of items, which can represent the diversified content of a large information base. To validate the effectiveness of REPSET, empirical evaluations were conducted based on the massive database accumulated on an internal blogging platform at a large telecommunications company. An illustrative example is provided depicting how our proposed method can facilitate enhanced diversity in coverage, resulting in more representative results. Results from data experiments and user evaluations demonstrate that REPSET and the extraction system based on it can provide outstanding performance, in comparison with benchmark methods.

From the perspective of design science research (Goes 2014; Gregor and Hevner 2013; Hevner et al. 2004), our work can be seen as positioned in the “exaptation” quadrant of the design science knowledge contribution framework (Gregor and Hevner 2013), as it focuses on the relatively new problem of representative information extraction on internal blogs and develops REPSET as an exaptation of related BI methods in response to the particular challenges of that problem. Therefore, this study aims to make contributions in the form of design artifacts including the REPSET method as well as the formalization and instantiation of the representative information extraction framework. Based on this motivation, this work has been conducted in line with the design science methodological principles (Hevner et al. 2004).

## Literature Review

In the BI literature, there are efforts to develop reliable methods for monitoring and analyzing online content. For instance, research on online opinion mining aims to determine the attitude of the public toward news, products, or services (Turney and Littman 2003; Wright 2009). Methods have been developed for collecting and analyzing social network content such as blogs (Chau and Xu 2012), extracting small sentiments from a relatively large message body (Turney and Littman 2003), and discovering relationships between information items (Ku et al. 2009). Meanwhile, models and systems have been built based on various techniques to achieve diverse managerial goals, such as classifying and summarizing product reviews (Hu and Liu 2004; Zhang et al. 2016), predicting sales performance (Liu et al. 2007), estimating the effectiveness of communication (Abbasi and Chen 2008), and providing decision support for business mergers and acquisitions (Lau et al. 2012). Underlying most of the research on online content mining, the most widely addressed BI techniques are topic clustering (Pons-Porrata et al. 2007; Zhao et al. 2005), which is the unsupervised classification of a given document set into clusters such that documents within each cluster are more similar between each other than those in different clusters, and text summarization (Das and Martins 2007; Gupta and Lehal 2010), which aims to condense the source text into a shorter version while preserving its information content and overall meaning.

Previous research on topic clustering is usually based on partitional methods, such as K-Means (Jain et al. 1999) and graph-partition techniques (Han et al. 1998), density-based methods (Ester et al. 1996), or hierarchical methods (Berkhin 2006). Among them, the partitional and density-based methods have a linear time complexity, which is very efficient. However, such methods may produce inferior clusters in some cases and the number of clusters is required to be predetermined, which is usually difficult in practice, as well as in the context of this study. Locality sensitive hashing (LHS) is another technique related to partitional methods (Gionis et al. 1999; Indyk and Motwani 1998). LHS maps similar data objects to the same buckets (Leskovec et al. 2014), so it can be applied to partition data objects. However, like other partitional clustering methods, the number of hash tables needs to be predetermined, which may restrict its application in the research scenario of this study. Compared to partitional methods, the complexity of hierarchical clustering methods is higher. Meanwhile, in hierarchical methods, if a data object is assigned to a wrong cluster at a stage, it will never be reallocated in the following clustering process, missing the possible opportunity to further refine the clustering results.

Furthermore, topic clustering in online content analysis usually focuses on finding the most common and dominant opinions, which is different from our emphasis on representativeness. For instance, K-Means clustering, the most commonly used partitional method, suffers from at least three well-known issues: (1) it tends to generate spatially equidistant clusters; (2) it fails to effectively consider different data densities; and (3) it fails to consider data sets with nonglobular structures effectively (Han et al. 2011). While certain methods fare better on some of these issues (for instance, DBScan is better for the density problem and nearest neighbor-based methods have alleviated the non-globular problem), no existing method effectively addresses all three of these issues. Considering that the issues of different sizes and densities are even more crucial and common in the context of representative information extraction, the deficiencies of such methods may be amplified with the emergence of novel ideas and opinions that are still not popular and of small sizes and densities.

Another type of techniques related to topic clustering is topic modeling, such as LDA (Blei et al. 2003), hLDA (Blei et al. 2004), and DTM (Blei and Lafferty 2006). These techniques typically decompose documents into topic terms, so that every document is associated to a topic with a certain probability. The probability that a document belongs to a topic is higher when the document contains more discussion on that topic (Blei 2012). Therefore, for each topic, the document with the largest probability may be selected as the representative item. These methods offer the advantage that the relationship between a document and the topic that it represents can be effectively explained. However, topic modeling methods generally focus on the frequencies of terms. Therefore, they tend to obtain topics based on frequently used terms while the “long tail” terms are likely to be ignored.

Additionally, text summarization methods may also be considered as a foundation for representative information extraction to some extent. In the existing literature, two types of text summarization techniques have been well developed, namely extractive methods and abstractive methods (Aliguliyev 2009; Cai and Li 2011). Between them, extractive methods are more well developed. The disadvantage of such methods, however, is that the extracted text summaries often lead to problems in overall coherence and are very difficult for users to understand in practice, as has been repeatedly pointed out by the existing literature (Gupta and Lehal 2010). In contrast, abstractive summarization relies on advanced language generation techniques to produce grammatical summaries, but the quality of the summaries heavily depends on sophisticated language processing tools that are not always adaptable in various contexts, making abstractive methods less usable and less studied than extractive methods (Cai and Li 2011). Furthermore, with both types of summarization methods, when the summaries are extracted, the integrity of every single article cannot be maintained and authorship information usually cannot be included. Consequently, it is difficult for readers to locate the sources of particular sentiments or to find a typical individual article that contains the sentiments.

Therefore, although text summarization can be used to obtain good information coverage in the extraction of representative content, it is still far from perfect because it suffers from the above-mentioned disadvantageous attributes. In this sense, we regard it as a worthwhile attempt to develop a novel method to achieve representative information extraction through a different path. In this paper, we focus on the method for extracting representative items. This new method is not intended to replace sentiment analysis or text summarization. Instead, we believe that the efforts on text summarization and representative set extraction can develop in parallel, from different perspectives.

## Design Framework

Our vision for the representative information extraction framework was initially shaped through our on-site observation on the use of two internal blogging systems in practice. Subsequently, we conducted a series of field interviews to refine the framework as well as to derive the specific design requirements. Prior design science studies have used interviews to understand artifact use cases, strengths and weaknesses, and potential areas for improvement (Adomavicius et al. 2008; McLaren et al. 2011). From 2010 to 2012, we investigated a large Chinese telecommunications company (X), a multinational software company (M), and a collaboration software vendor (Y). Both X and M had deployed and were using the internal blogging systems actively. Company Y was a vendor of internal blogging systems and had implemented their products in more than 10 customer companies. We visited companies X (four times) and M (one time) and observed the use of the internal blogging systems on-site. Our interviewees included five persons from X (CEO, one department head, two team managers, and one engineer), one person from M (department head), and two persons from Y (CEO and engineer). In the interviews, the envisioned framework was conveyed to the interviewees. All of the interviewees replied with a positive confirmation that such a representative information extraction system was desirable. We then asked them to describe what features they might expect for such extraction systems. Based on their responses, we developed the design requirements for the extraction method.

The overall design framework is for a system that can extract representative information from corporate internal blogging platforms using a business intelligence approach. As a highly conceptualized illustration, the framework is shown in Figure 1. It highlights the relationship between the corporate internal blogging platform and the representative information extraction system, of which the central component is the extraction engine. In the following, we will discuss the blogging platform and the extraction engine respectively, and then elaborate on the technical requirements for the extraction method implemented in the engine.

## The Enterprise Blogging Platform

The extraction system is designed as an add-on to the corporate internal blogging platform. Generally, blogs are frequently modified web pages, in which dated entries (articles) are listed in reverse chronological sequences (Jackson et al. 2007). Readers are usually allowed to post comments to specific articles in a blog. An article may contain both text and graphical elements, and even multimedia information such as video and music. In this paper, we focus on the text content of blog articles.

The blogging platform usually provides some predefined mechanisms for organizing the blogs and articles in a structured way. Typically, blogs are categorized according to the organizational structure. Additionally, a set of article categories (or tags) are defined. The author of a blog article can choose to attach the article to particular categories. Such structures may help readers browse the contents on the platform. Moreover, on some blogging platforms, readers are allowed to rate the articles that they have read. Based on reader ratings, top rated articles will be recommended to a new reader, in case that he/she cannot decide which articles to read first. Platform administrators may also rate the articles and mark some of them as “recommended” based on their own judgment. All of these mechanisms are designed in order to help readers more easily seize the essential articles that deserve reading. Although there are auxiliary mechanisms such as category structures, reader ratings, and administrator recommendations, it remains difficult for readers, especially senior managers who do not have much time to spend on browsing the blogging platform, to quickly and accurately find out the most information-rich items. In this regard, the extraction system should be of important help.

## The Representativeness Extraction Engine

The core component of an extraction system is the extraction engine, which fetches data from the blogging platform, analyzes the content of blog articles together with platform usage records (including records about user browsing, reading, and commenting), and outputs representative articles as results. The representative articles will be presented to users who are concerned with grasping the overall situation of the organization. Herein, representative articles are defined as a small set of blog articles, which, to a large extent, cover the content of all the blog articles posted on the blogging platform within a specific time period.

Representative articles extracted by the system are comparable to the top-rated articles generated by the user rating mechanism, considering the fact that they are both small sets of articles recommended to readers to help them select what to read. The difference, however, is that representative articles are selected with the aim of representing the whole population of blog articles posted within a specific time period, while top-rated articles are simply the posts most favored by general readers. From the perspective of a high level decision maker, apart from knowing what is most favored in the organization, it is at least equivalently important to capture an overview on the whole platform.

## Requirements for the Extraction Method

As described above, the principal requirements for the extraction engine were derived by studying two blogging systems in practice and interviewing typical users. In the interviews, the interviewees were asked to describe the features that they would expect for such extraction systems. In light of the responses, the three most desirable features turned out to be

(1) From the perspective of functionality, the extracted articles should be indeed representative, which means that they should cover the content of the targeted article population to a large extent.

(2) From the perspective of usability, users would like to freely specify the number of representative articles to display and the results should be presented in real time. For example, a user usually prefers to read 10 representative articles every day, but occasionally he/she may choose to read 20 or more articles when he/she has more time. Similarly, user A may prefer to read 10 articles every time but user B may prefer to read 30. Therefore, the system should allow the users to select the number of articles in real time according to their needs, rather than fixing it to a predetermined value.

(3) From the perspective of information granularity, users would like to “drill down” on representative articles (i.e., after reading a presentative article, they may want to read some more articles that are representationally related) and such queries should also be responded to in real time.

Based on these design requirements, we propose a BI method called REPSET (REPresentative SET), which incorporates a novel document clustering technique. Evaluated with multiple measures about representativeness, REPSET is proved to be an effective method supporting the extraction system. In the following sections, we will describe the REPSET method and present the empirical evaluations.

## The REPSET Method

According to the definition of representativeness, the target of representative article extraction is to find a small set of articles that, to a large extent, reflect the diverse content of the blogging system. Generally, this target can be achieved with two major steps: (1) categorize all of articles based on their similarities of content between each other; and (2) select a good representative article for each category. If the categorization of articles and the selection of representative items are more accurate, the degree of representativeness of the resulting set will be higher. Accordingly, the overall procedure of the REPSET method is shown in Figure 2. As illustrated, in the first step, the degrees of similarity between documents are calculated. That is, given n documents, an n × n similarity matrix would be constructed. Next, a clustering algorithm is utilized to cluster the documents into a dendrogram with different granularities of clusters. Finally, for all given levels in the document dendrogram, a representative document is selected from each cluster according to the degrees of similarity. From the document dendrogram, the manager could obtain various levels of clustering results and representative articles according to the need, instead of determining the cluster number k in advance.

The following three subsections will introduce the three components of REPSET in detail, respectively.

## Similarity Matrix Construction

The similarity between two documents can be calculated using the cosine-similarity measure (Salton 1971), which is widely used in web/text mining. Using Lucene, a commonly used word-splitting tool, each document can be converted into a set of words. With the standard treatment in the TF/IDF model (Salton 1971), a feature word list can be derived, based on which the corresponding keyword vector space can be generated. Each document d then can be mapped as a keyword vector denoted as $[ \omega _ { 1 } , \omega _ { 2 } , . . . , \omega _ { \mathrm { p } } ]$ with the TF/IDF model. An alternative way is to use “shingles” (contiguous subsequences of tokens), instead of keywords, to denote a document (Broder et al. 1997). In our study, keyword vectors are adopted to reduce computational complexity. Thereafter, the cosine-similarity between every two documents can be calculated based on their corresponding keyword vectors. Specifically, the cosine-similarity between any two documents $d _ { \mathrm { 1 } }$ and d $( d _ { 1 } = [ \omega _ { 1 1 } , \omega _ { 2 1 } , . . . , \omega _ { \mathrm { p l } } ]$ and $d _ { 2 } = [ \omega _ { 1 2 } , \omega _ { 2 2 } , . . . ,$ $\omega _ { \mathrm { p } 2 } ] )$ in a p-dimensional vector space can be calculated with the following Sim measure:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \operatorname{Sim} (d _ {1}, d _ {2}) & = \cos (\theta \text { between   vectors } d _ {1} \text { and } d _ {2}) \\ & = \frac {d _ {1} \bullet d _ {2}}{\| d _ {1} \| \times \| d _ {2} \|} \\ & = \frac {\omega_ {1 1} \times \omega_ {1 2} + \omega_ {2 1} \times \omega_ {2 2} + \dots + \omega_ {p 1} \times \omega_ {p 2}}{\sqrt {\omega_ {1 1} ^ {2} + \omega_ {2 1} ^ {2} + \dots + \omega_ {p 1} ^ {2}} \times \sqrt {\omega_ {1 2} ^ {2} + \omega_ {2 2} ^ {2} + \dots + \omega_ {p 2} ^ {2}}} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Given a set D with n documents, an n × n similarity matrix M can be calculated with Equation (1). In M, the element at row i and column j represents the similarity between the documents i and j.

## Clustering

Based on the three desirable features for the extraction engine, we derived three principal requirements for the clustering algorithm: (1) the quality (accuracy) of clustering results should be high, because this is the basis for high quality of representative extraction; (2) the number of clusters should not be arbitrarily determined in advance (it should be able to be flexibly adjusted when wanted, without having to restart the whole procedure of clustering), so that the system can respond immediately once the user has selected a different number of representative articles; and (3) there should be a multilevel structure in the resulting clusters, so that the navigation features such as drill down for representative articles can be facilitated.

Based on examining existing algorithms that are commonly used for clustering analysis, we found that they cannot sufficiently satisfy these three requirements simultaneously. In the partitional methods, the number of clusters needs to be arbitrarily set before the clustering algorithm is executed. In other words, one execution of the partitional algorithm can only result in one fixed and pre-given number of clusters. Meanwhile, partitional methods are not applicable for varying sizes, densities, and structures. In the density-based methods, the minimum numbers of points and radius need to be configured in advance, which indirectly fixes the number of clusters. In addition, both partitional clustering and density-based clustering typically create one-level partitioning of the data objects at once and cannot provide various levels of representative information. In contrast, the hierarchical clustering can meet both requirements (2) and (3). However, the accuracy of hierarchical clustering is relatively low (Steinbach et al. 2000). Therefore, in REPSET, a novel method is proposed based on hierarchical clustering in order to retain the two desirable features. Additionally, a heuristic strategy, namely the backward strategy for documents near boundaries, is incorporated to improve the quality of clustering. The detailed procedure of the clustering algorithm is as shown in Figure 3.

Given a set D with n documents, in the proposed clustering algorithm, each document is initially considered to be a single cluster. Therefore, at the first stage, there are n clusters, denoted as $C ^ { 1 } { } _ { 1 } , C ^ { 1 } { } _ { 2 } , . . . C ^ { 1 } { } _ { n } .$ Let $C _ { j } ^ { k }$ denote the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ cluster in the $k ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ stage in the clustering process of REPSET. At each stage, the average similarity of each pair of clusters $C _ { m } ^ { k } , C _ { n } ^ { k }$ is calculated as follows:

$$
\operatorname{Sim} \left(C _ {m} ^ {k}, C _ {n} ^ {k}\right) = \left(1 / n _ {m} ^ {k} n _ {n} ^ {k}\right) \sum_ {d _ {x} \in C _ {m} ^ {k}} \left(\sum_ {d _ {y} \in C _ {n} ^ {k}} \operatorname{Sim} \left(d _ {x}, d _ {y}\right)\right)\tag{2}
$$

In Equation (2), $n _ { ~ m } ^ { k }$ and $n _ { ~ n } ^ { k }$ represent the number of documents in clusters $C _ { ~ m } ^ { k }$ and $C _ { n } ^ { k } ,$ respectively. At stage k, two clusters with the maximum average similarity, $C _ { p } ^ { k }$ and $C _ { \ q } ^ { k } ,$ will be merged into a new cluster, $C _ { p q } ^ { k } ,$ which is demonstrated as Equation (3).

$$
\operatorname{Sim} \left(C _ {p} ^ {k}, C _ {q} ^ {k}\right) = \max _ {C _ {m} ^ {k}, C _ {n} ^ {k} \in \left\{C _ {1} ^ {k}, C _ {2} ^ {k},.., C _ {n - k + 1} ^ {k} \right\}} \left(\operatorname{Sim} \left(C _ {m} ^ {k}, C _ {n} ^ {k}\right)\right)\tag{3}
$$

Furthermore, given a threshold $\lambda ( 0 \leq \lambda \leq 1 )$ , documents with average in-cluster similarities less than λ (near boundaries) will be marked as boundary objects (to be considered to reallocate). Specifically, in the new cluster $C _ { p q } ^ { k } ,$ documents meeting the following condition will be marked as $d _ { b o u n d a r y } .$

$$
\frac {1}{n _ {p q} ^ {k}} \sum_ {d _ {x} \in C _ {p q} ^ {k}} S i m \big (d _ {b o u n d a r y}, d _ {x} \big) <   \lambda , \lambda \in [ 0, 1 ]\tag{4}
$$

where $n _ { ~ p q } ^ { k }$ represents the number of documents in the cluster $C _ { p q } ^ { k } .$ Notably, the value of the parameter λ may affect the accuracy of the clustering algorithms. Before the extraction of representative information, the value of λ can be tuned in light of the following measure:

$$
C S = \frac {\sum_ {p = 1} ^ {k} \left\{\frac {1}{| C _ {p} |} \sum_ {d _ {i} \in C _ {p}} \left\{S i m (d _ {i} , O _ {p}) \right\} \right\}}{\sum_ {p = 1} ^ {k} \left\{\max _ {q = 1 , 2 , \dots , k , p \neq q} \left\{S i m (O _ {p} , O _ {q}) \right\} \right\}}\tag{5}
$$

In the equation, the numerator represents the aggregate incluster similarity after clustering. $C _ { p }$ denotes a cluster and $O _ { p }$ is the centroid document of $C _ { p } .$ The denominator represents the aggregate between-clusters similarity after clustering, where $O _ { p }$ and $O _ { q }$ denote the centroid documents of two clusters, respectively. The clustering result is better when incluster similarities are high and between-clusters similarities are low. In the clustering process, given a certain λ value, boundary documents can be determined and reallocated when necessary. After the reallocation procedure, the CS value is calculated accordingly. In this sense, the value of λ will influence the clustering result and therefore have impacts on the final CS value. A higher CS value indicates a more appropriate value of λ. In other words, the proper input parameter of λ in REPSET can be determined by seeking for the maximized CS value. Since the expression of CS does not explicitly include λ, a commonly used parameter-tuning technique called stepwise self-tuning (Zelnik-Manor and Perona 2004) is adopted to achieve this goal. This technique has been widely applied in parameter tuning for various studies, such as cluster numbers and other related parameters in clustering (Gu and Zhou 2009; Mandala et al. 2014; Seref et al. 2013), topic numbers in LDA (Blei et al. 2003; Griffiths and Steyvers 2004), confidence and support degrees in association-rule classification (Chen et al. 2006; Coenen and Leng 2007), normalization parameters in information retrieval (He and Ounis 2003), and regularization parameters in logistic regression (Simon et al. 2011).

With stepwise self-tuning, values of λ with equal intervals are applied on a training dataset that is randomly sampled from the data set D. Typically, with a step length of 0.1, the value of λ can be set as $\{ 0 . 1 , 0 . 2 , 0 . 3 , . . . , 0 . 9 \}$ , respectively. Notably, in the clustering process of REPSET, CS values can be calculated at every level of the dendrogram. However, because the sample data set for tuning λ is usually much smaller than the whole data set D, it is not feasible to tune λ for every level independently. Therefore, a unified value of λ will be used for all levels of the dendrogram. In the tuning process, for each given value of λ, the clustering algorithm is run on the sample data set, during which CS values are recorded at all levels in the dendrogram. Subsequently, the average value of CS is calculated as the indicator for choosing λ. The λ value that results in the highest average CS value is chosen as the final input parameter for REPSET to perform the clustering in the dataset D.

The marked boundary documents will be reevaluated, and reallocated to some new cluster, in which the document will gain the highest average similarity, if necessary. This is called a backward strategy. Specifically, at stage k, there are $( n - k + 1 )$ clusters, denoted as $C ^ { k } { } _ { 1 } , C ^ { k } { } _ { 2 } , . . . , C ^ { k } { } _ { ( n - k + 1 ) } .$ . For the marked documents $d _ { b o u n d a r y } ,$ REPSET first calculates the average similarities between $d _ { b o u n d a r y }$ and the $( n \mathrm { ~ - ~ } k + 1 )$ clusters, which is shown as Equation (6).

$$
\operatorname{Sim} \left(d _ {\text { boundary }}, C _ {j} ^ {k}\right) = \frac {1}{n _ {j} ^ {k}} \sum_ {d _ {x} \in C _ {j} ^ {k}} \operatorname{Sim} \left(d _ {\text { boundary }}, d _ {x}\right)\tag{6}
$$

If the average similarity between $d _ { b o u n d a r ) }$ and $C _ { j } ^ { k }$ is the largest among all the clusters, then $d _ { b o u n d a r y }$ is assigned to the cluster $C _ { j } ^ { k } .$

In each iteration of updating clustering, two clusters with the largest similarity between each other is merged into one, so the number of clusters decreases by 1. Therefore, in stage k, the documents in D can be clustered into $( n - k + 1 )$ sets of similar documents. The $( n - k + 1 ) ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ level of the document dendrogram is constructed by these sets, in which the documents near boundaries have been appropriately processed. After n iterations of the proposed algorithm, the clusters will converge to 1 and the whole dendrogram will have been well constructed. Every level of this dendrogram will consist of document clusters with good clustering quality.

The backward strategy in REPSET is designed to overcome the disadvantage of traditional hierarchical clustering methods that the clusters cannot be dynamically adjusted during the construction process of the dendrogram. This heuristic strategy aims to improve the clustering quality by assigning boundary objects to more appropriate clusters, based on the rationale that the boundary objects in a newly merged cluster are likely to belong to another cluster. Specifically, in the traditional hierarchical clustering process, in each iteration, two items or clusters with the highest similarities will be merged. Consider the example in Figure 4, where each circle denotes an article. From the overall perspective, the desired clustering result should put all white circles into one cluster and all black ones to another. However, in the step-wise dendrogram, because the distance between $d _ { i }$ and $d _ { j }$ is very close (high similarity), these two articles will first be merged into one cluster and will never be separated in the subsequent procedures. In contrast, the backward strategy in REPSET can effectively solve this problem. Suppose that $d _ { i }$ and $d _ { j }$ have been merged in ${ \mathrm { C l u s t e r } } _ { i j } ,$ and this cluster is then merged with the other black articles forming ${ \mathrm { C l u s t e r } } _ { b l a c k } ,$ while the other white articles are clustered as $\mathrm { C l u s t e r } _ { w h i t e } .$ In this case, for each document in $\mathrm { C l u s t e r } _ { b l a c k } ,$ REPSET will calculate the average similarity between it and the other documents, and then accordingly mark out the boundary articles in this cluster. In the example, article $d _ { i }$ will turn out to have a low average similarity. Therefore, REPSET will recalculate the average similarities between $d _ { i }$ and all existing clusters. Consequently, REPSET will find that $d _ { i }$ has the largest average similarity to the $\mathrm { C l u s t e r } _ { w h i t e } ,$ which means $d _ { i }$ is more likely to be an article in ${ \mathrm { C l u s t e r } } _ { w h i t e } .$ Therefore, $d _ { i }$ is reallocated into ${ \mathrm { C l u s t e r } } _ { w h i t e } .$ Thus, the clustering accuracy will be improved. $\mathbf { A }$ more detailed illustrative example is provided in Appendix A.

As compared with traditional hierarchical clustering methods such as agglomerative clustering, the novelty of REPSET clustering lies in the heuristic design of the backward strategy that significantly improves the clustering accuracy. Therefore, it will be capable of overcoming the major weakness of hierarchical clustering algorithms and obtain higher accuracy. Meanwhile, although the computation time is a weakness of hierarchical methods, as well as REPSET, this is not a big issue in our context, since the clustering procedure only needs to run one time on a daily basis and it can be scheduled as an automatic task that runs at night. Particularly, as REPSET allows real time flexible query and there is no need to re-do the clustering when a new number of representative articles is set, the computation time issue is even more marginal for this method.

Furthermore, the method is able to capture more diversified content and, consequently, innovative ideas and emerging opinions are likely to be included in the extracted item set. Considering an extreme case of an innovative idea that is new and rarely discussed, it will have very low similarity levels with other articles. Therefore, in the clustering procedure of REPSET, this item is not likely to be merged with other items or clusters, because of its low similarities with others. In other words, this innovative idea will be maintained independent of other items or clusters all through the clustering process and eventually become an individual cluster. Subsequently, when selecting representative items, since this innovative idea is the only one item in its cluster, it will be certainly extracted.

## Representative Document Selection

Given a set of documents, the document with the highest average similarity to the other documents will offer the highest content coverage in the set (Liu and Croft 2008). Therefore, such a document can be selected as the representative in a cluster.

Particularly, the clustering process of REPSET can provide the whole dendrogram after one time running and every level of this dendrogram contains document clusters with good clustering quality. At each level of the dendrogram, REPSET can extract the corresponding representative documents in the clusters, forming the representative subset of documents at this level. Therefore, after running one time, REPSET can provide representative subsets at all levels of the dendrogram, guaranteeing real time flexible queries.

Lines 7–18 correspond to the component of the clustering procedure. Lines 19–25 are the component of representative document selection.

The overall time complexity of REPSET is low-level polynomial. At the first stage (initialization and similarity matrix construction), the main computational consumption is the calculation of the similarity matrix, which is of ${ \mathrm { O } } ( n ^ { 2 } )$ time complexity. At the second stage (clustering), suppose that the average scale of boundary documents in newly generated clusters is $n _ { a v g } ,$ under the best-case situation, where there is no document to be reallocated throughout the process, the clustering procedure degenerates into a typical hierarchical clustering method that is of O(n<sup>2</sup>logn) time complexity (Liu et al. 2007). Under the worst-case situation, where every boundary document in the newly merged cluster satisfies the backward strategy condition in each step of the procedure, the time complexity of the clustering procedure will be $\mathrm { O } ( n ^ { 3 } n _ { a \nu g } \mathrm { l o g } n )$ At the third stage (representative document selection), the time complexity is ${ \mathrm { O } } ( n ^ { 2 } )$ . Therefore, the overall time complexity of REPSET is between O(n<sup>2</sup>logn) (bestcase) and $\mathrm { O } ( n ^ { 3 } n _ { a \nu g } \mathrm { l o g } n )$ (worst-case). Since $n _ { a v g }$ is less than n, it can be concluded for simplicity that the average-case time complexity of REPSET is between O(n<sup>2</sup>logn) and O(n<sup>4</sup>logn).

## Benchmark Data Evaluation

In order to evaluate the performance of the clustering algorithm in REPSET, we compare it with 10 widely used clustering algorithms, including four K-Means type methods (RBR, DIRECT, Simple K-Means, and X-Means) (Marmanis and Babenko 2009; Pelleg and Moore 2000; Zhao et al. 2005), four variances of the agglomerative method with different intercluster similarity calculation methods (Agglo(group), Agglo(max), Agglo(min), and Agglo(centroids)) (Zhao et al. 2005), a graph partitioning method (GRAPH) (Zhao et al. 2005), and a density-based method (DBscan) (Ester et al. 1996), with respect to two major measures (i.e., entropy and purity) (Bezdek and Pal 1998; Liu 2007), on some benchmark datasets. Moreover, to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed backward strategy in clustering, REPSET with no backward strategy is also considered in the comparative experiments. The 10 algorithms cover the main categories with respect to the mainstream clustering methods (Liu 2007). Among them, RBR, DIRECT, Simple K-Means and Agglo are deemed effective document clustering algorithms (Steinbach et al. 2000) and are adopted by the well-known CLUTO clustering platform (Zhao et al. 2005). Graph is a typical graph clustering algorithm and DBscan is designed for discovery of clusters with arbitrary shape and good efficiency on large databases (Ester et al. 1996). On this basis, these 10 algorithms are suitable for the comparative experiment.

The entropy measure is based on information-theoretic considerations, which represents the difference of information entropy between the clustering by an algorithm and the actually true clustering, the smaller the better. In particular, assume that the given testing dataset D is composed of clusters $C ^ { + } { } _ { 1 } , C ^ { + } { } _ { 2 } , . . . , C ^ { + } { } _ { k }$ <sub>+</sub> (true clustering), and a clustering algorithm is applied to find clusters $C _ { 1 } , C _ { 2 } , . . . , C _ { k }$ in the dataset $D ,$ then the entropy measure can be defined as Equation (7).

$$
E n t r o p y = \sum_ {p = 1} ^ {k} \frac {\left| C _ {p} \right|}{n} \left(- \frac {1}{\log \left(k ^ {+}\right)} \sum_ {p ^ {+} = 1} ^ {k ^ {+}} \frac {\left| C _ {p} \cap C _ {p ^ {+}} ^ {+} \right|}{C _ {p}} \log \left(\frac {\left| C _ {p} \cap C _ {p ^ {+}} ^ {+} \right|}{C _ {p}}\right)\right)\tag{7}
$$

In Equation $( 7 ) ,$ , n represents the scale of the dataset $D .$ . The signal $\displaystyle { \hat { \mathbf { \Omega } } } ^ { \langle c | } \mid ^ { , } \mid ^ { , } \rangle $ means the size of the corresponding set. For example, $| C _ { p } |$ represents the size of $C _ { p } .$

The other measure for evaluating the performance of clustering algorithms is purity, which is based on counting the pairs of data points n, with which two clustering results agree/ disagree. The purity measure represents the degree that all clusters contain true documents, the larger the better. Specifically, given a testing dataset $D ,$ the true clusters $C _ { \ 1 } ^ { + } ,$ $C _ { 2 } ^ { + } , . . . , C _ { k + } ^ { + }$ , and the clustering results $C _ { 1 } , C _ { 2 } , . . . , C _ { k } ,$ the purity measure is defined as Equation (8).

$$
P u r i t y = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {p = 1} ^ {k} \max _ {p ^ {+} = 1, 2, \dots , k ^ {+}} \left| C _ {p} \cap C _ {p ^ {+}} ^ {+} \right|\tag{8}
$$

The metrics of entropy and purity evaluate the consistency between the clusters generated by an algorithm and the actual cluster labels assigned by experts, which are widely used in the evaluation of clustering (Bezdek and Pal 1998; Liu 2007).

In order to conduct a reliable comparison, 30 data sets are collected from multiple benchmark databases specifically for clustering and text mining algorithms (Boley et al. 1999; Chen et al. 2006; Han et al. 1998; Moore et al. 1997; Wu et al. 2010), including 15 data sets from the UCI Machine Learning Repository and 15 from the test-bed of the CLUTO Data Clustering Software. The data sets are randomly chosen from the databases, covering various kinds of data types and attributes. For RBR, DIRECT, AGGLO (all variances), and GRAPH, the CLUTO software package (Zhao et al. 2005) is adopted. The programs for DBscan and K-Means are provided by a commonly used text book (Marmanis and Babenko 2009). The program for X-Means is adapted from the original codes provided by the authors of the algorithm (Pelleg and Moore 2000). For agglomerative clustering, four types of intercluster similarity (group average, min, max, and distance between centroids) are used.

For each given data set, the target number of clusters is set to the ground truth cluster number in the data set. Formally, for a data set $D _ { \mathrm { i } } ,$ there are $k _ { \mathrm { i } }$ clusters indicated by the label field. In the experiment, for the three K-Means type methods (RBR, DIRECT, and Simple K-Means) and the graph partitioning method (graph), the parameter k is directly set to $k _ { \mathrm { i } } .$ For $X \mathrm { \mathrm { - } }$ Means, a range of $( k _ { \mathrm { i } } - 1 , k _ { \mathrm { i } } + 1 )$ is set to guarantee that it outputs the results of $k _ { \mathrm { i } }$ clusters. For DBscan, its parameters eps and minpts are tuned to make the cluster number equal $k _ { \mathrm { i } } .$ For the four variances of the agglomerative methods and REPSET, only the particular level of the document dendrogram containing $k _ { \mathrm { i } }$ clusters is extracted as the clustering results.

## Empirical Evaluations

Empirical evaluations of REPSET were conducted based on the massive database accumulated on an internal blogging platform in practice. Multiple approaches including data experiments and user evaluations were utilized.

## Research Site

Empirical data for our search were provided by Company X, a large telecommunications service provider in China, whose parent company is a Fortune 500 telecommunications firm. As of 2013, Company X had over 40,000 employees and provided mobile services for more than 90 million customers. The annual revenue of the company was about USD 13.5 billion, holding 8% of market share of mobile telecommunications service in China.

In 2006, an internal blogging platform was deployed in Company X, aimed at enhancing the communication among workgroups and employees, promoting knowledge sharing, and providing a stage for self-expression. Each blog on the platform is maintained by a specific business workgroup in the company. Since establishment, effective policies were made to encourage the use of the blogging platform. As of October 2013, almost all workgroups in the company were maintaining their blogs on the platform, among which about 3,000 blogs were active (with at least one article posted per week). Around 5,000 articles plus about 10,000 comments were produced every day.

We evaluated the function of REPSET to extract a small set of representative articles from all the blog articles posted within a specific time period. This was done by comparing the articles selected by REPSET with articles recommended by popularity-based methods and those obtained by other clustering methods. In light of the findings from research on search engines that users usually only pay attention to the first 10 resulting articles of the first page (Granka et al. 2004), we initially restricted the size of the representative set to 10 articles.

Using a subset of data from the blogging platform, an illustrative example is provided in Appendix C, which compares the results of representative article extraction between REPSET and X-Means, a typical traditional clustering method. The comparison intuitively shows that REPSET can facilitate enhanced diversity in coverage and therefore capture more representative results. In the following, more consolidated experiments will be presented to evaluate the performance of REPSET.

## Measures

The REPSET method is composed of two major phases. The first phase is to cluster the original blog articles and the second is to select a representative article for each cluster. Therefore, the evaluation of REPSET also includes two phases. As presented in the “Benchmark Data Evaluation” subsection, the measures of entropy and purity are used for the first phase, namely to evaluate the accuracy of clustering. These are the most commonly used measures for evaluating clustering accuracy.

The second phase of evaluation is focused on the quality of representative information extraction, namely how well the extracted items may represent the whole population. In this regard, the measures of coverage and redundancy are used. These measures have been developed and formulated by a series of related literature on e-commerce and information retrieval (Ma and Wei 2012; Pan et al. 2005). Generally, coverage is defined as the degree to which a small set of items cover the contents of the whole body of a large information base. The other measure for representativeness is redundancy, which is defined as the degree to which the contents of the items in an extracted set interleave with each other (Zhang et al. 2002). Since the target of the extraction method is to arrive at a small set of information items with good representativeness, it is also important that the resulting set should be concise, so that its readers can capture as much information as possible in a limited time. Therefore, a high degree of redundancy would indicate a low level of extraction performance. To comprehensively evaluate the overall performance including both coverage and redundancy, the balanced $F _ { - }$ measure $( F _ { 1 }$ measure) may also be considered (Arazy and Woo 2007; Powers 2011). The $F _ { 1 }$ measure is commonly used in testing the accuracy of classification analysis, the higher the better. In our research, the $F _ { 1 }$ measure can be calculated as the harmonic mean of coverage and redundancy.

In the context of blog article extraction, the coverage, redundancy, and $F _ { 1 }$ measures of the resulting articles can be evaluated from two perspectives. The first is the de facto perspective. The de facto results of the measures are defined as the degrees of coverage and redundancy obtained by calculating the textual similarities between blog articles. The other perspective is user perception. The perceived results of the evaluation measures are defined as the degrees of coverage and redundancy reported by readers of blog articles. In the spirit of the measures generally used in research on reading behavior (Allington 1984), perceived coverage may be measured by the number of meaningful words that a reader remembers after reading the articles. For perceived redundancy, self-reported scales are usually utilized.

## Benchmark Methods and Evaluation Plan

We compared the 10 representative articles generated by REPSET with 3 types of reader-based recommended articles. The first is the top 10 rated articles based on reader ratings; the second is the top 10 most read articles; and the third is the top 10 most commented articles. Furthermore, based on the results of the benchmark data evaluation, we chose four clustering algorithms, namely Direct, Graph, RBR, and X-Means, to analyze the company blogs and compared them with REPSET. Three typical topic modeling methods, namely LDA, hLDA, and DTM, were also considered. Additionally, randomly generated articles were also taken into account as a method for comparison. Altogether, 11 benchmark methods were involved.

Therefore, the evaluation plan was to compare REPSET with each of the benchmark methods in terms of three de facto measures (coverage, redundancy, and $F _ { 1 } )$ and three perceived measures (coverage, redundancy, and $F _ { 1 } )$ . Two types of experiments were involved. First, we conducted data experiments to evaluate the de facto results of the measures. Second, we conducted user experiments to evaluate the perceived results of the measures. The de facto and perceived results will verify each other and provide a solid base for the evaluation conclusions.

Meanwhile, topic modeling methods do not perform well in these results. In contrast, although REPSET is also dependent on term frequency for the cosinesimilarity measure, the impacts of low-frequency words are still preserved all through the process of clustering.

## User Experiments

Although the results of blog data analysis have demonstrated the performance of the proposed method, aiming to secure higher quality for the evaluations, we decided to go beyond that and incorporate user experiments so that the results of the two types of evaluation can be cross validated. Using the same data set, user experiments were conducted among voluntary participants recruited from a prestigious university in China. The experiment process was designed as follows. First, participating subjects were randomly divided into groups, which were assigned to REPSET and the benchmark methods respectively. Thereafter, each subject was asked to review the 10 articles of his/her group independently in 20 minutes. The subjects were not aware of the group to which they belonged, and they did not know whether the articles they were reviewing came from REPSET extraction or other methods. After a subject had read the 10 articles assigned, he/she was asked to fill in a short questionnaire consisting of four scale questions about perceived redundancy. After that, the subjects were given a word list consisting of 267 feature words collected from all the 2,239 blog articles. These blog articles were first preprocessed with a series of processing strategies including stopword removing, stemming, and case folding, according to the common practice of document indexing (Witten et al. 1999). They were then segmented into keywords using a widely used Chinese language processing toolkit called NLPIR (http://ictclas.nlpir.org/). Moreover, infrequent keywords were removed, because they usually have little semantic meaning and may introduce ambiguity (Sedding and Kazakov 2004). Finally, all of the remaining keywords constitute the word list of 267 feature words. The subjects were asked to mark out all of the feature words that were mentioned (explicitly or implicitly) in the 10 articles that they had just read. They were not allowed to refer back to the articles when they were marking out the words. In the end, the subjects were also asked to write down any of their feelings or thoughts about the 10 articles they had read. The experiment script and screenshots for the experiment system UI are provided in Appendix E and Appendix F, respectively.

Perceived coverage was then measured by calculating the percentage of words in the list of feature words that were marked as mentioned. This is consistent with the research in reading behavior in which the degree of content coverage is measured with the number of new words learned (Allington 1984). Perceived redundancy was reported by the short questionnaire that the subjects answered. The four questionnaire items for measuring perceived redundancy, in the form of seven-point Likert-type scales, were developed through two rounds of discussion and a pilot study to ensure its validity.

Two rounds of experiments were conducted. The first round was conducted in December 2012, during which we recruited 212 qualified participants from the students of the university and compared REPSET with top rated, most read, most commented articles as well as the Graph and X-Means methods. The second round was conducted in January 2015, during which 166 qualified students were recruited and REPSET was compared with the Direct, RBR, LDA, hLDA, DTM, and random methods. According to self-reporting, volunteers that were not familiar with blogs were excluded. The experiments were conducted in the Behavior Lab of a national research center at the university. When dividing the participants into the comparison groups, attributes including gender, age, and grade were controlled, so that there are no significant differences between the groups. Although the blog articles were collected from a company, students did not have much difficulty in understanding the contents. The business of the company is mobile phone services, with which most students were very familiar. In our experiments, we asked the subjects whether there was anything they did not understand after they read the articles. Nobody answered that he/she felt difficulties, and very few people raised questions. In the rare cases when subjects felt confused, our research assistants gave explanations. All of the participants finished the entire process of the experiment smoothly in the time allocated. Tests using the Cronbach Alpha indicator show that the questionnaire data are reliable.

## Concluding Remarks

Satisfying user information needs on internal blog platforms is a broad topic area that deserves much research efforts in various directions. Our work on the information extraction framework and the REPSET method offers a new and meaningful approach to utilizing blogging information in an organizational contexts, which is different from other mechanisms such as the popularity-based or preference-based methods. Specifically, popularity-based methods such as most-read and most-commented blogs are focused on providing the information in which most people are interested.

In contrast, representative information extraction is focused on extending the range of information covered by the extracted set. In practice, multiple methods can be used alongside each other to provide multiple perspectives.

The research presented in this paper draws upon the prominent needs for representative information in organizational context. Nevertheless, the design artifacts proposed in this paper may be adapted and applied to other scenarios where information representativeness is a concern. Apart from internal blogs, when managers and employees seek to leverage blogs on the external web for customer-oriented insights (Chau and Xu 2012), the representativeness extraction problem will also be a compelling application need. Meanwhile, situations such as online news, discussion boards, online magazines, and newsletters can also benefit from showing a more representative sample of contents. Most news websites (such as cnn.com) carry a section on most-read news stories, but readers may find it useful if the top representative articles are also provided. Furthermore, information overload scenarios in offline contexts such as corporate reports, news articles, and legal documents can also potentially benefit.

Our research has been positioned as an exaptation study (Gregor and Hevner 2013) and executed in line with the principles of design science (Hevner et al. 2004). We formulated the relatively new problem of representative information extraction on internal blogs, which is meaningful for tackling information overload and utilizing accumulated data for managerial effectiveness. The postulated artifact was evaluated using multiple approaches involving both objective data and subjective perceptions, with all experiments carefully designed, organized, and executed, in order to guarantee the validity and reliability of the results. In this way, our contributions are two-fold: (1) we contribute to the design science literature with the formulated research question of representative content extraction on internal blogs, as well as the design framework and extended clustering technique to address this problem; (2) we contribute to the business intelligence literature with the novel clustering method developed through the course of solution design, which offers greater understanding of clustering research and may be generalized to other contexts.

One limitation of our current work is that we did not empirically test whether and how the extracted representative articles can eventually help with managerial decision making. Additionally, the time efficiency of the proposed method needs to be improved, if it is to be applied in real-time stream data processing scenarios such as extracting representative information from microblogging platforms like Twitter. Another direction for future research is to explore other BI techniques that could also be helpful for further exploiting the massive data accumulated on enterprise social media platforms, so as to support management and decision making on various aspects.

## Appendix A

## An Illustrative Example for the Clustering Process of REPSET

Figure A1 illustrates the clustering process of the REPSET method. There are nine documents represented as five round points and four square points. The distances between data points correspond to the pairwise similarities between documents. Intuitively, these nine documents can be divided into two clusters, i.e., the round point cluster and the square point cluster.

At the beginning, each point is treated as an individual cluster. In each step, two clusters with the largest similarity are merged into a new one. The backward strategy is applied after the generation of a new cluster. In the example, in steps (b)–(f), no boundary document is found in the generated cluster and reallocation does not occur. In step (g), two documents located in the middle are merged into one cluster, since they have the largest similarity. If the clustering process continues as the traditional hierarchical clustering method, the nine documents will finally be grouped into two clusters as shown in step (h), which turns out to be inaccurate, because one square document is assigned to a cluster in which most documents are round. In contrast, in REPSET, the square document will be marked as a boundary object (outlined in red), since its average similarity to the whole cluster is less than λ. The backward strategy will then reevaluate the red document and reallocate it if needed. As shown in (i), because the red document’s similarity to the left cluster is than that to the right cluster, it is reallocated to the left cluster.

## Appendix C

## Representative Article Extraction Example

As an example, we compare the results of representative article extraction between REPSET and X-Means, a typical traditional clustering method. We used the blogging data of a typical region of Company X, during the time period of July 2010, with a total of 2,239 blog articles. Using REPSET and X-Means, respectively, we extracted 10 articles from the data set. Figure C1 shows the comparison of the sizes of clusters generated by the two methods. It can be seen that the sizes of clusters generated by X-Means is much more even than those of REPSET. These results illustrate the fact that X-Means tends to select clusters that are spatially equidistant, which is deficient for representativeness extraction. In contrast, the sizes of clusters generated by REPSET are more discrepant, indicating the capability of REPSET to capture more diverse content.

![](/api/attachments/9S45QY73/fulltext/images/c790fa6099c0b09522c02c3d73389aef6189f56132f39f8bde82889557b87f53.jpg)

Figure C1. Cluster Size Comparison

Table C1 lists the titles of the representative articles extracted by REPSET and X-Means, respectively. It can be seen that the articles extracted by X-Means are mostly life-related and emotional essays. In the whole data set, about 70% of articles are life-related and 30% are work-related. In the results of X-Means, work-related articles are drown in life-related content and fail to be revealed. Meanwhile, most of the extracted life related articles are similar to some extent. In contrast, in the results of REPSET, six extracted articles are work-related, covering diverse topics including work balance, industry analysis, and customer alerts. The four life-related articles are also diverse, covering topics such as love, dining, and life advices. Such results intuitively show that REPSET can facilitate enhanced diversity in coverage and therefore capture more representative results.
