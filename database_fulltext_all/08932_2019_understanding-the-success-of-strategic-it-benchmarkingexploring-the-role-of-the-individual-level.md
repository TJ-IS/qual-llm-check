## Abstract

IT organizations use strategic IT benchmarking (SITBM) to improve business-IT alignment or for developing IT strategies. However, SITBM often does not result in the desired outcomes. Because the extant literature does not contribute to improving SITBM success, we conduct a deductive-inductive study to understand which factors distinguish successful and unsuccessful SITBM. We find that traditional project-level factors do not explain SITBM success; they are necessary but not sufficient. Rather, we show that the individual level is instrumental for explaining SITBM success, especially by ensuring the buy-in of relevant project team members – a perspective not yet discussed by pertinent literature.

Keywords: Strategic benchmarking; strategic IT management; strategic planning; case research; analytic induction; multi-level model

## 1. Introduction

Benchmarking has long become an established approach for IT organizations to assess their performance (Alshawaf et al., 2005; Basili et al., 2010). Gartner alone conducts more than 5,500 IT benchmarking projects per year (Gartner, 2013). Beyond cost benchmarking, especially strategic IT benchmarking (SITBM) has been gaining more attention among IT executives due to its ability to contrast internal and external perceptions of an IT organization (Adebanjo et al., 2010; Trento et al., 2016). Especially in light of the recent trend toward increasingly digital business strategies (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Merali et al., 2012), SITBM’s relevance will likely increase further: in traditional, more static environments the need for constantly provided up-to-date information about an IT organization’s strategic situation was not as high as it is in today’s volatile environments that require ongoing adaptation of IT/IS strategies. Hence, comparing an IT organization along with its digital services and products with that of competitors is an indispensable part of strategic IT management (ITM) in the digital era (Mithas et al., 2013; Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

As a special kind of strategic benchmarking, SITBM is a structured comparison of the various domains of ITM to a peer group (i.e., a group of organization-external companies the benchmarking company is compared to) in a level of detail suitable for IT executives (Chang & King, 2005). It is used to improve business-IT alignment (Khaiata & Zualkernan, 2009; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995), as a basis to develop or revise IT strategies (Basili et al., 2010; Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Mueller et al., 2009b), internal marketing (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Seddon et al., 2002), or other IT-related strategic actions. By comparing an IT organization to an external peer group, it is assured that strategic ITM will not lose sight of the external environment, both in terms of the IT department’s organizational context as well as in terms of the company’s overall environment.

However, and despite SITBM’s long tradition (Smith & McKeen, 1996), many such initiatives still fail to deliver actionable outcomes (Adebanjo et al., 2010; Williams et al., 2012). In fact, there are only a few known cases of strategic benchmarking being successful in the sense that it resulted in improvement initiatives (Yip et al., 2009). This situation is problematic because an SITBM project consumes significant time and resources (Adebanjo et al., 2010; Gräuler & Teuteberg, 2013) and should provide a sound basis for decision-making regarding strategic improvement initiatives. Further, given that benchmarking cycles may become shorter because of increasing business dynamics (El Sawy et al., 2010) and digital business models based on short-term opportunities (Bharadwaj et al., 2013), the need for reliable and actionable benchmarking results and successful benchmarking projects will even increase to stay competitive in the market. We accordingly suggest that: An SITBM is successful if it leads to initiatives intended to improve an IT organization’s strategic position.

Several SITBM success factors have been identified such as top management support, integration into an organization’s strategy process, appropriate benchmarking partners, or process quality. However, we lack a deeper understanding of these factors’ effects (Ebner 2014; Ramabadran et al., 2004). For example, many researchers argue that the quality of the chosen process model is one of the most important preconditions for successful benchmarking (Anand & Kodali, 2008; Hong et al., 2012), especially in strategic contexts (Basili et al., 2010; Chen, 2005). They suggest that better process models result in higher quality benchmarking results and increase the probability of subsequent improvement initiatives. Hence, they continue to suggest new process models in reaction to the increasing number of failing or underleveraged SITBM initiatives. Nevertheless, the number of unsuccessful SITBM initiatives has remained high (Adebanjo et al., 2010; Trento et al., 2016). To us, there are three possible explanations for this observation. First, the literature on success factors along with their antecedents and impacts is still fragmented and overarching theoretical integrations of benchmarking success are still scarce. Second, the effects of the various success factors are more complex than prior research suggests. Third, we still lack insights into relevant factors influencing SITBM success. For example, researchers in the broader benchmarking context have realized that, beyond methodological aspects, various contingent factors such as team or cultural characteristics may influence benchmarking success (Bhutta & Huq, 1999; Drew,

1997; Ramabadran et al., 2004). Yet, these insights remain fragmented and anecdotal. Given this background, we address the following research question:

What factors distinguish between successful and unsuccessful SITBM initiatives? We approach this question in two steps. First, we deductively derive an initial model on SITBM success and test it against three cases of IT organizations that have conducted SITBM (deductive analysis). Second, using inductive case analysis, we extend the current understanding of SITBM success by refining our propositions, as well as by adding new constructs and propositions to our initial model. Such deductive-inductive approaches (Gilgun, 2001; Patton, 2002) are particularly suitable to shed new light on existing phenomena by allowing “contradictory observations to change what we know” (Gilgun, 2001, p. 3). Consequently, we contribute to research and practice by providing a richer theoretical and more holistic understanding of how SITBM results are transformed into outcome. We add to other researchers’ attempts to provide a theory of benchmarking (Moriarty & Smallman, 2009) and help practitioners to better leverage their benchmarking efforts.

Our paper is structured as follows: in Section 2, we introduce SITBM success as the focal outcome of our research. Next, we derive our initial model for SITBM success in Section 3. Section 4 introduces our study’s design and empirical basis and describes how the analytic induction approach was used to build and test our initial model and to inductively refine it. Section 5 provides a detailed description of our three cases along with the results of the deductive analysis, before the results of our inductive analysis are presented in Section 6. The separation of both analysis steps illustrates the value of the interplay between the deductive and inductive elements, especially in light of the fact that neither deductive nor inductive analysis alone was sufficient to answer our research question. We synthesize the findings into our revised model, and discuss them in Section 7. Section 8 concludes the paper.

## 2. SITBM Success

Strategic benchmarking evolved at the beginning of the 1990s and was soon recognized as a powerful strategic management tool that allows a structured assessment of an organization (Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Watson, 1993). There are different definitions of strategic benchmarking. For example, while Watson (1993) anchors his definition in process benchmarking, Clayton and Luchs (1994) focus on an assessment of general strengths and weaknesses as preparation for strategic planning. Drew (1997) provides the most specific definition: “strategic benchmarking is used to compare organizational structures, management practices and business strategies” (p. 428). We follow this approach and define SITBM as a structured comparison of ITM to a peer group with the objective to improve the organizational and cost structures of an IT unit, the ITM practices, the business-IT alignment and the IT strategies.

There is a plethora of research on benchmarking classification and process issues, which have also been summarized in various reviews (e.g., Anand & Kodali, 2008; Dattakumar and Jagadeesh, 2003; Hong et al., 2012; Yasin, 2002). These reviews either provide a general structuring of research based on benchmarking, or focus on specific topics such as (lack of) theoretical foundations of benchmarking, frameworks and approaches to benchmarking, or ethical and legal aspects of benchmarking. A key finding from these reviews is that research papers dealing with matters of benchmarking success are scarce. The few authors who consider SITBM success (e.g., Williams et al., 2012; Mueller et al., 2009b; Ramabadran et al., 2004) conclude that methodological factors alone are not sufficient to explain success, but that various contextual and social success factors should be considered as well. Still, of those authors only few do not only identify the factors, but also attempt to understand successful benchmarking holistically (e.g., by linking the factors to the whole benchmarking process and investigate their effects in depth).

Successful strategic benchmarking results in a number of short and long-term initiatives targeted at improving an organization’s strategic position and its performance (Trento et al., 2016; Watson, 1993). In IT organizations, such an improvement might be reflected by improved business-IT alignment, more effective and efficient processes, or overall higher value contribution (Khaiata & Zualkernan, 2009; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Seddon et al., 2002; Smith & McKeen, 1996). Hence, the benchmarking results should function as an input for the subsequent strategic planning steps (Chen, 2005; Mueller et al., 2009b) and as a starting point for further in-depth analyses of the identified problems (Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Trento et al., 2016). Consequently, we maintain that an SITBM is successful if it produces useful information about an IT organization’s strategic position that is translated into sustainable initiatives to improve this position. We thus define SITBM success as the degree to which the SITBM leads to initiatives intended to improve an IT organization’s strategic position.

A key aspect in our definition of SITBM success is the concept of a strategic position. Rooted in the ideas of strategy as a strategic position (Porter, 1996), a strategic position is thought of as entailing a unique mix of operational activities and strategic priorities that, if implemented consistently, allow an organization to perform well in its market. The positioning school of strategic thinking (Mintzberg & Lampel, 1999) correspondingly emphasizes the importance of analytical preparation of strategizing. In this context, we see SITBM as strongly contributing to successful strategic planning both in terms of a functional positioning of the IT department within the company it serves (i.e., business-IT alignment) as well as in terms of understanding how IT can contribute to the company’s overall strategic positioning (competitive positioning). These two aspects, in turn, depend critically on how well SITBM and the analyses it provides are integrated into an IT department’s strategic planning process.

## 3. Initial Model Development

## 3.1 Initial Model of SITBM Success

Our deductive-inductive research is conducted in two steps. First, we try to confirm existing research (deductive analysis). Second, contradictions that were uncovered during the deductive analysis (i.e., real-world observations that cannot be explained by existing research) are being resolved by identifying missing factors, links, or effects (inductive analysis). Hence, we started our research by deductively deriving an initial model for SITBM success and, thereafter, testing and inductively refining it by using the insights from cases we studied in the field (compare to Lapointe & Rivard, 2011).

To guide our research, we structured our propositions along a benchmarking’s basic phases. Although various approaches to benchmarking differ in this regard, they all build on the “plando-check-act” (PDCA) model (Deming, 1982). Several authors argue for keeping strategic

#

benchmarking endeavors close to the easy-to-understand PDCA model because companies should put “their efforts into the benchmarking project itself” (Watson, 1993, p. 65) and not into the completion of a complex sequence of steps (Moriarty & Smallman, 2009). We, too, draw on the PDCA model as a framework for our research: (1) prepare the SITBM (plan), (2) collect the necessary data (do), (3) analyze the data for performance gaps and strategic potentials (check), and (4) improve by confronting performance gaps and leveraging potentials (act). ## 3.2 Strategic Planning Process Integration

SITBM success manifests itself during the action phase in Figure 1 as a result of improvement initiatives being initiated. In practice, this phase should overlap with the strategic planning of an IT organization (Ward & Peppard, 2003). To ensure this overlap, strategic planning process integration is important for successful SITBM. This strategic planning process integration refers to the degree to which the benchmarking takes place right before strategic planning processes start that utilize benchmark results for initiating strategic action (Watson, 1993). In this regard, SITBM is often performed as part of the preparation for IT strategizing because it fosters inside-out and outside-in analysis (Chen, 2005; Trento et al., 2016).<sup>1</sup> It exposes an IT organization’s strength and weaknesses and assures that the resulting strategy does not lose sight of the external environment, both in terms of the IT department’s organizational context as well as in terms of the company’s overall environment. The need of such an external perspective is particularly important where business models increasingly become digital and IT serves as a backbone for digital products and services. When organizations pursue digital strategies organization-wide as opposed to mere functional IT strategies (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2010), planning cycles may shrink and efficient and effective competitive intelligence (like benchmarking) becomes even more important (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). In line with our earlier reference to the positioning school, the integration of SITBM into strategic planning processes should not simply be understood as feeding into an IT-centric, inward-focused strategic plan, but should rather be seen to support the planning of the position of IT within the organization and IT’s impact on the organization’s position overall (Chen et al., 2010). Hence, efforts should be made to maintain the integration even if the SITBM project encounters problems (e.g., time delays or data inconsistencies). Strategic planning process integration should be tackled from the beginning of an SITBM project to assure that the results are available in time, and are not overlooked during the strategic planning, but can actually influence it (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Mueller et al., 2009a; Mueller et al., 2009b). If the results are available very late, the IT strategy might have already been agreed upon and changes might be difficult if not impossible. We, thus, also anchored it into the planning phase of Figure 1 and formulate our first proposition as follows:

P1: A high degree of strategic planning process integration influences SITBM success positively.

## 3.3 Methodological Quality

Questions regarding appropriate methodologies have been most investigated in benchmarking research. Methodological quality is anchored in the planning phase of Figure 1, because the SITBM methodology has naturally to be chosen at the beginning of the project. It is a multidimensional construct, referring to the quality of the adopted process model, the quality of the used instruments, and the quality of the benchmarking peer group.

An adequate process model is an important precondition for successful benchmarking (Ahmed & Rafiq, 1998; Moriarty & Smallman, 2009). This adequacy not only relates to the structure and sequence of steps to be taken in benchmarking, but an adequate benchmarking process should also be meaningful and comprehensible for the people involved (Elmuti & Kathawala, 1997; Zairi, 1998). Hence, process models often have to be adapted to fit the specificities of a certain organization in order for them to be accepted. Additionally, inexperienced benchmarking teams may struggle if the process model is complex (Elnathan et al., 1996; Ramabadran et al., 2004). We thus define process quality as the degree to which the benchmarking instrument used is of high quality and captures the right content, the processes adopted are adequate in terms of complexity and meaningfulness, and the chosen peer group is comparable to the IT organization.

Benchmarking instruments may take different forms, such as questionnaires, interview guides (typically used as part of on-site visits), as well as discussion and moderation guides (as used in focus groups and panels) (Camp, 1989). Questionnaires are often applied in strategic benchmarking because they facilitate a high level of anonymity; a crucial concern in strategic benchmarking (Chen, 2005; Panagiotou, 2007). Three important quality criteria can be distinguished regarding SITBM instruments. First, they have to cover ITM’s relevant content (Chang & King, 2005; Newkirk et al., 2003) because a sound strategic situation analysis requires an assessment of the most important IT services and a good understanding of the IT organization’s processes and structures (Mocker & Teubner, 2005; Mueller et al., 2009b; Riempp et al., 2008; Saunders & Jones, 1992; Serafeimides & Smithson, 2000). The content to be captured should be meaningfully structured in the SITBM instrument. For example, questionnaires used to capture the SITBM data often structure the relevant contents into single subquestionnaires (e.g., application landscape, IT projects) to allow for easier distribution and completion by the respective experts. Second, the relevant content has to be carefully quantified to assure the results’ reliability (Carpinetti & Melo, 2002). Third, to foster interpretation of the results, instruments used for SITBM should allow for collecting context information (Smith & McKeen, 1996; Williams et al., 2012). The appropriate contextualization of strategic benchmarking results has been shown to be a key contributor to their quality (Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Mueller et al., 2009b). We therefore define instrument quality as the degree to which the instrument applied in the SITBM properly covers, operationalizes, and contextualizes the relevant contents of strategic ITM.

An adequate peer group is often regarded as one of the most important antecedents of benchmarking success (Camp, 1989; Moriarty & Smallman, 2009; Panagiotou, 2007; Ramabadran et al., 2004). The peer group comprises the group of companies to which the benchmarking company is compared. These companies usually stem from outside the organization.<sup>2</sup> Thus, the peer group has implications for the results’ comparability and the ability to observe best practices. However, in SITBM, building a suitable peer group is challenging: companies often strive to obtain benchmarking data from their direct competitors but do not want to share their own strategic information (Elmuti & Kathawala, 1997; Elnathan et al., 1996; Panagiotou, 2007; Trento et al., 2016). In practice, many IT organizations therefore join a benchmarking consortium of companies that might not necessarily all reside in the same strategic environment, but are similar enough to reveal helpful insights (Drew, 1997; Elnathan et al., 1996; Watson, 1993). In summary, we define peer group quality as the degree to which the benchmarking peer group is comparable to the organization in terms of strategic position, structural characteristics, and overall performance. On the whole, methodological quality is a vital cornerstone for high-quality benchmarking results. We thus propose:

P2: A high degree of methodological quality influences the quality of the SITBM results positively.

## 3.4 Quality of the Benchmarking Results

Benchmarking results’ quality entails the degree to which the results of the SITBM are valid, reliable, and useful. Reliability and validity are relevant to assure the benchmarking results’ trustworthiness: if a few (or all) stakeholders have doubts about certain conclusions, they may refuse to implement the respective measures (Camp, 1989; Drew, 1997; Williams et al., 2012), especially if they were not involved in the data collection process (Ramabadran et al., 2004). Methodological quality is an important prerequisite for the quality of the results: capturing very detailed, incomplete, too imprecise, or irrelevant data does not provide the required insights and the resultant focus may be wrong (Ahmed & Rafiq, 1998; Gimbert et al., 2010; Smith & McKeen, 1996). Consequently, improving the strategic position may be difficult, if not impossible. Still, a trade-off between the results’ quality and the collection effort is important: at a certain quality level of the results, the quality of the interpretations and the measures will no longer increase (Watson, 1993). In summary, the higher the benchmarking results’ quality the more effort will be put into data analysis and the results will be implemented with higher probability (Carpinetti & de Melo, 2002; Maleyeff, 2003; Williams et al., 2012). Hence, high-quality benchmarking results are much more likely to be implemented and lead to better outcomes than moderate or low-quality results. We propose:

P3: High quality benchmarking results influence SITBM success positively.

The quality of the results usually becomes visible during data analysis when the results are discussed and interpreted as a basis for deriving strategic measures. Consequently, we have anchored this construct in the analyze data phase in Figure 1.

## 3.5 Project Sponsorship Quality

Project sponsorship quality reflects the degree to which there is clear and committed project for providing adequate resources and communicating the project’s importance and overall objectives clearly, which contributes to the project results (e.g., Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Ohinata, 1994; Ramabadran et al., 2004). Without a sufficiently high quality in project sponsorship, there are insufficient resources to collect benchmarking data appropriately. Hence, employees responsible for data collection might not prioritize it sufficiently, which would adversely impact data quality. Such behavior often results in unreliable “guesstimates” (Watson, 1993, p. 49), which merely allow coarse benchmarking analyses; the potential outcome impact of the results is limited. Consequently, many studies identify poor-quality project sponsorship during the data collection phase as one of the most important barriers to benchmarking success (Adebanjo et al., 2010; Camp, 1989; Drew, 1997; Hinton et al., 2000). Based on these arguments, we anchor project sponsorship quality as most relevant during data collection in Figure 1 and propose:

P4: High degree of project sponsorship quality influences the quality of the benchmarking results positively.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1 Research Design

Studying complex phenomena, such as projects with strategic implications, requires a rich data set. Hence, we investigated three companies conducting an SITBM (e.g., Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009). Owing to the rich body of knowledge in the field of interest, we relied on a deductive-inductive approach (Gilgun, 2001; Patton, 2002). Such deductive-inductive approaches (Gilgun, 2001; Patton, 2002) are particularly suitable to shed new light on existing phenome-

#

na by allowing “contradictory observations to change what we know” (Gilgun, 2001, p. 3). Research projects using a deductive-inductive setup are conducted in two steps. First, an initial model is derived from existing literature, which is subsequently confirmed (deductive analysis step). Second, contradictions resulting from the deductive analysis step – that is, the initial model cannot (fully) be confirmed – are being resolved by exploring missing factors, links, or effects (inductive analysis step). This leads to a revised theoretical model.

As will be shown in our confirmatory deductive analysis step (see Section 5), prior research on SITBM success alone is not sufficient to explain our cases. The analysis revealed that the success factors identified in the previous section were all moderate to high in our cases, but each case had a different outcome – one was very successful, one partly successful, and one unsuccessful. To overcome this contradiction, we used the inductive analysis step to uncover additional success factors and effects that may have been overlooked by prior research. Consequently, we can confirm existing knowledge about SITBM success, while simultaneously allowing new insights (e.g., new factors or propositions) to emerge. As a result, we are able to advance current knowledge about SITBM success as captured in our initial theoretical model and propose a revised theoretical model to reflect this updated understanding. Our case analysis followed an analytic induction logic, which is particularly useful when analyzing multiple case data in deductive-inductive research settings (Gilgun, 1995; Patton, 2002; Robinson, 1951). Further, we relied on established case research guidelines to assure our finding’s validity and reliability (Dubé & Paré, 2003; Gibbert et al., 2008).

## 4.2 Case Selection

We studied three IT organizations conducting an SITBM. We specifically investigated how these IT organizations approached the generation of data through SITBM and how they transferred the results to their strategic IT planning. The cases constitute a convenience sample as only a limited number of companies allowed us access to their confidential planning activities. However, retrospective analysis showed that the cases allowed for theoretical replication (Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009), because all the cases had various points in common, but differed in various characteristics such as industry, company size, IT expenditure, degree of

#

centralization, and degree of outsourcing quota. Such structurally different cases help ensure that our proposed theory is not idiosyncratic to a specific type of organizational context. The cases also differed in their initial situation, their SITBM purpose, their benchmarking approach, and their success: one case was successful, one only partially successful, and one failed. Such cases are valuable for illuminating and elaborating concepts and relationships that can be subsequently theorized. Additionally, theoretical replication logic is of special value regarding deductive-inductive approaches, because the cases potentially allow the falsification of past and emergent findings (Gilgun, 2001; Robinson, 1951). Our cases were similar as they were IT organizations from medium to large companies that primarily operated in German-speaking countries. Unlike small firms, IT organizations from medium to large companies are more likely to have formal strategic processes implemented and, thus, use benchmarking for strategic purposes. ## 4.3 Data Collection

Data on each case were collected in two rounds. The first took place during the SITBM projects between July 2007 and December 2008 and lasted between 2 and 5 months. The second round, conducted to determine whether and how the SITBMs had shaped the IT organizations’ strategies and initiatives, took place from October 2010 to November 2011. We used

#

three sources of evidence to reach an appropriate degree of internal validity: observations, documents, and interviews (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Mingers, 2001).

During the first round, we focused on understanding the overall progress of the SITBM. At least one of the authors spent several days at each case site. These days were distributed throughout the course of the projects. For example, the case companies’ data collection for the SITBM was observed at dedicated points in time and most workshops were attended by two of the authors. The resultant observations were documented in field notes and complemented by documents such as project status reports, project-related documentation (e.g., documents used for benchmarking analysis or project plans), meeting protocols, meeting minutes, and emails.<sup>3</sup> Moreover, interviews were conducted with major project actors, such as the CIOs, middle IT management (ITM), core business stakeholders, as well as consultants, if present (see Appendix C for details). A snowball strategy was used to identify further potential interview candidates (Patton, 2002). The interviews lasted between 1 and 2 hours and served two purposes.<sup>4</sup> First, the respondents were asked to provide data on the SITBM and, second, they were queried about their perception of the project’s course. The interviews were transcribed and approved by the interviewees and were corroborated by analyzing emails and postproject review questionnaires from the core project actors. Furthermore, notes taken during the interviews helped to contextualize and interpret the interviews. Additionally, we had access to the questionnaires the case companies used as tools to collect data for the SITBM. These questionnaires provided important insights into the organizations’ current strategic position and potential problems they might have to address. On completion of the SITBM projects, we compiled rich case logs documenting the course of all three projects, including all contact records.

During the second round, we conducted interviews to generate insights into the effects the SITBM projects have had on the respective organization, especially in terms of any projects or initiatives that were started based on the SITBM results. We analyzed these projects and initiatives to identify the benchmarking’s outcomes. In Alpha and Gamma, we were able to reconnect with the CIOs and interviewed them regarding the progress made since the SITBM and their retrospective perceptions of it. Access to Beta’s CIO was, however, no longer possible, because the she had been promoted and could not be contacted. Beta’s new CIO was not familiar with the matter. However, the consultants who had conducted the original SITBM project remained on site thereafter and had been intensively involved in follow-up projects since. This allowed us to generate insights into how the IT organization had changed after the SITBM and generate data comparable to that obtained for Alpha and Gamma.

Overall, we collected extensive empirical material (see Table 2), which we compiled into a case database (Gibbert et al., 2008; Yin, 2009). Using this database, narratives of each case were developed as well as detailed case diaries (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994). The case diaries were especially helpful to reconstruct each case’s time line and link relevant persons, emails, documents, meeting minutes, interviews, etc. to specific events.

## 4.4 Data Analysis

Our case analysis follows analytic induction (Gilgun, 2001; Patton, 2002; Robinson, 1951). In analytic induction, the results of the first case analysis form the basis for the second case analysis; these results form the basis for the third case analysis, and so on. As an additional step at the end, we also went back to the first two cases to see whether the emergent theory continues to fit them (Figure 2). At least one of the authors coded the collected empirical materials (Miles & Huberman, 1994) using the qualitative analysis software Atlas.ti (version 6.1). To do so, we used the compiled case diaries as a basis and coded the cases historically from the first contact to the last meeting using the empirical materials linked in the diaries. In this way, we assured that we would not overlook evidence that helped interpreting the different events occurring. For example, various documents (e.g., working papers, power point presentations, strategy blueprints, contracts with suppliers, and service level agreements) were used to reflect the reactions of specific stakeholders in interviews and workshops and put them into context. In addition, comments provided in the questionnaires used to collect the SITBM data allowed for valuable insights concerning hidden dissonances and the project members’ overall perception of the SITBM project.

We operationalized our initial constructs and propositions (Miles and Huberman, 1994) and prepared a corresponding code list containing 27 initial codes (see Appendix A). We then performed the deductive analysis (Gilgun, 2001; Lapointe and Rivard, 2011; Patton, 2002), that is, we coded the data top-down according to the code list (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Patton, 2002). We examined our initial model by reflecting whether the theory could explain the observations made in the cases. If necessary, we revised existing constructs and propositions to better fit the data (Robinson, 1951); the first step in our data analysis as shown in Figure 2.

However, as we will share later, the purely deductive analysis based on our initial model of SITBM success was only partially able to explain the differences in the outcome and success of the SITBM projects we studied. To address this matter, we complemented our deductive analysis with inductive data analysis to refine our model; the second step in analytical induction. We performed the inductive analysis using open and pattern coding. We focused on identifying key concepts that influenced the course of the project and seemed related to SITBM success. Using pattern coding, emergent themes were grouped together into synthesizing categories. High-level code categories and the relationships between them were organized with help of network displays (Miles & Huberman, 1994), which fostered the building of logical chains of evidence (Yin, 2009).

Overall, we identified 44 new codes and five new synthesizing categories across the cases (see Appendix E, Table A.4 for exemplary quotes supporting the constructs). All the authors discussed the coding and categories to increase the reliability and reduce coding bias. Additionally, two researchers, who were not involved in the research project, were asked to provide input regarding the coding of randomly chosen excerpts or those whose interpretation was difficult (Dubé & Paré, 2003; Patton, 2002). Moreover, to exclude rival explanations, we discussed our interpretations and the resulting theory with neutral researchers who were academic subject-matter experts on ITM and benchmarking. The analysis was concluded onc no new insights emerged from the analysis or discussion and our findings were stable in terms of rival explanations (Bogdan & Biklen, 2007; Gilgun, 2001).

## 5. Episodes of SITBM: Deductive Case Findings

Following analytic induction, we investigated how well our initial model explained SITBM success in one case. Next, we inductively broadened our understanding of SITBM success through newly emerging or changing aspects. The resulting model functioned as input for the next case. To ensure readability, we do not present the results case-wise, but start with the results of the deductive analysis of the initial model in this section, and, thereafter, in Section 6, illustrate our findings from the inductive step.

If prior research explained SITBM success sufficiently, we would expect the cases to confirm our initial propositions. However, as will be shown in the next sections and in Table 3, our initial model is insufficient to explain success (or lack thereof) in the different cases. For ex-

#

ample, although the assessment of the four first constructs in Table 3 is identical for Alpha and Gamma, only Gamma’s benchmarking was fully successful. At Beta, the SITBM did not result in any outcome, although the strategic planning process integration and the quality of the project’s sponsorship were high and the quality of the methodological and benchmarking results was at least moderate. Clearly, the quality of the results was moderate and some of the findings were rather unreliable due to the rough estimates used at the end of data collection. ## 5.1 Case 1 – Alpha

In the context of a company-wide pre-sales due diligence project, Alpha’s IT department was challenged to demonstrate their value contribution to the overall company and to suggest measures how to further improve their value contribution. In order to inform and support the corresponding revision of the IT strategy and the identification of relevant strategic initiatives, Alpha started an SITBM. For this, the manager in charge of IT at Alpha, as the SITBM’S project sponsor, decided to partner up with an external institution in conducting an SITBM. The project lasted from July 2007 to November 2007 (see Appendix D, Figure A.1 for details on the case). This particular timeframe was chosen to allow the SITBM results to be fed directly into the strategic planning process that usually started at the end of the year. Because Alpha’s project results were meant to show IT’s value (functional positioning of the IT department) and find starting points for strategy adjustment (competitive positioning of the IT department), the intended strategic planning process integration was high.

Alpha had experience in price and service benchmarking. Thus, although Alpha had never conducted a strategic benchmarking before, the project sponsor was generally aware of SITBM’s potential pitfalls. He scheduled a meeting with the senior executives of the institution conducting the SITBM to understand their methodology. During the meeting, the externa senior executive demonstrated the service provider’s experience gained in several strategic benchmarking projects from the past and how they used it to incrementally improve the methodology. He also described the structure and size of the peer group they would use for comparison and gave a short overview over their process model and instrument.

During the kick-off that took place approximately 2 months after the initial meeting with the project sponsor, two project managers from both the organizations were assigned to carry out the project. The project sponsor had provided them with comfortable resources and stressed the importance of the project toward them. They discussed how they could best meet the project’s objectives and decided to capture relevant information for the benchmarking with a questionnaire. Based on his experience with benchmarking, Alpha’s project manager was critical regarding the suggested SITBM instrument that only consisted of a questionnaire. While he appreciated the instrument’s content and structure, he stressed that full comparability with the peer group would be hard to achieve unless the instrument accounted for the context of the collected data as well. Hence, the two project managers included documents as an additional data source and a workshop with all project team members (i.e., all the functional mid-level managers from within the IT department identified as experts designated to supply data and work with the SITBM results in their strategy revision) to discuss conflicting perceptions and inconsistent data. These adjustments of the instrument and the process model improved the project’s methodological quality. Next, Alpha’s project manager was briefed as key user of the benchmarking instruments. As a result of this engagement, he was positive about the project.

One day after the official kick-off, the data collection phase started. Alpha’s project manager identified subject matter experts who could provide the relevant information. As the SITBM’s project sponsor was not responsible for the project unit and the unit had not been scheduled to be involved at that point, no expert was identified regarding project management. Instead, Alpha’s SITBM team answered the respective questions based on their perceptions. The questionnaire distribution went well; all specialists were motivated: the project sponsor had ensured that all of them had sufficient time to collect the data and made clear how important

#

As data collection progressed, considerable inconsistencies emerged between the experts and various contentrelated questions showed up. The latter were eliminated by clarifying definitions and operationalizations used in the questionnaire and two internal workshops were scheduled with all the experts to discuss the inconsistencies. These workshops took place 4 weeks after the data collection had started and were conducted within 4 days. Each of the workshops led to lively discussions and the consistency of the collected data increased substantially. Ten days later, the initially planned workshop with all the project team members took place and the remaining data were collected, accompanied by a fourth, spontaneous, internal coordination workshop.

## 5.2 Case 2 – Beta

Beta’s project sponsor, the executive in charge of the company’s central IT (referred to as the CIO), had hired a consulting company for an IT strategy development project for the company’s central IT division between August and December 2008. Beta is a holding for three autonomous companies. ring the initial setup project, the project schedule timed the SITBM such that it was completed strategy would be defined. The project included four workshops: one initial workshop to introduce the SITBM methodology to all team members and to perform a first, rough SWOT analysis, a second workshop to detail the SWOT analysis and derive first vision and mission statements, a third workshop to discuss the SITBM results and integrate them into the strategy process, and a fourth and fifth workshop to derive the strategic goals, respective indicators, and a balanced scorecard. This specific structure indicates a high degree of strategic planning process integration. The project team consisted of three consultants, Beta’s CIO, and six managers responsible for the various ITM domains within Beta’s central IT. Furthermore, the project was pitched to the IT heads of the three business units (see Appendix D, Figure A.2 for details). Beta’s CIO put high emphasis on the project and stressed its importance toward all project team members. She also assured that the project team members had sufficient time for the project, indicating that project sponsorship quality was high.

During the kick-off at the beginning of August, the consultants provided a rough introduction to the project’s structure and methodological facets. During this introduction, members of the ITM team indicated their interest and buy-in into the project and methodology applied, but also worried that the benchmarking might produce incomparable results. Based on a discussion of this matter in the workshop, the CIO chose a combination of a questionnaire-based SITBM and interviews with representatives of important company functions central IT was working with on a regular basis (e.g., stakeholders external to the project team such as the managing directors and IT heads of the company’s three business units). Furthermore, additional documents with strategic relevance were to be analyzed to account for company specifics. To save time, the CIO decided that stakeholder interviews were to be performed in parallel to the principal data gathering of the SITBM project, and not afterwards as suggested by the consultants.

The first workshop took place with the entire project team. During methodology introduction, questions concerning the peer group’s comparability and the SITBM questionnaire’s suitability were raised. The issues concernin comparability were addressed in detail, while the project team members agreed that the CIO would later judge the questionnaire’s fit. She later agreed that it fitted her company. Taking into account the adjustments made to the instrument as discussed in the previous paragraph, it can be subsumed that the methodological quality was quite high in the project; the peer group was similar enough to provide meaningful insights and the instrument fit the company well. The adapted structure of the process model with the parallel interviews was not optimal, but also not very problematic. During a SWOT analysis conducted as part of the first workshop 1 day after the kick-off workshop, the stakeholders actively discussed the IT organization’s various facets; but the discussion’s intensity decreased as the CIO’s comments increased. For example, when disagreements between the project members were discussed, the CIO observed this for a while and finally made own statements indicating that further replies were not desired.

The first interviews started soon after, and the head of IT accounting was briefed as key informant concerning how to complete the questionnaire; but he was not very enthusiastic. In addition, the specialists for data collection were identified and the questionnaire was distributed. Because the key informant went on vacation just after data collection started, the specialists could not address emerging questions. Instead of contacting the consultants, as offered, they provided nearly no data and often also stressed their operative pressure as a reason for the delay. Data collection soon petered out. The interviews also did not proceed well: many interviewees provided only the most mandatory information. The consultants addressed the employees’ passivity in the next status call with the CIO. She had observed this as well and promised to talk to her team.

In the second workshop, the results of the conducted interviews, the derived vision and mission statements, and strengths and weaknesses of the central IT were discussed. The morning workshop went well and a lively discussion ensued. When the CIO joined in the afternoon, however, the team became increasingly passive and discussions faded away. Moreover, the CIO rearranged the morning’s results according to her perceptions. The dissatisfactory data collection was also addressed: almost nothing had been achieved. Again, the project members stressed their operative pressure and stated that they troubled in understanding the performance indicators used and, therefore, could not provide information. The CIO again stressed the importance of the project and encouraged her team to talk to her if there were resource shortages. It was also agreed that the remaining data collection would be preceded by individual briefings on the questionnaire. Overall, the second workshop hardly led to any progress. This situation and the team’s passivity worried the CIO, which she also articulated in a later call with the consultants’ project leader. The project leader suggested having a detailed discussion with the team and using the results as a basis for the following steps, but no such discussion took place.

A few days later, the individual briefings on the questionnaire started. Although many stakeholders had asked for more support, most of them were either not or poorly prepared; they again stressed operative issues. Afterwards, all the briefed employees had promised to provide the required information. Simultaneously though they started complaining to the CIO: they perceived the data collection process as unproductive and did not understand how it contributed to strategy development. Many of them refused to provide more data. The planned data collection completion date was close, but there was no significant progress. During the interviews, the employees were increasingly unwilling to share their opinions. Instead, the team members started mentioning risks such as drawbacks for their careers or loss of reputation in the company in case their opinion was not in line with the CIO’s view. Finally, a call between the CIO and two consultants was scheduled to deescalate the project: the CIO no longer felt that the benchmarking could contribute to the strategy development. In addition, she criticized that the benchmarking questionnaire was very detailed and left too much room for discussions. It was decided to fill out the remaining parts with rough estimates. The results would only be contextualized by means of the documents analyzed and only the interviews conducted up to that stage.

Finally, the data collection was completed only a few days before the third workshop, during which the strategic goals and respective indicators were derived. The benchmarking results workshop took place almost 2 weeks after this workshop. The overall quality of the benchmarking results was moderate, because in later project stages rough estimates were used and no further documents analyzed or interviews conducted. Still, important key insights were presented. While most concerns regarding the results’ validity and reliability could be eliminated, the team members did not see much value in the results because they were available very late. Further, the CIO strongly argued against the benchmarking as a whole. During the last two workshops, a balanced scorecard for the new IT strategy had been set up. Again, the CIO countered the consultants’ and the project team’s interpretations, but she was not as dominant as before and discussions were mostly constructive. The CIO made the final review a few days later – with a surprising outcome: she renamed nearly all indicators and reassigned and reprioritized many activities to new strategic targets. Even today, people at Beta continue to work with this balanced scorecard although contents changed since the project.

With respect to our initial model, we find that all constructs were either high or moderate – P2 and P4 are confirmed (see Table 3). Again, however, P1 and P3 are not confirmed. Despite the similarity between these observations and the ones made at Alpha, we observe that the benchmarking at Beta was not successful.

## 5.3 Case 3 - Gamma

Gamma conducted an IT strategy revision between August 2008 and December 2008. Due to their experience with previous benchmarking projects (mainly cost benchmarking), Gamma’s CIO – who acted as the SITBM project sponsor – decided to conduct SITBM prior to the strategy revision to provide his management team with a basis for informed decision-making. To pursue this, he was looking for an SITBM approach not only focused on quantitative data. He found an external partner who used a systemic and sophisticated SITBM method, which reflected especially in the advanced process model. The CIO assigned 12 people to form the project team, including himself and his deputy, Gamma’s chief technology officer. Together with Gamma’s head of management accounting and another IT accountant, two consultants from the external partner formed the core project team (see Appendix D, Figure A.3). Like with Alpha and Beta, strategic planning process integration was high in the project, both with respect to the timerelated scheduling and the intended use of the benchmarking results.

The project started with a kick-off workshop with the CIO and IT managers. From the beginning, the CIO emphasized how important the project was and that the IT managers should take the project very serious. The high project sponsorship quality also reflected throughout the project in ongoing encouragement of the team by the CIO whenever it came to inconsistencies and conflicts. During the workshop, it showed that Gamma’s team was critical of benchmarking due to their previous experience. They focused their criticism on issues of comparability with the peer group and reliability of the data (i.e., they would provide precise and correct data as required by the questionnaire). To overcome these reservations, the consultants introduced their approach to SITBM; that is, how to obtain data and how to interpret it consensually. They suggested a series of workshops to assure correctness and consistency of the collected data, as well as stakeholder interviews and document analyses to contextualize the data. Additionally, the sultants introduced the questionnaire used to collect the benchmarking data. Gam team agreed that the questionnaire structure depicted their internal structure well a identify key informants and important documents. Finally, many of Gamma’s reservations were mitigated and Gamma’s project team was eager to be involved in the SITBM. With respect to process model and instrument, the methodological quality was high (see Table 3 for details).

The data collection phase started 1 week after the kick-off workshop. The two internal accountants were briefed such that they could coordinate the data collection at Gamma. Both were motivated and did their best to understand the structure and logic of the questionnaire. Two weeks later, subject matter experts were briefed on the questionnaire and assigned responsibility to fill out parts of the questionnaire corresponding to their expertise. In a status call 7 days later, the coordinators reported a positive project mood. The consultants confirmed this impression. Altogether, the data collection phase was concluded after 11 weeks in the middle of October. When the questionnaires came back, the core team resolved minor issues concerning consistency in an internal workshop involving the coordinators and the experts who had reported the inconsistent data. Afterwards, the consultants compiled an SITBM report and the core team prepared the second workshop, which took place in the first week of November.

In the second workshop, the core team presented the SITBM results to the whole project team (excluding the CIO) and described Gamma’s peer group. The documents analyzed at that stage and the information captured to resolve inconsistencies complemented the results. As a result of the various validation rounds, the quality of the results was high. The team lively discussed to which extent the results justified an IT strategy revision. Consequently, lingering conflicts became apparent and led to clashes. Where interpretations differed, the team used the data from the different sources to justify their individual points of view. They only considered an issue resolved when all involved parties agreed on a position. For example, concerning supplier man agement, the benchmarking analysis revealed that the IT department’s capabilities were rather weak and needed improvement. The result matched the perception of the IT accounting and application services team. However, the IT procurement manager, Gamma’s head of IT project management and the chief technology officer had a different view. When the project team turned to the document analysis to resolve this tension, the data indicated that the importance of external suppliers was likely to increase in the future: Gamma wanted to reduce its focus on infrastructure and compensate by using strategic suppliers. The team therefore agreed that existing competencies regarding managing single suppliers should be extended to supplier portfolio management and that a dedicated supply strategy had to be designed. The joint discussion of the issues relevant to IT strategy at Gamma increased the project team members’ buy-in.

Some issues could not be resolved in the second workshop. Consequently, the team members with conflicting views worked on a joint interpretation in smaller workshops. Besides, additional interviews were conducted with embers and other stakeholders relevant to Gamma’s IT strategy. For example, to resolve inconsistencies regarding project success, the corporate COO was interviewed as well as the heads of corporate accounting and corporate project management. The results of the interviews and workshops were discussed in the third workshop at the end of November to solve the open issues. Due to the critical nature of the issues, the discussions in the third workshop were much more heated than in the first two. However, there were no lasting dissonances and, eventually, the team established a shared perception of all discussed ITM domains. For example, in the case of project success, the COO had clarified the expectations toward IT: it should have a solid know-how of the business’s core processes and offer them support. As the business units often used external consultants to define their requirements,

#

Gamma’s IT was mostly confronted with detailed technical specifications and the IT did not obtain insights into its customers’ real needs. Furthermore, due to misspecification or misinterpretation of requirements, IT often did not deliver what the business units had expected. Consequently, it was agreed that an additional division would be created within the IT department to offer business customers support for their requirements analysis.

In the final workshop in mid-December, team members (now also including the CIO) discussed the new IT strategy. This workshop focused on translating the SITBM results into a set of strategic initiatives. While the team members had gained consensus, the CIO challenged some interpretations, but let his team justify and explain their conclusions. In turn, he illustrated his point of view and tried to make his team understand why certain interpretations should be changed. Finally, some results were adjusted, but the majority remained untouched. After this workshop, team members indicated their satisfaction with the SITBM results and the whole project. In addition, the results were used to communicate the new IT strategy to the business units and executive boards of Gamma. By the end of 2014, Gamma’s IT was still in the process of implementing the strategy.

In summary, Gamma is the only case that completely confirms our initial model. All constructs were high, and the project was successful. However, in light of the challenges revealed by the other two cases, there is a need to critically revisit the initial research model and the data to find explanations for the discrepancies between the initial research model and the cases.

## 6. Revisiting Episodes of SITBM: Inductive Case Findings

During the inductive analysis step, we refined and extended our initial model with newly emerging constructs and propositions. As we only gained new insights concerning the data collection and analysis phase, in the next sections, we will focus on these two phases.

## 6.1 Data Collection Phase

## Project Team Member Commitment

SITBM data collection progressed differently in the cases, but project team members’ commitment influenced the process strongly. At Alpha, all the project team members were highly com mitted to providing good data. This reflected in their enthusiasm when data collection started:

“This special situation [due diligence] caused some excitement, but positive excitement, throughout all levels of ITM. […] Thereby, all the people involved took this project seriously and, specifically, did not regard it as threat but as a chance to show their actual value contribution to the company” (Alpha external project manager in his post-project summary).

In addition, the project sponsor encouraged this positive excitement throughout the entire project by stressing the project’s importance and providing the resources needed for data collection. The project team members’ high level of commitment was also reflected by the efforts made to discuss the data inconsistencies during the internal coordination workshops. This commitment influenced the results’ quality positively and fostered shared interpretations.

Beta’s project team members had initially also been highly committed. Many of them were enthusiastic about the methodological approach, which allowed them to contribute their perspectives to the new IT strategy. However, this commitment decreased steadily up to the point where there was none: people did not put much effort into providing reliable data and reverted to rough estimations or ignored inconsistencies:

“When I suggested that the middle managers had to be more involved in document and data collection, one of the middle managers commented that people here are busy with daily operations and that it would be good if the additional efforts the project caused could be minimized” (Beta consultant in his post-project reflection).

Consequently, the results’ quality was moderate. Turning to Gamma, again the project team members had been highly enthusiastic about the approach to SITBM, and this high level of commitment remained stable throughout the project. This was possible because Gamma’s CIO not only continually emphasized the project’s importance but also provided sufficient resources for it. As a result, the quality of the data delivered was high and there were almost no inconsistencies.

Based on these observations, we added the project team members’ commitment as a new construct in the data collection phase. It refers to the degree to which a project team member actively participates during data collection and analysis by putting best efforts into getting precise and complete information and interpreting them adequately. Furthermore, we changed our initial proposition P4 on the role of project sponsorship quality and added two new propositions:

P4 (adjusted): A high degree of project sponsorship quality influences the commitment of the stakeholders in an SITBM project positively.

P5 (new): A high degree of methodological quality influences project team members’ commitment in an SITBM positively.

P6 (new): A high degree of project team members’ commitment influences the quality of the benchmarking results positively.

In the 1970s, Salancik (1977) already stressed the importance of project team member commitment for project success: only when project team members are committed to working toward a project goal will they do their best to reach it. Since then, commitment has been established as a key facilitator for many kinds of projects (Jha & Iyer, 2007; Kappelmann et al., 2006; Meyer & Herscovitch, 2001). In particular, IS projects achieve a higher quality when the project team members are committed to them (Sabherwal et al., 2003). To foster commitment, the project team members should not just understand what they have to do, but also why. In this context, top management support – through a high degree of project sponsorship quality – has often been stressed because project team members then better understand the requirements needed for the project output and their role regarding these requirements (Clayton & Luchs, 1994; Dezdar & Ainin, 2011; Gattiker & Carter, 2010). Furthermore, project team members should be

#

convinced that the methodological approach will help produce high-quality results (Carpinetti & de Melo, 2002; Chang et al., 2010) as this fosters buy-in, motivation, and commitment regarding applying the methodology (e.g., Chang et al., 2010). In this light, it is surprising that contemporary benchmarking researchers have disregarded commitment-related constructs – especially because most benchmarking project team members have to undertake data collection parallel with their routine work (Camp, 1989; Hinton et al., 2000). Without a sufficient degree of commitment, project team members may lower the priority of the benchmarking initiative in favor of routine tasks and put less effort into data collection.

## Participatory Project Style

Keeping these insights in mind, we took a deeper look at our second case, Beta, where project team members’ commitment had diminished dramatically. At first, we assumed that there was a lack of project sponsorship quality, because the project employees neither prioritized the project nor had they been given sufficient resources to collect data:

“We have pressing issues in our day-to-day business. We hire external consultants for the strategic part of our business. […] We do, of course, expect them to do their work and deliver what they promised” (Beta manager of application development when asked why involved project team members did not put effort into the data collection).

The CIO argued that a lack of resources or priority could not be the cause of the poor commitment and the consultants confirmed the CIO’s arguments. Inductive analysis revealed that the way discussions were conducted had an impact on the stakeholders’ behavior: discussions were not constructive and there was little room for an open exchange of views. Whenever the project team started discussions, or reflected on different interpretations, the CIO intervened, pushing her interpretations or stifling discussions. She often made statements like:

“Such discussions are largely unproductive. It is obvious that the solution has to be the following… [she rearranged the post-its of the SWOT analysis on the white board]” (Beta CIO during the first workshop).

This behavior explains the project team members’ passivity: although there was sufficient project sponsorship quality per se, project team members felt that their opinion was not really taken into account and they decided to put their efforts into more fruitful tasks. Thus, commitment was retracted in the interviews and data collection. With this observation in mind, we looked at Gamma and found the reverse situation. Here, project team members were explicitly encouraged to state and discuss their views. Many of them followed this call, which led to several disputes, for instance:

“You go totally overboard with security – this boosts our costs, as well as the complexity of our infrastructure, hugely. I don’t think it’s necessary to this degree”. (Gamma chief technology officer to chief information security officer in a data consolidation workshop) “You are totally wrong. It’s absolutely necessary. And there is no way we can do it differently or we would not have the level of security we need” (chief information security officer replying).

One of the external team members later reflected on this discussion:

“This conflict was to be expected – both guys pursue different goals in the organization. […] From this perspective, the project was also a catalyst for the explication of hidden dissonances” (Gamma consultant after data consolidation workshop).

This constructive, discourse-oriented project atmosphere at Gamma had a positive effect on project team members’ commitment as compared to the “no discussions” project style at Beta, which had a negative effect. Consequently, we added a new construct participatory project style to our data collection phase, which we defined as the degree to which a constructive and inclusive project atmosphere is prevailing during the SITBM project (i.e., the project team members act in a goal-oriented rather than opportunistic fashion). Moreover, we propose:

#

P7a (new): A highly participatory project style influences project team member commitment in an SITBM project positively.

Scholars in different research streams have researched participatory behavior. For example, researchers on strategic decision making have shown that considering all team members’ opinions during the decision process (Korsgaard et al., 1995), a positive climate for dissent, and teams’ ability to resolve disagreements (Rowland & Parry, 2009) influence the implementation potential of the decisions made positively. In addition, a participatory project style leads to higher degrees of commitment among the actors involved in a project (Locke et al., 1988). It thus increases task solving efficiency and effectiveness, because “high levels of involvement and participation create a sense of ownership and responsibility” (Denison & Mishra, 1995, p. 214). Furthermore, research on ITM has long identified the advantages of open-minded approaches to strategic planning: those approaches that focus on actively incorporating all relevant actors lead to the highest acceptance of the IT strategy (Earl, 1993; Segars & Grover, 1999). Such incorporation is particularly important during the strategic analysis and strategy conception phase (Mentzas, 1997; Mueller et al., 2009b). However, research lacks understanding of how such participative approaches help overcome resistance to change and foster SITBM success. In particular, few benchmarking researchers stress the notion of project team members’ involvement during the benchmarking data collection phase (e.g., Bhutta & Huq, 1999; Ohinata, 1994; Ramabadran et al., 2004). They mostly refer to having the “right” specialists in the benchmarking team because their participation will increase the data quality as they can provide more accurate information. This is remarkable, because Hinton et al. (2000) already observed that both practitioners and researchers lack understanding of the relevance and effects of actors’ involvement in benchmarking.

## 6.2 Data Analysis Phase

## Satisfaction with Benchmarking Results

Our first finding in this phase is that the quality of the benchmarking results is not as important as the way the quality is perceived. For example, although the results’ quality was mediocre at Beta, many insights were preserved and presented by the consultants in the fourth workshop. The participants, though, did not perceive the results as useful:

“I can’t see how these aspects contribute to our strategy development or to the understanding of where we stand” (Beta IT manager during the fourth workshop).

We took a deeper look at Gamma and found the reverse situation. We found that the overall data quality was good, but several data points were imprecise. The stakeholders were, however, satisfied with the results and perceived them as valuable for their new strategy. Specifically, they had a shared understanding of the results, and mostly agreed with each other regarding possible future measures. Comparing Beta and Gamma in this regard revealed a major difference between the two cases. Gamma’s project team members were committed and, due to the project’s participatory project style, discussions were fostered. Through these discussions, Gamma’s team had gained a certain confidence regarding their IT organization’s strengths and weaknesses even before they received the SITBM results. The latter were then used to confirm their assumptions, identify further areas of improvement, and help prioritize strategic initiatives. At Beta, both project team member commitment and the degree to which the project style was participatory were low; varying views were not exchanged or discussed. Consequently, points of discussion and surprising findings were not discussed in the fourth workshop either, but regarded as wrong conclusions or totally ignored. Another interesting effect was that Beta’s project team members, like those at Gamma, had already gained a certain understanding of their strengths and weaknesses during data collection. However, when they found that the benchmarking results confirmed their assumptions and understanding, they showed little satisfaction with the results:

“Didn’t we know all that already? Is what you show us really new? We discussed all these issues during the interviews with [the consultant conducting the interviews]” (Beta IT manager during the fourth workshop).

This reaction was in strong contrast to Gamma, where the same situation emerged, but was perceived differently by the participants. In sum, these observations led us to several modifications of our initial model. First, we changed and renamed our construct quality of benchmarking results to satisfaction with benchmarking results, which refers to the degree to which a project team member is satisfied with the quality of the benchmarking results in terms of usefulness, reliability, and validity. Second, we added a new proposition to our theoretical model of SITBM success based on our observation of the project styles’ impact at Beta and Gamma:

P7b (new): A high degree of participatory project style influences satisfaction with the benchmarking results positively.

Prior research on strategic decision-making points toward similar effects when involving stakeholders in decision processes. For instance, Locke et al. (1986) state that knowledgeable individuals should be given the chance to contribute to the results. They argue that this contribution will not only increase the quality of results, but also satisfaction with and buy-in of the decisions made. Such buy-in is a crucial antecedent for implementing strategic decisions (Korsgaard et al., 1995; Meyer & Herscovitch, 2001). Furthermore, it has been shown that higher levels of commitment among actors involved in a project are positively related to satisfaction with work outcomes (Leung et al., 2004; Mowday et al., 1982). In previous benchmarking research, few researchers have discussed satisfaction with the results. For instance, Ramabadran et al. (2004) surveyed factors related to project team behavior during data collection and analysis, as well as partner characteristics. They demonstrate that a participatory project atmosphere influences satisfaction with the benchmarking results positively. However, they do not show if and how this satisfaction contributes to benchmarking success. In addition, Lacity and Hirschheim (1995) suggest including data validation meetings with the relevant actors in the benchmarking process to foster the data quality, and, consequently, satisfaction with the results. However, they also do not explain how results satisfaction relates to successful benchmarking. Conversely, our model posits that this relationship is mediated by the individual project team member’s willingness to act.

## Willingness to Act

At Alpha, the project team members – who mainly came from the company’s IT division, not from the project unit – perceived the project unit’s capabilities as rather weak. The benchmarking results and various discussions in the final workshop partially confirmed this perception. They showed that the project unit’s methodology had certain flaws, which were compensated for by the individual project managers’ wide experience and skills. The project unit’s manager therefore did not expect a bad benchmarking result:

“I am really surprised by these results. I perceive my team differently: they are all experienced project managers using their own approaches. Just because we don’t follow these [standardized approaches analyzed in the benchmarking], does not mean that we are doing a bad job” (Alpha project unit manager during the final workshop).

She did agree that the project unit should put more effort into communication and codification of their approaches, but also stressed that she was not willing to initiate further actions and refused to see any improvement potentials. By contrast, the IT unit’s team consented with most of the interpretations and were eager to start improvement initiatives. Finally, the SITBM project sponsor at Alpha kicked-off several initiatives, but there was no willingness to do so in the project unit. Consequently, Alpha’s SITBM was only partly successful. In our other two cases, we found little willingness to act at Beta and high willingness at Gamma. Beta did not use the benchmarking results any further, while Gamma started a strategy revision project based on the results. Gamma’s CIO reflected the situation:

“By discussing all these issues in the large project team, the project ensured that each of the key players knew all the relevant issues. And as everyone could contribute to our final outcome, they were eager to follow up on the ideas we had generated and to implement the new IT strategy” (Gamma CIO in his post-project summary).

Consequently, we added willingness to act as a new construct to our theoretical model, capturing the degree to which a project team member is willing to execute the actions that the SITBM team has defined as a result of the data analysis phase. It mediates the relationship between satisfaction with the benchmarking results and SITBM success. Accordingly, our initial proposition P3 was split into two new propositions:

P3 (adjusted): A high degree of satisfaction with benchmarking results influences the willingness to act positively.

P8 (new): A high degree of willingness to act influences SITBM success positively.

Previous research in other fields also supports these propositions. As depicted in the previous section, all the team members must back strategic decisions to implement them successfully (Korsgaard et al., 1995; Raman, 009). In this context, both team member commitment and the satisfaction with the results are key antecedents of willingness to act (Salancik, 1977, p. 74). Similar notions can be found in the research stream on commitment continuity in projects, in which satisfaction with the outcomes of preceding steps influences the willingness to follow a course of action (Bateman & Zeithaml, 1989; Neale, 1983). A negative drawback of such continued engagement is that, for example, projects that are about to fail are provided with more resources (Sabherwal et al., 2003). In SITBM, this effect also has positive implications: complex strategic undertakings will be pursued even when difficulties arise (Weissbein et al., 1998). In benchmarking research, we find no pertinent studies that investigate the role of willingness to act, or that address how and under which conditions benchmarking results are transformed into further actions, even though this seems to be a problem many practitioners face. Therefore, our finding can be regarded as a first step toward addressing this problem.

## Project Sponsor Goal Alignment

Having understood the effect of willingness to act, we took another look at the three project sponsors’ reaction to the benchmarking results: in all three cases, this reaction seemed to impact their willingness to act. For instance, at Alpha we remembered a comment the project sponsor had made at the beginning of the benchmarking project:

“I want an independent voice in favor of process improvements outside my regime” (Alpha’s manager responsible for the IT unit at the beginning of the project).

However, what he meant by “outside my regime” only became evident after the project had been completed: he wanted to demonstrate to the project unit’s manager that she needed to improve her unit. One of the external team members clarified this:

“In the end, I feel, they [the CIO and project unit’s manager] were in competition with each other. [The CIO] was responsible, but could only influence one half. […] [The project unit’s manager] had obvious weaknesses in her unit, but she refused to believe this...” (Alpha external senior executive in a post-project interview).

This hidden agenda influenced the project strongly: the project unit was not incorporated or even informed about the project. Instead, it was intentionally confronted with the rather unpleasant results for the first time during the final workshop. Consequently, the unit’s manager refused to reflect more deeply on the findings or take any action. The project sponsor’s strategy did not work: despite concessions concerning communication and the codification of the project unit’s approaches, no initiatives were actually defined; the willingness to act was low. Looking at Beta, the CIO’s hidden agenda had a negative impact on the whole benchmarking project and its success. As the SITBM progressed, it became apparent that the CIO had already decided what the new IT strategy would look like; she only needed an “objective” justification. Therefore, whenev-

#

er she felt that potential results might differ from the outcomes she desired, she intervened by interrupting her team’s discussions or by readjusting the strategic agenda after she had purported to agree with it. Accordingly, when the benchmarking results were discussed, the CIO publicly refused to accept or act on them: they were not in line with her actual goals; there was no will ingness to act. At Gamma, we found no indications suggesting that the project sponsor had a hidden agenda. Conversely, the CIO openly communicated his personal objectives to the project stakeholders from the start. He was also open toward the results, although his team’s findings were not always in line with his interpretations. By letting the team convince him of most of their suggestions, the CIO encouraged their willingness to act. The project team members started the initiatives defined with great enthusiasm: the SITBM was successful.

Reflecting on these insights, we added a new construct, project sponsor goal alignment, to our model. Project sponsor goal alignment refers to the degree to which the SITBM results are aligned with the actual goals of the project sponsor(s), regardless of whether these goals are stated openly or take the form of hidden agendas. In addition, we added a final proposition to our model:

P9 (new): A high degree of project sponsor goal alignment influences the willingness to act positively.

Only few benchmarking researchers point toward the phenomenon of project sponsor goal alignment. For instance, Elmuti and Kathawala (1997) maintain that executives are less willing to implement benchmarking results when these contradict their perceptions. Further, Love et al. (1998) report on three cases in which the results of a strategic benchmarking endeavor were not in line with executives’ expectations. However, they did not report any effects of this lacking expectation alignment. Finally, Lacity and Hirschheim (1995) report that IT executives manipulated their benchmarking results to gather a good “report card” (p. 174) that supported their goals. These authors also show that such behavior fosters mistrust in an IT organization’s actual performance and in the IT executives. In IT project management research, we find that although goal alignment and hidden agendas have been acknowledged (e.g., Grover et al., 1988; Moynihan, 2002), only a few publications have specifically investigated the effects of goal alignment (e.g., Sarker & Lee, 1999), specifically regarding decision-making (Winterfeldt, 1983). ## 7. Discussion: Individuals’ Impact on SITBM Success

Previous research on SITBM success suffers from three important limitations. First, while extant studies propose a number of SITBM success factors, much of this research treats these factors as unrelated concepts. As a result, little is known about these factors’ mutual interdependencies and detailed effects on SITBM success (Hong et al. 2012; Moriarty and Smallman 2009). Second, prior models of benchmarking success focus solely on the project level, thus ignoring the social dynamics that are typical for projects with strategic implications (Ebner 2014; Ketter et al. 2016). Third, despite more than 30 years of benchmarking research, existing research often fails to explain SITBM success in a satisfactory way due to a conceptual gap between existing success factors and actual benchmarking success (Anand & Kodali, 2008; Ebner et al. 2016a).

Our work contributes to address these limitations by providing a more comprehensive view of SITBM success. To that end, we not only integrate existing success factors into one model for SITBM success to foster understanding of their interdependencies. We extend these factors with further concepts and refine their mutual relationships. In particular, and in line with related research on IS strategizing, we stress the significance of the individuals acting in benchmarking projects with strategic implications (Peppard et al. 2014). Reflecting on our findings, we find that our new or updated constructs nomologically reflect two different levels of analysis (Figure 3): the project level and the individual level (Bélanger et al. 2014; Hox 2010). Multilevel models are of particular relevance to organizational research in an information systems context because they reflect the interplay between technologies and human actors as well as the interaction between different organizational entities (Aubert et al. 2008). In this context, researchers frequently differentiate between a micro level and a macro level: the micro level focuses on an individual’s behavior or perceptions (=individual level), while the macro level focuses on the actions and behavior of some larger entity, such as a (project) team, organization, or industry (=project level in our case) (Bélanger et al. 2014). Hence, in our case, the individual level reflects the behavior and perceptions of the individual project team members involved in and affected by an SITBM, while the project level represents project-related procedures, decisions and all activities related to managing the project (Aubert et al. 2008). In contrast to the individual level, these procedures, decisions, and activities are shaped by a collective of individuals and their complex social interactions rather than single individuals alone.

We depict our revised model in Figure 3 and highlight three observations. First, the success factors addressed in previous research all represent project-related procedures, decisions and managerial aspects, thus reside on the project level. Second, the individual-level constructs form an integral chain of effects toward SITBM success. In particular, the key antecedents of SITBM success relate to individual acceptance and buy-in into the SITBM results (represented by satisfaction with the results and the individual willingness to act). Third, the project-level constructs facilitate the effects on the individual level. Compared to our initial model, the resulting effects are a lot more nuanced than assumed in previous research. We therefore contribute to closing a conceptual gap between benchmarking results and benchmarking success by introducing a more multifaceted perspective on SITBM. In doing so, we hope to direct future researchers’ attention more on the individuals acting in SITBM projects. With these new insights gained, we can now understand how and why the different outcomes of our three cases originated. In Beta, our unsuccessful case, we observe low degrees for all our new constructs. In particular, we find an overall negative effect chain on the individual level. As a result, the seemingly quite professional project setup that had been defined on the project level during the preparation phase of the SITBM had been completely overshadowed by the negative mood between the different project team members. These members, in turn, were affected by problematic project level effects (i.e., low participatory project style and management goal alignment). The opposite was true for Gamma, our successful case. Not only did the CIO opt for a sophisticated SITBM setup during the preparation phase, he assured a participatory project style throughout the whole project that very positively affected the project team members’ commitment and overall atmosphere. Because the CIO was also very transparent on his goals related to the SITBM, the project team members could act in a

#

goal-oriented fashion and were later eager to implement the results from the SITBM. With Alpha, our partly successful case, the situation was, in overall, very similar to Gamma – with one difference: Alpha had two independent IT units – one responsible for operations, one for projects. Alpha’s CIO was responsible for the operations unit and excluded the project unit from the SITBM project. When the project unit’s members were confronted with the – for them surprising and not very positive results – they were not satisfied and not willing to implement any actions. Because the manager of the project unit was also not involved, there was no project sponsor goal alignment on this site, thus further contributing to the low willingness to act on the project site. By contrast, the high commitment and willingness act of the operations unit’s project team members lead to many subsequent initiatives. This dichotomy led to Alpha’s partial success in SITBM.

With our work, we also shed new light on previous findings that have started examining social and individual dynamics of benchmarking initiatives. For instance, we extend work by Drew (1997) and Ramabadran et al. (2004) who were among the first to recognize that team problems are barriers to benchmarking success. While Drew (1997) only analyzes problems with the benchmarking partners, Ramabadran et al. (2004) extend this focus toward the benchmarking team. They find that both the communication style between the actors involved and their behaviors toward each other have an impact on the satisfaction with the benchmarking results. Still, they were not able to confirm a conceptual link between satisfaction with the benchmarking results and success. We hold that our findings regarding the individual level could offer one possible explanation for the missing conceptual link: similar to other authors (e.g., Camp, 1989; Elmuti & Kathawala, 1997), Drew (1997) and Ramabadran et al. (2004) focus on the project level in their analyses and do not consider interactions with individual level constructs. We do not only challenge previous research’s prevailing assumption that the benchmarking team members will do their best to provide high-quality data. By introducing willingness to act as a mediator between results satisfaction and SITBM success, we provide insights into the yet unanswered question of why and under which conditions benchmarking results are transformed into outcome.

Our findings regarding the multilevel nature of SITBM success also connect with similar findings in the larger IT strategy discourse. For example, commitment-related factors have been found to positively influence the success of strategic information systems planning (Galliers, 1991). With strategic information systems planning a part of overall IS strategy, it seems reasonable that SITBM as a preceding exercise developing inputs for IS strategizing will also this project feeds into the IT strategizing process (willingness to act).

In a similar vein, the importance in recognizing the individual level in IT strategy related activities such as SITBM is also in line with the literature’s recent turn toward practices (Karpovsky et al. 2014; Peppard et al., 2014; Whittington, 2014). Much like this stream of research, we argue that only the understanding of what people are actually doing and why they are doing it allows for a true conceptual appreciation of the complexity of IS strategy in practice. Correspondingly, our research suggests that SITBM should also be seen less as a process following a number of strict stages (Jarzabkowski and Spee 2009). Rather, interlocked and often emergent group processes may play a conceptually more decisive role. While our shift toward the individual level is a first hint in that direction, future research will have to dive deeper into the relevant actors’ interplay to extend the early understanding we provide here. Our research helps managers understand how they should design their SITBM endeavors so that they not only produce output, but outcome. Specifically, they will understand that all the relevant stakeholders should be actively involved in projects with a strategic context and that IT executives’ behavior may influence the outcomes of such projects positively or negatively. Hence, managing and conducting SITBM projects is more complex than most practice guidelines for benchmarking suggest. In this light, our revised model provides a guide that practitioners can use to understand which aspects have to be considered during the various phases of an SITBM project. This helps them anchor the results in the organization. Similarly, our insights is also important for the design of SITBM instruments (e.g., Ebner et al., 2016b). In this vein, we provide added insight into the various stakeholders these instruments need to address and involve in order to provide the degree of commitment and willingness to act our research reveals.

## 8. Conclusion

The objective of our research was to provide a more holistic understanding of the factors that differentiate between successful (i.e., outcome-producing) and unsuccessful (i.e., only output-producing) SITBM. Although we confirmed the success factors that previous literature identified, we found that incorporating a multilevel view provides a better understanding of SITBM success. The social dynamics between the stakeholders and the context in which they act influenced the entire benchmarking project and its outcomes across our three cases. In order to counter common concerns about case-based research (Patton, 2002), we rely on established quality criteria for positivist, qualitative research (Dubé & Paré, 2003; Gibbert et al., 2008). Nevertheless, the generalizability of our findings should be approached with caution: we focused on IT organizations of medium to large companies in different industries and we can thus not know whether spun-off or outsourced IT departments would follow a similar SITBM approach. Additionally, the companies chosen constitute a convenience sample from one geographic region (Germany). It is possible that the cultural homogeneity of the sample impacts our findings. For instance, the interplay of the individual and project level constructs we proposed might play out differently in groups characterized by a higher power distance. Regardless of these caveats, we argue that our findings allow for analytical generalizability

#

(Lee & Baskerville, 2003) because the theoretical replication in our cases fosters generalization toward theory (Yin, 2009). It would, however, be fruitful to see how successful SITBM initiatives progress in other settings. Future research could replicate our findings and extend the research to smaller companies, other organizational setups, or new geographic regions. Another aspect that may be investigated by future research is a reconsideration of the role of prior benchmarking experiences. While we decided to exclude it from our revised model for the reasons discussed earlier, we made an interesting observation: all our case companies actually had benchmarking experiences, but they impacted the course of the projects very differently. For instance, Alpha, due to their experiences, strongly focused on the instrument perspective initially (thus, trying to prevent potential pitfalls in that regard), while G amma had decided to try out a completely new SITBM approach they had no experience with instead of improving their past approaches. Further, Beta’s CIO, based on her experience, fostered the emergence of specific, targeted results in line with her goals (and, thi way, hampered the project’s success). Consequently, prior benchmarking experiences ma not necessarily have a positive effect on benchmarking success, but their effects seem to be more nuanced. By addressing these effects, future research could potentially also better explain some of the conflicting results of past research regarding the effects of prior benchmarking experiences.
