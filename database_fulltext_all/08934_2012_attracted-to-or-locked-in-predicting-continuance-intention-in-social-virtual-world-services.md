Abstr act: Internet-based social virtual world (SVW) services have aroused extensive interest among academicians and practitioners. The success of SVW services depends heavily on customers’ continuance usage, a topic not yet adequately investigated in information systems research. It is unclear to what extent, and how, the existing theories can be extended to explain the continuance usage of such services. In consideration of the distinctive features of these services, this study adapts the dedication-constraint framework of commitment and develops a model of SVW continuance, which is assessed empirically using data collected from 438 experienced users of Second Life, a typical SVW service. Results indicate that SVW customers’ continuance intention is jointly determined by two mechanisms: affective commitment (being attracted to) and calculative commitment (being locked in), with the former playing a more central role. Perceived utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital promote affective commitment directly and indirectly through satisfaction, while service-specific investments in personalization and relational capital increase calculative commitment. Theoretical and practical implications and future research directions are subsequently discussed.

Key word s and p hr ases: commitment, continuance intention, dedication-constraint dual model, Second Life, social virtual world services.

The exp losiv e d if usion and ev olution of the Inter net in recent years have fostered the proliferation of social virtual world (SVW) services. Historically rooted in multiuser domains/dungeons (MUDs) and virtual reality [62], SVW services are deemed as transformational technologies that will become mainstream within the next few years [57, 63]. Originating as environments for play, today’s SVW services have gained legitimacy in business as well as in educational and government settings for their application to serious endeavors such as distributed collaboration, multimedia meetings and training, and real-time simulation [62, 63]. Indeed, tens of millions of consumers have participated in SVW services [63]. Famous companies have invested millions of dollars in SVW services for advertising, meetings, recruitment, training, prototyping, and simulation [52, 64]. Social institutions, such as universities, hospitals, and the military, are using SVW services for action learning, immersive training, and innovation [51, 63, 64].

Despite tremendous investments, SVWs have experienced a decline in their customer bases in recent years, and are thus receiving a worrisome signal with regard to their prospects [57, 62]. Since a sound customer base is a prerequisite for the survival of an SVW [41], failure to retain existing customers would seriously jeopardize the viability of SVW services. This observation is echoed by the network effects and critical mass theory of interactive media, which has long suggested that the diffusion and success of Internet-based services, SVW services being one of them, hinge on the retention of a “critical mass” of persistent customers [50]. Moreover, as compared to attracting new customers, retaining existing customers is more cost-effective [7, 42]. It is widely held that “the relative costs of customer retention are substantially less than those of acquisition” [55, p. 33]. This is especially true in SVW services, wherein the continuous participation of customers is pivotal to the realization of desired goals. Therefore, understanding why customers are willing to continue using a particular SVW service is critical to customer retention and the ultimate success of the service.

Despite this practical pertinence, the extant information systems (IS) literature is limited in offering a theoretically grounded understanding of customer continuance with SVW services. An increasing number of studies have investigated the continuance issue with respect to traditional online services (e.g., Web portals) by adopting appropriate theories [13, 42]; however, they are limited in their ability to offer insights specific to the SVW service context. As we argue in our study, SVW services, as a special type of online service, have a number of distinct features (e.g., broad and openended applications, immersive presence interfaces, and collaborative platforms) that render the existing theories inadequate in explaining customer continuance with SVW services. Clearly, there is a strong need to extend the extant literature by developing and empirically examining a research model of SVW service continuance.

With that motivation, this study aims to address the following research question:

## RQ: What factors and mechanisms affect customer continuance intention in SVW services?

To answer this question, we develop a research model to explain customer continuance intention with respect to SVW services by drawing upon the recent literature on SVW services and customer commitment to online service continuance [42, 44]. Specifically, we build on the dedication-constraint framework [5] by integrating the concepts of affective and calculative commitment [44] to represent two distinctive commitment mechanisms leading to SVW service continuance intention, and identifying an encompassing set of antecedents to commitment specific to the SVW service context. Empirical data are collected from field users of a popular SVW service (i.e., Second Life) to test the model.

This study makes several contributions to the emerging literature on SVW services. First, our study is among the first to provide theoretical insights into understanding user continuance with SVW services. In substantiating this theoretical contribution, we extend the dedication-constraint model of commitment by incorporating a set of antecedents and intervening variables that are unique to the SVW service context. Second, while the dedication-constraint theory of commitment has been used to explain the postadoption of traditional online services [42], our findings contribute to this theory by adapting it (and hence extending its applicability) to the SVW service context. Finally, our study offers rich insights into retaining the viability of the SVW service for practitioners in the emerging virtual world.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the SVW literature to discuss the distinctions between different types of SVW services. Second, we review the existing literature explaining user continuance with traditional online services and discuss the need to advance a theory specific to the SVW service context. Our theoretical model and hypotheses are then developed in the subsequent section, followed by a detailed description of our methodology and results. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and managerial implications of the research, its limitations, and the directions for future research.

## Theoretical Background—Social Virtual World Services

SVW serv ices ar e thr ee-d imensional (3D) immersive multi-user virtual environments wherein customers use their virtual representatives (i.e., avatars) to interact with, and within, these environments for various purposes [9, 16, 27]. SVW services are distinct from other traditional online services in terms of their open-ended, immersive, and collaborative nature.

First, SVW services are open-ended, allowing users to explore the virtual world for diverse purposes [41, 75]. Many SVW services are designed to be open-ended to allow the possibility of users’ pursuing multiple ends by integrating multiple areas such as games, education, and business [51]. From the users’ perspective, SVW service users do not need to base their behavior on predefined themes; rather, they enjoy the autonomy of determining their own virtual experiences [52], which empowers users to pursue a variety of valued goals in the virtual world. Indeed, the literature has shown that work and play blur in SVWs [9], driving users to participate for both utilitarian (e.g., commercial and educational) and hedonic reasons [16, 75]. Furthermore, group collaboration and social activities are also available in SVW services to fulfill user needs for social bonding [27]. As a result, the potential value of using SVW services can be multifaceted, providing a broad range of utilitarian, hedonic, and social benefits.

Second, compared with traditional online services, SVW services are immersive. SVW services make users immersive to the virtual world by allowing them to create their own 3D avatars and to construct a unique experience of living [27]. Likewise, by integrating other innovative features such as 3D interface design and voice communication, SVW services involve multiple senses that induce the impression and experience of immersion [38].

Third, SVW services are collaborative systems that facilitate social presence [27]. Concurrently supporting a large number of users, SVW services are persistent 3D virtual environments with social networking capabilities. Focusing on creating computer-mediated, shared experiences within which participants can interact [11], SVW services support a wide range of socializing activities, such as real-time text and audio-based conversations, playing and creating together, developing friendships, and forming social networks [80]. All these activities have the potential of profoundly affecting the way people interact in the future [51]. For instance, as one of the recent SVW features, Second Life started providing its premium members with virtual Linden Homes, “a private, secure and stylish place to hang out with [your] friends” and to facilitate members to “entertain, socialize, and meet new friends in the neighborhood” (http://secondlife.com/land/lindenhomes/). Hence, the facilitation of social networks and communities is more salient in SVWs than in other online services, such as portals or game-oriented virtual worlds [27].

While each of these distinctions may not be necessarily unique (e.g., an online social network is also collaborative), SVWs are distinct in that they possess these features simultaneously. Bearing in mind that the usage context of any information technology (IT) artifact is essential in explaining user behavior [70], when we review the existing relevant literature on user continuance, it is instrumental to advance the theory so as to meaningfully account for factors that manifest these distinctive features. In the next section, the existing theories in the IS continuance literature are reviewed, before we elaborate how they can be adapted and extended to explain user continuance with SVW services.

## Theoretical Background—User Commitment to Online Services

User continuance behav ior with new technology has been explained in the IS literature based on two streams of thought: user satisfaction and commitment. User satisfaction has long been the predominant means of evaluating IS continuance [7, 33] and success [22, 25, 70]. However, drawing on the literature on relationship marketing [5] and organizational behavior [53], IS researchers have increasingly recognized the importance of commitment in predicting the volitional and continued use of organizational IT applications in general [49], and Web-based online services in particular [42, 44].

Commitment is defined as a “psychological state that compels an individual toward a course of action” [53, p. 303]. Commitment conceptually differs from satisfaction and is suggested as a better predictor of behavioral intention than satisfaction [68] for the following reasons. First, while satisfaction is “backward looking,” commitment is more “forward looking” [31], hence more action oriented. Second, commitment has various means by which to create a level of “stickiness” to keep customers loyal to a target service provider, even when the level of satisfaction may be low (e.g., too costly to terminate a dissatisfying service) [31]. Third, while satisfaction tends to be more transitory and to reflect a response to day-to-day events and changing environments, commitment is more enduring over time, and stable in the event of short-term changes in service performance and other situational influences [72]. As a result, commitment is often seen as an outcome of satisfaction, mediating the effect of satisfaction on behavioral intention [36, 72]. For these reasons and consistent with the prior literature on online services [42, 44], we anchor our investigation of user continuance with SVW services, a special type of online services, on the perspective of commitment.

The notion of commitment has recently been leveraged to investigate customer postadoption behavior with respect to online services. The dedication-constraint framework of commitment [5] is a major theoretical lens through which to view this issue. Based on social exchange theory, the dedication-constraint framework suggests that postadoption behavior results from two mechanisms that underlie an individual’s development of commitment: personal dedication and constraints. Personal dedication refers to the desire of an individual to maintain a relationship, which centers on the prospect of long-term mutual benefits [5, 67]. Constraint refers to the “forces that constrain individuals to maintain relationships regardless of their personal dedication to them” [67, pp. 595–596], centering on locked-in economic, social, or psychological investments that make it difficult for individuals to quit [5].

Building on this framework, Kim and Son [42] explain the postadoption behavior of online portal services by proposing a dedication-constraint dual model. The model uses loyalty to capture the personal dedication mechanism and switching costs to capture the constraints mechanism [42]. Their model further proposes that loyalty is primarily explained by two antecedents that represent benefits: perceived usefulness and satisfaction, whereas switching costs are affected by two other antecedents that represent service-specific investments: personalization and learning [42].

Li et al. [44] draw on the organizational commitment literature [1] to identify affective commitment and calculative commitment to explain continuance intention of online services. Affective commitment refers to the psychological state of a user demonstrating an affective and emotional attachment to the service [1], whereas calculative commitment (also known as continuance commitment) occurs when the user determines that the costs associated with terminating his or her use of a service or switching to another one are too high [44]. Li et al. [44] further suggest that trust and the quality of alternatives are antecedents to these two types of commitment within the Web service context.

While these previous studies are useful in predicting online services continuance in general, they are inadequate to provide nuanced insights into user continuance intention with SVW services. First, the dedication-constraint dual model developed to explain the postadoption of online portal services uses switching costs, defined as one’s perception of the investments made in one portal that are nontransferable to another portal, to represent the constraint-based mechanism of commitment development [42]. This construct is appropriate in the context of online portal services, one that is indispensable to today’s online customers. Indeed, it is reported that over 80 percent of Internet users in the United States and China go online through an online portal [18, 76], suggesting that an online user’s discontinuance from one portal is most likely to involve switching to another portal. Unlike online portals, today’s SVW services are not indispensable to online users; thus, discontinuance with an SVW service could involve not only switching but also attrition. Indeed, SVWs are reported to be characterized by a high user attrition rate [40]. Customers who plan to withdraw from the current SVW may simply quit their virtual life adventure for good (i.e., completely returning to their real life) instead of switching to another SVW because SVWs are not necessary for many of them [40].

Second, the model may also be limited in its ability to capture the antecedents of commitment that are specific to SVW services. Kim and Son [42] define perceived usefulness and satisfaction as the enablers of the dedication-based mechanism, which is appropriate to the online portal service context because customers use online portals primarily for utilitarian purposes (e.g., information searching). However, as discussed earlier, users participate in SVWs to pursue a broader set of benefits (i.e., utilitarian, hedonic, and social benefits). Moreover, Kim and Son [42] identify only two types of service-specific investments as the antecedents of constraint-based commitment (i.e., personalization and learning). However, as a collaborative social virtual environment, SVW services can induce the users to make additional investments (e.g., the time and effort to cultivate trustful and respectful relationships), thus giving rise to the need for identifying the additional, relationship-related antecedents of constraint-based commitment (e.g., relational capital) [67].

Likewise, Li et al. [44] are limited in offering customized insights into the antecedents of commitment to SVW services for similar reasons. The quality of alternatives as a key antecedent of commitment to Web services [44] is less relevant to SVW services, as customers may quit without having available alternative SVWs per our earlier discussion. Given the limited theoretical grounding available to understand SVW services continuance through applying the commitment lens, it is compelling to advance a theory of commitment specific to the SVW service context. We do so in the next section.

## Theory Development and Research Hypotheses

We d ev elop our theor y by anchor ing on the dedication-constraint framework of commitment [5], which is also the theoretical base introduced by Kim and Son [42]. We believe that it is an appropriate theoretical base for our purpose because continuance intention with SVW services is subject to the dual consideration of benefits and service-specific investments. However, we substantially extend this framework by adapting it to the SVW context.

In extending this framework, we postulate that an SVW user’s continuance intention is determined by two distinctive, yet interrelated, mechanisms: affective commitment as the manifestation of the dedication mechanism and calculative commitment as the manifestation of the constraint mechanism. Furthermore, situating model development in the SVW context, we identify utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital as perceived benefits that predict affective commitment not only directly but also indirectly through user satisfaction. We then identify personalization, learning, and relational capital as service-specific investments that affect calculative commitment to SVW services. Note that relational capital is proposed to play a dual role in the two mechanisms (i.e., both a perceived benefit and a service-specific investment). ## Dedication-Based Mechanism

In our study, the concept of affective commitment plays a central role in the dedicationbased mechanism. Following the commitment literature [1, 4], we define affective commitment as a desire-based attachment that attracts a user into the relationship with SVW. This construct is somewhat similar to that of loyalty appearing in Kim and Son’s work, since loyalty is defined in their study as a “deeply held affective commitment” [42, p. 52]. However, we reason that it is more appropriate to use affective commitment here for the following reason: the dedication mechanism in the SVW context should be particularly characterized by users’ dedication to favorable experience, because, as discussed earlier, SVW services emphasize enriching users’ immersive experience in constructing their own virtual life [38]. Since the development of affective commitment is typically attributed to favorable experiences that fulfill one’s psychological needs for comfort and confidence [1], affective commitment better reflects one’s involvement in, and belonging to, a relationship due to a related rewarding experience [37]. In comparison, loyalty has a stronger bearing on the behavioral tendency to maintain an existing relationship [55], but with less emphasis on the experience that leads to the tendency. For these reasons, we use affective commitment as the central factor in the dedication-based mechanism leading to SVW services continuance.

Indeed, it has been long established that affective commitment enables an individual to continue in a relationship because of favorable attitudes, affect, emotion, and perceptions of his or her experience in the relationship [39]. As such, affective commitment is found to be an important predictor of intention to continue using online services [44, 49]. Based on the same theory, we expect that this relationship holds with SVW services. This expectation leads to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Users’ affective commitment to an SVW service is positively related to their continuance intention with the service.

According to the dedication-constraint framework, the dedication mechanism is enabled by the realization of net benefits, including satisfaction and perceived benefits [42]. Satisfaction refers to a customer’s favorable feelings toward a particular service [42] and has long been found to be an important factor affecting the continuance intention of using IT applications [7, 15, 33, 70]. Furthermore, numerous studies have suggested that satisfaction positively affects dedication, or affective commitment, in both offline and online business relationships [5, 36, 42, 72]. Consistent with the consensus reached in prior literature, we expect that satisfaction will be positively related to continuance intention with, and affective commitment to, SVW services. This expectation leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2: Users’ satisfaction with an SVW service is positively related to their continuance intention with the service.

Hypothesis 3: Users’ satisfaction with an SVW service is positively related to their affective commitment to the service.

Perceived benefits refer to users’ perceptions of the value of using an SVW service. As discussed earlier, relative to online portals wherein users normally stay for utilitarian reasons [42], SVW services, which are open-ended in nature, offer the possibility for users to attain benefits from a wider variety of sources. Based on the SVW literature, we specify three types of perceived benefits of using SVW services: utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital. While utilitarian value, which is not entirely new, is included in our model for the sake of theoretical completeness, hedonic value and relational capital are the new antecedents, which are more distinctive of the SVW context.

Utilitarian value is conceptually similar to perceived usefulness [34], which refers to users’ cognitive evaluation of the utility of using an SVW service in terms of purpose fulfillment and problem solving [3]. Prior research has reported that users are often motivated to use SVW services for accomplishing purposive tasks, such as learning, education, research, and many commercial endeavors [16, 27, 51, 52, 80]. Thus, SVW services can provide utilitarian value for their users across a wide variety of domains. The positive effect of utilitarian value on satisfaction has been demonstrated in a large number of studies [7, 15, 73, 75]. We expect this effect to hold within the SVW context:

Hypothesis 4: Users’ perception of utilitarian value derived from using an SVW service is positively related to their satisfaction with the service.

Based on prior research on online services, we suggest that utilitarian value may have a direct positive effect on affective commitment to SVW services. This is because users who find the SVW service useful for solving a problem or for meeting other needs are more likely to feel involved in (and attracted to) the service because the service fulfills their desires, leading to the formation of affective commitment [49]:

Hypothesis 5: Users’ perception of utilitarian value derived from using an SVW service is positively related to their affective commitment to the service.

Two more antecedents for the dedication-based mechanism highlight the distinctions of SVW services. One is hedonic value, defined as users’ positive emotions or feelings (e.g., fun and playfulness) that are derived from the appreciation of an experience for its own sake, rather than from task completion [3]. As discussed earlier, relative to many other online services (e.g., portals) and various IT productivity tools, the boundary between work and play blurs in SVWs [16], making hedonic usage commonplace in SVW services [75].

While hedonic value has been found to affect user satisfaction in various settings [15, 73], we argue that this effect is particularly salient in the context of SVW services because the entertainment component is embedded in the virtual world, in the form of exploration, game playing, escapism, and fantasy [80]. The fulfillment of these hedonic needs leads to user satisfaction with the Internet in general [66], and with the virtual world in particular [75]:

Hypothesis 6: Users’ perception of the hedonic value derived from using an SVW service is positively related to their satisfaction with the service.

Furthermore, SVW users’ perception of hedonic value serves as an inherent affective reward (e.g., happiness and pleasure) for their continuance behaviors [61], and consequently will likely promote their positive attitudes and psychological attachment (e.g., affective commitment) toward the service [39]. Therefore, hedonic value is proposed to also have a positive impact on affective commitment:

Hypothesis 7: Users’ perception of the hedonic value derived from using an SVW service is positively related to their affective commitment to the service.

The other type of perceived benefit is relational capital, which characterizes the social component of users’ perceived benefits. Relational capital is defined as the level of trust, respect, and friendliness that characterize the interpersonal relationships between an individual and his or her peers. Relational capital is applicable in the SVW context because of the high degree and salience of social interaction in SVW services [11]. Given the collaborative and social nature of SVWs, relational capital plays a critical role in motivating users to retain SVW services. Indeed, SVWs can profoundly change the way in which people communicate, socialize, and collaborate [51]. And building mutually trustful, respectful, and friendly relationships with peers is deemed to be one of the predominant activities for virtual life in SVW services [41]. By enabling the development of relationship capital, SVWs help fulfill users’ need to relate to others (i.e., relatedness [2]). The fulfillment of such a need brings users numerous desired benefits, thus leading to satisfaction [2, 66]:

Hypothesis 8: Users’ extent of relational capital in an SVW service is positively related to their satisfaction with the service.

Meanwhile, relational capital promotes users’ dedication or affective commitment to SVW services. Consistent with the dedication-constraint dual model [42], in which perceived benefits serve as the basis for the formation of dedication commitment, the rationale behind this proposition is that users’ current evaluation of potential benefits resulting from relating to other users in an SVW service is considered to be an important means by which to infer the future value of the service. Hence, in the existence of benefits such as relational capital, SVW users tend to desire or favor a long-term relationship with the provider, being in anticipation of future value regarding social relationships in SVWs. In this way, users would develop a personal dedication or affective commitment to an SVW that currently offers these benefits. By contrast, without these social benefits, or relational rewards, users would have fewer positive feelings or less psychological attachment toward the service [55]. Indeed, there has been evidence in the literature that social relationships characterized by high-level trust or friendship are positively associated with affective commitment [4, 44]. Therefore, we hypothesize that relational capital has a direct and positive effect on affective commitment:

Hypothesis 9: Users’ extent of relational capital in an SVW service is positively related to their affective commitment to the service.

## Constraint-Based Mechanism

To explain the constraint-based mechanism of the framework in the SVW context, we draw on the concept of calculative commitment [5]. Calculative commitment refers to the degree to which an individual recognizes that he or she is locked in a relationship because of the potential costs associated with discontinuance [1, 39, 44]. Rooted in the side-bet theory [1], calculative commitment has been treated in the relational marketing literature as a constraint-based force that binds the customer to the service provider [4]. While Kim and Son [42] use switching cost to manifest the constraintbased mechanism in the context of postadoption behavior of online portals, we indicated in the preceding section that it may not be appropriate to include switching cost in our model, because discontinuance with an SVW service could result from not only switching behavior but also from attrition. Calculative commitment better suits the SVW service context because it focuses on the concern with the cost associated with discontinuance in general.

The effect of calculative commitment on continuance intention has been long established in the organizational behavior literature [39]. In theory, calculative commitment forces a person to continue with a relationship because the cost of withdrawal is too high. Similarly, the IS literature has found that calculative commitment is an important predictor to continuance intention in online services [44]. We expect that this relationship holds in SVW services:

Hypothesis 10: Users’ calculative commitment to an SVW service is positively related to their continuance intention with the service.

The dedication-constraint framework suggests that the constraint mechanism of commitment is determined by the level of service-specific investments [1]. We identify three types of service-specific investments in the context of SVW services: personalization, learning, and relational capital [42, 71, 74]. Personalization and learning are hypothesized in ways consistent with the literature on traditional online services [42], and are included in our model for the sake of theoretical completeness. Relational capital, which was conceptualized earlier as a type of perceived benefit, is simultaneously considered as a distinct service-specific investment in the SVW context.

Personalization refers to the extent to which a system has been modified by a user to suit his or her own needs [42]. Learning refers to the amount of time and effort that has been spent by a user to learn the routines, procedures, and features of a particular service. In the online service context, learning costs reflect investments “sunk” into becoming familiar with, and acquiring skills required by, an online service [71]. Both personalization and learning have been identified as major sources of constraint commitment in online portal services, leading to the heightened cost associated with switching to an alternative service [42]. In the context of SVW services, investments in personalization and learning include the time and effort that a user spends in personalizing his or her experience, such as customizing an avatar and viewer preferences and in acquiring skills specific to the avatar’s virtual world. Like online portal services, SVW users’ investments in personalization and learning are not necessarily recoverable or transferable to another SVW or to real life. These investments would diminish or be completely lost if the user withdrew from the SVW service. Therefore, we expect that personalization and learning are positively associated with calculative commitment:

Hypothesis 11: Users’ extent of personalization in an SVW service is positively related to their calculative commitment to the service.

Hypothesis 12: Users’ extent of learning in an SVW service is positively related to their calculative commitment to the service.

While personalization and learning are common antecedents of the constraintbased mechanism in traditional online services and SVW services, relational capital is considered a service-specific investment unique in the context of SVW services. In contrast to personalization and learning, relational capital involves a relational investment in social bonding [12]. In other words, it represents a constraint associated with customers’ social dependence [67], which forces constraint-based commitment through increased dependence on a relationship partner [5]. The loss of relational capital, which means the breaking of social bonds, would cause psychological or emotional discomfort. Indeed, a close social relationship featuring mutual trust, respect, and intimacy underscores the economic and psychological investments (e.g., time, effort, and emotions) put into developing and maintaining the relationship. A large portion of these relationship-specific investments made in an SVW cannot be recovered once the user quits because these relationships are virtual, and are specific only to the concerned virtual world. Therefore, as with personalization and learning costs, relational capital serves as an important source of calculative commitment to SVW services. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 13: Users’ extent of relational capital in an SVW service is positively related to their calculative commitment to the service.

Furthermore, the constraint and dedication mechanisms are not independent from each other. Constraint commitment may positively influence dedication commitment due to a psychological process called “self-justification”—that is, users who are locked into a service tend to convince themselves that they have committed the resources because they like the service [42, 71]. As long as the service provider is not opportunistic, users will keep reinforcing their “postcommitment rationalization” that the past service-specific investments represent their dedication to the service provider [5]. Following this reasoning, we propose that calculative commitment will positively affect affective commitment in the context of SVW services. This leads to our final hypothesis:

Hypothesis 14: Users’ calculative commitment to an SVW service is positively related to their affective commitment to the service.

## Methodology

To test our r esear ch mod el, a survey study was conducted in Second Life, a typical SVW service [52]. Second Life is one of the Internet’s largest user-created 3D virtual world communities and supports a broad range of applications, including socializing, shopping, work, playing, and exploring (www.secondlife.com). Opened to the public in 2003, Second Life attracted about 24 million registered accounts by June 2011. At any time, around 30,000 users are in Second Life, clocking up to 20 million user hours every month [16]. The large number of active users in Second Life constitutes a valuable source of data for academic research.

Given the research site selected, real data from the “field” are requisite to empirically assessing the proposed model. In this study, an online questionnaire survey was used to collect data from real users in Second Life. Compared to a traditional paper-based survey, an online survey has a number of advantages, including a minimal amount of missing data, accurate and efficient data entry, as well as largely comparable reli ability, validity, and quality [10].

## Measures

To ensure validity, all of the measures were adapted from previous studies to fit the context of this study (Appendix A). We assessed all the measures for their content validity using card sorting [54] and used a pilot study to test for internal validity by assessing the nature of the relationships among the constructs.

The use of self-reported measures in this survey is believed to be necessary and appropriate because all the constructs evaluate customers’ cognition, emotion, and intention, rather than behavior. All the scale items used a seven-point Likert scale, which ranged from 1 = “extremely disagree” to 7 = “extremely agree,”<sup>2</sup> except for satisfaction, which was measured with four items based on a seven-point semantic differential scale [7].

We measured all ten constructs as first-order reflective constructs with multiple items. It is noted that, in the literature, hedonic value was sometimes measured as a higherorder reflective factor composed of multiple, mutually exchangeable dimensions (e.g., enjoyment and entertainment), each of which was measured with reflective items [78]. Higher-order constructs can also be directly measured using items of their lower-order constructs because all of the items are in essence mutually exchangeable [17]. Thus, for parsimony, hedonic value is directly measured in this study as a first-order reflective construct with three items being adapted from the literature [30, 69].

## Data Collection

The survey was posted on a widely used online survey-servicing Web site and was activated for access globally for a time window of six weeks. Repetitive submissions of the questionnaire from the same respondent were forbidden, as verified with hyperlinks and IP (Internet Protocol) addresses. To increase the response rate, numerous rounds of reminders were sent out. In addition, an economic incentive of 150 L inden Dollars (the virtual currency in Second Life) per person was provided, with an extra raffle prize of 1,000 L inden Dollars to the winner.

Two measures were taken to ensure the representativeness of the sample. First, to get opinions from a wide variety of Second Life customers, we distributed invitation messages (with a link to the survey) through multiple channels. These channels included (1) group e-mails to Second Life–related mailing lists, (2) postings to the official Second Life forum (www.secondlifeforum.com) sponsored by the Second Life vendor, (3) Second Life (taking the form of instant messaging and profile postings), and (4) Facebook and Twitter sites (profile postings and postings to special interest groups based on a key word search of “Second Life” and “SecondLife”). We believe that this practice enhanced the overall reach of the survey. Second, to reach as large a variety of Second Life users as possible, we recruited people from various “geographical” sectors in Second Life; we also controlled the timing of the invitation distribution in order to cover users located in diverse time zones.

## Respondents’ Profile

In total, 438 valid responses were received out of 1,374 views. Appendix B shows the demographic details of the respondents based on their real-life (rather than virtualworld) characteristics, as well as their usage experience with Second Life. In general, while our sample is balanced across various demographic features, it primarily reflects the opinions of experienced and heavy users. The sample appears to fit the objective of the study: investigating repeated-use intention.

Nonresponse bias was assessed by verifying that early and late respondents were not significantly different [33]. Early respondents (104) were those who responded within the first two weeks of the survey; late respondents (334) were those who responded in the following four weeks. The two groups were compared based on their demographics (age, gender, education, and real-life occupation) and usage experience with Second Life (length, frequency, and duration). All the chi-square statistics showed nonsignificant differences between the two groups.

## Measurement Model

To assess the psychometric properties of the measures, a LISREL-based confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed. The CFA results showed that the first item of utilitarian value (UV1) had a poor loading (0.38) and a high error variance (0.86), indicating that it may not be a good indicator of the construct. Also, it is worth noting that there were four items in the original scale for measuring relational capital, but only three of them were finally adopted in our study. The fourth item (RC4), which describes “close, personal interaction” between two parties, did not as precisely capture the deep relational underpinnings of this construct as did the other three items. After removing UV1 and RC4 [71], all the constructs in the model were of good reliability and had good convergent and discriminant validity. As shown in Table 1, composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE) values for all of the constructs exceeded 0.70 and 0.50, respectively, demonstrating good construct reliability [26, 72]; all of the t-tests of item loadings were significant (t > 1.96, p < 0.05), indicating high convergent validity for all the indicators [28].

For each construct, the square root of AVE was higher than 0.70 and larger than all of the correlations (Table 2), indicating adequate discriminant validity [28]. In addition, we compared the chi-square of the original measurement model with its ten latent variables against the alternative measurement models with only nine latent variables “where every possible combination of two constructs was examined, thus considering every possible pairwise discriminant validity check” [29, pp. 69–70]. Since the chi-square of the original model was significantly better than any possible combination of any two latent variables (the details of the analysis are omitted here due to the page number limitation but are available from the authors upon request), adequate discriminant validity of all constructs was further validated.

We evaluated the measurement model fit using multiple fitness indices (Table 3), including χ<sup>2</sup>/degrees of freedom (df), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), adjusted goodnessof-fit index (AGFI), normed fit index (NFI), nonnormed fit index (NNFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). We obtained $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 2 . 1 4$ , GFI = 0.89, AGFI = 0.87, NFI = 0.97, NNFI = 0.98, CFI =0.98, and $\mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 5$ , which all fell into acceptable ranges, indicating an acceptable level of fit of the measurement model [6, 32, 35, 65].

## Discussion

This stud y ex amined the f actor s and mechanisms that influence users’ continuance intention in SVW services. Specifically, we investigated the extent to which, and how, the three types of perceived benefits or values (i.e., utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital) and the three sources of service-specific investments (i.e., personalization, learning, and relational capital) influence continuance intention through satisfaction, calculative commitment, and affective commitment. With 11 out of 14 hypotheses supported, the results reveal a rich set of interesting findings.

First, the dedication-based mechanism is found to be important in predicting SVW continuance intention. In particular, while both satisfaction and affective commitment significantly affect continuance intention with SVW services, the latter appears to have a stronger impact. In terms of their antecedents, hedonic value and relational capital are both highly important factors influencing satisfaction and affective commitment, albeit with hedonic value perhaps exerting a stronger effect. As indicated in the previous section, both hedonic value and relational capital provide a substantial amount of additional explanatory power on affective commitment.

Second, in terms of the constraint-based mechanism, the results show that both personalization and relational capital impact calculative commitment, suggesting that they could be an effective means by which to increase the quitting cost for SVW users. As shown in the Data Analysis and Results section, we find that relational capital, as an SVW-specific investment, provides a notable amount of explanatory power on calculative commitment.

However, three hypotheses (H5, H10, and H12) are not supported, thus leading to an opportunity for further exploration. The first unexpected finding relates to H5: the effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment is found to be nonsignificant. The explanation was provided by post hoc tests of mediation using nested model comparisons [60], which indicated that this relationship was fully mediated by user satisfaction. This result suggests that users’ dedication-based commitment to SVW services cannot be directly affected by utilitarian value. Instead, utilitarian value must be converted to satisfaction before it can take effect. Given that utilitarian value is conceptually equivalent to perceived usefulness, this result differs from Kim and Son’s [42] study of online portal services, where perceived usefulness was found to directly influence dedication commitment (i.e., loyalty in their case). Indeed, users would remain loyal to a particular portal service if they found that the service was useful, even though sometimes the service might fail to reach their required satisfaction level. This may be because portal services are mainly targeted toward fulfilling utilitarian needs: users stay on in order to gain utility, even though sometimes they are not completely satisfied. However, as our study suggests, although utilitarian value still indirectly influences affective commitment in the SVW context, it is less important there than in utilitarian online services. For instance, a shortage of utilitarian value for some SVW users may be simply compensated for by improving the level of satisfaction by some other means, such as by increasing the hedonic value or the relational capital.

The second unexpected result is that the effect of learning costs on calculative commitment (H12) is found to be nonsignificant. This result was surprising at first sight because the same prediction was verified in the online service portal context [42]. One possible explanation is that learning in the SVW context is more like a subconscious by-product of living (i.e., participating in the social world), which makes learning less of a purposeful investment. After all, users are constructing a “second” life in the open-ended SVW where experience is wide open for creation (rather than through acquisition in the case of online services with predefined structures). In this SVW context, although users do learn through participation, they may not necessarily relate their experience of “living” in the virtual world to the conventional way of learning, where learning is explicitly separated from doing anything else [43]. This observation is consistent with Wenger’s [77] notion of practice-based learning in communities of practice, which regards learning as being deeply situated in, and therefore inseparable from, everyday practice.

Third, and perhaps most perplexing, is that the effect of calculative commitment on continuance intention is found to be significantly negative, which is the opposite of our hypothesis (H10). A post hoc analysis revealed that calculative commitment served as a negative suppressor variable for affective commitment in relation to continuance intention [20, 47] (details shown in Appendix D). In this case, we cannot simply conclude that calculative commitment has a negative effect on continuance intention, because the real relationship between the negative suppressor (i.e., calculative commitment) and the dependent variable (i.e., continuance intention) is concealed by the negative suppression effect [23].

With little prior knowledge to explain this unexpected effect, we explored possible explanations based on the available data. We scanned our sample and identified 92 subjects who reported the most notable negative association between calculative commitment and continuance intention (i.e., those with a below-mean value of calculative commitment and an above-mean value of continuance intention, or with an above-mean value of calculative commitment and a below-mean value of continuance intention). A demographic check on these 92 subjects revealed no obvious patterns, except for their consistently short tenure in Second Life (no more than two years). This suggests that the negative relationship between calculative commitment and continuance intention might be most salient among users with a short tenure in the SVW, implying a moderating effect of user tenure. To preliminarily test for this moderating effect, we divided the overall sample into two subsamples: long tenure (over two years, subsample size = 142) versus short tenure (no more than two years’ experience, subsample size = 296). A post hoc between-group LISREL analysis demonstrated that the moderating effect was indeed significant $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 9 . 0 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ [59]. In particular, we found that the relationship between calculative commitment and continuance intention remained significantly negative for the short-tenure subsample, confirming our earlier conjecture that the negative suppression effect was salient among short-tenure users. Interestingly, the relationship between calculative commitment and continuance intention became insignificant for the long-tenure subsample, thus showing no negative suppression effect of calculative commitment. A follow-up mediation test on this long-tenure subsample using nested model comparisons [60] revealed that the effect of calculative commitment on continuance intention was fully mediated by affective commitment, consistent with the results of Kim and Son’s [42] study.

The above informed exploration suggests that calculative commitment may have an indirect effect on continuance intention with SVW services through the mediation of affective commitment; however, this effect only exists among users with a relatively long tenure in the SVW. This may be because veteran users are better positioned to postrationalize their service-specific investment as an indicator of their dedication to SVW. The post hoc self-justification mechanism may not necessarily apply to users with limited tenure. Clearly, future research is needed to conduct a theory-driven investigation of this relationship.

## Theoretical Implications

One major contribution of this research is the development of a theoretical model explaining customers’ continuance usage intention in the distinctive context of SVW services. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to theorize about SVW services continuance, which is a practically pertinent, although scholarly understudied, phenomenon in the IS domain. In adapting the dedication-constraint framework of commitment [5, 42] to the SVW context, the results of this study offer rich insights into how dedication- and constraint-based mechanisms of commitment impact user continuance intention with SVW services.

Specifically, we advance our current understanding of SVWs by finding that dedication-based mechanisms, affective commitment in particular, are the most important in explaining continuance intention with SVW services. Moreover, we also offer nuanced insights into the antecedents of commitment to SVW services. While prior studies have already investigated the antecedents of commitment to other online services (e.g., utilitarian value for dedication-based commitment and personalization and learning for constraint-based commitment) [42], we advance this knowledge base by reexamining the importance of these existing antecedents in the SVW context, and more important, by theoretically introducing new context-relevant antecedents to the explanatory model. For instance, we contribute to our theoretical understanding by finding that hedonic value is the most important driver of the dedication-based mechanism in the SVW context, and that relational capital plays a dual role in influencing customer continuance with SVW services, that is, as both a social component of benefits (leading to satisfaction and affective commitment) and a service-specific investment (enhancing calculative commitment).

Our empirical test further enhances the existing knowledge base by not only confirming the theoretical relationships and the notable explanatory power of the new antecedents but also by unveiling a set of surprising, yet theoretically interesting, results with regard to the existing antecedents originally validated in other online service contexts (e.g., the full mediation effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment through satisfaction and the insignificant effect of learning cost). This effort not only further highlights the value of developing a distinctive theoretical model to explain user continuance with SVW services but also provides a sound theoretical base for future scholarly inquiries in this area.

Furthermore, this research contributes to the commitment literature by extending the applicability of the dedication-constraint framework of commitment [5] to the SVW context. Prior research has built this framework of commitment into the online service context [42], along with the application of other similar theoretical lenses to examine the issue of commitment [44]. We contribute to the commitment literature by adapting this theoretical framework to the distinct SVW context, thus extending its applicability by contextualizing the theory for enriched explanations in another domain.

## Practical Implications

The results of our study provide practitioners with a set of rich insights into how to improve users’ continuance intention toward SVW services. First, our study advises practitioners to focus on dedication-based mechanisms to improve user continuance intentions. Specifically, practitioners should enhance users’ positive psychological reactions to SVW services through satisfaction and affective commitment. It is particularly essential to focus on developing users’ affective commitment to the SVW service. Meanwhile, SVW practitioners should not ignore the constraint-based mechanisms for their significant roles in directly facilitating the dedication-based mechanisms and indirectly promoting long-tenured users’ continuance intention.

Second, our study advises that to leverage the dedication-based mechanism to increase users’ continuance intention, the practitioners should focus on delivering hedonic value, relational capital, and utilitarian value to SVW users, prioritized in this order.<sup>3</sup> In fact, SVW services developers may increase the hedonic value of SVWs by designing features to enrich users’ immersive and entertaining experiences. For example, using anthropomorphic interfaces supporting humanoid embodiment and multimodal presentations would help enhance users’ perceived hedonic value (e.g., enjoyment) by affording a strong sense of social presence [58]. In this regard, it would be useful for promoting hedonic value by improving such features as voice output and real-world simulation. SVW services developers might also increase the relational capital for users by instituting more social networking–related functions.

Third, to the extent that practitioners are concerned with managing calculative commitment, our study advises them to introduce a higher level of the users’ personalization and relational capital into the SVW service, instead of adding to the users learning cost. To introduce the users’ investment in personalization, it might be wise to incorporate functions that would allow users to capitalize on their past experience with the service. Also, the social networking functions mentioned earlier could play a role in inducing users to make a larger investment in their online relationships.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

As with any other research, the findings of our study should be interpreted with certain limitations in mind. First, the results are based on cross-sectional data, and therefore only the interrelationships are tested. While we have controlled for this threat to internal validity by developing all of the hypotheses in our model on the basis of well-established theories and by providing clear causal relationships between constructs [21], longitudinal investigations are encouraged in future research. Second, the survey data were collected from a single SVW service, which introduces a potential threat to the external validity of the research findings [21]. We mitigated this concern by carefully choosing our research site. Second Life is one of the most popular SVW services, having a lot in common with other SVW services (e.g., purpose, place, platform, population, and profit model) [52]. However, we urge future researchers to enhance the generalizability of the current findings by replicating this study in other SVW services. Third, while our study uses continuance intention as the dependent variable, future research is recommended to test the model with actual SVW usage behavior.

Furthermore, future research could consider extending the nomological framework by studying IT design features that can be used to induce benefits attractive to SVW users and service-specific investments effective for locking in users. In particular, research efforts could be exerted to find SVW design features related to hedonic value, as well as personalization and relationship investment.

## Conclusion

This paper developed a research model explaining continuance intention in SVW services by building on the dedication-constraint framework of commitment and adapting it to the context of SVW services. Our study found that users’ continuance intention with SVW services is determined by two intertwined commitment-based mechanisms: dedication and constraint. While the dedication mechanism (affective commitment) has a more salient and straightforward effect on continuance intention, the impact of the constraint mechanism (calculative commitment) is more complex. Furthermore, the study found that the hedonic, social, and to some extent, utilitarian values derived from using SVW services are all useful in enabling the dedication-based commitment, while personalization and relational capital (but not learning) invested in SVW services are instrumental in triggering the constraint-based mechanism for commitment. These findings serve as a starting point for researchers to gain a broader understanding of the factors affecting continuance with SVW services.

## Not es

1. Although affective commitment implies an emotional bond, recent research has conceptualized and measured affective commitment in ways beyond emotional attachment [39]. The consensus in the literature is that the emotional content seems much less important to affective commitment than individuals’ desire to stay and willingness to exert effort [39]. This recent development of affective commitment increases our confidence in the fitness of this construct to the dedication mechanism, which originated from the cognitive domain.

2. We acknowledge that “strongly agree/disagree” is a more standard wording, particularly for native English speakers. When conducting the survey, however, we realized that our respondents included not only native English speakers but also people for whom English was not their first language. In Second Life, there are as many users who are not native English speakers as there are those who are (if not more), as is evident in our sample. The pretest (card sorting) and pilot study conducted before the main survey indicated that nonnative English speakers felt more comfortable with “extremely agree/disagree” and that no participant in the pilot study had a problem with this statement. Therefore, as a trade-off solution, we decided to use “extremely” to accommodate those respondents whose native language was not English and believe that doing so would not cause interpretation problems for native English speakers. This practice is also consistent with some previous studies in the literature (e.g., [8, 79]). We thank an anonymous reviewer for pointing this out.

3. Perhaps this rank-ordering explains the recent decline in usage of SVW services. We thank an anonymous reviewer for pointing this out.

Thr ee method s wer e used to ex amine common method bias in this study. First, a post hoc Harman’s single-factor test suggested that the hypothesized single-factor model was a poor fit for the data, providing no evidence for CMV [48, 56].

Second, we included in the model a direct measure of a latent common method factor, which allowed all the items to load on their theoretical constructs as well as on a latent CMV factor, examined the significance of the structural parameters both with and without the latent CMV factor in the model, and calculated each indicator’s variances that were substantively explained by the principal construct and by the method factor [14, 45, 56]. The results showed that the ratio of the average substantively explained variance to the average method-based variance was very large, and that most of the method factor loadings were not significant, suggesting that the CMV factor was unlikely to be a serious concern for this study.

Third, a partial correlation procedure based on the marker variable analysis was executed by partialling out a “marker variable” as a surrogate for method variance [56]. Using both the smallest and second-smallest positive values in the correlation matrix as a conservative post hoc estimate of the correlation between the marker variable and the other variables [46, 48], we computed a complete matrix of the CMV-adjusted correlations and estimated the CMV-adjusted path coefficients and explained variance. The results showed that the differences in the correlation coefficients between the original and adjusted values were less than 0.050. Furthermore, the significance of almost all of the CMV-adjusted correlations remained the same as the original ones. Regarding the path coefficients, the biggest difference between the original and the adjusted estimates was 0.03; no changes were observed in the statistical significance of any of the structural relationships; the reduction in the squared multiple correlations was from 0.2 percent to 2.2 percent. These results suggest that the conclusions based on the original estimates would be comparable to those derived from the CMVadjusted estimates.

## Appendix D: Dealing with the Negative Suppression Effect

A supr essor v ar iable is “a variable which increases the predictive validity of another variable (or set of variables) by its inclusion in a regression equation” [20, p. 36]. Negative suppression occurs when the following happens: (1) the independent variables have a positive zero-order correlation with the dependent variable, (2) the independent variables correlate positively with each other, and (3) one of the independent variables (the negative suppressor) receives a negative regression weight after its inclusion in the regression equation [20, 47]. In this research, calculative commitment had a positive zero-order correlation (0.18) with continuance intention and was highly correlated (0.61) with affective commitment, suggesting that calculative commitment might serve as a negative suppressor variable in the model [20, 47]. To check for the suppression effect, different regression models were performed with calculative commitment and one of the other two antecedents of continuance intention (i.e., satisfaction and affective commitment) as predictor variables. The path coefficient between calculative commitment and continuance intention became negative only when affective commitment was also included as a predictor of continuance intention, but it was positive in other alternative regression model specifications. This result indicated that calculative commitment played the role of negative suppressor for affective commitment in relation to continuance intention.
