# An alternative perspective on citation classics: Evidence from the first 10 years of the European Conference on Information Systems

Edgar A. Whitley <sup>a,\*</sup>, Robert D. Galliers

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems and Innovation Group, Department of Management, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

<sup>b</sup> Bentley College, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452, USA

Received 12 August 2005; received in revised form 21 February 2006; accepted 14 April 2007 Available online 2 July 2007

## Abstract

We present an alternative perspective to the many citation classics papers that have appeared in the IS literature by presenting citation data from the first 10 years of the European Conference on IS (ECIS, 1993–2002), identifying the most frequently cited texts in this sample. We compared the ECIS citation classics with those presented by others and suggest that the ECIS data highlight the ways in which the IS field is more nuanced than previously reported. We thus provided insights how the field has developed internationally.

Keywords: Citations; Classics; European research tradition; Social theorists; Information Systems Research

## 1. Introduction

Walstrom and Leonard [W + L] [69] argued that young and growing fields, like Information Systems (IS), gain benefits by identifying ‘‘classic’’ texts (both journal articles and books) commonly cited in their field. They use the definition of classic texts proposed by de Sola Price [18], who said that classics are texts that have been cited at least four times in a year. They suggested that lists of such classics could provide a useful snapshot of the field that could help new researchers and practitioners determine what was important in current academic literature.

Walstrom and Leonard further suggested that the most frequently cited texts were likely to reflect the research issues being addressed at the time as well as the approaches being used in investigating key phenomena and issues. Thus these literatures would provide insight into the field’s core issues and help to identify boundaries [6,35,53]. Central to this argument was the assumption that the most ‘‘important’’ items would be cited most frequently [42]. However, this assumption is not without its critics, who have emphasised the political economy aspects of decisions about who and what to cite [25,36,37,66].

Walstrom and Leonard further reviewed citations from nine ‘leading’ IS journals between 1986 and 1995. From the 3752 articles they explored, they identified a number of articles that they argued characterised the field. They relate these to classics identified in earlier studies by Culnan [16,17] during the period 1972–1985.

We complemented this work by providing data from an alternative sample, presented in Europe—a region with a long history of IS research. These contributions, while key, have sometimes been overlooked in North America. They include the first business computer [29], the early university program developed by Langefors in 1965 at the Royal Institute of Technology and the University of Stockholm in Sweden and the first International IS Conference. (The Research Seminars in Information Systems (IRIS) which commenced in 1978; the International Conference on Information Systems was first held 2 years later in 1980. For a review, see [26].) It is also believed that European IS researchers draw heavily on alternative theories and approaches, including social theory [60].

Our data were taken from the papers presented at ECIS during its first 10 years (1993–2002). We took an alternative perspective in order to address three limitations of the prior studies. First, there is growing evidence that the publication practices of the journals have not necessarily been representative of the IS field internationally [2] (although Lowry et al. [45] and Mylonopoulos and Theoharakis [50] have attempted to be more representative). In particular, Galliers and Meadows demonstrated that there was a strong relationship between the ‘‘nationality’’ of the journals and the ‘‘nationality’’ of the authors acccepted in them. Chen and Hirschheim [14] similarly demonstrated that some top journals had a bias towards certain methodological approaches; for example they noted that interpretative studies in Information Systems Research – one of the leading IS journals in North America – were virtually non-existent during the period they studied, despite the journal’s claim for combining rigour and relevance [38]. In a recent editorial of MIS Quarterly (What does it take for a journal to be global?) the statement was made that MISQ was actively taking steps to be more representative of the global community. Europe may therefore provide an alternative perspective to that presented by others (c.f. [23]).

Second, a closely related issue was the question of path dependency [27]: Walstrom and Leonard developed their list of top journals by drawing on those in three earlier studies [28,34,68], which in turn were based on earlier studies [20,32,33,63]. Thus there is a risk that the sources had a tendency towards analysing a limited set of papers, approaches and ideas. Given the North American genesis of many of the journals considered, a European perspective could lead to different results. Recent citation data papers [e.g. 19,31,43,52,62,64] indicate that this path dependency remains: all focused their data around four ‘leading journals: MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems and Communications of the ACM, each of which is published in North America and mostly had North American editors and authors.

A third concern with prior studies is the time lag between submission and publication of the papers. Some of this delay arises because of (multiple) rounds of reviews and the backlog of accepted papers [30]. Conferences, in general, have a much shorter lead time and Chan et al. [11] have argued that they provide a missing perspective for citation analysis research. For ECIS, papers are normally submitted in November and appear in print the following May/June. Most conferences have similar delay times [71].

The choice of ECIS allowed us to determine the citation classics for research in Europe and also to explore how they differed from those primarily published in the US as well as to try to explain any differences.

## 2. Replication and repeatability of scientific studies

Studies of scientific practice have shown that in the physical sciences, replication of studies and experiments is actually undertaken far less frequently than might at first be expected. With a little reflection, however, this counter-intuitive situation is easily understood. Scientific institutions are focused around making contributions to our base of knowledge (i.e. being the first to make a particular claim or discovery). For studies whose results are accepted, therefore, there is no incentive merely to confirm a particular result. Indeed, such replication would simply reinforce the status that is accorded to the researchers who made the initial discovery [15].

Methodologically, however, it is the potential for replication that is significant. If the knowledge claims being made are controversial, then other scientists require sufficient detail about how the scientific study was undertaken to be able to replicate it. Shapin and Schaffer [61] argue that much of the success of the dissemination of early scientific method arose because of the use of ‘virtual witnessing’ that involved the production in a reader’s mind ‘‘of such an image of an experimental scene as obviates the necessity for either direct witness or replication . . . What was required was a technology of trust and assurance that the things had been done and done in the way claimed’’.

Again, in the physical sciences, it has been shown that there is considerable so-called tacit knowledge that cannot be easily stated so as to allow other scientists to replicate their studies [47]. While repeatability of studies is also problematic in the human sciences, in this paper we seek some degree of replication of W&L’s study, but in a different setting. Clearly there is more than one difference between our study of ECIS papers and W&L’s study of journal publications. In Table 1 we therefore highlight the key similarities and differences between the two studies. We draw on the differences between the two studies to guide our analysis and seek to ensure that the results we highlight are not likely to be simply due to interpretations of differences between the two samples used.

## 3. ECIS in context

The origins of ECIS can be traced back to two parallel initiatives. The first was led by Professor Dan Remenyi, then at Henley Management College, UK and the second arose from discussions following the publication of the European Journal of Information Systems by the UK’s Operational Research (OR) Society. The editors of EJIS were then based at the London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE) and Edgar Whitley began working with the OR Society to organise a European Conference that covered the same area as the new journal. Professor Frank Land learned of both initiatives and helped ensure that rather than duplicating work, the two initiatives merged. This effort led to the First European Conference on Information Systems in 1993.

Twenty-six countries have had at least 10 papers published in ECIS over the first 10 years, with the UK having the largest proportion, see Table 3. Interestingly, the second largest contributors are Australia and the USA, both of which are outside the normal catchment area for ECIS. This is probably because of the large IS communities in these countries and the fact that the official language of ECIS conferences is English. Whilst the UK has had a consistently large number of papers at each ECIS, for Australia and the USA the patterns have varied.

A total of 134 texts were cited by ECIS authors at least 10 times in the period under study (i.e. potentially at least once per year). W&L followed de Sola Price suggestion that particular attention should be focused on texts that had been cited at least four times in a year.

## 5.2. Comparison with previous studies

W&L presented four tables of classics which, for ease of reference, are here identified by the table number of the W&L paper. Thus, the W&L Table 2 (‘‘Classics 2’’) lists the 34 texts from Culnan’s first cocitation analysis for the period 1972–1982, ‘‘Classics 3’’ lists the 26 texts from Culnan’s second co-citation analysis, covering the period 1980–mid 1985. In order to test whether these earlier classics still applied in the ECIS sample, each of these lists was checked against the citation lists from ECIS.

Table 8 compared items found in the Classics 2 list with those found in the ECIS list. Only Ackoff’s 1967 paper appeared in the ECIS Classics list, being cited 12 times in ECIS. Only 18 of the 34 items on the Classics list were cited by ECIS at all and the top five other papers are also presented in Table 8. The table presents those items from the Classics 2 list that are cited at least four times in ECIS, in descending order of their number of citations. The table also compares their rank in the Classics 2 list with those in the table of ECIS classics.

Table 9 presents a similar list of the Classics 3 texts. Of the 26 items on this Classics 3 list, 19 were cited at ECIS. Those items cited at least four times in ECIS were compared via their ranking (or not) in the ECIS classics list and in the Classics 3 list. Thus, in terms of whether Classics 2 and Classics 3 texts were cited at all in ECIS, the proportion rose from 53% of the Classics 2 texts to 73% of the Classics 3 texts.

Similarly, the three texts that W&L found to appear in both Classics 2 and Classics 3 [1,46,48], also appeared in the ECIS data, although they were only cited a total of 25 times in the 10 years. These papers remained classics for the period of Culnan’s two studies (1972–mid-1985) but their perceived utility, for the ECIS community at least, had decreased considerably in our study.

Classics 6 was W&L’s list of top cited texts for the period 1986–1995. Table 10 lists the most frequently cited of these items in ECIS 1993–2002. Of these, only 12 appeared in the ECIS Classics list of Table A.1. As can be seen, these all appeared at the top of the Classics 6 list. There is a very low correlation (0.34) between the rankings in Table 10 and those in W&L’s Classics 6.

## 6. Discussion

ECIS classics are noticeably distinct from those reported by W&L. Referring back to Table 1, we ask whether these differences can be explained in terms of outlet type, geographical location or time frame.

## 6.1. On the role of books and management reviews

Perhaps the most puzzling results of our analysis were the extensive citation of books as classic texts. It is unlikely that the outlet type (conference rather than journal) would directly explain this, nor is it likely to be the result of the particular time frame for the data analysed.

One possible explanation is that books are cited more frequently by PhD students than established academics and that PhD students form a higher proportion of ECIS authors than the sample studied by W&L. There is evidence that suggests that an ECIS paper can be used as part of a PhD by publication [70]. However, the recent discussion by Dennis et al. about tenure processes and publication, which focuses on the research performance of junior faculty members, suggested that many (mainly US-based) junior faculty published in ‘elite’ journals. The particular role of (European) PhD students in the ECIS data may therefore only partially explain the differences. Moreover, if taken to its extreme, this difference would suggest that either non-European PhD students read fewer books or that they stop citing them upon completion of their PhDs.

In order to explore this further, a subset of the ECIS classic books (those that had been cited at least 40 times in the 10-year period) were checked against the MIS Quarterly online archive and the last 5 years of ICIS proceedings. In each case, other than Yin [72] and Rogers [59], they were cited fewer than 15 times. Moreover, some – Walsham [65], Earl [21] and Checkland [12] – were cited almost exclusively in papers written by non-North American authors.

The extensive citation of the Harvard Business Review and the Sloan Management Review was also puzzling, especially given the recent debates about rigour and relevance e.g. [49]. Part of the reason for this might be found in the use of these articles to justify the importance of an issue [5], in showing the research to be a real business concern. However, this does not explain the low use of these sources in the W&L lists. The move by Communications of the ACM to become a self-styled ‘‘magazine’’ accessible to the practitioner community, rather than a research journal, might suggest that there will be fewer classic texts in it, but the relevance of CACM articles for ECIS authors appears to have been maintained and may well continue.

## 6.2. Social theory

Although some of the authors listed in Table 12 are non-native English speakers (e.g. Latour, Callon, Habermas), their texts are widely available in English translation, so there is no particular reason to believe that these social theorists are less accessible outside the ECIS community. There is no particular time lag evident in the citation of these social theory texts (Table 13) and there is no particular reason to suggest that the choice of publication outlet would explain the order of magnitude difference between the MJI for ECIS and the MJI for IS journals emanating from North America.

## 6.3. Themes

The major themes identified in ECIS classics differed markedly from those in W&L. Some of the variation is likely to be due to differing time frames with, for example, the advent of electronic commerce leading to citation of texts on electronic markets and economics. It has been suggested that some of the explanation for the variation could arise from the practice of ECIS being organised around thematic tracks. For example, ECIS 2000 in Vienna had general tracks on: research methodology, teaching methods and practical relevance; IS development: methodologies and tools; technology (databases, internet and multimedia); the economics of IS; globalisation and culture and IS policy, strategy, innovation and the management of knowledge. It also offered industry-oriented tracks in media, banking, insurance, software, retailing, tourism, public administration, health care and higher education. Whilst this might be seen to encourage submissions around particular themes, it is unclear whether this drives the kinds of submissions made or is, in fact, a reflection of the state of the IS field, where many IS groups are found in schools of computing and informatics, as well as in business schools as is commonly the case in North America, given its MIS tradition.

## 7. Conclusions

We have argued that the use of the ECIS citation data could provide a useful complement to the data presented by W&L as it drew on a more diverse set of authors and institutions, from a different region of the world. In so doing, we argued that the ECIS data were less likely to suffer from problems of path dependency. The rapid turnaround of conference papers also meant that citation classics were likely to appear more quickly than they would in journal articles. By drawing on conference data rather than journals, we have shown that citation classics can emerge soon after they appear in print. Thus, conference proceedings may give a more up-todate picture of future classics than can be found by analysing journal articles alone.

We also demonstrated that social theorists are more widely cited in European IS research than in the North American tradition and we identified the key authors in this area. Social theorists provide important insights, bringing broader understanding of the role of technology in society, making IS a reference discipline for other fields.

The data presented here provides a strong message. The most popular classic sources are books that address key themes and approaches and do so in a pioneering way. As a result, later contributions and developments are likely to make marginal refinements, with the pioneering texts being cited alongside any refinements. Many classic texts are cited ‘‘in passing’’ rather than being discussed in detail. Similarly, while edited volumes are useful for bringing together key texts, they are cited much less frequently.

In terms of outlets, the ECIS Classics seem to favour the management reviews (like Harvard and Sloan and to a lesser extent Communications of the ACM) over more traditional academic outlets. That said, ECIS authors are more inclined to use both US and European sources than their North American counterparts.

The above discussion of themes, outlets and key citations provides strong evidence for a key difference between European and the ‘mainstream’, US-based, IS research in terms of the differing focus between rigour and relevance. It would seem that there is still a marked difference between the two research traditions, with the US favouring rigour, while relevance is more dominant in ECIS proceedings.

Bob Galliers was Appointed as Provost of Bentley College in 2002. He was previously Professor of Information Systems and Research Director in the Department of Information Systems at the London School of Economics. national journals on Information Systems and has also co-authored a number of books, the most recent being: Exploring Information Systems Research Approaches: Readings and Reflections (Routledge, 2007). His research focuses in the main on information systems strategy and the management of change associated with the adoption and appropriation of IT-based systems within and between organisations.
