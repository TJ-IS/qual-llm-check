# Determinants of success in IS offshoring projects: Results from an empirical study of German companies

Markus Westner <sup>a,1</sup>, Susanne Strahringer <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> European Business School, Oestrich-Winkel, Germany <sup>b</sup> Dresden University of Technology, Dresden, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 29 July 2009 Received in revised form 4 February 2010 Accepted 20 May 2010 Available online 11 June 2010

Keywords: Information systems offshoring Offshore project success Project suitability Knowledge transfer Liaison quality Offshoring expertise Trust in offshore service provider Partial least squares

## A B S T R A C T

High labor cost in western countries allows cost savings by companies engaging in IS offshoring. However, studies worldwide indicate that a large number of companies that engaged in IS offshoring are not satisfied with the outcome. Our study examined the determinants of IS offshore project success: We developed a model and empirically tested it with data collected from 304 experts who reported on projects offshored from Germany to a wide range of near and distant countries. The model posited a direct effect of offshoring expertise and trust in offshore service provider (OSP) on success, as well as an indirect effect mediated by project suitability, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality. An analysis using partial least squares (PLSs) provided significant support for almost all these relationships. However, it showed that offshoring expertise played a minor role in explaining success and the mediating constructs. Trust in OSP had a small direct effect on success and a medium to large effect on the mediating constructs. Project suitability, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality all had small direct effects on success.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Background and motivation

IS offshoring describes the transfer of IS services to an offshoring service provider (OSP) in a near or distant country. The OSP can be an internal subsidiary (so-called captive offshoring), a partially owned unit, or an external service provider (so-called offshore outsourcing). The services themselves are partially or totally transferred.

IS offshoring has specific characteristics that distinguish it from the field of IS outsourcing: in offshoring, service occurs at a distance from the consumer; physical distance, time zone and cultural differences play a part; also complexity increases due to the geographical dispersion of team members. Thus IS offshoring creates organizational challenges because offshore staff partially replace domestic onshore staff.

High labor cost in western countries and resulting cost savings are the main reasons why companies engage in IS offshoring.

Accordingly, the market volume has been growing in the past years, with India becoming the most popular offshoring destination. Application development and maintenance activities are especially likely to be performed offshore. However, recent studies indicate that a large number of companies using IS offshoring are not satisfied with the outcome.

The situation is especially noticeable in Germany where offshoring levels are rather low. German companies also experience difficulties in performing IS offshoring successfully; this may be due to language and cultural barriers.

## 1.2. Research questions

Our study examined determinants of IS offshore project success in German companies. Our research questions were:

\- Do offshoring expertise, trust in OSP, project suitability, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality directly impact offshore project success, and if so, to what extent?

\- Is the direct impact of trust in OSP and offshoring expertise on success mediated by project suitability, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality, and if so, to what extent?

We employed a confirmatory-quantitative research approach to address these questions. In line with the current state of IS offshoring research we focused on success/outcome factors with projects being the unit of analysis. However, we added original content in our research model that was built on existing research. We ensured methodological originality by gathering a broad empirical dataset and analyzing the research model with PLS as our method of analysis.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and hypotheses

## 2.1. Overview

Our research model is shown in Fig. 1; it posits that offshoring expertise has a direct positive effect on the project’s success. Additionally, offshore expertise is positively associated with a project’s suitability for offshoring, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality; these act as mediators for offshore project success. Trust in the OSP also positively influences knowledge transfer, liaison quality, and thus offshore project success.

## 2.2. Offshore project success

Offshore project success is the dependent construct in the research model. As Erickson and Ranganathan [6] stated, success can be measured in many ways, including the organization’s satisfaction with the results, the degree of fulfillment with expectations, the computed cost/benefit [30], the psychological belief of fulfilled obligations [15], and the strategic fit view [17].

Several studies have measured success as the satisfaction of outcomes, sometimes depending on the initial expectation [1,5,34]. Dahlberg et al. decided that satisfaction with outcomes can be evaluated along four factors: strategic, economic, technological, and social. Overall satisfaction also forms a part of the success.

Strategic, economic, technological and social factors may also apply to projects but they are not applicable in all cases. For example one might think of projects that completely lack a specific strategic proposition. Since a project is, by definition, an effort bound by schedule, budget, functionality, and quality, it makes sense to use these dimensional factors together with overall satisfaction as a way to measure offshore project success.

Therefore, we interpreted the dependent construct offshore project success as the perceived satisfaction with the outcome of the offshore project in total, including the dimensions of schedule, budget, functionality, and quality.

## 2.3. Exogenous determinants of offshore project success

## 2.3.1. Offshoring expertise

We defined expertise as organizational experience in managing or conducting offshoring in an efficient and thus successful way and following a learning curve. Individuals and organizations can benefit from past experience and cope better with offshore-specific challenges.

The positive impact of expertise has already been addressed in offshore research. Carmel and Agarwal [2] developed a maturity model and gave recommendations on how to move along this maturity curve. In a study of an 8-year offshore outsourcing alliance, Kaiser and Hawk [13] described how the alliance evolved towards a more beneficial co-sourcing model, for both the consumer and the supplier. Rottman and Lacity [24] in a study of offshoring practices at 21 U.S. companies also found a positive effect of expertise on offshoring success. However, there is a paucity of research about the impact of expertise on success in IS offshoring.

Higher levels of expertise help to cope with the potential challenges of offshoring and thus increase the probability of project success. Thus, expertise has a positive impact on all three mediating constructs because based on past experience it is likely that a company will select projects that are most suitable for offshoring. Additionally, the organization and its employees know how to manage knowledge transfer and improve liaison quality based on this expertise. Thus we hypothesized:

H1. IS offshoring expertise is positively and directly associated with offshore project success.

H2. IS offshoring expertise is positively associated with project suitability.

H3. IS offshoring expertise is positively associated with knowl edge transfer.

H4. IS offshoring expertise is positively associated with liaison quality.

## 2.3.2. Trust in the OSP

We defined trust as the expectation that an actor will fulfill obligations, behave predictably and act fairly when the possibility of opportunism is present. Interorganizational trust is produced in the partner organization by its members [16]. Since we focus on offshore projects as the unit of analysis and not the overall arrangement between client and OSP, we employed an interpersonal trust perspective in our study. If organizations trust their counterparts, they are more willing to cooperate and put in extra effort as needed.

Higher levels of trust seem to positively influence the relationship between client and vendor. Recent empirical-confirmatory studies show that trust is positively related to the extent of knowledge sharing and that trust, mediated by cooperative learning, has a significant positive influence on knowledge transfer [21]. With respect to IS offshoring research, trust is mentioned as a critical success factor at the interface between offshore consumer and supplier [12]. Kaiser et al. confirmed this in a case study and perceived the creation of trust as a best practice for successful offshoring: with it, offshore staff rapidly become productive and projects progress faster. Winkler et al. [33] showed that trust positively influenced the degree of connection between an offshore consumer and a service provider in their aim to achieve specified goals. Rottman [23] illustrated how trust facilitates knowledge transfer in an offshoring arrangement, because it increases the willingness to share knowledge and collaborate.

Trust influences knowledge transfer, especially of implicit knowledge. Additionally, trust fosters and facilitates collaboration, communication, and increases liaison quality among team members. Thus we hypothesized:

## H5. Trust in OSP is positively associated with knowledge transfer.

## H6. Trust in OSP is positively associated with liaison quality.

We might also hypothesize a direct effect of trust on offshore project success, but prior studies have not supported such an association. They examined the impact of trust on constructs such as relationship or partnership quality, assuming a fully mediated effect. We nevertheless included a potential direct impact of trust on offshore project success in our model. However, because the theoretical backing is weak, we treated it with caution regarding its direct impact in the model on success and in the interpretation of our results:

H7. Trust in OSP is positively and directly associated with offshore project success.

## 2.4. Endogenous determinants of offshore project success

## 2.4.1. Project suitability

We defined project suitability as the degree to which a project’s attributes and task characteristics make it more amenable for delivery in a dispersed, inter-cultural environment. Prior research has shown that there is a link between the function being outsourced and the overall success [7]. They suggested focusing on routinely performed non-core functions. Applying the lens of transaction cost theory and operations management models, Stratman [27] found that well understood, standardized service processes that are noncore are best candidates for successful offshoring. Stringfellow et al. [28] showed that it is more challenging to offshore complex, loosely defined and non-standardized tasks that require complex judgment and implicit knowledge. If projects or tasks show these characteristics, offshore delivery incurs additional costs that might threaten project success. King [14] suggested a framework for determining whether an IS activity should be considered for offshoring. He posited that activities should be kept in-house if they require proximity and the risk of offshoring was too great, or if the activity was business-critical. Schaffer [25] developed a similar framework that suggested refraining from offshoring projects that were very short, required much personal interaction, and involved high security risks and criticality for the business. Mirani [19] said that small applications or components of low complexity for which specifications can be communicated completely, and whose development process is highly structured, were more likely to be successfully delivered in an offshore process.

Since most of these studies were conceptual in nature or relied on a small set of empirical data, we carried out a qualitative prestudy with 47 German offshoring experts from different companies to find out whether project suitability was actually important for project success and what the evaluation criteria should be [32]. In the interviews, our experts confirmed that a project’s characteristics and its suitability for offshoring have an impact on project success. Criteria such as project size, project duration, operating language, degree of codification, and business specificity were most frequently mentioned as determining a project’s suitability for successful offshoring. If projects have a minimum size and duration, the project language is English, the degree of codification is high, and business specificity or required domain knowledge is low, it takes less time and effort to make OSP staff fully productive. Therefore we hypothesized:

H8. Project suitability is positively associated with offshore project success.

## 2.4.2. Knowledge transfer

We defined knowledge as a mix of experience, values, contextual information, and expert insight allowing the evaluation and incorporation of new experiences and information. Knowledge transfer is an activity between individuals and organizations [31] that allows the person or organization to absorb knowledge, apply it, and use it in project delivery [20].

To profit from offshoring, the offshore staff must replace more expensive onshore staff. Accordingly, all project-relevant explicit and implicit knowledge needs to be transferred to the offshore staff. This happens at the beginning of the project but is also a continuous activity. Correspondingly, offshoring process models used in the industry recognize knowledge transfer as a specific activity [29].

A case study by Chua and Pan [4] examined how a financial institution transferred knowledge within a captive offshoring arrangement, and highlighted the importance of knowledge transfer. Another case study, by Oshri et al. from the OSP perspective, investigated best practices for managing dispersed knowledge among onshore and offshore sites and acknowledged that knowledge transfer was a key part of successful offshoring. Previously, Ganesh and Moitra [8] identified knowledge transition and the absorptive capacity of the OSP as one of the CSFs of successful service transition. Rottman et al. developed some best practices for success; most of them fundamentally linked to ensure successful knowledge transfer. Finally, Lee et al. [16] examined IS outsourcing arrangements between Korean firms and found significant support for the hypothesis that knowledge sharing is positively related to successful outsourcing.

Thus we hypothesized that:

H9. Knowledge transfer is positively associated with offshore project success.

## 2.4.3. Liaison quality

We defined liaison quality as the degree of connection between onshore and OSP staff in order to achieve the goals, i.e., in our case, a project’s objectives. Liaison between staff should involve reciprocity and closeness [35].

The environment of IS offshoring delivery can have negative impacts on liaison quality. Due to distance, communication frequency decreases, collaboration is likely to be reduced, and individuals tend to feel they are not equal members of the team [11].

Erickson et al. highlighted the need for clear roles, responsibilities, communication mechanisms, and conflict resolution in the management of global virtual teams. Rottman recognized the impact of liaison quality on success and suggested building personal connections between OSP and client staff by regular visits and face-to-face meetings. Furthermore, he proposed integration of offshore into onshore staff with synchronized training of all. Similarly, Heeks et al. [10] found that a high degree of congruence between provider and client improved chances of project success. They recommended building bridging relationships and using straddlers, dedicated individuals responsible for facilitating and moderating the interaction. Levina and Vaast [18] mentioned that liaison quality lessens the negative effect of distance, thereby improving performance. They suggested good onshore middle managers, frequent communication, constructive communication, and the efficient use of technology to improve liaison quality. Other research mentioned the positive effect of liaison quality on performance, facilitation of informal communication, the presence of expert intermediaries, and supplier presence on-site [3].

Liaison between onshore and offshore staff is vital for collaboration, working efficiency, and productivity. Thus we hypothesized:

H10. Liaison quality is positively associated with offshore project success.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research approach

Our research was empirical and we used a survey for data gathering. Our study’s unit of analysis was the offshore application development or maintenance project at German corporations.

## 3.2. Operationalization of constructs

The variables in our model were latent and could not be measured directly. Thus, we generated a set of measurement indicators to operationalize each construct, using existing indicators from previous studies when available or adapting them if necessary. All constructs were measured reflectively, with the exception for project suitability which was measured formatively. Table 1 illustrates this.

We measured each of the indicators using a 7-point Likertscale. These interval scales ranged from 1, fully disagree, not important, or not at all satisfied to 7, fully agree, very important, or totally satisfied. Survey data regarding demographics and descrip tive data was primarily measured using nominal scales.

We pre-tested the survey by distributing the questionnaire to academic staff at various universities and to industry experts and practitioners not included in the full sample. We specifically asked them to comment on validity, quality, and comprehensibility of the questions and their presentation. Pre-test feedback was positive. Apart from minor word changes, the structural quality of the survey instrument was considered consistent. Based on this pre-test, we adapted the questionnaire and sent it out as the final version.

## 3.3. Instrument administration

The population for our study was IS offshoring projects conducted at German companies. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no database that aggregates data for IS projects across Germany. Therefore, we adopted a key-informant approach by identifying offshoring experts in Germany and asking them to contribute data about one specific completed IS offshoring project.

We relied on the largest German business social network XING for expert identification; this involved contacting all people registered at XING who had an affiliation with near- or offshoring in Germany. Our sampling method was a convenient and non-stratified sampling, which could negatively impact the study’s external validity. However, this was the only way to gather an adequate amount of cross-company data given the study’s budget constraints.

We used the search string ‘‘offshor\* OR nearshor\* OR off-shor\* OR near-shor\*’’ in XING’s ‘‘I offer’’ to identify experts with the appropriate affiliation. Furthermore, we limited the search to ‘‘Germany’’ in the ‘‘region’’ search field. As a result, 1472 experts with potentially relevant expertise were contacted by e-mail.

The invitation to participate contained an explanation of our rationale and a link to the web page that hosted the questionnaire. A second e-mail was sent to non-respondents 4 weeks later to request participation within 14 days.

Of the 1472 e-mails, 475 experts (32.3%) responded. Of these, 171 did not agree to participate; their main reasons were that they had not been involved in application development or maintenance (42), that the experts worked for an OSP (42), and that they considered their expertise level as being insufficient to participate in the survey (12). Thus, we were provided 304 analyzable responses for analysis; this represented a response rate of 21%. Of these respondents, 218 (72%) responded within 14 days.

## 3.3.1. Non-response bias

We analyzed for non-response bias by comparing survey data submitted by late respondents to that submitted by early respondents. We therefore divided the sample into two parts based on the time difference in days between dispatching the first contact e-mail and return date of the questionnaire. The early responders, 218 data sets, contained all respondents who responded within 14 days. The late responders, 86 data sets, contained all respondents who replied after 14 days.

We tested for statistical significance in responses between the two groups. Since the data was not normally distributed (a K–S test of normality resulted in D(304) between 0.14 and 0.39, p < 0.001) and the group sizes were different, we used a non-parametric Mann–Whitney test to check for differences between the two groups. The test showed no statistically significant differences. Thus our data was not influenced by non-response bias.

## 3.3.2. Missing values

Since we used a web-based questionnaire tool for data gathering we could ensure that all questionnaire items were answered by participants; they were not able to proceed or submit it unless it was complete. Also, built-in validation rules prevented participants from entering obviously wrong values.

## 4. Sample characteristics

## 4.1. Participants

Study participants held managerial positions (141 or 46%), were Vice Presidents/Directors (67 or 22%), and CXOs, i.e., CIOs, CEOs, or CTOs (17 or 6%). The remaining 79 (26%) worked in nonmanagement roles.

Participants showed a high level of experience in the field of IS and of IS offshoring. Most (279 or 92%) had accumulated six or more years of experience in the field of IS and 227 (75%) had three or more years of experience in offshoring.

## 4.2. Projects

Most projects were conducted at companies in the sectors of telecommunications (91), information technology (79) and manufacturing (48). Other sectors were banking and insurance (34), transportation (25), retail and distribution (24), consulting (14), healthcare (12), public sector (9), utilities (8), construction (5), and other sectors (20). For 23 projects, study participants did not specify any sector.

To assess company size we asked for the overall number of employees and the number of internal IS staff at the time the project was conducted. Data showed that primarily large companies populated the sample: 109 projects (36%) were executed at companies with more than 25,000 employees, 34 projects (11%) with 5001–25,000 employees, and 35 projects (12%) with 1001– 5000 employees.

The three main reasons for offshoring were cost reduction (285 projects), strategic reasons (159), and perceived resource shortage (115)—multiple answers were allowed for this question. This is in line with existing research that mentions similar reasons why companies offshore [22].

We had previously found that German IS services were primarily offshored to India rather than to closer countries. This was true of the new data: 171 projects (56%) were delivered from India. Other countries included Russia (16 or 5%), Poland and Romania (each with 14 or 5%), Hungary (11 or 4%), Belarus (10 or 3%), etc.

To assess project size, we asked for the project’s effort in person-months. Most of the projects were smaller than 300 (195 or 64%). By looking at the offshored parts of the projects we could see that the majority of the projects had an offshore share of 41% or more (213 or 70%), representing a significant amount of the overall volumes.

The majority of reported projects (220 or 72%) were finished between 2007 and 2009. Only 84 projects (28%) were finished before 2007.

The majority of study participants (258 or 85%) were in managerial roles on the projects for which they submitted data. Only 46 (15%) said they were in non-managerial roles. This indicated that we addressed the key informants about the projects in our sample.

## 4.3. Partial least squares model analysis

## 4.3.1. Analysis method

We tested the research model by using the variance-based PLS technique which is suitable for research that attempts to predict a target variable or explain its variation.

Additionally, PLS does not demand identical distribution of residuals, i.e., it also works in case of heteroscedasticity.

The research questions addressed the determinants of offshore project success, so we took advantage of the PLS predictive power in model validation. In addition, the sampled data was not normally distributed and the research model included formative as well as reflective constructs. With our sample size of 304 items, we achieved a high level of statistical power. For conducting the actual analyses, we use the PLS algorithm as implemented by the software SmartPLS in release 2.0.M3.

## 4.3.2. Evaluation of the measurement model

4.3.2.1. Unidimensionality. Unidimensionality can be assessed using an exploratory factor analysis (EFA). We first tested explicitly for it before making any other analysis. The number of factors is determined by finding those that have an Eigenvalue exceeding 1. Indicators should load high on their own factors and low on others. Indicators load high if their coefficient is above 0.6 and low if it is below 0.4. Indicators with a low loading coefficient were removed from the research model. We used SPSS 16 to perform a principal components analysis (PCA) with varimax rotation to support interpretation of the results.

4.3.2.2. Convergent validity. This occurs when each measurement item correlates strongly with its assumed construct; the degree of convergent validity is determined by the CFA, where the numbers of factors are specified a priori. Convergent validity is assumed when indicators’ loadings on their respective constructs is significant at the 95% level using Bootstrapping. Indicator loading values should exceed 0.7 on their constructs, so that more than 50% of the indicator’s variance is caused by the construct. Furthermore, the average variance extracted (AVE) should exceed 0.5; this value ensures that the explained variance is greater than the variance caused by a measurement error. Table 3 shows that all quality criteria are fulfilled: indicator loadings exceeded 0.7, were highly significant (p < 0.001), and AVE scores exceeded 0.5 for all constructs.

4.3.2.3. Discriminant validity. Discriminant validity was assessed using the criterion that required that the AVEs of the constructs should be greater than the square of the correlations among them, thereby indicating that more variance was shared between the component and its block of indicators than with any other component. Table 4 shows the squared construct correlations, while its last row contains each construct’s AVE. As can be seen, each construct’s AVE exceeded the squared correlations of this construct with any other construct.

## 5. Discussion of results

## 5.1. Exogenous determinants of offshore project success

## 5.1.1. Offshoring expertise

We showed support for three of the hypotheses related to offshoring expertise. Despite their statistical significances, corresponding effect sizes were small for offshoring expertise’s impact on project suitability and almost non-existent for its impact on knowledge transfer and liaison quality. We could not find support for a direct positive impact of offshoring expertise on offshore project success. Apparently, offshoring expertise plays only a minor role as a determinant of offshore project success and of the other endogenous constructs in our model.

In summary, we can state a low impact of offshoring expertise in our model. Presumably, this is due to the overall low level of organizations’ and individuals’ expertise in our sample.

## 5.1.2. Trust in OSP

The PLS analysis showed support for all three hypotheses about trust in OSP. Levels of trust had a significant positive impact on knowledge transfer (H5), liaison quality (H6), and offshore project success (H7). These effects were not only significant but also large. Obviously, trust in OSP seems to play a major role as a determinant of success and of the other constructs in the research model.

However, we were surprised to find a direct – albeit small – significant impact of trust on offshore project success. Although we considered such an effect in the model as expressed by hypothesis H7, we assumed the effect of trust as being fully mediated by knowledge transfer and liaison quality.

## 5.2. Endogenous determinants of offshore project success

## 5.2.1. Project suitability

This had a significant positive effect on offshore project success. However, its effect size was small, which was unexpected. We expected a stronger impact, based on the results of our qualitative pre-study in which, experts almost unanimously stated the importance of selecting suitable projects for offshoring to be successful.

## 5.2.2. Knowledge transfer

As expressed by hypothesis H9, knowledge transfer showed a significant positive impact on offshore project success, but the effect size was small, which was unexpected.

Indicator values for SUITA4 showed that documentation levels were not exceptionally high within the projects analyzed. Therefore, we cannot argue that an overall high degree of explicit knowledge reduced the need to transfer knowledge in our setting.

## 5.2.3. Liaison quality

Levels of liaison quality had a significant positive impact on offshore project success as postulated by hypothesis H10 but the effect was slight.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Contributions

Our study represents one of the few empirical-confirmatory studies on IS offshoring in general, and for Germany in particular. The quality of the results was increased by the large size of the sample. Furthermore, our study was one of the first to operationalize and measure offshoring expertise and project suitability in empirical research design.

Finally, our study was the first to embed offshoring expertise, trust in OSP, project suitability, knowledge transfer, and liaison quality in an overall model so that these constructs could be analyzed jointly and in relation to one another.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

The level of offshoring expertise seemed to be less important for offshore project success. Practitioners should therefore focus on establishing mutual trust in the OSP and sufficient levels of liaison quality within the project. Apparently, these factors had greater impact than the others. Tangible suggestions for management actions in order to achieve these goals should be to conduct crosscultural workshops before starting an offshore project, facilitate open and frequent communication, and physically bring domestic and offshore staff together for some time.

## 6.3. Limitations

Our focus on Germany as a region could limit the generaliz ability of results for other countries and cultures.

Possibly the sample data did not contain sufficiently different levels of expertise in order to detect its impact on the results. This is. however, not a flaw in the research design but rather because IS offshoring is a new option for German companies.

Finally, the method of data collection could be a limitation; we did not draw a statistical representative sample because there was no way for us to access the basic population, since the unit of analysis was projects.
