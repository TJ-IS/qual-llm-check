# Rethinking organisational size in IS research: meaning, measurement and redevelopment

## Abstract

While organisational size is a popular construct in information systems (IS) research, findings from its use have been inconsistent. Few studies have explored this inconsistency or attempted to address this problem. This paper uses Churchill’s measure development paradigm to conduct three separate but related investigations into the size construct. Study 1 explored the domain and dimensions of size. Some 2000 research papers published in six leading IS journals over an 11-year period were read in order to determine what researchers thought size meant and how they measured it. The study found 21 constructs underpinning the size construct and 25 ways of measuring size, but no clear relationship between size meaning and measurement. Study 2 assessed the construct’s content validity using a concept map exercise involving 41 participants. Multidimensional scaling clustered the constructs into three conceptual groups. Study 3 administered the size construct in a survey with a sample of 163 Australian firms. The study found that the data supported the constructs observed in Study 2 and that a group of eight constructs could be used to differentiate between smaller and larger firms in the sample. Analysis revealed that organisational levels, risk aversion, geographic distribution and employment reflected respondents’ self-nominated size.

## Introduction

Organisational size is important in the analysis of organisations and technology (Eisele, 1974; Kapur, 1995; Dong & Saha, 1998; Zhu et al., 2003; Hausdorf & Duncan, 2004). Mabert et al. (2003, p. 236) observed that ‘organizational size is the most frequently examined structural variable and has been used to study issues relating to innovation, R & D expenditures and market power’. However, its application continues to deliver inconclusive results. For example, Zhu et al. (2006, p. 605) argued, ‘different opinions exist regarding the role that size plays’. Similarly, Yao et al. (2002, p. 80) warned that ‘using size as a variable without careful classification may not yield desirable results’.

This paper aims to better understand the nature and use of organisational size in IS research and is motivated by several important issues. First, whereas many theoretical constructs, such as IS success, have received considerable analytical attention, organisational size has received relatively little rigourous scholarly concern. Despite this, organisational size continues to receive critical application in many studies. Second, while some studies using organisational size have successfully presented significant findings, other studies have delivered inconclusive results, prompting some researchers to call for a reassessment of the phenomenon (Duncan, 1995). Third, many practitioner models (such as those assessing productivity) make calculations based on organisational size. However, models developed using ‘large’ companies may have tenuous application in ‘small’ organisations (Fayad & Laitinen, 1997). The popularity of and discrepancy associated with organisational size suggests that re-assessment of the size construct makes fertile ground for research. To quote Rogers (1995),

Size is probably a surrogate measure of several dimensions y these unidentified variables have not been clearly understood, or adequately measured in most researches.

This paper considers organisational size as a ‘construct’. A construct is a ‘conceptual definition of a variable’ (Schwab, 1980). Bagozzi & Fornell (1982) defined a construct as ‘an abstract entity which represents the ‘true’, non-observational state or nature of a phenomenon’. Researchers may quantify this construct using one or more indicators (Winsten & Hall, 1961). Ideally, there is a sound relationship and agreement between phenomena, constructs and indicators and observers can transact about phenomena on equal terms.

Poor correspondence may result in error variance (Cote & Buckley, 1987), false acceptance of null hypotheses (Nunnally, 1978), validity and reliability error (Bagozzi et al., 1991) and improper inference (Iacobucci et al., 1999), among others. To this end, Cote & Buckley (1987) argued, ‘in the future, researchers must be more resolute in their desire to develop construct measures that are valid and free of measurement error’. Because the construct is as yet unobservable, researchers cannot be sure that their findings are correct, or that the construct itself even exists.

This paper analyses the organisational size construct, with three research questions:

1. What does the organisational size construct mean? Is there more than one meaning? Is it a construct with a number of sub-dimensions or are there a number of different constructs?

2. How is the size construct measured? How should the size construct (or constructs) be measured?

3. What does this enquiry into the nature of the size construct mean for theory? How can theory that uses the size construct as an independent variable or dependent variable be made more sound?

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. First, the paper discusses the construct development programme to be used. This is followed by three studies into the nature of size measurement and meaning. Conclusions, limitations and future directions are then discussed.

## Analysing the size construct

Firm size, while a popular construct in the IS research literature, is problematic. Chwelos et al. (2001, p. 306) argue that ‘organization size has consistently been recognized as a driver of organizational innovation’, yet Ranganathan & Brown (2006) find that firm size has no significant relationship with ERP investment, and Meron˜o-Cerdan & Soto-Acosta (2007) find no relationship with website use. Levina & Xin (2007) theorise a strong positive relationship between size and compensation, yet find no or negative effects in empirical testing. Zhu & Kraemer (2005, p. 69) note that ‘different opinions exist as to the role that firm size plays in the process of innovation diffusion’, observing a negative relationship between e-business use and firm size. Zhu & Kraemer (2002, p. 283) note, ‘Among several possible measures of firm size, such as total assets and sales, we chose to use number of employees, as a firm size indicator, following the tradition of the IT literature’, yet Ferratt et al. (2005) and Lee et al. (2004) control for size using annual revenues. Bajwa & Lewis (2003, p. 32) wrote,

While some innovation studies suggest a positive relationship between organization size and adoption behaviour y a negative relationship between size and adoption behavior has also been observed. In summary, past studies have yielded mixed results on the relationship between organization size and adoption behaviour.

## Choe (1996, p. 216) provides additional comment:

In terms of organization size, Gremillion has suggested no relationship between IS use and organizational size as measured by geographic area, staff and budget levels, and so on. However, Yap empirically suggested a positive relation between IS use and organization size measured by annual turnover. The results of the two studies were contradictory. Raymond explained these conflicting results through system sophistication. He reported that the effect of organization size on IS usage is mediated by the system sophistication.

Authors in other domains have recognised inter-study inconsistency. For example, Gupta (1980), wrote, ‘the ‘size effect’ has plagued researchers for decades, perhaps because most discussions have adopted a uniform definition of organisational size, namely, the number of members’. Temtime (2003, p. 55) observed that the ‘definition of firm size varies, not only from one economy to another, but also from industry to industry within the same economy’. Similarly, Banz (1981, p. 161) wrote, ‘We do not even know whether the factor is size itself or whether size is just a proxy for one or more true but unknown factors correlated with size’. Barber et al. (1999, p. 844), in the psychology literature, wrote,

y integration of results from these empirical studies is made difficult by the fact that y firm size is categorised differently across studies. For Pritchard and Fidler (1993) and Deshpande and Golhar (1994), firms with fewer than 500 employees are classified as ‘small’, a classification consistent with the standards of the Small Business Administration. But Bertram et al. (1995) included only firms with fewer than 25 employees as ‘small’, Heneman and Berkley defined firms with less than 100 employees as ‘small’, and Marsden’s (1994) ‘large’ category included firms with more than 250 employees.

Similarly, Dalton et al. (1980, p. 51), in the administrative science literature, wrote,

Measurement can also be problematic y Hrebiniak and Alutto, for instance, used number of beds as an indication of organisation size, a common practice in differentiating hospitals. Bidwell and Kasarda used average daily student attendance, an accepted criterion of school size. Reimann counted the number of full-time employees. Each method is reasonable; comparison of these studies is complicated, however because the measures are neither identical nor interchangeable. Moreover, Reimann, and Bidwell and Kasarda used a logarithmic conversion to normalise size. The others do not do so. Again, this makes responsible comparison difficult.

Given the importance of organisational research and analysis, this inconsistency is a significant problem. Because so few studies have adequately explored the reasons for this disagreement, researchers are unsure about whether the problem of organisational size is due to its measurement, its meaning, or its theory. These problems need to be disentangled.

This study used Churchill’s (1979) measure development paradigm, a staged process whereby a construct is developed from basic domain items and subsequently assessed in terms of validity and reliability. Churchill’s paradigm is appropriate for three reasons. First, it presents a prescriptive, structured approach to measure and construct development. Second, the method was developed with particular reference to validity and reliability. Third, the method has a rich history of prior literature application, including Torkzadeh & Doll (1999), Sethi & King (1991) and Wang et al. (2001).

In Stage 1, the researcher specifies the construct’s boundary and domain. Based on this delineation, the researcher then acquires a sample of items that constitute or describe the construct. Finally, the researcher assesses content validity, the degree to which the items already identified reflect the entire construct (Anastasi, 1982). Stage 2 of the paradigm refines the understanding of the construct’s meaning and measurement using focus groups and experts (Churchill, 1979; Smith et al., 1996). The original dimensions are re-assessed in terms of completeness in order to determine an appropriate mix of dimensions. In Stage 3, the researcher assesses the construct’s validity and reliability. Here, the final construct is employed in several real-world contexts to gauge suitability.

## Study 1: determining the dimensions of size

An understanding of the size construct is likely to require some analysis of meaning and measurement. This is consistent with the arguments of Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991).

## Unit of analysis

The unit of analysis in this study was a selection of leading IS academic journals. Six IS journals were selected: MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Information and Management, Information Systems Management, Journal of Management Information Systems and Communications of the ACM. It was felt that this selection would give a healthy indication of the dimensions in use and was similar to the journals used in other studies such as Barki et al. (1988), Straub (1989), Baroudi & Orlikowski (1989), Delone & McLean (1992), Holsapple & Johnson (1994) and Chan (2000).

The time interval for articles was 1989–2000. Research from this period encompasses more traditional firms in addition to those that have not followed ‘classical’ growth patterns (such as electronic commerce organisations) and those that are subject to unconventional technology-enabled operating environments (such as virtual offices and telecommuting). Studies had to be ‘primarily concerned with organisational size and structure’ in an operational sense.

For both analysis stages, the first author conducted the search. In cases of ambiguity, the opinion of a second senior academic staff member was sought. At the end of the process, the entire list was verified by the researcher and two others also involved in organisational research (as in Venkatraman, 1989).

## Procedures and materials for examining the meaning of size

This stage of the study explored researchers’ understanding of the meaning of the size construct. Strauss & Corbin’s (1998) and Neuman’s (1994) program of textual post-coding was followed. Papers were first ‘open coded’ to identify the major theories behind what researchers believe ‘size’ to mean. Labels were applied to relevant phrases or words in the text. In this initial stage, concepts are broad and largely disorganised: of greatest importance is the capture of contextual meaning within the passage (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

An example from this study follows: the relevant part of the passage is underlined and the label for the concept follows in bolded square brackets (following Strauss and Corbin’s convention). In this example, Yap (1990) refers to several themes that have been highlighted as candidate concepts. The discussion of size was as follows:

Size is probably a surrogate measure of several dimensions that lead to the adoption of innovations: resources [resources], organisational structure [structure] and so on. Another explanation is that as the size increase, organisational structure becomes more complex [complexity], and coordination of work activities [co-ordination] more complicated.

Another line of reasoning is that increasing size leads to economies of scale [economies of scale] which enhance the feasibility [feasibility and capability] of adopting innovations [adoption]. It is likely that the large volume of information processed [volume of information and need for communication] by large organisations gives them the advantage of scale [scale]. There is some evidence to suggest that large organisations enjoy some cost advantage [cost advantages and economies of scale], as the price of computer systems increases at a rate equivalent to the square root of their power y It is also likely that small organisations have more difficulty justifying investments in IT [investment and acquisition]. They face a number of problems arising from limited financial [investment resources] and human resources [employment resources]’. (p. 102)

The next stage involved ‘axial coding’ of the text, to discover differences and similarities among the categories (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999). This stage revealed several redundant classifiers from the previous stage. For instance, the term ‘cost advantages’ was rarely used and was incorporated into the larger category of ‘economies of scale’. The final stage involved selective coding of the established categories, to relate each category to the central category, possibly using diagrammatical representation (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

## Procedures and materials for examining the measurement of size

This stage aimed to determine how researchers measure the size construct, and involved searching for indicators of size in the literature. Studies had to use the term, ‘size’, ‘larger’ or ‘smaller’ in an organisational context. Papers that examined an aspect of an organisation but did not use it as an indicator or proxy for size were excluded. For example, studies that examined total assets as an indicator for size were admitted to the study; those that simply examined a firm’s total assets were not.

## Results of the literature search for meaning

The analysis found 21 separate constructs in 214 papers, as shown in Table 1. Some 17 studies offered no identifiable discussion of their understanding of organisational size. There did not appear to be an accepted view of the dimensions underpinning organisational size and what the size construct actually means. For instance, studies of system adoption feature in almost all of the categories. Third, the concept of ‘employment’ featured only once in Table 1, despite the popularity of Number of Employees as a size indicator. Although researchers think size has a definite meaning, they may not reflect on the link between the measures and meaning of the underlying constructs.

## Results of the literature search for measurement

The results show 25 indicators in use across 214 studies. In the main, papers offered little supportive discussion regarding their choice of indicator. In total, 27 studies (over 10%) did not declare the indicator used, with five of these studies using organisational size as an independent variable. For resource-related indicators, Number of Employees was by far the most common indicator, consistent with Raymond (1985), Delone (1988) and Choe (1996). Number of Employees has, for some time, been seen as a suitable indicator of size, and is relatively easy to determine from official documents, particularly for publicly listed firms (Miller, 1991). Its use, however, has tenuous application to those work environments that experience substantial fluctuation, such as telecommuting, virtual offices (Snizek, 1995) and network organisations (Ching et al., 1996). Total Assets was the next most popular indicator. Almost unanimously, studies that examined hospital environments used Number of Beds as their indicator.

The most popular sales or revenue measure of size was Annual Sales. Sales and revenue measures may only be suitable in certain circumstances. While ‘larger’ firms may have to lodge financial documentation with a relevant regulatory body, ‘smaller’ firms are not necessarily bound by this formality: determining such figures for these firms may be difficult. The application of financial measures to firms in financial sectors may be error-prone (Grover & Teng, 1992), either because these firms do not sell physical products or exhibit fluctuating revenue streams, which may obscure size analysis. Additionally, some firms may downplay unfavourable financial aspects, such as operating costs, and exaggerate favourable aspects, such as revenue (Mitchell et al., 1996).

Some studies used more than one size indicator. Particularly interesting is the study by Iacovou et al. (1995), which used two indicators of organisational size to test for EDI adoption: of Number of Employees and Total Sales, only the latter indicator was found to be significant.

Five studies used indicators that did not fit into any of these three categories. In the main, these studies were specific to certain industries or business types. Such indicators included number of patient days (used in a study about hospitals), Fortune 500, Geographical Spread and respondent-nominated. None of these studies offered convincing discussion as to why these unconventional size indicators were chosen over more conventional indicators.

## Study 2: content validity of construct items

Stage 2 of Churchill’s programme explores content validity in order to refine the construct. This was undertaken using the concept map method.

## Unit of analysis

The unit of analysis for Study 2 was a group of human participants, selected according to two criteria. First, participants had to be involved in business-related academic research, such as IS, management, accounting or organisation science. Researchers in these areas would be in frequent contact with organisational research, with a readily available tacit understanding of organisational size. Bruce (1983), Turner & Turner (2001) and Panteli (2002) used a similar selection. Second, the researcher had to have physical access to the participant in order to administer the exercise. This participant selection process may be open to three main biases. First, there is no guarantee that the group represents the wider research population, although respondents had a range of backgrounds. Second, the process may be subject to ‘interviewer effects’ (Webb, 1968) whereby participants already knew the researcher and were amenable to participating in the study (Meyers & Grossen, 1974). Third, the process assumes group homogeneity across membership and time (consistent with Webb, 1968).

## Procedures and materials

Two materials were used in this experiment. First, plain white index cards were used to represent each construct. Each card showed a number, a construct dimension identified in Study 1 and an example description of that construct. The second aspect of material was an index sheet containing information about the study, a description of the index cards and broad directions for undertaking the card sorting exercise. At the start of each exercise, the index sheet was read to the participants so that they understood the exercise. Each participant received identical preparatory direction.

The concept map method was used, following the instructions of Trochim (1989), and Johnsen et al. (2000). Participants sort the index cards into as many conceptually similar piles as they deem appropriate (Rosenberg & Kim, 1975; Trochim, 1989). Interpretation of these piles is then conducted using a method such as multidimensional scaling (Trochim, 1993).

## Control

In the interests of consistency, a list of potential questions and prepared answers was developed so that each respondent would be given the same information about the study (Dillman, 1978). All index cards had the same appearance and size so that participants would not bias individual cards based on card size alone. Experiments were held on different days at different times in order to attempt to ameliorate confounding environmental effects (Cook & Campbell, 1979). To partially alleviate potential participant acquiescence (Holden et al., 2003), the researcher left the room for the duration of the exercise as part of a ‘method of removal’ (Cook & Campbell, 1979). Task activities were held constant across exercises in order to control for variance (Meyers & Grossen, 1974).

## Administration

The pool of participants was taken from a ‘judgement sample of persons who can offer some ideas and insights into the phenomenon’, as advocated by Churchill (1979, p. 67). Participants were approached within the author’s academic department, and the ACIS and PACIS conferences. Ethical clearance was obtained for this stage, as it involved interaction with human participants. The exercise was conducted as follows. The researcher read the instructions to the participant, who was then shown the cards and asked if they had any further questions before beginning the exercise. Participants completed the exercise in their own time and notified the researcher when they were finished.

## Data analysis

Table 3 shows the demographic information of the 41 participants.

Consistent with the study’s goals, all 41 participants were involved in some type of organisational research. Academic qualifications were mostly spread between masters’ and Ph.D. degrees (31.7 and 51.2%, respectively). A small number of participants (7.3%) only had undergraduate degrees.

Multidimensional scaling method Multidimensional scaling was selected as the most appropriate method for analysing the construct groupings (Trochim, 1993). The method allows for group analysis in terms of conceptual proximity: its ‘primary purpose is a parsimonious spatial representation of objects’ (Dunn-Rankin, 1983, p. 190), balancing model dimensional complexity against explanatory power. The method makes few assumptions about the data set and the underlying constructs, and it has a rich field of prior research use (Davison, 1983). Figure 1 shows the resulting multidimensional scaling diagram.

The constructs appear to cluster into three distinct groups. The first group, including expenditure, investment and employment, could be described as ‘descriptive dimensions’, possibly containing construct dimensions that are directly observable based on physical characteristics (or organisational structure). For this group alone, indicators such as Number of Employees and Total Assets may be appropriate. The second group, including need for control and need for communication, could be described as ‘behavioural dimensions’, possibly reflecting the managerial or behavioural requirements necessary to maintain the first cluster: for instance, a larger pool of resources may require increased co-ordination and control. The third group, containing capability and competitiveness, could be described as ‘resultant dimensions’. This group could reflect those constructs that result from the combination of the first two groups. However, the flexibility construct does not appear to cluster as closely with the third main grouping.

Interestingly, these groupings were reflected in some participant comments. One participant created a group to reflect ‘behavioural factors or factors that drive behaviour’. This participant described two other clusters, the first comprising ‘the presence (as opposed to the potential) of the organisation’ and the second describing ‘issues involved in managing structural complexity and ability’. Another participant created two groups, including capability, competitiveness and market power, which reflected ‘consequences for the firm of being large’ and ‘consequences for the market of being large’.

Some constructs appear to be closely associated. Participants saw risk aversion and risk tolerance as closely related concepts. A number of participants placed these two dimensions in a group on their own, describing them as an organisation’s ‘risk preference set’ or ‘risk/return profile’. Similarly, structure and organisational levels were also closely situated. One participant described these two dimensions as being heavily dependent on human resources. Interestingly, another participant argued that these constructs, along with complexity, expertise, need for co-ordination and need for communication reflected the internal requirements of the firm. Finally, expertise and scale were also closely aligned. One participant discussed these in terms of ‘outright implications of size’ and that in certain circumstances these could be proxied for by purely structural indicators.

## Study 3: administering the construct

The third stage of Churchill’s programme involves administrating the construct in a pilot test. A questionnaire survey was selected for use in this study.

## Unit of analysis

The unit of analysis in Study 3 was a group of Australian businesses. Organisations within the population had to be of a business nature. This included companies, partnerships and sole traders. Government agencies and departments were excluded from the population. The selection of sample businesses was conducted using random selection from the Telstra Yellow Pages business telephone directory for each Australian state and territory. For each business, the postal address was selected in preference to the street address.

Selection of businesses was subject to two important limitations. First, listings are restricted to firms that had paid for inclusion and existed when the directory was created. Second, the telephone directory may be a substitute advertising technique for ‘smaller’ businesses with fewer financial resources, that they must choose between either an advertisement in the directory or another medium such as the World Wide Web. It was felt that source representativeness was high despite these weaknesses.

## Instrument and materials

Following advice from Berenson & Levine (1999) and Miller (1983), the survey instrument followed the following format. In Section 1, the survey solicited general demographics from respondents. Section 2 contained questions about general IT adoption and use. Section 3 contained questions regarding operationalised versions of each of the constructs identified earlier. Section 4 solicited financial demographics from respondents. Section 5 contained space for additional comments. Sections were arranged so that respondents had to read as few questions as possible, and more sensitive questions were placed towards the end of the instrument. Lengthy survey instruments have been associated with poor response rates (Church, 1993), so the instrument was limited to four pages.

Indicator items were required for each construct. Where possible, questions were adapted from existing instruments in the literature that had already undergone testing. Where no questions were available, new questions were constructed and pre-tested. A research assistant then checked the list of indicators for completeness and prima facie appropriateness.

The market power, competitiveness and economies of scale constructs proved difficult to develop indicators for every indicator found for these constructs either required intimate knowledge of internal cost structures, or a good understanding of each industry’s behavioural and competitive structure. Diwan (1966), Bos & Kolari (2005) and Gropper (1991) suggested that the exact nature of economies of scale is largely unresolved, the dimensions of which are not ‘always analysed with sufficient care’ (Silberston, 1972, p. 369). Economies of scale appears to be a relationship that may change over time (Winsten & Hall, 1961; Burns, 1983), within specific industries (e.g. Silberston, 1972) and whose measurement may also depend heavily on each individual firm’s operating and regulatory environment (Mullineaux, 1978) and risk aspect (Chambers, 1983). Market power is also complex, frequently requiring accurate assessment of internal firm cost functions and market behaviour (Azzam, 1997) and the environmental conditions pertinent to each firm (Pleatsikas & Teece, 2001). Kadiyali et al. (1998) and Corts (1999) highlight the debate and problems surrounding measuring market power. For competitiveness, Oktemgil et al. (2000), Laitinen (2002) and Casper & Matraves (2003) related the construct directly to ‘market share’. Bhatnagar & Sohal (2005) also incorporated flexibility into their measure of competitiveness. Cooper (1989) describes measuring competition with regard to industry structure. Shen et al. (2003) used a comprehensive measure, incorporating 27 items including ‘equipment depreciation rate’, ‘bank credibility grade’ and ‘number of technical patent transfers’. This information may be difficult to acquire, especially for firms that are highly competitive or are in niche industry sectors. It was decided to exclude these from the instrument. The constructs and the indicator questions used in the survey instrument are shown in the Appendix.

## Instrument pre-testing and administration

The first working version of the survey was tested on nine academic staff members, for clarity and completeness. The next stage of the pre-test programme involved two cognitive interviews with candidate respondent businesses. Each interview followed the general programme of cognitive interviewing outlined by Waldron (1986) and Scott et al. (1991). First, each survey question was related to the interviewee in terms of the information sought. The actual question was then read to the interviewee. If the interviewee so desired, the question was repeated for clarity. The interviewee’s answer was then recorded on the sample survey instrument. The interviewee was then directed to confirm their understanding of the question and its meaning.

The first interview was with the Chief Project Information Officer at an agricultural firm. This interview was useful as it revealed three issues. First, the interviewee had difficulty answering questions concerning the number of departments and product modules/components in the firm (need for co-ordination construct) and ‘number of production orders per year’ (complexity construct). Second, the interviewee didn’t know what the firm’s accounts receivable was and also remarked that she felt wary of divulging this information: ‘We get lots of contractors in, so costs vary y some firms are on the knife edge and can’t or don’t want to talk [about costs]’. The interviewee also signalled that they would have to refer some questions to other staff due to a lack of knowledge in that area.

The second interview was with a senior networking consultant at a Canberra-based IT firm. The survey instrument benefited markedly from this particular cognitive interview. As in the previous cognitive interview, the interviewee observed that few smaller firms would be inclined to divulge their internal cost structures. Candidate indicators for the constructs of competitiveness, market power and economies of scale were discussed: few such indicators could be found without probing for sensitive financial information (and hence risking response rates). The interviewee also noted that smaller firms might lack the knowledge to answer all of the questions.

A final version of the instrument was then created. Each business on the sample list was to receive one questionnaire, and a cover letter explaining the origin and purpose of the survey. Included with this letter was an addressed reply-paid envelope. As an incentive, businesses could request a copy of the final report by ticking a box at the end of the survey and enclosing a business card (Berenson & Levine, 1999). Each survey was mailed using official envelopes bearing the university crest (Miller, 1983). Each letter was individually addressed and signed, and included a clause assuring respondents of confidentiality (Thorndike & Hagen, 1969).

## Survey response and data validation

After a 2-week period in which no responses were received, a cut-off date was imposed. Each survey was vetted for completeness and suitability for analysis, and was coded into usable data. Following data coding, distance modelling and cluster analysis of items were conducted. Cluster analysis is used to see whether the constructs clustered in actual firms as they did in theory. Logistic regression was used to determine which constructs reflected how survey respondents saw the size of their own firms. Finally, two-step cluster analysis was used to determine whether the construct group could be used to distinguish between small and large firms.

In total, 179 responses to the survey were received. Of these, 163 responses were usable, yielding an overall response rate of 16.3%. Table 5 shows the breakdown of the response set. Eight firms declined to participate: six firms wrote or telephoned to this effect.

## Response bias

The 16.3% usable response rate was disappointing, but is similar to other IS studies in the Australian commercial sector such as Berrill et al. (2004) (112 responses or 19%), Choe (2004) (47 responses or 25%), Lin & Pervan (2003) (69 responses or 13.8%), Sohal & Ng (1998) (81 responses or 15.6%) and Low et al. (1995) (69 responses or 51%). To assess non-response bias, respondents were split into two groups according to the date of their response in relation to the mean response date (Dillman, 1978). A Mann– Whitney analysis of Number of Employees, Respondent Years with the Firm and Business Age revealed insignificant differences between earlier and later respondents. The bias due to non-response is not known for certain, but is estimated to be negligible.

## Cluster analysis

To examine how the variables cluster in actual firms, cluster analytical methods were chosen in preference to factor analysis. In conducting the study, the researchers were particularly concerned about not ‘leading’ the analysis or outcome. Cluster analysis makes no assumptions about the number of final groups, nor inter- or intra-group structure (Johnson & Wichern, 1982; Norusˇis, 2008). Factor analysis, however, requires at least some ex ante assessment of the likely factor result (Tabachnick & Fidell, 1989). Factor analysis also assumes, ideally, normality of and linearity between candidate variables. At least some variables were unlikely to be normally distributed because there are more smaller, younger firms than larger, older firms in existence (Hart, 1962). In addition, a number of studies (such as Hickson et al., 1967; Kimberly, 1976) have shown that organisational size and age can exhibit significant skewness and kurtosis. While normality analysis and transformation were conducted on some of the variables that were distinctly non-normal, the researchers were wary of using an analytical approach that depended upon this normality. While interpretation of the cluster analysis models may be different to those of factor analysis, the cluster approach also gives the researcher an indication of how soon items and sub-clusters form into larger clusters. It was felt that this heightened the interpretability of the relationship between the variables. In addition, cluster analysis has been used successfully in prior IS research. Examples include Doherty et al. (1999), Segars & Grover (1999), Grover & Saeed (2004), Albert et al. (2004) and Ferratt et al. (2005). Mingers & Harzing (2007) used cluster analysis in their journal study.

## Group membership according to variables

Having determined the layout of item groups, it was necessary to determine whether the constructs can be used to differentiate ‘larger’ and ‘smaller’ organisations. Importantly, the criteria for what constituted a ‘large’ and ‘small’ firm were not yet known. Two-step cluster analysis is a relatively new technique for conducting cluster analysis. As with conventional hierarchical cluster analysis, the method groups similar cases across a range of variables (Johnson & Wichern, 1982), but does not require the researcher to specify ex ante the number of clusters. The number of natural clusters is determined using either the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (according to Fraley & Raftery, 1998). A lower BIC value indicates a superior model. The most appropriate clustering occurs when the ratio of BIC changes shows the greatest jump after the initial case merge commences.

## Advice on using the organisation size construct

Size is a multidimensional construct, with both structural and non-structural relevance in the research literature. The analysis of meaning in Study 2 revealed three main clusters underpinning size: using only one indicator, such as Number of Employees, may yield inconsistent results between studies because it does not capture the entirety of the construct. Using indicators for all of the 21 dimensions of meaning found in Study 1 would make a survey instrument unfeasibly large. In this analysis, eight constructs appeared to distinguish between firms. These were resources, investment, expenditure, scale, employment, organisational levels, risk aversion and distribution. Logistic regression revealed that four constructs that underpin self-nominated size are organisational levels, risk aversion, distribution and employment: this reduced set of constructs may also be able to distinguish between firms. ## Conclusions

The study’s findings are presented in terms of these research questions, as follows.

1. What does the organisational size construct mean? Is there more than one meaning? Is it a construct with a number of sub-dimensions or are there a number of different constructs?

Study 1 explored the literature meaning and measurement of size. The analysis revealed 21 underlying dimensions of the construct. Deeper exploration of each of these constructs revealed that some, such as employment, were well understood and measured in the literature. However, others, such as Economies of Scale, were more complex. Additionally, the literature evidence suggested that authors lacked an accepted view of the dimensions behind organisational size. The analysis of the size as a construct in the IS literature showed a range of applications and perspectives. The literature search identified 21 dimensions of size. While there did not appear to be significant agreement among authors about what size means, there also appeared to be little overt disagreement between studies. One reason for this could be that few papers had gone into significant critical detail about the construct, so that the various meanings of size were not explicit or exclusive.

Evidence suggests that the different meanings of size have not been well distinguished in the literature. A number of studies offered no discussion about their understanding of what the size construct meant. Some papers did not mention their choice of, or justification for, the indicator used to measure size. As a result, there was little evidence that the indicator used was able to adequately capture all of the dimensions of size. Further, because there was a range of size indicators available to the researcher and there was no clear match between measurement and meaning, the extent of this disagreement was not immediately clear.

Study 2’s card sort and multidimensional scaling analysis revealed that these 21 underlying dimensions could be clustered into three distinct groups. The revised size construct comprised three main dimensions, tentatively labelled structural, behavioural and resultant. Some dissimilarity was present in the card sort among these three clusters. One interpretation of this is that there are three separate meanings present across all the constructs.

Study 3 developed indicators for the constructs used in the previous two studies. These were operationalised in a survey of 1000 firms. Cluster analysis of data from the sample of firms revealed two main clusters. One of these clusters, which focused on resources, was similar to a resources cluster present in the card sort exercise.

2. How is the size construct measured? How should the size construct (or constructs) be measured?

Size is measured in a variety of ways, with 25 indicators for organisational size in use in the research literature. These included Annual Sales, Annual Revenue, Number of Employees, Self-nominated Size and Total Assets. Of these indicators, the Number of Employees was the most common.

The reasons for this multitude of indicators were not immediately clear. It may be because many studies took their definitions from the seminal literature of structuralist perspectives, such as the Aston Business School studies, which typically focused on structure and ‘configuration’, without capturing behavioural aspects of the construct. The availability of quantitative data and ease of application may also explain the frequent use of some indicators (Kimberly, 1976).

In determining how size should be measured, analysis showed that, for firms in this data set, eight constructs (resources, investment, expenditure, scale, employment, organisational levels, risk aversion and distribution) were sufficient to distinguish between firms, tentatively in terms of their size. A reduced set of indicators based on these constructs may provide a balance between faithfully representing the construct and reducing survey length.

3. What does this enquiry into the nature of the size construct mean for theory? How can theory that uses size construct as an independent variable or dependent variable be made more sound?

Given the variety of theory types in IS, this research question is answered, first, in terms of theory about size itself, and second, in terms of theory involving size as a construct.

## For theory about what size means

Based on two-step clustering, the following contentions regarding ‘small’ and ‘large’ firms can be made. Based on the first cluster, ‘smaller’ firms may possess fewer structural resources, with fewer employees, less experience, lower degrees of investment and fewer slack resources. Iacovou et al. (1995) wrote, ‘Because small organisations tend to lack such resources, their ability to receive all strategic benefits of the technology is usually limited’. The findings strongly suggest, however, that the mere sum of resources on its own does not faithfully reflect size, as this neglects how those resources are employed and managed within the firm, in addition to the resultant effects of this management. Based on the second cluster, smaller firms may have fewer organisation levels, less need for communication, co-ordination and control and less complexity. If such firms have greater requirements for communications and co-ordination, then this may explain why some studies have found that ‘larger firms’ (according to the Number of Employees) adopt IS. However, even if the Number of Employees is high or the firm’s distribution is large, the organisation may not adopt IS if their level of expenditure or slack resources is low.

Each construct in the third cluster is still subject to substantial literature debate. Tentatively, smaller firms may have less market power, fewer capabilities, may be more flexible but less competitive and are unlikely to possess economies of scale. Additionally, these firms may be more averse to risk and less tolerant of actual exposure to risk. For instance, a firm may have large amounts of resources and hence greater complexity. However, increased complexity may result in lower flexibility.

## For theory involving size

Research that uses the size construct can be made more sound in a variety of ways. First, research can be improved through an improved understanding of what size itself actually means. Evidence suggests that very few authors have attempted to narrow and define the size construct. Greater agreement in this regard may address some of the inconsistencies observed in the extant literature. A more consistent understanding of the size construct should support comparability between studies. In this way, researchers can be more certain that the differences observed are due to the phenomenon under analysis and not the constructs or indicators in use. Following this, the use of indicators that better reflect this conceptual understanding of the size construct will also improve research.

Research can also be improved by using more than one size indicator. The evidence presented here suggests that, most importantly, if proper attention is not paid to the size indicator used, studies risk delivering erroneous results that may not be consistent with the literature theory. In the words of Hitt & Brynjolfsson (1996, p. 122), ‘Our findings do highlight the fact that the answers one gets will depend on the questions one asks and how one addresses them, even when the same data are used’.

The results also suggest that, consistent with Kimberly (1976), a univariate size indicator may not be applicable to all organisations at once because firms may possess contextual differences which render them idiosyncratic. Further, if size indicators are not interchangeable, then perhaps each item is capturing a different aspect of the firm. This raises the possibility of intervening effects between measurement items such that simply using more than one indicator (as in Child, 1973) will also deliver unpredictable effects if the indicators are not used in the correct proportions.
