# THE EFFECT OF AI-ENABLED CREDIT SCORING ON FINANCIAL INCLUSION: EVIDENCE FROM AN UNDERSERVED POPULATION OF OVER ONE MILLION<sup>1</sup>

Chunxiao Li

Department of Information Systems, Faculty of Business in Scitech, School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China Hefei, Anhui, CHINA {Chunxiao.li@ustc.edu.cn}

Hongchang Wang Department of Information Systems, Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas Dallas, TX, U.S.A. {hongchang.wang@utdallas.edu}

Songtao Jiang Department of Data Science, CreditX Inc. Changning, Shanghai, CHINA {jiangsongtao@craiditx.com}

Bin Gu Department of Information Systems, Questrom School of Business, Boston University Boston, MA U.S.A. {bgu@bu.edu}

We studied the effect of a major bank adopting an AI-enabled credit scoring model on financial inclusion as measured by changes to the approval rate, default rate, and utilization level of a personal loan product for an underserved population. The bank serves over 50 million customers and previously used a traditional rule-based model to evaluate the default risk of each loan application. It recently developed an AI model with a higher prediction accuracy of default risk and used the AI model and the traditional model together to assess loan applications for one of its personal loan products. Although the AI model may be more accurate in estimating default risk, little is known about its impact on financial inclusion. We investigated this question using a differencein-differences approach by comparing changes in financial inclusion of the personal loan product that adopted the AI model to that of a similar personal loan product that did not adopt the AI model. We found that the AI model enhanced financial inclusion for the underserved population by simultaneously increasing the approval rate and reducing the default rate. Further analysis attributed the enhancement in financial inclusion to the use of weak signals (i.e., data not conventionally used to evaluate creditworthiness) by the AI model and its sophisticated machine learning algorithms. Our findings are consistent with statistical discrimination theory, as the use of weak signals and sophisticated machine learning algorithms improves prediction accuracy at the individual level, thus reducing the reliance on group characteristics that often lead to financial exclusion. We elaborated on the development process of the AI model to illustrate how and why the AI model can better evaluate members of underserved populations. We also found the impacts of the AI model to be heterogeneous across subgroups, and those with missing weak signals saw smaller improvements in the approval rate. A simulationbased analysis showed that simplified AI models were also able to increase the approval rate and reduce the default rate for this population. Our findings provide rich theoretical and practical implications for social justice by documenting how an AI model designed for improving prediction accuracy can enhance financial inclusion.

Keywords: Financial inclusion, credit scoring, AI models, weak signals, social justice

## Introduction

Financial inclusion refers to the accessibility of useful and affordable financial products and services that cater to the need of individuals and businesses, including transactions, payments, savings, credit, and insurance—delivered in a responsible and sustainable manner. (World Bank, 2023)

Financial inclusion is an important driver of social justice and has significant implications for improving living conditions and supporting the economic development of vulnerable and marginalized groups (Dev, 2006). Research has found that the measures of financial inclusion, such as small business loans, student loans, and others, have a far-reaching effect on employment (Armstrong et al., 2014), education (Gross et al., 2010), health care (Burtch & Chan, 2019), and housing (Zhu, 2011). In this sense, financial inclusion is one of the foundations for social justice that can provide a solution to social injustice.

While financial inclusion is a cornerstone of development (The Global Findex Database, 2021), one main hurdle to financial inclusion (as measured by access to capital) is the lack of credit history, which financial institutions conventionally rely on to assess an applicant’s creditworthiness. Credit history is critical in this evaluation process because it provides detailed and comprehensive information on an applicant’s past usage and loan repayment. For example, The Fair Isaac Corporation’s (FICO) Score uses the consumer’s debt level, length of credit history, and on-time payment history to determine their creditworthiness. However, the heavy reliance on credit history poses a huge challenge to financial inclusion and social justice because members of marginalized groups (“the underserved population” hereafter.<sup>2</sup>) often have a thin credit history or none at all (Abrahams & Zhang, 2008). Due to historical discrimination and long-lasting disadvantages in socioeconomic status, the underserved population has difficulty accessing capital, so they never have a fair chance to build a credit history (O’Neil, 2017). This phenomenon represents a classic “chicken-and-egg” problem: the underserved population needs access to capital to build their credit history, but the lack of credit history prevents them from getting approved by financial institutions in the first place (Engel & McCoy, 2001).

Financial institutions have developed multiple approaches to provide access to capital for the underserved population, including opening more branches in rural and underserved areas, lowering eligibility criteria, and designing specific financial products (Tantri, 2021). These endeavors have enhanced financial inclusion, but also face significant limitations: first, the operational costs are high because new facilities and staff are often involved; second, the default risks are high because the underserved population, on average, has a higher default rate than the regular population; third, these solutions cannot be easily applied at large scale (Bao & Huang, 2021). Therefore, finding a solution that can enhance financial inclusion at scale without incurring significant costs is crucial and urgent.

The advances in information technology, particularly in machine learning, big data, and artificial intelligence (AI), provide a new way to address this notorious financial inclusion problem. In the context of consumer lending, AI is most widely adopted as a credit-scoring model trained on historical loan performance data and used to evaluate applicants’ creditworthiness (Di Giuseppe, 2021; Dobbie et al., 2021; Liu, 2022). Compared to the traditional rule-based credit scoring models developed by underwriting experts based on human experience and descriptive statistics (traditional models hereafter), AI-enabled credit scoring models (AI models hereafter) have two outstanding advantages: first, AI models can incorporate a broader range of information, especially weak financial signals or even nonfinancial signals that are usually not considered in traditional models (Iyer et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2016); second, AI models can utilize advanced machine learning or deep learning algorithms to consider complex relationships between these signals and creditworthiness (Fu et al., 2021; Hurley & Adebayo, 2016). As a result, AI models often have higher prediction accuracy and, thus, a stronger capability to identify the creditworthiness of individual applicants (Gunnarsson et al., 2021). This effect would be more profound for the underserved population because they often lack credit history (i.e., strong signals hereafter), which is used extensively in traditional models. However, concerns and criticism have continued since the invention of AI models, and many of them are still relevant in the financial inclusion context. One data-related concern is that AI models may carry historical bias because historical data are used to train AI models (Gianfrancesco et al., 2018; Martin, 2019; Teodorescu et al., 2021). In addition, using a wide range of data domains may bring new disadvantages and even discrimination to some subgroups, especially those who do not have information in these data domains (Hao & Stray, 2019). One model-related concern is that there is no guarantee that all features make logical sense and no protected features are used because AI models can be “black boxes” (Garfinkel et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2019; Zech et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2021).

Given the promises and pitfalls of AI models, we are interested in understanding how AI models influence financial inclusion. Considering the competing theoretical arguments and uncertain prior expectations of the impacts of AI models, we are motivated to investigate the following three research questions without proposing formal hypotheses:

RQ1—treatment effects: How do AI models influence financial inclusion regarding the approval rate, default rate, and utilization level of the underserved population?

RQ2—underlying mechanisms: Through what mechanisms and logic chains do AI models impact financial inclusion, and what are the key drivers?

RQ3—theoretical boundaries: Under what conditions and for what subpopulations are AI models effective at enhancing financial inclusion?

To answer these research questions, we cooperated with a traditional state-owned bank in China, which serves over 50 million customers (the focal bank hereafter). One decade ago, China started publishing a series of financial inclusion policies to motivate banks to serve the underserved population better. Among all the suggestions provided by these policies, one was to use financial technologies (e.g., AI models) in the evaluation process. Motivated by these policies, the focal bank developed and tested an AI-enabled credit scoring model in one of its personal loan products. This AI model incorporated weak signals and used sophisticated machine learning algorithms to improve the prediction accuracy of default risk. The bank tested the AI model by using it in combination with the existing traditional rule-based model to make the final lending decisions. We also identified a similar product that used the traditional model only during this time period (i.e., the control product) and applied a difference-in-differences (DID) strategy to estimate the impacts of the AI model on financial inclusion.

Our sample covered seven months and nine million applications. Our analysis yielded several important results. First, the adoption of the AI model increased the approval rate for the underserved population and reduced the default rate for both the underserved and regular populations. <sup>3</sup> It also increased the utilization level of the whole population. Second, the enhancement in financial inclusion came from the improved prediction accuracy of the AI model. The use of both weak signals and sophisticated machine learning algorithms contributed to this accuracy improvement because they could help generate novel features that are predictive of creditworthiness and connect these features to creditworthiness in complex and novel ways. While the weak signals provide the raw information for meaningful features and the machine learning algorithms connect features to creditworthiness, the combination of them brought the largest improvement (compared to using weak signals by humans only or using machine learning algorithms on strong signals only). This improvement benefited the underserved population significantly more because they were overlooked by the traditional model and the higher prediction accuracy reduced the reliance on group characteristics that often lead to financial exclusion. Third, the impacts of the AI model were heterogeneous, but almost all subgroups of the underserved population witnessed an increase in the approval rate and a decrease in the default rate. For subgroups with one or more missing data domains of weak signals, the impacts of the AI model were similar, though with a smaller magnitude. Fourth, our simulation analysis showed that the positive impact of AI models on financial inclusion was potentially generalizable to financial institutions without strong IT capability or access to rich weak signals. We found that simplified versions of the focal AI model (e.g., built with a simple AI algorithm or with only one domain of weak signals) could still increase the approval rate and reduce the default rate for the underserved population. These findings held across multiple robustness checks and the consideration of alternative explanations.

Our findings make a theoretical contribution to the AI and social justice literature by revealing how and why AI models can influence financial inclusion. More specifically, we show that AI models with a goal to improve prediction accuracy can have a significant and positive impact on financial inclusion. This effect is driven by AI models’ ability to utilize both weak signals and sophisticated machine learning algorithms to improve prediction accuracy, which reduces the bank’s reliance on traditional strong signals in loan evaluation. Because the underserved population often lacks strong signals, the reduced reliance on such signals leads to enhanced financial inclusion. Therefore, AI models can widen access to capital for the underserved population. Our findings also make a practical contribution by demonstrating a feasible and powerful AI tool to enhance financial inclusion, even when it was trained to enhance the prediction accuracy of default risk. We provide rich information and detailed discussions on the design process of AI models, the compliance issues in using AI models and privacy-sensitive data, the implementation of AI models with traditional models, and the heterogeneous impacts of AI models. All these findings could help practitioners decide how to develop their own AI models and how to deploy AI models in their business settings. We also discuss the limitations associated with AI models. First, AI models do not necessarily benefit all applicants. A small portion of applicants might have a lower chance of approval because AI models can better understand their creditworthiness, though negatively. Second, AI models cannot solve financial inclusion issues caused by structural social injustice (e.g., structural racism or discrimination that has led to unfair opportunities/wealth allocation, which in turn has affected applicants’ creditworthiness). These issues would require systematic policy interventions. As our research shows, AI tools can, however, be quite effective at addressing financial inclusion issues caused by statistical discrimination (Laouenan & Rathelot, 2022). Third, policymakers should collect more empirical evidence to understand the impacts of AI models and work on actionable AI regulations to deal with privacy or financial exclusion issues.

## Literature Review

Our study is related to three streams of research, including the literature on financial inclusion and social justice, the literature on AI in lending, and the literature on weak signals and AI-enabled credit scoring.

## Financial Inclusion and Social Justice

The concept of financial inclusion emerged more than a decade ago, advocating for access to valuable and affordable financial products and services (United Nations, 2015; World Bank, 2010). Financial inclusion is a critical component of social inclusion, which promotes a just society for all (i.e., all people should have access to education, capital, employment, health care, political rights, and housing) (United Nations, 1995). Financial inclusion is the foundation for social justice as it provides key financial resources (United Nations, 2006). For example, the underserved population needs financial resources such as personal loans to improve their immediate surroundings and to create new opportunities for themselves and their families. Adequate financial access can provide them with better healthcare, potential home ownership, and career and employment opportunities (Mitlin, 2008).

The key challenge in financial inclusion is that the underserved population often lacks sufficient financial literacy and credit history, which prevents financial institutions from accurately assessing their risk and creditworthiness (Loufield et al., 2018). Because traditional models tend to rely heavily on credit history, the lack of credit history is considered a sign of uncreditworthiness (Yawe & Prabhu, 2015). Therefore, the underserved population is likely to be classified as high risk and thus is likely to be directly rejected, charged high interest rates (Brown et al., 2019), or provided with low-limit lines of credit or loan amounts (Jappelli, 1990). As a result, the underserved population may have difficulty obtaining loans or lines of credit from traditional financial institutions (Leyshon & Thrift, 1995) and fall victim to predatory lending (Lusardi & Scheresberg, 2013). To summarize, the tendency for financial institutions to lend to borrowers with good credit history creates a “chicken-and-egg” problem that keeps preventing the underserved and marginalized population from gaining access to capital.

Multiple initiatives and actions have been taken to solve this problem, including opening more bank branches in rural and underserved areas, reducing eligibility criteria for the underserved population, and designing specific products (Tantri, 2021). These efforts have solved the financial inclusion issue to some extent. However, they come with high operational costs and default risks (Awaworyi-Churchill, 2020; Cull, 2011). Our research aims to study whether the advances in information technology, especially AI-enabled credit scoring models, could help financial institutions enhance financial inclusion for the underserved population.

## AI in Lending

AI has been widely used in the finance industry for algorithmic trading, identity verification, customer service, fraud detection, risk assessment, asset valuation, etc. (Gomber et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2017; Kankanhalli & Mellouli, 2019; Lee et al., 2020). Our literature review focuses on the application of AI in credit scoring and lending decisionmaking. A few traditional financial institutions and many alternative lenders (e.g., Lending Club, Prosper.com, OnDeck) have already started using AI models to consider alternative data and mine complex patterns between borrower data and loan performance (Jagtiani & Lemieux, 2019; Liu et al., 2015). For example, Lending Club and Prosper.com used hundreds of variables in their AI models to evaluate the creditworthiness of their applicants. As a result, they rely less on FICO scores and have thus been able to lend money to applicants with relatively low FICO scores (Nowak et al., 2018). OnDeck and Kabbage included social media data, ecommerce data, and transportation data in their AI models to evaluate small businesses and have outperformed traditional banks that use sales data and credit data only (Godbillon-Camus & Godlewski, 2005).

Extant studies have documented the impacts of AI models on consumer and small business lending, including both the reduction in the default rate (Serrano-Cinca et al., 2015) and the changes in approved borrowers’ features (Agarwal et al., 2020; Bartlett et al., 2022). However, there are also concerns and criticisms about the adoption of AI in general and of AIenabled credit scoring models in particular (Hiller, 2020). One concern is that the selection of the training sample may carry historical bias and discrimination (Gianfrancesco et al., 2018; Mejia & Parker, 2021). The other concern is that AI models are typically “black boxes” that do not clearly show the relationships between data input and creditworthiness (Neumann et al., in press; Rudin, 2019). If not handled carefully, these two issues may strengthen the historical bias or lead to new biases (Cowgill et al., 2020).

Given the promises and pitfalls of AI models in lending, the adoption of AI-enabled credit scoring models by traditional financial institutions has been expected for a long time but has yet to materialize. Because traditional financial institutions face more scrutiny, AI models’ design, deployment, impacts, and implications would also be expected to differ from those of alternative lenders. To fill this research gap, we cooperated with a traditional bank to understand the impacts of a compliance-compatible AI model on the approval rate and default rate of the underserved population. We also examined the underlying mechanisms and the conditions under which AI models can effectively enhance financial inclusion.

## Weak Signals and AI-Enabled Credit Scoring

One advantage of AI models is their ability to utilize weak signals. We adopt the concept of “weak signals” from the existing literature to refer to data that is not directly related to creditworthiness or not conventionally used in traditional credit scoring models (Mendonça et al., 2012). Existing studies also used similar terms—for example, “alternative data” (Loufield, 2018; Lu et al., 2023; Serrano-Cinca and Gutiérrez-Nieto, 2016), “soft information” (Iyer et al., 2016; Liberti & Peterson, 2019), or “nonfinancial data.” We prefer the term “weak signals” because it indicates that the data is not only unconventional but also noisy in nature. Traditional models rely heavily on credit history data, e.g., repayment information, utilization level, and frequency of credit inquiries (Ozler, 1992). They sometimes also consider employment, income, assets, and home ownership (Johnson, 2019). We call these domains of information strong signals because they have strong and intuitive relationships with creditworthiness and are largely available in numerical values with structural patterns (Liberti, 2019).

Unlike strong signals, weak signals can cover a broader range of financial or nonfinancial data domains. Previous studies have discussed the following data domains of weak signals: electronic footprint and trajectory (Kim, 2020; Lu et al., 2023), social networks (Lin et al., 2013; Lu et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2022), educational background (Li & Hu, 2019), lenderborrower communication (Xu & Chau, 2018), mobile phone usage (Ma et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2023), facial information (Chen et al., 2023), and other soft information (Iyer et al., 2016, Hou et al., 2023). Weak signals have been underutilized by traditional models due mainly to three reasons (Autor, 2014; Fügener et al., 2022; Liberti, 2018; Monk et al., 2019): (1) they do not have a clear and direct relationship with creditworthiness, so including them challenges underwriting transparency and explainability; (2) they may contain noisy information and unstructured contents, so significant efforts are required to make sense of them and incorporate them into traditional models; (3) they are often difficult to obtain and suffer from missing data issues.

Although taking advantage of weak signals can be challenging, they can be extremely valuable in credit scoring, especially for the underserved population who often have a thin credit history or none at all. The value of weak signals stems from the fact that weak signals can provide helpful information on an applicant’s willingness or ability to repay loans (Lu et al., 2023). For example, data collected from mobile financial apps can reveal an applicant’s financial management skills. Data related to social connections and social media activities can reflect an applicant’s social capital, which is known to influence career success (Seibert et al., 2017). To fulfill the potential of weak signals, modern machine learning algorithms such as random forests or neural networks can learn complex models from vast training data sets (LeCun et al., 2015; Schmidhuber, 2015). These algorithms can considerably reduce the cost and difficulty of utilizing weak signals and discover interaction effects between strong and weak signals that no traditional model has ever exploited. Due to these advantages of AI models, we posit that AI models can help financial institutions better understand their applicants, especially members of the underserved population who lack credit history.

## Data and Empirical Strategy

## Research Setting and Institutional Background

We collaborated with a state-owned regional bank to understand the impacts of AI models on financial inclusion. The focal bank serves over 50 million people in its home province, providing various financial products such as saving accounts, bill payments, social security funds, credit cards, personal loans, mortgages, and investments. The focal bank previously applied traditional rule-based models in its loan evaluation process. However, it was motivated by a series of government policies advocating banking financial inclusion and, starting in 2020, decided to experiment with AI-enabled credit scoring models in one of its personal loan products. Different government agencies at various levels published these policies, and the essential purpose was to advocate for banks to extend access to capital for the underserved population by using AI models to better assess their creditworthiness. We summarize the major policies in Table 1. This setting provided us with a unique empirical opportunity to investigate the impacts of AI models because the use of AI models was previously not allowed or not encouraged for use by major banks.

The focal bank had multiple personal loan products and planned to apply AI models to all these products eventually. It selected one product as a pilot (the treated product hereafter) because the business team was more open to using new technologies. A development team spent about six months training an AI model to predict applicants’ creditworthiness (as reflected by credit scores ranging from 300 to 850) and implemented the AI model into the treated product on February 4, 2021. It is important to note that the AI model focused solely on default risk, without any adjustment to favor applicants from the underserved population.

The treated product provides each approved borrower a credit allowance, typically between 2,000 and 20,000 CNY. Once the borrowers use their credit, they must repay the principal and interest within one month. The product’s interest rate was determined by a time-variant base point of interest and did not vary across individual applicants. Upon approval, the credit allowance was determined by a separate formula (rather than the traditional model or the AI model), which was mainly related to the bank’s available funds and the borrower’s income. <sup>4</sup> The AI model was used only in the underwriting process to determine whether to approve a loan application. Before the adoption of the AI model, only 15% of loans were issued to the underserved population, who accounted for 80% of the province’s population. In contrast, 85% of the loans were issued to the regular population, who accounted for only 20% of the population of this province (the regular population hereafter). We were able to identify a similar personal loan product provided by the focal bank that had a similar design and served the same customer pool (the control product hereafter).<sup>5</sup> During our observation period, no significant adjustment was applied to the control product (the focal bank started to develop an AI model for the control product in 2022). Figure 1 displays the timeline for the initial adoption of the AI model and a follow-up update on the AI model (more on the update later).

We collected data on the treated and control products from October 1, 2020, to April 30, 2021.<sup>6</sup> We ended up with a sample of 2.5 million applications and 1.2 million approved loans for the treated product and a sample of 6.8 million applications and 3.2 million approved loans for the control product. We also collected rich data on applicants’ characteristics. We report the descriptive statistics of the regular population in Table 2 and those of the underserved population in Table 3. To fully reveal the dynamics of the applicant pool after the adoption of the AI model, we provide detailed statistics in two-week time slots.

As reported in Tables 2 and 3, before the adoption of the AI model, around 50% of applicants from the regular population were approved, whereas only 17% of applicants from the underserved population were approved. Three aspects of applicants’ characteristics may explain this phenomenon: the first distinction between the underserved and regular populations is that applicants from the underserved population often had a limited credit history and a higher default risk; the second distinction is that applicants from the regular population were often employed by large firms and government agencies, whereas applicants from underserved population were often self-employed (e.g., small business owners or farmers); the third distinction is that applicants from the regular population often lived in big cities, whereas applicants from the underserved population often lived in small cities, towns, or rural areas. The descriptive statistics indicate that the launch of the AI model dramatically increased the approval rate for the underserved population and reduced the default rate for both the underserved and regular populations.

As shown in Figure 2, the development team collected data from both internal and external sources in Step 1, including not only the aforementioned strong signals (e.g., credit data and asset data) but also weak signals (e.g., app data). In Step 2, the team cleaned the data by connecting multiple data sources, checking extreme values, handling missing values, and converting categorical variables. The team then generated features from all data domains using different feature generation techniques in Step 3 and trained four individual learners using different combinations of features to predict loan performance (i.e., default or not) in Step 5. After the initial individual models were built, the team reviewed the feature importance index of each feature, the consistency of the index of each feature, and the meanings of top features to remove unimportant, inconsistent, or hard-to-interpret features in Step 4. Steps 4 and 5 formed an iterative process because features were selected after models were trained and new models were further trained with new features. Once all features were confirmed, the winning algorithm was selected for each individual model.<sup>8</sup> Then in Step 6, the team trained the ultimate ensemble model using the four individual models and tested its performance. The final AI model is a two-layer model that combines four individual learners and one ensemble learner (see Figure A1 in the Appendix for the kernel of the AI model). In Step 7, before the implementation of the AI model, the development team and the validation team reviewed the model again and removed hard-to-interpret features or features related to protected features (e.g., gender and disability). This change pointed the process back to Step 4 and resulted in a slightly adjusted AI model. After the model was eventually approved and implemented, the development team continued to monitor its performance over time and its potential impacts on marginalized subgroups (i.e., minority races) in Step 8.

The development team used data samples from July 1, 2020, to December 31, 2020 (around 300,000 issued loans), to build the AI model. The sample was randomly split into three data sets, including 50% training data, 30% validation data, and 20% testing data. The training data was used in Step 5 for training individual learners. The validation data was used in Step 5 to confirm individual winning algorithms and in Step 6 to train the ensemble learner. The testing data was used in Step 6 to determine the winning ensemble learner. The training process followed a common supervised learning approach and ended up with a LightGBM model as the final winner. It is worth emphasizing that the training data set contained the natural proportion of the underserved population, and the training process did not assign higher weights to the underserved population. The regular and underserved populations shared the same features and were trained using the same model. To summarize, compared to the traditional model, the AI model (1) uses both strong and weak signals, (2) is trained using machine learning algorithms, and (3) allows complex connections between features and creditworthiness. Due to financial regulations, the focal bank did not replace the traditional model with the AI model entirely. Figure 3 shows how the AI model worked with the traditional model.

As indicated by Figure 3, the traditional and AI models first evaluated the applicants independently. Then, a decision matrix was used to determine the final lending decision. The cutoff score was 520 before the adoption of the AI model; thus, applicants with a score above 520 would have been approved if the traditional model had been used. However, once adopted, the AI model could overturn the recommendations from the traditional model (as indicated in the shaded cells in Figure 3), from approval to denial, from denial to approval, or from approval/denial to pending (pending means the bank needs to collect more information from the applicant and feed the information into this evaluation process again to decide). When the traditional and AI models shared the same recommendation, the evaluation process resulted in a straightforward decision.

## Identification Strategy and Empirical Specifications

The adoption of the AI model for the treated product by the focal bank provided us with a unique opportunity to investigate the impacts of AI-enabled credit scoring models on financial inclusion. We applied a difference-in-differences (DID) identification strategy to estimate their impacts. We took several steps to remove potential confounding effects in our research setting.

The first challenge came from the potential impacts of government policies on financial inclusion. One potential impact of the government policies was the change in the applicant pool from the underserved population. Because many of the policies were issued several years ago before the adoption of the AI model, we do not believe they would have confounded our results. Furthermore, these policies would influence both the treated and control products similarly, which would be handled by the DID strategy. Another potential impact of government policies is on the bank’s loan evaluation process and eligibility criteria. We communicated with the focal bank to fully understand their actions to comply with these government policies. The focal bank explicitly decided to use the same cutoff score for loan approval decisions for both the underserved and regular populations, as it intended to enhance financial inclusion without hurting lending performance. Besides adopting the AI model, the focal bank also took two actions. During the third quarter of 2020, they proactively promoted all their financial products to the entire population in their home province and adjusted the traditional rule-based models. We therefore excluded this period from our sample to eliminate any confounding effects (this is why our sample starts on October 1, 2020). The focal bank also had a few bankers and loan officers reach out to some applicants from the underserved population to promote its products and evaluate the applications manually during our observation period. This behavior would have confounded our findings, so we removed all the applications that were evaluated manually (manual evaluations were marked in the bank’s database).

The improvement in financial inclusion occurred against the backdrop of the traditional model, which evaluated applicants using strong signals only. Because the underserved population does not necessarily possess such strong signals, the traditional model often categorically rejects them. This is a form of statistical discrimination (Fang & Moro, 2011). The AI model addressed this issue by incorporating weak signals and using advanced machine learning algorithms, thus providing more accurate estimations of applicants’ creditworthiness. Our results show that the AI model identified some “bad” regular applicants and denied access to capital for them, leading to a slight (though insignificant) reduction in the approval rate and a substantial reduction in the default rate. At the same time, the AI model increased the approval rate and decreased the default rate for the underserved population. Our results are consistent with the AI model update, which further increased the approval rate and decreased the default rate for the underserved population.

The findings from Tables 4 and 5 suggest that the AI model can better distinguish good applicants from bad applicants. This has two implications: first, the approved applicants should have a lower default rate, which we have already seen; second, the rejected applicants should have a higher default rate if approved. In other words, the default rate of the rejected population should be positively related to the adoption of the AI model. However, their loan performance would have no ground truth because their applications would have been rejected. To approximate their loan performance, we identified applicants rejected by the treated product or the control product but approved by other financial products within one week.<sup>9</sup> We set one week as the time interval to increase the likelihood that these applicants’ creditworthiness would not change. Using their default status with other financial products as a proxy, we conducted a DID analysis of the impact of the AI model on the lending performance of the rejected population. Table 6 reports the results and shows that the applicants rejected by the treated product are associated with a higher default rate after the adoption of the AI model. In addition, the applicants rejected by the treated product are also associated with lower utilization levels after the adoption of the AI model.

Our main findings suggest that the AI model simultaneously reduces Type I errors (false approval) and Type II errors (false denial), consistent with our theoretical expectation that the AI model reduces statistical discrimination against the underserved population.

## Mechanisms

## Prediction Accuracy

To understand the seemingly contradictory finding that the AI model simultaneously increases the approval rate for the underserved population and reduces the default rate and increases the utilization level for the whole population, we note that an essential requirement of the AI model is that it offers a more accurate creditworthiness prediction model for both the regular and underserved populations. To assess the prediction accuracy of the AI model, we provide descriptive statistics on the approval rate and the default rate by looking at the distributions of credit score brackets generated by the traditional and AI models in Table 7.

Several interesting findings emerge from Table 7. First, the traditional model often underestimated the underserved population. For example, the traditional model assigned only 6.8% of the underserved population a credit score between 620 and 850. However, this number was 15.7% for the AI model. Given that the traditional model used an absolute cutoff score (i.e., 520) to approve applicants, the adjustment on credit score distribution by the AI model helped approve more applicants from the underserved population. Second, the credit score from the AI model had better prediction accuracy, which can be seen from the correlation between the credit score and the default rate. The traditional model did a poor job for the underserved population as the credit score was not closely correlated to the default rate (e.g., the default rate in the 620-850 range is even higher than the default rate in the 520-620 range). The AI model vastly improved prediction accuracy, as the default rate of the above-cutoff population was significantly reduced, and the differences in the default rate between various credit score brackets were significantly enlarged. These two facts collectively explain why the AI model can simultaneously increase the approval rate and decrease the default rate. The more accurate and fair distribution of the credit scores for the underserved population qualifies more applicants from the underserved population and better distinguishes the creditworthy ones from the uncreditworthy ones.

## Data Domains

To strengthen and deepen this logic chain, we further investigated the factors in the AI model that contribute to the increase in prediction accuracy. As we mentioned earlier, this increase may come from the use of weak signals (including the features generated from them), advanced machine learning algorithms, or both. We first compared the data foundations for the traditional and AI models, and then estimated each mechanism’s magnitude. The traditional model of the treated product mainly relies on human expertise to generate features and rules using strong signals (e.g., Rule 1: the number of overdue credit products is more than 3). Experts assign each rule a score, and the collection of rules hit by each applicant determines the final credit score and lending decision. The traditional model eventually includes about 80 rules built upon 50 features. Unlike the traditional model, the AI model utilizes both strong and weak signals to predict the default rate (see Table 8). Specifically, the traditional model uses data domains 1-6. In addition to these strong signals, the AI model also uses social security fund data, provident fund data, in-app financial behavioral data, and in-app nonfinancial behavioral data (i.e., Data Domains 7-10).

As defined earlier, weak signals refer to information that is not directly related to creditworthiness and suffers from noisy patterns and missing data issues. First, none of the weak signals is directly related to creditworthiness. For example, the provident fund can only be used to purchase real estate but cannot be used to pay off credit card bills or other loans; the social security fund can only be withdrawn after retirement, and, again, cannot be used to pay off credit card bills or other loans; the in-app financial behaviors include depositing money, paying bills, reading statements, which have no direct relationship with creditworthiness; in-app nonfinancial behaviors include reading news, checking advertisements, and watching videos, which again have no direct relationship with creditworthiness. Second, human experts have limited ability to infer complex relationships between weak signals and creditworthiness. For example, given the employment status and the nature of their jobs, the underserved population may exhibit various patterns on their social security fund deposits, which makes it difficult for human experts to infer repayment capability. Therefore, making weak signals into rules is largely impractical for traditional models.

## Weak Signals and Advanced Models

Given the data domains mentioned above, we first investigated the extent to which the impact comes from weak signals. If the enhanced financial inclusion is due to the inclusion of weak signals, we would expect traditional models to work better using both strong and weak signals than using strong signals only. To examine this expectation, we compared three decision models. The first one was the actual traditional model in use on February 4, 2021; the second one was a simulated traditional model, which used rules built on features generated from both strong and weak signals. Because the actual traditional model uses Data Domains 1-6 only, we had to borrow some important features of weak signals from the AI model and convert them into rules to add to the traditional model; the third one was the actual AI model. The prediction target was whether a loan had defaulted, and we used the loans approved after February 4, 2021, to evaluate the prediction accuracy of each model. Table A2 reports the results. The results show that weak signals can help the traditional model improve prediction accuracy. The deep root of this improvement stems from how features are generated from weak signals and why they are predictive of creditworthiness. Table A3 demonstrates this feature generation process and provides further details and evidence on the value of weak signals.

The second channel we investigated is the extent to which the increase in prediction accuracy comes from advanced machine learning algorithms. If this were the case, we should expect the AI model to work better than the traditional model with the same set of information (i.e., domains 1-6 in Table 8). To examine this expectation, we compared three decision models. The first one was the actual traditional model in use on February 4, 2021; the second one was the actual AI model in use on February 4, 2021, but we only fed strong signals into the AI model to generate credit scores; the third one came from a simulation approach in which we used strong signals only to train a new AI model with loans approved before February 4, 2021. We report the results in Table A4. The results show that the use of advanced algorithms enables AI to achieve higher prediction accuracy, even when the AI and traditional models use the same set of information. The deep root of this improvement stems from the advanced feature generation techniques and the complex yet reliable connections between features and creditworthiness. Table A5 demonstrates this feature generation process and provides further details and evidence.

To better understand how the two engines of the AI model influence financial inclusion for the underserved population, we summarize the findings from Tables A2 and A4 in Table 9 based on the AUC values.<sup>11</sup> Column 1 in Table A2 serves as the baseline in Table 9. Columns 2-4 in Table 9 reflect the relative improvement in AUC from the inclusion of weak signals, the use of advanced algorithms, and a combination of using weak signals and advanced algorithms. We subtracted 0.5 (i.e., the minimum AUC value) from the raw AUC values and then compared the relative change in that new value.

Consistent with our previous findings, Table 9 shows that both weak signals and advanced algorithms contribute to the improvement in prediction accuracy. Comparing the magnitudes in Column 2 with those in Column 3, we conclude that the improvement from advanced algorithms is greater than that from weak signals. Table 9 further shows that the improvement brought by weak signals and advanced algorithms to the underserved population is stronger than that brought to the regular population. Because of the lack of strong signals from the underserved population and the limited ability of human experts to create nuanced features and infer complex relationships between weak signals and creditworthiness, the AI model can take better advantage of weak signals with advanced algorithms. The last finding is that the underserved population benefits more from including weak signals than the regular population when using advanced algorithms. The additional lift from Column 3 to Column 4 is 43 percentage points for the regular population but 128 percentage points for the underserved population. This is because strong signals are largely available and precise for the regular population, so weak signals provide little additional value. However, as strong signals are largely missing for the underserved population, weak signals become particularly important to the underserved population in evaluating their creditworthiness.

## Additional Predictive Power of Weak Signals

To further understand the value of weak signals, we explored two aspects of weak signals: their predictive power on default likelihood and the relationships between weak signals and strong signals. Table 10 reports the importance/power of each data domain in explaining default likelihood. We employed a single-domain explainability approach, which uses features from only one data domain to train the AI model each time. The performance reflects the highest potential predictive power of each data domain. The results suggest that weak signals contain reasonable predictive power, which is close to the power contained by strong signals. This predictive power is the foundation for weak signals’ value in improving prediction accuracy.

To assess the relationship between weak signals and strong signals in predicting default, we focused on correlation scores. However, we note that within each data domain there can be dozens or hundreds of features. Therefore, we first applied a principal-component analysis (i.e., PCA) transformation and used the primary principal component to capture the maximum variation in all features from the same domain. We then used the primary component from each data domain to produce a correlation table. Table 11 shows the results. Strong signals are correlated mainly among themselves, but weak signals are not highly correlated to strong signals. An exception is that social security fund data and provident fund data are highly correlated with each other and both are highly correlated to identity information and income asset information. However, they are not highly correlated with other strong signals. In-app financial behavioral data and inapp nonfinancial behavioral data are highly correlated, but neither of them is correlated to any other data domains, including both strong signals and other weak signals. To summarize, Tables 10 and 11 collectively support that weak signals provide valuable and additional information beyond strong signals in predicting creditworthiness.

## Heterogeneous Impacts and Statistical Discrimination

Although the adoption of the AI model increases the average approval rate for the underserved population, it may have unequal effects across the underserved population. It is also possible for the AI model to hurt certain subgroups in the underserved population. We first investigated the heterogeneous impacts of the AI model on five subgroups categorized based on the five criteria of the underserved population. We reran the basic DID model within each subsample and report the coefficients of “the initial launch of the AI model” on the approval rate and default rate in Table 12. The AI model increases the approval rate for four subgroups (except for applicants without permanent residence) and reduces the default rate for all five subgroups. The results indicate that the AI model largely improves financial inclusion across the underserved population except those with no permanent residence and helps banks better understand the creditworthiness of the underserved population.

As one key engine of the AI model is the use of weak signals, we further analyzed the impact of the AI model on subgroups with incomplete or missing weak signals. We generated dummy variables to represent the status of missing data in each data domain and interact them with “the initial launch of the AI model” in Equation (1) to investigate how the impact of the AI model on the approval rate varies with missing data. Because inapp financial behavioral data and in-app nonfinancial behavioral data come from the same data source, we combined them to generate one dummy variable. We also generated one dummy variable to represent cases in which all weak signals were missing. Table 13 reports the results.

The negative coefficients of the interaction terms indicate that missing weak signals generally reduce the impact of the AI model on the approval rate. However, because the main effect of the AI model is positive for the underserved population, subgroups with missing data are still better off, and the absolute impact of the AI model on the approval rate is still positive and significant. One potential reason is that all data domains of weak signals help the AI model better understand the creditworthiness of the underserved population, and most of the time, they help correct the underestimated credit score generated by the traditional model. When one data domain is missing, the other data domains can still provide helpful information to the AI model.

The ability of the AI model to enhance financial inclusion for the underserved population comes from its improved prediction accuracy, which comes from the use of weak signals and advanced algorithms. As a result, the AI model reduces reliance on strong signals which often favor the regular population and disadvantage the underserved population. To further investigate the AI model’s ability to reduce statistical discrimination, we focused on three strong features that are considered important in traditional loan evaluation: urban household designation, self-employed status, and the availability of credit history. These three features are also the key differentiators between the regular and underserved populations. We posited that the importance of these features in underwriting will be reduced after the launch of the AI model. To assess this proposition, we reran Equation (1) by including interaction terms between “the initial launch of the AI model” and these three features. The coefficients of these interaction terms indicate how the approval likelihood changes for each applicant type. Table 14 reports the results.

Table 14 shows that applicants who were not self-employed, were living in urban areas, and had a credit history had a higher likelihood of being approved before the adoption of the AI model. After the adoption of the AI model, their advantages shrank significantly within both the regular and underserved populations, as indicated by the fact that the coefficients of these interaction terms all have opposite signs. This effect is even more substantial for the underserved population: the approval advantage/preference shrank by 47.1% (i.e., 0.008/-0.017) for employed applicants, 52.0% (i.e., -0.013/0.025) for applicants living in urban areas, and 27.3% (i.e., -0.003/0.011) for applicants with a credit history. Although applicants who were self-employed, living in nonurban areas, and lacking a credit history were still considered unfavorable compared to applicants who were employed, living in urban areas, and having a credit history, the bias toward them (or the disadvantage faced by them) was reduced significantly by the adoption of the AI model.

Equation (3) extends Equation (1) with one change. It splits the $A I M o d e l _ { j t }$ variable into multiple relative time dummies based on how far away the current observation is from the adoption time of the AI model in terms of weeks (we used a week dummy rather than a day dummy to smooth the trend). These relative time dummies are always 0 for applications of the control product. One and only one of them would be 1 for each application of the treated product. When τ is negative, the observation is τ weeks before the adoption date. We collapsed all observations five weeks or more than five weeks before adoption into the -5 relative time dummy and removed the -1 relative time dummy to serve as the baseline. When τ is nonnegative, the observation is τ weeks after the adoption date. We also collapsed all observations five weeks or more than five weeks after adoption into the +5 relative time dummy. These nonnegative relative time dummies estimate how soon the AI model takes effect and whether the effect persists. Table 15 reports the results.

We detected no heterogeneous trends or significant differences between the treated product and the control product for the approval rate and default rate before the adoption of the AI model. The utilization level showed one significant difference between the treated and control products, but it was far away (i.e., 5+ weeks) from the adoption date and is unlikely to have confounded our findings. These results support the parallel trends assumption and alleviate the concern that the selection of the treated product, rather than the adoption of the AI model, leads to the enhancement in financial inclusion.

Although the control product seems to be a good counterfactual based on the results of the relative time model, there are other concerns about the self-selection bias of the AI model at different levels and the confounding effect of the focal bank’s other actions. ## Compliance and Generalizability

The generalizability of our findings is important, given that using AI models and privacy-sensitive data is challenging from both a legal perspective and a technical perspective.<sup>12</sup> As both strong and weak signals are often privacy sensitive, their use is strictly regulated by data privacy protection laws in different countries. These laws specify customers’ data rights and regulate firms’ data collection, data disclosure, data sharing, and data protection practices. Different countries have developed various laws and regulations related to the use of AI models and personal data, so it is important to understand their details and the practices complying with them. Although the focal bank operates in China, it developed the AI model with multiple potential regulations in mind because China often moves quickly on new regulations. Table 17 summarizes the focal bank’s actions and solutions to regulatory requirements. We also complemented Table 17 with potential solutions from the literature, which can serve as a roadmap for practitioners looking for solutions to specific regulatory issues. With the compliance issue in mind, businesses and financial institutions have developed novel ways to incorporate weak signals in their assessment of customer creditworthiness, as reported in Table A12. For example, NeuroID, a company using applicants’ online behavior during loan or insurance application processes to predict their default rate and fraud risk, has experienced threedigit growth in recent years.<sup>13</sup>

Another challenge to generalizability is the technical capability and data availability. Not all financial institutions have the capability to build a sophisticated AI model or have the resources to access and process weak signals. We conducted a simulation analysis to address this generalizability concern by investigating the potential impacts of simplified AI models. We trained different versions of simplified AI models and assessed their performance. The first version used one LightGBM model/learner only (instead of four individual learners and one ensemble learner), and the second to fifth versions used one data domain of weak signals only (instead of all four data domains). We reran Equations (1) and (2) by assuming these AI models were implemented at the adoption date and report the results in Table 18. The results show that although the impacts of these simplified AI models are weaker, they can still enhance financial inclusion to some extent. The impacts on the approval rate for the underserved population ranged from 3.3% to 8.6%, whereas the impacts on the default rate ranged from -0. 2% to -0.4%. In conclusion, even simple AI models can still enhance financial inclusion in a sizeable and significant way.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Financial inclusion is essential for members of the underserved population, who are often from rural areas, are self-employed, and lack a credit history. Enhancing financial inclusion is a major task for promoting social justice because access to capital may influence opportunities for education, healthcare, employment, housing, etc. Common solutions, such as opening more branches in rural areas or reducing requirements for the underserved population, can help enhance financial inclusion but often come with high operational costs or default risk. We examined the financial inclusion issue from another perspective by investigating the role of modern information technologies. More specifically, we investigated the impacts of an AI-enabled credit scoring model on the underserved population’s approval rate, default rate, and utilization level. We collaborated with a regional bank in China and took advantage of a rare event in which the focal bank deployed an AI model to work with its traditional model to evaluate one of its personal loan products. This setting allowed us to identify a similar personal loan product during the same period as a control group and apply a DID strategy to estimate the impacts of the AI model.

Our findings suggest that the AI model enhances financial inclusion without sacrificing loan performance. The AI model significantly increases the approval rate for the underserved population while decreasing the default rate and increasing the utilization level for the whole population. These results are encouraging because they help solve the persistent dilemma of how to balance financial inclusion and loan performance. Our study solves this dilemma using an AI model to improve financial inclusion without hurting lending performance. This is feasible because the AI model can leverage weak signals and advanced algorithms, thus reducing statistical discrimination against the underserved population and improving their access to capital.

Our study also delves into the underlying mechanisms of why AI models can simultaneously increase the approval rate and reduce the default rate for the underserved population. The direct reason is that the AI model improves the prediction accuracy of the evaluation/underwriting process. Weak signals and advanced algorithms are two engines for improving prediction accuracy. Human experts and advanced techniques can generate novel and meaningful features from weak signals, which are then used by machine learning algorithms to predict creditworthiness. Our empirical analysis finds that both weak signals and advanced algorithms can contribute to prediction accuracy and the combination of them brings the largest improvement, especially for the underserved population.

Our study contributes to the social justice literature by investigating one of its foundations—financial inclusion. We focused on one potential solution enabled by modern AI technologies and found that AI models can enhance financial inclusion without hurting lending performance. Our study also contributes to the AI and consumer lending literature by revealing the impacts of an AI model on financial inclusion. Although previous studies have documented the value of machine learning models and weak signals in improving crediting scoring models, few studies have considered their impact on financial inclusion (Di Giuseppe, 2021; Liang et al., 2018; Liu, 2022). Leveraging a unique opportunity arising when a financial institution introduced an AI model to one of its personal loan products, we were able to use a DID identification strategy to assess the impacts of the AI model on financial inclusion for the underserved population.

While our analysis demonstrates the value of AI models in reducing statistical discrimination against the underserved population and improving their financial inclusion, it is important to recognize the limitations of AI models. First, AI models cannot address structural discrimination, which can cause inequity in wealth distribution, access to education, or career advancement and in turn affects applicants’ creditworthiness. Such structural discrimination would require systematic policy interventions. Second, our analysis shows that the effect of the AI model on financial inclusion is weaker for subgroups with missing weak signals and that less sophisticated AI models have a weaker impact on financial inclusion. Future studies could seek to better understand the heterogeneous impacts of various AI models. It would also be interesting for future studies to investigate the impacts of AI models on other financial products. Third, the “black-box” problem of AI models and the use of personal data should be handled better by financial institutions. Policymakers should work on actionable AI regulations to deal with data privacy and financial exclusion issues.

Developed by Microsoft, LightGBM, short for light gradient-boosting machine, represents an avant-garde gradient-boosting framework, catering predominantly to ranking, classification, and regression tasks (Ke et al., 2017). LightGBM is based on decision tree algorithms and gives a prediction model in the form of an ensemble of simple decision trees. LightGBM shares many advantages with XGBoost, including sparse optimization, parallel training, multiple loss functions, regularization, bagging, and early stopping (Wikipedia 2023). Diverging from XGBoost’s level-wise tree growth strategy, LightGBM employs a leaf-wise tree growth strategy, which consequently prunes inefficient splits to optimize operational speed. It embarks on an innovative trajectory with its histogram-based optimization for continuous features, compressing the search space during node splits, and minimizing memory usage during training. Compared to frameworks like XGBoost and CatBoost, LightGBM comes with enhanced computational efficiency and reduced memory consumption, maintaining commendable model accuracy.

Positioning itself at the forefront of financial analytics, LightGBM transcends traditional models, as evidenced by its commendable achievements in academic research and real-world applications. In academic research, Ma et al. (2018) showed its efficacy in enhancing P2P lending models, marking a tangible reduction in loan defaults. Sun et al. (2020) demonstrated LightGBM’s superior predictive accuracy in forecasting cryptocurrency trends compared to traditional models like support vector machines and random forests. Wang et al. (2022) utilized LightGBM to evaluate the finance risk of 186 firms. Their exhaustive experiments, benchmarking LightGBM against other algorithms, consistently underscored its superior predictive prowess in this domain. In industry, within the competitive milieu of Kaggle, from 2016 to 2019, LightGBM notched more than 30 top-three placements, distinguishing itself in flagship contests such as CIKM AnalytiCup 2017 and IEEE Fraud Detection. Amazon has also incorporated LightGBM into platforms like SageMaker for distributed training since January 2023.

In this project, the development team utilized the open source code of LightGBM (https://github.com/microsoft/LightGBM) and applied it five times in the AI model. The four individual learners were trained separately by LightGBM using different sets of features. The ensemble learner then integrated their predictions and generated the final scores (Sawhney et al., 2020). The development team applied this two-layer structure for three reasons: (1) Training distinct LightGBM models tailored to specific objectives enhances model efficiency. Conversely, merging all features into a singular model heightens the risk of overfitting; (2) This structure not only magnifies model interpretability by demarcating feature contributions but also ensures optimal performance across models; (3) LightGBM’s inherent “exclusive feature bundling” mechanism can amalgamate highly collinear features. Thus, when strong and weak signals coexist in a single model, their inter feature bundling can obscure the individual contributions of each to the predictive model.

Table A10 reports the results from three subsample analyses that split the whole sample based on the location of bank branches. Given the concern that the effect of manual promotion and evaluation may be at play and last for a while, we distinguished the bank branches that were not likely to suffer from this intervention (i.e., bank branches in big cities) from those that were likely to suffer from this intervention (i.e., bank branches in medium and small cities). The working assumption is that because the focal bank sent almost all its special personnel to the branches in medium and small cities/towns, the impact identified for applications via branches in big cities likely comes from the AI model only. The results are consistent with our main findings, with an increase in the approval rate and a decrease in the default rate for applicants from the underserved population in big cities

Table A11 reports the results from three placebo tests. Given the concern that the focal bank might conduct multiple efforts to echo the government policies, we assume that the focal bank has indeed taken some unobserved actions to promote financial inclusion and call them fake treatments. Relying on the same specifications and using the time period before the adoption of the AI model, we were able to identify the impacts of these fake treatments. As is shown in Table A11, the level of human intervention is largely insignificant, and the magnitude of the impact of human intervention on the approval rate is about 1/10 of the impact of the AI model (i.e., 0.018 compared to 0.150). More importantly, the impact of human intervention, if there was any, is positive on the default rate. The results further support that AI models can increase the approval rate and reduce the default rate simultaneously, which manual interventions cannot easily achieve.
