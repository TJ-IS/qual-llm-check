# ICT innovation in emerging economies: a review of the existing literature and a framework for future research

Xiao Xiao<sup>1</sup>, Christopher B Califf<sup>2</sup>, Saonee Sarker<sup>3</sup>, Suprateek Sarker<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, Denmark;

<sup>2</sup>Department of Management, Information Systems, and Entrepreneurship, Washington State University, Pullman, WA, USA; <sup>3</sup>McIntire School, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, USA

Correspondence:

## Abstract

ICT innovation is known to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly elevate a country’s growth and to enhance productivity. It is now well-acknowledged that emerging economies are beginning to innovate at a rapid rate despite some of the challenges they face. Given that these countries with such economies now comprise a third of the world’s largest 25 economies, and that they are likely to be critical for the success of a global economy, it is important to understand how these economies innovate, what factors affect innovation in such nations, and what are the impacts. However, to the best of our knowledge, little research till date has been conducted on this topic, and the few existing studies have failed to catch the attention of the mainstream IS research community. We believe that the absence of studies is primarily due to a lack of understanding of what has been found with respect to ICT innovation in emerging economies, what needs to be studied, and how they should be studied. We attempt to contribute in this area by: (1) providing a comprehensive framework of existing research on ICT innovation in emerging economies, (2) highlighting the gaps that have been left behind, and (3) providing speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines to future researchers, including a research model summarizing the salient issues that need examination. We believe that our study makes an important contribution to research on ICT innovation in emerging economies, and can be a useful resource for future researchers interested in this topic. ## Introduction

he Sussex Manifesto in the 1970s highlighted the importance of innovation in ‘raising economy-wide productivity and output’ (Kaplinsky et al., 2009). Since then, it has been widely acknowledged that innovation is critical for a Tcountry’s development, and that countries with high innovative activity tend to have higher productivity and income than those with less innovative activity (Fagerberg, 2005). Initially, innovation was associated almost exclusively with advanced and developed economies. This was based on the argument that only such economies have the infrastructure required for innovation (e.g., Chang et al., 2006). It is thus not a surprise that innovation in developed economies has captivated the interest of academics for long. Given that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of innovations are increasingly related to or being enabled by information and communication technologies (ICTs) (Avgerou, 2008), innovation is an area of speci<sup>fi</sup>c interest for IS researchers. However, as many academics have highlighted, much ICT innovation research has focused on contexts associated with advanced economies (Kiss et al., 2012). In recent years, with: (1) increases in globalization, (2) ‘liberalization of emerging economies and their integration into the worldwide economy’ (Kiss et al., 2012: 266), (3) the emerging economies realizing that they need to continuously innovate to ‘catch up’ (Chang et al., 2006), and (4) developed economies increasingly looking towards emerging economies to either jointly innovate or to ‘push’ their innovations, a new innovation landscape has emerged (Li and Kozhikode, 2009). In this paper, we address this emergent landscape by speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally focusing on ICT innovations in emerging economies.

Today, emerging economies comprise a third of the world’s largest 25 economies (Kiss et al., 2012), and this proportion is still growing sharply. Indeed, experts acknowledge that emerging economies are innovating at a rapid rate despite the challenges they face (Porter and Stern, 2001). One of the reasons for a spark in the interest on innovation in emerging economies likely extends from the popularity of the innovation literature that compares developed and developing countries (Avgerou, 2008). This literature acknowledges that while innovation may not represent the primary driver in the disparities between developed and developing countries, it is without a doubt one of the critical factors. For example, this literature reveals that innovation drives much of the technological development in developed and developing countries (Fagerberg, 2005), which has been positively correlated with a country’s human capital stock (Anderson and Hansen, 2008), and in turn, leading to investing in education and job skills training (Simkovic, 2011). While this literature is important, we acknowledge that emerging economies represent a unique environment that differs from developed and developing countries. Such differences are de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the next section.

Notwithstanding the differences, innovation remains an important driver of economic growth in emerging economies (Radas and Božić, 2009). In this vein, we argue that because emerging economies are critical in shaping the future of the ICT-landscape (Avgerou, 2008), ICT innovation in emerging economies should materialize as an important research stream for IS scholars. Unfortunately, as Avgerou (2008) indicated in her authoritative review of literature on ICT innovation in emerging economies, research in this area is yet to attract the interest of mainstream IS researchers. As is the pattern with nascent sub-disciplines that do not receive deserved attention from the mainstream, articles on this topic were found to be published primarily in specialized or ‘niche’ conferences and journals. A similar sentiment has been expressed by Kiss et al. (2012: 267) who argue that studies surrounding innovation in emerging economies ‘are both methodologically and topically diverse, fragmented, and scattered across a plethora of generalized and specialized journals,’ thereby hindering the development of a cumulative body of literature.

Four years have passed since Avgerou’s review article that aimed at helping raise the visibility of this body of work within the mainstream IS research community, yet we see limited increase in studies on this topic in mainstream outlets. We believe, based on our review of the literature, this absence can partially be explained by an implicit belief among researchers that <sup>fi</sup>ndings from studies involving advanced/developed economies can easily be adapted to the emerging economies. However, because there is widespread acknowledgement that emerging economies differ from advanced economies and developed countries in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ways, studies involving emerging economies require new paradigms, new theoretical efforts, and new methodological approaches (e.g., Hoskisson et al., 2000). For example, Li and Kozhikode (2009: 332) argue that emerging economies experience ‘frequent changes in their regulatory frameworks and direct interference from various levels of governments and thus, models surrounding such economies need to be more dynamic, and take these forces into account.

Another key reason for the lack of studies on ICT innovation in emerging economies is the absence of an understanding among researchers on what gaps exist in the literature on innovation in emerging economies, what areas need more focus, what theoretical perspective might be suitable, and what methodologies should be adopted. While Avgerou (2008) provides a valuable review of the existing literature on innovation in emerging economies including a discussion of the different ‘discourses’ that exist in the literature, we believe that in order for research on ICT innovation in emerging economies to move forward, we need to have an understanding of not only ‘what has been done’ in the past, but also what issues should be examined with respect to this topic in the future, and how those issues should be examined. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we attempt to contribute toward a diagnosis of the current state-of-affairs and enabling greater innovation levels by (a) developing a research framework on ICT innovation in emerging economies, and (b) providing speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines for future researchers in this area.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows: First, we provide de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of key terms such as emerging economies and ICT innovation. Next, we discuss our review of the literature including the methodology we followed, guiding frameworks we used, and results of the review. We then present an integrated framework that we believe can help inform future research on ICT innovation in emerging economies. We conclude with a discussion of the intended contribution.

## De<sup>fi</sup>nitions

## Emerging economies

Emerging economies, often referred to as emerging markets, are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as those countries experiencing a ‘rapid pace of economic development’ and where the government policies favor ‘economic liberalization and a free-market system (Hoskisson et al., 2000: 249). Emerging economies are often clustered under the umbrella of developing countries (i.e., lessdeveloped countries) which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned by World Bank as ‘countries with low or middle levels of GNP per capita’ and those with subpar ‘economic structure’ (2004). As an example, the International Monetary Fund in its 2012 Word Economic Outlook groups emerging economies and developing countries into one category. In this sense, emerging economies are in essence developing countries and therefore overlap often exists between the two terms (see Avgerou, 2008, as an example). However, emerging economies also differ from pure developing countries; they are typi<sup>fi</sup>ed by their rapid growth rate and liberalized economic system. So, in this paper, we consider emerging economies as a subset of developing countries characterized by a higher economic growth rate and free-market economic policies. While developed countries may share a similar liberalized free-market policy as an emerging economy, emerging economies differ from developed countries by a lower GDP, lower per capita income and a less advanced technological infrastructure (Tseng, 2009).

Innovations in emerging economies are ‘growing at around three times the pace of the advanced ones’ (Kiss et al., 2012: 266). Moreover, by 2025, it is projected that ‘eight of the largest emerging economies are likely to be equal or larger than that of the eight largest advanced economies’ (Kiss et al., 2012: 266–267). The lowering of trade barriers and the improvement of IT infrastructure including telecommunication networks have triggered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant development in these emerging economies, and have spurred their innovating spirits. In fact, the International Finance Corporation identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 51 such economies from Asia, Latin America, and Africa, including countries such as India, China, Brazil, and South Africa (Hoskisson et al., 2000). This list is set to grow, with economists arguing that many of the so-called ‘transition economies’ such as those from the prior Soviet Union should be included in the list of emerging economies.

Within emerging economies, innovation is often viewed as the ability of a country to ‘catch-up’ and ‘converge,’ where catching-up refers to ‘narrow[ing] the gap in productivity and income vis-à-vis a leader country,’ and convergence refers to ‘… [the] reduction of the overall differences in productivity and income in the world as a whole’ (Fagerberg and Godinho, 2005: 514). However, as we mentioned above, emerging economies have certain unique characteristics, such as immature capital markets, lack of resources necessary for innovation development, poor legal framework to protect property rights, and weak pools of vertical intermediaries (Mahmood and Mitchell, 2004), that make the innovation process different from developed countries.

## ICT innovation

It is a well-acknowledged fact that there are multiple de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of innovation (e.g., Rogers, 1998; Baregheh et al., 2009). It must <sup>fi</sup>rst be noted that innovation is not a synonym of invention. Invention is ‘the <sup>fi</sup>rst occurrence of an idea for a new product or process, while innovation is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to carry it out into practice’ (Fagerberg, 2005: 4). Baregheh et al. (2009: 1324) argue that ‘innovation is tightly coupled to change.’ Rogers (1998: 6), drawing on Schumpeter, de<sup>fi</sup>nes innovation as the introduction of a new product or a ‘qualitative change’ in a product, a process that is new to an industry, the opening up of new markets, or the development of new sources of supplies, or some other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes in industrial organizations. Innovation has also been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the new ideas that enable change of production or as a process through which new ideas in<sup>fl</sup>uence industrial transformation (Dosi, 1982; Teece, 1986). Furthermore, Anderson et al. (2004) adopted West and Farr’s (1990) de<sup>fi</sup>nition to distinguish innovation from creativity. They emphasized that innovation should consist of application, meaning that innovation is more than just an idea – it involves the process of generating ideas and putting the ideas into practice. On a related note, Damanpour (1996) de<sup>fi</sup>ned innovation as ‘a means of changing an organization, either as a response to changes in the external environment or as a preemptive action to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the environment,’ emphasizing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of innovation to organizations.

Innovation consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve types: (1) new products, (2) new methods of production, (3) new sources of supply, (4) the exploitation of new markets, and (5) new ways to organize business (Fagerberg, 2005). Each type of innovation is embedded in a continuous process whereby a ‘single innovation is often the result of a lengthy process involving many interrelated innovations’ (Fagerberg, 2005: 5). Broadly, innovation can be characterized as ‘product innovation’ and ‘process innovation’ (Fagerberg, 2005), whereby a product innovation is the market introduction of a new or signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved good or service with respect to its capabilities, user friendliness, components or sub-systems, and a process innovation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as changes in throughput technology for an enterprise or an operating unit (Ettlie and Reza, 1992).

Following the above conceptualizations of innovation, we consider innovation in general as being both a product and a process that promote a means of change at multiple levels of analysis, including individual, intra- and inter-organizational, and societal (Gupta et al., 2007). In other words, innovation process(es) could fuel innovation product(s), and in turn, innovation product(s) can drive innovation process(es). We consider this ‘product-process’ interdependence especially relevant to emerging economies that are often riddled with habitual <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in leaders, government authorities, and policies, among others things.

Similarly, information and communication-related technological (ICT) innovation is viewed as a product or process ‘related to new technology’ (Baregheh et al., 2009: 1326). For example, an ICT product innovation is any new or improved ICT product ‘whose characteristics differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly from previous products’ (Rogers, 1998: 7) while an ICT process innovation is any new or improved method of production or delivery of an ICT product (Rogers, 1998: 7). Following our view on innovations in general, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne ICT innovation as an interdependent technology product and/or a technologically driven process meant to promote a change at multiple levels of analysis. In other words, we conceptualize ICT innovation as both a development process to produce a technological artifact, and one powered by one or multiple interconnected technological artifacts.

Innovation is assumed to have various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on productivity and economic growth at organizational and national levels. At the organizational level, innovation and innovative activity has been linked to enhancements in productivity (Griliches, 1998). However, it has also been suggested that critical contingent factors, such as organizational culture and structure, need to be in place before the positive effects on productivity can occur (Baer and Frese, 2002). Similarly, innovation has been associated with economic growth at the national level. Such conclusions are usually made by adopting either a (1) neoclassical viewpoint of economic growth whereby measuring innovation often times loses ‘realism (Verspagen, 2005), or (2) an evolutionary perspective, wherein, technological innovations’ impacts on economic growth are steeped in the ability to adapt growth models to ‘local circumstances’ (2005), which vary from developed countries to emerging economies. Other perspectives are also prevalent in explaining growth in emerging economies at the organization and national levels. For example, one perspective argues that institutions can in<sup>fl</sup>uence intra- and inter-organizational networks (Hoskisson et al., 2000), and, in turn, impact innovation at a national level. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, one study found that cooperation among <sup>fi</sup>rms, research institutions, and intermediary institutions and organizations was positively associated with innovation performance of small-to-medium size enterprises (Zeng et al., 2010). Another perspective argues that entrepreneurship contributes to economic growth at organizational and national levels. Such growth is driven by networking, utilizing local resources, and ‘reach[ing] out beyond the limitations of their immediate contexts’ (Hung and Whittington, 2011: 526) to adapt a business to an emerging economy environment. Entrepreneurs also play the role of an ‘informant’ to multinational enterprises by helping them ‘develop new knowledge by combining knowledge obtained from foreign partners with local knowledge’ (Meyer, 2004: 267). The above discussion highlights the following with respect to innovation: (1) innovation can have several positive effects on individuals, organizations, and nations, (2) the impact of innovation depends on a wide range of contextual factors that needs investigation, and (3) innovation and growth can be a product of many perspectives that warrant further study.

Thus far, our review of the literature has underscored the fact that ICT innovation in emerging economies has been neglected by IS researchers, and is absent from top journals. Such neglect spawns from a lack of: (1) understanding about the context of emerging economies and how they differ from developed countries, (2) theorizing based on novel paradigms, and utilization of new theoretical perspectives and methodologies, and (3) a framework to guide research on ICT innovation in emerging economies. We address points two and three in the following sections. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we argue that because of this unique nature of emerging economies, research on ICT innovation in emerging economies should not be laced with commonly used IS theories, research methods, and the idea that emerging economies should be seen as ‘catching-up.’ Instead, ICT researchers should embrace perspectives and research tools that adapt to the uniqueness of the emerging economy context, rather than adapting the context to the perspectives established in developed economies.

## Research method

## Our methodological approaches

In creating our framework and providing speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines for future research, we followed the principles outlined by Dubé and Paré’s (2003) inductive categorization method. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we did the following: (1) carefully selected relevant journals, and those that are pertinent to the context of our study, (2) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the articles published in those journals which are relevant to our study by using carefully selected keywords, (3) categorized the selected (and shortlisted) articles by drawing on different theoretically grounded categorization schemes, (4) assessed (and calculated) the number of articles in different categories and sub-categories, (5) analyzed the trends and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed gaps, and (6) developed a comprehensive model surrounding ICT innovation in emerging economies as guide for future researchers.

## Journal selection

First, we selected journals based on their reputation in the IS discipline and also based on their relevance to our topic. To decide on these journals, we consulted a formal statement written by senior scholars after the 2006 ‘Senior Scholars Consortium’ at ICIS (AIS, n.d.). The letter underlined the importance of establishing a benchmark for recognition of exceptional scholarship and it identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a basket of six plus two journals, which we draw upon here. To the ‘Basket of 8 Journals,’ we added two internationally recognized peerreviewed journals owing to their topical relevance to the study (i.e., social and technical factors accompanying systems development in emerging economies). Our assessment of journal articles thus allowed us to ensure that most high-quality papers on the topic were considered, and indeed, each journal contained articles about ICT innovation in emerging economies. The <sup>fi</sup>nal 10 journals included in our literature review were:

● European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS)

● Information Systems Journal (ISJ)

● Information Systems Research (ISR)

● Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ)

● Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS)

● Journal of Information Technology (JIT)

● Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS)

● Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS)

● The Electronic Journal of Information Systems in Developing Countries (EJIS-DC)

● Information Technology for Development (ITD)

## Identifying relevant articles

The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of relevant articles relies heavily on the keywords used during the search process. Accordingly, we chose keywords based on our readings of seminal works on innovation in emerging economies. We also selected keywords that we felt captured articles that addressed the phenomenon of interest. Based on this, we elected to search using the following keywords: innovation, emerging economies, developing countries,<sup>1</sup> BRIC, Brazil, Russia, India, China, Asia, South America, and Africa. After the initial search, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicated that an ‘innovation’ is often considered the focus of an implementation process (see Kwon and Zmud, 1987). For example, a few articles explored the implementation of a geographic information system, which was treated as an ICT innovation in a particular context (e.g., Walsham and Sahay, 1999). Moreover, Cooper and Zmud’s (1990) IT implementation model is grounded in the innovation and diffusion literature, and thus we deemed IT implementation as an instantiation of the ICT innovation product and process conceptualizations, as discussed in the previous sections. In this sense, we reason that articles about IT implementation often examine an innovation being implemented, and thus IT implementation is a relevant substitute for ICT innovation. Therefore, to ensure that we did not overlook relevant articles, the search was re<sup>fi</sup>ned to include ‘implementation’ as a keyword. In certain cases, we had to modify our keyword search. For example, when searching in EJIS-DC, the phrase ‘developing countries’ was omitted because the journal’s name contained the phrase, and thus had the potential of identifying articles that were not relevant to our literature review.

To conduct our primary search, we used EBSCO Business Source Complete for each journal except EJIS-DC and JSIS, which were searched through the journal websites. For EJIS-DC, the search box presented on the journal’s website was utilized. JSIS was searched by way of sciencedirect.com. After collecting our initial batch of articles, we narrowed down the articles to include only those focusing on ICT innovation in emerging economies. For the most part, those that were discarded included ones in which the authors discussed ICT innovation in a developed country, but only mentioned that emerging economies should be considered. Other omitted articles included book reviews and editor commentaries, as they were not considered empirical research. All in all, a total of 50 manuscripts were considered for analysis.

## Review of the existing literature and <sup>fi</sup>ndings

As we have mentioned earlier, our objective in this manuscript is to offer an overview of existing research on ICT innovation in emerging economies, and provide suggestions for the future. Our overview aims to provide a thorough representation of the literature, to describe the context, and to discuss works in relation to one another. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used the following criteria to organize our review: (a) phases of ICT innovation, (b) context of study, (c) nature of the role of ICT, (d) level of analysis, (e) nature of theory, and (f) methodology. Level of analysis, theory, and nature of the role of ICT were chosen based on Markus and Robey’s noted work on causal structures (1988). As discussed earlier, ICT innovation involves a process with many different sub-process/phases (e.g., Baregheh et al., 2009). As a result, we wanted to capture through our literature review the phases of innovation that have received most attention, and those that have escaped attention, to help us understand the gaps for future research. Furthermore, we examined the context of ICT innovation (e.g., whether the study was conducted only within emerging economies or involved comparisons between emerging economies and developed countries) because context is critical to understanding the nature of innovation in emerging economies (Lundvall et al., 2009). Finally, given some of the unique characteristics of emerging economies which could potentially call for the use of speci<sup>fi</sup>c research methodologies, our review also attempted to capture the types of methodologies frequently used in studying innovation in emerging economies. Next, we discuss the review of the literature based on the categories mentioned above. One issue that we would like to mention upfront is that despite the call by Avgerou (2008) for mainstream IS research to focus more on emerging/developing economies, we see little change in the patterns of scholarly publications since the call. Our review revealed that majority of the relevant papers are published in ITD (11 articles) and EJIS-DC (22 articles), the two journals focused on developing economies. The mainstream IS journals (such as those in our Senior Scholars basket) published between 1 and 6 articles only, in this intervening period.

## Phases of ICT innovation

ICT innovation consists of two key phases: design and implementation. The majority (43/50) of the articles focused on the ICT innovation implementation, given that typically, process innovation was focused upon. The other articles focused on the design process of innovation (i.e., before implementation begins) or the impacts/outcomes concerned with implementing an innovation (i.e., what happens after the implementation of an innovation). Given that it has been widely acknowledged in the literature that IT implementation itself consists of multiple phases (Kwon and Zmud, 1987; Cooper and Zmud, 1990), we then sought to classify articles based on the exact type of implementation phase a particular study focused on. In conducting this categorization, we followed Kwon and Zmud’s (1987) model of IT implementation which referred to the following six phases: initiation; adoption; adaptation; acceptance; routinization; and infusion. We augmented this set with the design of innovations and also the after effects of implementation.

As discussed above, the majority of the articles focused on the IT implementation phases with little or no focus on the design of an innovation or its impacts. It should be noted that while a total of 43 articles addressed implementation issues, each article could potentially address multiple categories (e.g., initiation as well as adoption) and therefore the sum is more than the number of articles. First, the initiation stage was found to contain 13 papers, each of which centered on examining an environment for problems and/or opportunities caused by an innovation (Cooper and Zmud, 1990). Common <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this stage consisted of enabling factors such a executive support, organization structure, policy concerns, and IT knowledge and awareness. For example, Gray and Sanzogni (2004) examined the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an ICT innovation in Thailand. Next, 21 articles focused on the adoption phase, with themes ranging from perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness to government pressure and support, cultural and privacy concerns, market competi tion, and knowledge building/deployment negotiations. Montealegre (1999), on the other hand, highlighted the role institutions played when adopting the Internet in developing countries. Third, <sup>fi</sup>ve articles addressed the adaptation phase, examining the extent to which an innovation contributed, or was about to contribute, to a change in an environment. Namely, Silva and Hirschheim (2007) explored how the process of implementing an information system may cause radical changes to an organization’s deep structure. Fourth, nine articles were found to address the acceptance stage, and examine the extent to which ‘members are induced to commit to the innovation (Cooper and Zmud, 1990). Common factors in these articles included active support, participation, and collaboration among users, changing social norms, and technology competence. For example, Zhu and Kraeme (2005) explored the necessary conditions for acceptance of e-Business applications in both developed countries and in emerging economies. Fifth, routinization papers offered insights into how an innovation becomes a part of an organization’s or an individual’s routine. Two articles from our set of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed studies seemed to focus on this phase (e.g., Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Rodríguez, 2005). Lastly, the infusion stage, which describes how ICT innovations reach their full potential, was discussed in one article. The sole article was by Musa et al. (2010), who investigated how an ICT innovation enhanced the quality of services for Nigerian construction <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Whereas the bulk of manuscripts resided in the implementation phase of ICT innovation in emerging economies, two articles examined the design aspects of innovation. For example, Puri (2006) collaborated with key users of India’s National Spatial Data Infrastructure to determine the necessary conditions for a suitable infrastructure design. Moreover, Igira (2008) examined how to devise a health information system (HIS) based on the congruency among local health-care workers’ daily routines and their shared objectives for the system.

## Context of the study of innovation

With regards to the context, 38 out of the 50 articles were devoted to studying ICT innovations exclusively within an emerging economy. In other words, efforts were committed to identifying the issues surrounding ICT implementation in a single country. For example, Molla et al. (2006) proposed a temporal process that governs the role institutions play in promoting the diffusion of Barbadian e-commerce systems. In a similar light, Câmara et al. (2006) employed Rogers (1995) diffusion of innovations model to study how geographical information systems (GIS) were introduced in Brazil. Walsham and Sahay (1999) investigated how districts in India attempted to use GIS to support district-level administration, and attributed much of the challenges in GIS implementation to the absence of a map-based culture in India.

An additional (and somewhat surprising) <sup>fi</sup>nding was that only four articles made comparisons between developed countries and emerging economies. Of those that did, some examples include Dewan et al. (2010), who highlighted the symbiotic relationship between PCs and the Internet as they relate to innovation diffusion in both developed and developing countries; Çayhan (2008), who compared e-Government implementation in Turkey and the EU; and <sup>fi</sup>nally, Kauffman and Techatassanasoontorn (2005), who examined the antecedents that drive digital wireless phone diffusion by contrasting developed and developing countries.

Further, our review sheds light on the fact that there is little consideration (actually, we found no article) of the ICT innovation process resulting from the collaboration/co-creation efforts of two or more emerging economies or those resulting from collaboration/co-creation efforts between developed and emerging economies.

In the next sub-section, we discuss our literature by drawing on Markus and Robey’s causal structures. Markus and Robey (1988) argue that a sound theoretical structure, that is the researchers’ conceptions about the nature and direction of causality is a critical feature of well-undertaken studies of social systems. According to Markus and Robey (1988), there are three elements to causal structures: causal agency (or the nature of causality), logical structure (time span of theory), and levels of analysis (entities about which theory poses concepts).

## The nature of causality: the nature of ICT, enablers/inhibitors, and possible outcomes

The three conceptions of causal agency are: (1) Technological imperative (techno-centric), (2) Organizational imperative (socio-centric), and (3) Emergent imperative (socio-technical). Twenty-seven of the articles that resulted from our search can be categorized as being ‘organizational imperatives (Markus and Robey, 1988). As an illustrative example, Newman and Zhao (2008) studied how enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation is hindered by the historical factors embedded in Chinese small-to-medium enterprises. Another example is one by Sreekumar (2007) that investigated the relevant social dynamics for successfully implementing an e-Government initiative.

As discussed earlier, the studies we reviewed unearthed various enablers and inhibitors that affect the process of ICT innovation, including (1) socio-related factors, such as user behavior, management behavior, and organizational characteristics, (2) technology-related factors, such as ICT infrastructure, IT capabilities, IT knowledge, and IT resources, and (3) environmental factors, such as regulation, policy and standards, cultural environment, economic environment, and governmental behavior (see Figure 1). Notably, most of the studies focused on environmental factors and conditions vital to innovation occurrence. For instance, Brown and Thompson (2011) found that government policy, such as legislative regulations regarding economic practices, played a crucial and important role in the diffusion of ICT innovations in emerging economies (Jamaica in their case). Also, these articles seemed to focus primarily on the initial stages of the implementation process. For example, Abukhzam and Lee (2010) uncovered the key factors such as lack of management support and IT knowledge and skills that affect Libyan banks’ challenges of e-banking adoption. We also found very few articles that focused on technical aspects of ICT innovation. Intaganok et al. (2007) discussed how basic ICT skills were important to ICT adoption and Al-Ghaith et al. (2010) examined issues such as system complexity and system compatibility.

Further, we examined the type of ICT studied. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the most common technologies studied were either IT systems (e.g. ERP systems, HIS, e-Government, and e-Business) or IT infrastructure.

Very few articles (5) focused on the impacts of ICT innovation. For example, Meso et al. (2009) revealed that national ICT innovation (i.e., national IT infrastructure) can contribute to socio-economic development of a country, both indirectly and directly. Similarly, Puri (2007) investigated whether ICT innovation (Geographic Information System) improves the lives of ‘poor and marginalized communities’ in India. Other possible outcomes of ICT innovation that were extracted from the literature review include increased productivity (Bollou and Ngwenyama, 2008) and added business value (Okoli et al., 2010).

## Logical structure

We also examined the logical structure of the theoretical arguments in each article by checking whether the authors developed a variance or process model (see Markus and Robey, 1988). We found that 10 articles developed a variance model. For example, Okoli et al. (2010) proposed a ‘National infrastructure model of e-Business’ to test how national ICT policies, ICT infrastructures and institutional and commercial environment will impact e-Business outcomes. Six articles developed a process model, and an example is Maldonado (2010) who described the innovation adoption process in the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela. Interestingly, in 34 of the articles reviewed, neither a variance model nor a process model was found, which we speculate might be due to two related reasons: (1) a large proportion of articles adopted an interpretive approach to describe a particular case while focusing on empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings; (2) accordingly, there appears to be a lack of effort on theorizing or on constructing abstractions in the form of process or variance models.

## Levels of analysis

The levels of analysis (see Markus and Robey, 1988) presented in each article were also noted. The most frequently investigated levels of analyses were: (a) the societal level (20 articles) and (b) the organizational level (27 articles). The 20 societal level studies were concerned with the enabling conditions for ICT innovation to be implemented successfully across a society. For example, Meso et al. (2009) uncovered that socio-economic development in emerging economies is dependent on national information infrastructure and governance. The 27 organizational level studies observed how ICT innovation is implemented and diffused within an organization embedded in a given national context. For example, Sahay et al. (2009) studied and discussed the challenges of HIS implementation in Indian organizations.

Through this examination, we conclude that little attention has been paid to the individual level of analysis. While a few articles did investigate technology acceptance issues (i.e., perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use), none provided insights into what drives individuals to innovate. Put in another way, current research on ICT innovation in emerging economies does not investigate how to initiate and facilitate the innovation process for individuals. Future research on this front might focus on individual innovations, or on individuals collaborating to develop innovations.

## Nature of theory used

Next, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the theory or theories used in each article. In doing so, our analysis revealed that actor-network theory (ANT), institutional theory, the technology acceptance model (TAM), and theory of the diffusion of innovation were most commonly used. For the most part, the theories used were not speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the emerging economy contexts. This implies that there is a lack of theorizing and/or theory-building based on the context in which the phenomenon exists. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the majority of the theories were essentially imported from western thought and simply fastened on to ICT innovation concerns in emerging economies. For example, He (2004) applied the resource-based view (RBV) to assess major ERP challenges in China, which might lead to neglecting the impacts of context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors present in emerging economies (e.g., Zhang et al. 2008). Thus, our results indicate that theorizing speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the sociotechnical nature of the ICT innovation process in emerging economies needs to be encouraged.

## Nature of methodology used

Our <sup>fi</sup>nal categorization venture was to seek out the metho dology present in each article. Our review revealed that 28 articles employed a case study methodology or variations of case studies such as ethnography. For example, Miscione (2007) ethnographically examined the implementation of an HIS in an emerging economy. Similarly, Nidumolu et al. (1996) used a case study method to provide insight about implementation of information and decision support centers in Egypt. Our analysis also revealed some studies using limited action and design research (3 articles), surveys (13 articles), and secondary data analysis (6 articles).

## Summary of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings

In summary, the bulk of the articles did not include a variance or a process theory. Moreover, 38 manuscripts addressed ICT innovation issues within an emerging economy, and none considered a between emerging economy perspective. The most common method used was case studies (or qualitative variants), which comprised 28 articles. In addition, the organizational and societal level of analysis dominated, with 27 and 20 articles, respectively. The majority of the articles embraced the organizational imperative and emergent causal agencies. Last, the design and impacts phases appeared to have been neglected by researchers, while the implementation phase of ICT innovations in emerging economies boasted 47 articles.

The present overview of ICT innovation research in emerging economies indicates that researchers have addressed innovation at the organizational level, national level, and individual level. Moreover, researchers have considered the role of infrastructure (i.e., IT and institutional) and policy standards relevant to the ICT innovation. The contexts involve those of emerging economies and of innovations essentially being imported from developed countries to emerging economies. Further, studies have also examined the role of culture especially in the form of ‘embedded traditions’ that have potentially affected ICT innovation in emerging economies.

To facilitate the generation of guidelines for future research on ICT innovation in emerging economies, we also brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviewed relevant articles in three innovation focused journals: Research Policy, Technovation, and Journal of Product and Innovation Management. On one hand, we found themes similar to the ones extracted from IS journals. For example, many studies adopted western theories and applied them in the context of emerging economies without examining the characteristics of the local environment (e.g., Chen and Fu, 2001; Narayanan and Bhat, 2011). Also, quantitative approaches such as survey and secondary data were the many methodologies being used to conduct the studies. On the other hand, some different themes emerged: there are several instances of articles investigating the role of local innovation systems and the interplay between these local systems and the broader national innovation systems (e.g., Chaminade and Vang, 2008; Hung and Whittington, 2011); local policies supporting organizational innovations have also been examined in a few studies (e.g., Chaminade and Vang, 2008); there are also articles taking a holistic perspective on the development of ICT innovation landscape (e.g., mobile technology; e-market) in various emerging economies (e.g., Chircu and Mahajan, 2009; Yang and Wang, 2013). We note that the latter themes contributed to a sharpening of the development of our future framework for ICT innovation in emerging economies, which we discuss in the next section.

## A framework for the future

In this section, we introduce a framework that highlights the potential areas of future research on ICT innovation in emerging economies (see Figure 2). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ve aspects of ICT innovation research in emerging economies, including the phase of design (creation), importance of dependent variables/outcomes, role of theory, role of methodology, and nature of ICT, that should warrant attention and consideration in the future.

## Design (creation)

There is a clear shortage of research addressing the design (creation) phase of ICT innovation in emerging economies. Although scarce (two articles), the current literature does a compelling job portraying individuals as central components in the design phase. For example, Igira (2008) emphasizes that when designing an HIS, the objectives and outcomes for the system must be congruent among those who use it (e.g., doctors and nurses), thereby taking the local context into consideration. Similarly, Puri (2006) highlights the need for user participation in the design phase of a National Spatial Data Infrastructure in India. In this vein, both authors allude to the fact that there is an underlying co-creation aspect involved in the design phase of ICT innovation. However, given the dearth of design-centric manuscripts about ICT innovation in emerging economies, we propose that researchers consider more approaches to examining the innovation phenomena at multiple levels of analysis, including the individual, intra-organizational, inter-organizational, and national level. Further, researchers should recognize the potential for integration across each level. For instance, Hung and Whittington (2011) made such cross-level connection by studying the interplay between entrepreneurial <sup>fi</sup>rms of IT industry and the national innovation system in Taiwan, revealing a process of how these IT <sup>fi</sup>rms adopted different strategies to evolve from the ‘taker’ of the national innovation system to the ‘giver’ of the system.

One approach is to take a ‘grassroots’ perspective. At the individual level, such a perspective would harness the talent of those individuals in an emerging economy not necessarily working for an organization. In this respect, Hanna (2009) argued that a ‘grassroots innovation network’ among individuals ‘would focus on innovation and adaptation – largely initiated at the grassroots level and based on local peoples knowledge and practices’ (p. 212). Research focused on cocreation between the individual and organizational level can adopt service-centric logic (Vargo and Lusch, 2004; Spohrer and Maglio, 2008), whereby organizations and individuals collaborate during the innovation process through user com munities to co-create value for both parties (Sawhney et al., 2005). Moreover, a grassroots approach could apply to an intra-organizational network. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Ciborra (1992) argued that the advantage of grassroots innovation within an organization is to utilize employee talent so that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovation strategy is not leaked. An integration of the two vantage points, along with an assumed embracing of an interorganizational network, could occur through the ‘open innovation paradigm,’ which ‘combines internal and external ideas into architectures and systems’ (Chesbrough et al., 2008: 1). Such an open-innovation or grassroots-centric point of view not only addresses localized context in which the innovation spawns, but also taps into the knowledge base of an emerging economy’s citizens, employees within an organization, and inter-organizational network(s). This grassroots approach could also take place between the individual and the nation in the area of e-government research, where ICT offers ‘new platforms for civic engagement, [and] opening up for potential mass collaboration in the innovation process’ (Bason, 2010). Co-creation among the various levels can underlie a national innovation ecosystem, or national system of innovation, whereby all entities interact to mutually support ‘the development, protection, <sup>fi</sup>nancing, or regulation’ of innovations (Lundvall et al., 2009: 4) (e.g., Chaminade and Vang, 2008). In other words, ICT innovation occurs through an underlying individual network, an inter- and intra-organizational network, and a national network of individuals and organizations in which each level is mutually supportive (see Figure 2). Thus, a bi-directional <sup>fl</sup>ow of information, rather than the commonly studied unidirectional <sup>fl</sup>ow from organization to individual is possible. In addition, this places emphasis on the value of co-creation through the distinct modes of exchange, addition, and synergistic integration both at isolated levels of analyses and across all levels, depending on the nature of innovation and the surrounding context (Sarker et al., 2012).

Another approach to studying the design phase of ICT innovation in emerging economies is to research the agility of a business process within an organization. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, researchers could investigate how the ever-changing nature of an emerging economy’s regulatory environment disturbs an organization’s ICT innovation process and how it may be possible to anticipate changes and respond to these changes rapidly and effectively. Researchers need to provide insights into how critical it is to anticipate regulations at the beginning (i.e. in the design phase of ICT innovation) rather than further down in the innovation process. As such, researchers would call attention to the agility of the innovation process in emerging economies.

## The importance of the dependent variable or outcomes

As our review indicated, there is a lack of examination of the dependent variable in the context of ICT innovations in emerging economies. While several articles have alluded to the importance of examining the effects of ICT innovation (e.g., Kauffman and Techatassanasoontorn, 2005), little empirical research has been conducted on this topic. Accordingly, we urge researchers to develop criteria on which we may assess the impacts of ICT innovation in emerging economies, overall, and for each stage.

The impacts of innovation should be examined at multiple levels of analysis, and should be done continuously (Bagchi, 2011). At the individual level, studies should speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine the extent to which ICT innovations could increase the availability of technology to citizens, thereby resulting in more access to knowledge and to networks of individuals or organizations (Puri, 2007), an elevated socio-economic status (Bollou and Ngwenyama, 2008), and an overall improved quality of life. At the organizational level, researchers should speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focus on understanding how ICT innovations can increase the diffusion of knowledge and knowledge alliances within and across <sup>fi</sup>rms (Puri, 2007), enhance organizational productivity and business value (Okoli et al., 2010), and contribute to the global competitiveness of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. At the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, another important dependent variable that should be examined is the intellectual property-related metrics (e.g., whether <sup>fi</sup>rms have applied for patents, IP or not, and how many). However, it should be noted that IP rights are conceptualized differently and play different roles across industrial sectors and countries (Granstrand, 2005), suggesting that extra caution is needed when using IP and patent systems as a means to measure successful innovative activities. It is worth noting that in many emerging economies the IPR system is not well developed, and thus IP as the dependent variable may not be able to accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the innovativeness of the country or region, or the impacts of ICT innovation process. As Rogers (1998) argues, in such contexts, even failed or absence of IP applications could be associated with success of the innovation. For example, failed applications may still highlight the <sup>fi</sup>rm as one that engages in innovative behaviors.

At the national level, ICT innovations could aid in increasing GDP and socio-economic growth (Corea, 2000), facilitate infrastructure diffusion, and positively impact an emerging economy’s purchasing power parity and HDI. Other important national-level measures include total expenditure on R&D, business expenditure on R&D, high-tech exports, and total R&D personnel, among others (Bagchi, 2011).

Researchers may also consider exploring the recursive nature of the ICT innovation process. For example, Ngwenyama and Morawczynski (2009) argue that individual-, organizational-, and national-level impacts, such as GDP, literacy rate, education attainment, and tertiary institutions, propel ICT infrastructure diffusion. The authors treat the potential impacts of ICT innovation as factors that feed into the ICT implementation phase of ICT innovation in emerging economies. Following this, we propose that future researchers should be mindful of the importance of the feedback mechan ism in ICT innovation research in the context of emerging economies, wherein ICT innovations at a particular point of time can be seen as triggering future ICT innovations (at a later time).

## The role of theory

Future research should make greater effort to develop theories or frameworks of ICT innovation in emerging economies. Our review revealed that to study ICT innovation in emerging economies most researchers adopted an established theory and applied it to the local context with minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. First, the theories being used are mostly based on western thinking developed within the context of developing countries; such theories may not be appropriate to study phenomenon in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different context faced by emerging economies. Even though minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of existing theories were often made to <sup>fi</sup>t the emerging economies’ context, due to the nature of these theories, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to capture the uniqueness of the local phenomenon using western-based theories. As Walsham (2001) indicated, country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics, such as histories and cultural foundations, will determine the path and the pattern with which each country experiences and internalizes the globalization process, suggesting that researchers should adopt a ‘glocalization’ perspective to take into account not only the globalization aspect but also the localization component when studying a modern phenomenon in a particular context (in this case, ICT innovation in emerging economies). In this sense, researchers should consider infusing, more prominently, the philosophy to study ICT innovation in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c local context. For example, Rai et al. (2010) made an attempt to study Indian logic and philosophy to understand software development in India. We believe that such contextual philosophical and theoretical paradigms should be used to studying ICT innovations in emerging economies, as opposed to applying existing western theories in that context. For example, Confucianism and guanxi networks (Zhang et al., 2008) or Thai values (Thanasankit, 2002) might be useful perspectives that could be employed by researchers when investigating ICT innovation in Asia. Moreover, using ‘narratives as theory,’ a strategy used by Rai et al (2011), could allow innovation researchers to develop a ‘collective interpretation of innovation practices and events, thereby lending a deeper insight into the nature of ICT innovation’ in emerging economies. Second, we have found that no study has adopted a process perspective to unfold the complex process of ICT innovation in emerging economies in existing literature. Future researchers need to work towards adapting process-based theories (e.g., innovation diffusion theory (Rogers, 1998)) or developing a processual framework (Markus and Robey, 1988) to capture a holistic and dynamic view of ICT innovation in emerging economies. Further, they need to examine the different stages of the ICT innovation process and identify (and empirically validate) key factors that will impact the success of each stage.

## The role of methodology

Our review has shown that case study was the most used methodology in existing literature. However, most articles were based on a single success case or failure case (e.g. Nidumolu et al., 1996; Maldonado, 2010). While a study based on a single case can be valuable, we propose that, where possible, both failure cases and success cases be examined to explore a given phenomenon, especially comparison between failure cases and success cases in similar contexts will help unearth the critical factors what determine whether ICT innovations will happen and succeed in a certain environment. Furthermore, given the dominance of qualitative and inductive research approaches in the articles we reviewed, future researchers should consider deductive theory testing using large cross-sectional surveys. For example, Al-Ghaith et al. (2010) conducted a large-scale survey in Saudi Arabia to test a framework of e-service adoption and usage based on the diffusion of innovations theory.

In addition, as discussed above, different phases exist in the entire ICT innovation process; therefore, future researchers should strongly consider investigating what factors impact which phases in order to gain a speci<sup>fi</sup>c understanding of each ICT innovation phase, and hence be able to use appropriate interventions in each phase. Such factors should spawn from the context in which ICT innovation exists, and be sensitive to the country or countries under investigation. Factors adopted from previous research should be seen as potentially relevant, but not relied upon without careful examination in a given context. Such reliance may hinder the progress of ICT innovation research in the context of emerging economies.

Finally, as we had discussed earlier, we believe that the effects of ICT innovations in emerging economies in a particular point of time could trigger other ICT innovations. In light of that <sup>fi</sup>nding, methodologically, we believe that future researchers should engage in more longitudinal studies as opposed to single case studies or cross-sectional surveys alone.

The context of emerging economies also demands the use of approaches that are different from those used by IS scholars in the west. A much higher level of cultural sensitivity is required of researchers conducting interviews, participant observations, or analysis of texts and symbols. For example, recording interviews may not be allowed in some developing countries, especially when dealing with government-related issues (e.g., Puri, 2007). Methodological guidelines from the reference discipline of anthropology could be helpful (Bernard, 1998), especially when survey data responses are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain and language and concept barriers make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to get valid data. Likewise, secondary data are less reliable and sparsely available (Zhang et al., 2008).

## The nature of ICT

First, it must be noted that the ICT innovations that have been examined in existing literature are mostly ICT systems (ERP system, HIS, GIS, etc.) and ICT infrastructures enabling telecoms and systems (e.g., e-Service, e-Government). Future studies should consider other types of ICT innovations that are emerging in a global range, such as mobile technology-enabled innovations and cloud computing-related innovations.

We also found out that heavy focus has been put on the social aspects of ICT innovations among the articles reviewed, re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the fact that over half of the studies adopted an organizational imperative perspective. Therefore, we strongly urge future researchers to give greater attention to technical aspects of ICT innovation so that errors of exclusion can be avoided (Benbasat and Zmud, 2003). Such a focus on the technological issues with respect to ICT innovations in emerging economies will help studies be more relevant to IS researchers and practitioners, and thereby help more studies to be published within the mainstream IS journals as opposed to just specialized developing countries-related outlets. To do so, researchers could consider taking a truly (rather than marginally) ensemble view of ICT innovations (Orlikowski and Lacono, 2001) by looking at how the unique social context in emerging economies shapes the creation of ICT innovation as well as how certain ICT innovations will impact the social context of a community, an organization, or even a society. Studies investigating the technological issues related to innovation might also focus on the characteristics of the innovation itself, or the technological platforms enabling innovations. Further, it would be useful to know what types of innovation characteristics (e.g., relative advantages, complexity, compatibility, observability, and trialability) in the context of different emerging economies lead to the highest potential for success, going beyond the seminal work of Rogers (1998). Further characteristics could spawn from the context in which ICT innovation exists, and be inherent to the country or countries under investigation. Through the application of the existing characteristics to innovation in emerging economies might also help identify/conceptualize speci<sup>fi</sup>c ICT innovation characteristics that are relevant for emerging economies only.

## Contribution and conclusion

In this manuscript, we sought to understand the state of research on ICT innovation in emerging economies, and to provide a framework for future researchers. Our study was inspired by Avgerou’s (2008) review of the existing research on IT-related issues in the context of developing nations, where she issued a call to future researchers and IS academics to ensure that studies involving information systems in emerging economies are embraced by the mainstream IS journals, as opposed to being relegated to the specialized outlets alone. Four years have elapsed since Avgerou’s (2008) piece was published. Unfortunately, we believe that research on emerging economies is yet to be wholeheartedly welcomed within the mainstream journals in the IS discipline. We believe that much of this lack in relevant studies could be due to a paucity of understanding in the IS research community in terms of what has been accomplished with respect to ICT innovation in emerging economies, and what needs to be studied, and how.

Our study attempted to contribute to the literature by addressing the above-mentioned issues. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we sought to provide a overview summarizing the existing research on ICT innovation in emerging economies (Figure 1), through a systematic process of selecting journals, searching for articles using carefully selected keywords, and then categorizing the articles based on Markus and Robey’s (1988) causal structures, and Kwon and Zmud’s (1987) phases of innovation implementation, among others. We would like to note that while Avgerou (2008) reviewed the state of research with respect to developing economies, our study differs from her in<sup>fl</sup>uential work in many ways. While the study by Avgerou (2008) has undoubtedly made a valuable contribution, and is arguably among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to open the IS discipline’s eyes to the very relevant context of emerging economies, her study was not based on a systematic analysis of the existing literature, as ours is. Further, the study focused on IS research in general, without any speci<sup>fi</sup>c focus on ICT innovation, as in the case of our study. This makes our study more relevant to the special issue. Finally, Avgerou (2008) sought to provide her thoughts on the three broad ‘discourses’ that she identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with respect to IS research on developing economies. While she shared her thoughts on what could be done as part of future research, our study goes one step further by providing a framework that can serve as a more speci<sup>fi</sup>c guide for future researchers (see Figure 2). ICT innovation in emerging economies. Further, we attempt to provide more speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines with respect to choices of theories and methodologies for researchers beginning their journey of examining ICT innovation issues in emerging economies. We hope that the review and the model presented will initiate and encourage many fruitful studies on this topic in the future.
