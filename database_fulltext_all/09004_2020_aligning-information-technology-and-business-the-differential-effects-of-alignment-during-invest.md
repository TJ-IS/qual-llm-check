# Aligning Information Technology and Business: The Differential Effects of Alignment During Investment Planning, Delivery, and Change

Terence J. V. Saldanha,<sup>a</sup> Dongwon Lee,<sup>b</sup> Sunil Mithas<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. When does information technology (IT)–business alignment matter most for leveraging IT investment for firm performance? Drawing on dynamic capabilities theory, we posit that firms that focus on IT–business alignment at the later stages (i.e., the IT delivery stage and IT change stage) of the IT lifecycle are better able to translate their IT investment into revenue than firms that focus on IT–business alignment at the early stage (i.e., IT investment planning stage) of the IT lifecycle. To test our theory, we leverage archival data from more than 120 firms in India that differ in terms of where in the IT lifecycle they focused on IT–business alignment as the key priority. We find that the positive relationship between IT investment and firm revenue is stronger for firms that focus on IT delivery alignment or IT change alignment than for firms that focus on IT investment planning alignment. In addition, we find that at higher levels of IT investment, firms that focus on IT change alignment or IT delivery alignment have higher revenue than firms that focus on IT investment planning alignment, whereas at low levels of IT investment, firms that focus on IT investment planning alignment outperform firms that focus on IT delivery alignment or IT change alignment. Overall, the study contributes by documenting that IT–business alignment at the IT delivery stage and IT change stage of the IT lifecycle are more critical for leveraging IT investment for firm performance than IT– business alignment at the IT investment planning stage.

## 1. Introduction

According to a recent study of information technology (IT) trends, alignment of IT with business is a topmost management issue and among the three most worrisome issues that managers face (Kappelman et al. 2017). Achieving IT–business alignment is hard in practice because alignment is a time-consuming and costly process (Gerow et al. 2015). Because alignment is a dynamic process (Hirschheim and Sabherwal 2001), one approach is to align IT with the business during the early stages of the IT lifecycle, such as during IT investment planning, and then leave it up to the IT department to implement the systems.<sup>1</sup> For example, ITS (a pseudonym for an Indian IT services company) achieved high IT–business alignment during the initial phase of investment planning of its knowledge management system by aligning IT with the firm’s strategic focus to increase its client base. But ITS did not maintain the strength of the IT–business alignment as the scope of the system expanded beyond the initial phase; as a result, “the existing strength of the strategic alignment was no longer sufficient to maintain the success of the KMS [knowledge man agement system]” (Ravishankar et al. 2011, p. 48).

A second approach is to focus on IT–business alignment at later stages of the IT lifecycle, such as during the delivery of IT systems. For example, the IT staff at De Beers Jewelry worked closely with production managers at each mine to develop IT applications that met the specific needs of mine managers (Shpilberg et al. 2007). Such close interaction between IT application developers and mine managers is a case of IT–business alignment at the stage of delivery of IT systems, that is, beyond the IT planning stage. In this example, the firm focused on align ment during delivery of the IT system by the IT organization. Contrast this with the approach of Metalco (a pseudonym), a leading global producer of metal products. Metalco invested over \$87 million to implement a global enterprise resource planning system. Although executives in Metalco had a clear understanding of the business strategy being pursued, they failed to implement an appropriate IT execution process that supported its business goals (Grant 2003). As a result, the system’s financial module did not get sufficient buy-in at the business-unit level (Grant 2003). The misalignment of IT delivery and business operations created implementation difficulties and led to resistance to the system in Metalco. Similarly, Walmart’s difficulties in digital growth are often attributed to the tension, that is, low alignment, between IT system delivery by the revenue-focused IT teams at its Walmart.com office in California and the cost-focused business executives at its headquarters in Arkansas (Banjo 2013).

A third approach is to align IT with the business during the stage of IT changes. For instance, Starbucks brought about IT changes to its IT systems, such as mobile payments and social media, that aligned with its need to change customer relationships, business operations, and the business model (Koehn et al. 2014); this alignment resulted in a tenfold increase in its stock price in four years (Fitzgerald 2013).<sup>2</sup>

The anecdotal examples above illustrate how some firms leverage IT investment by complementing it with IT–business alignment focused at an early stage of the IT lifecycle (i.e., the IT investment planning stage), whereas others focus on IT–business alignment at later stages of IT lifecycle (i.e., the IT delivery or IT change stage). Yet, we know little about which of these IT–business alignment approaches provide greater returns to IT investment. For instance, would De Beers be better off by focusing on IT–business alignment at the IT investment planning stage rather than at the IT delivery stage? Would Starbucks be better off by focusing on IT–business alignment at the IT investment planning stage rather than at the IT change stage? These are conjectural, hypothetical or counterfactual questions and they have significant implications for firms to better understand whether the stage of the IT lifecycle where IT–business alignment is focused matters.<sup>3</sup>

On one hand, one can argue that IT–business alignment during the early stage of the IT lifecycle (i.e., IT investment planning) is more critical than at the later stages because it may provide firms with a strategic capability to make focused IT investments at the planning stage itself (Kearns and Lederer 2000). On the other hand, one can argue that IT–business alignment during later stages, that is, during IT delivery and IT change stages, may be more crucial because it helps firms to adapt by aligning changes in business needs and technology environments. This viewpoint is built on the premise that because of changes in business scope and needs, alignment may change as information systems are implemented, and the firm may be unable to adjust its alignment (Chan and Reich 2007); hence, alignment is a “moving target” (Ravishankar et al. 2011, p. 40). This view is echoed in practitioner press that argues that “it is not only necessary to get IT aligned but to keep IT aligned too” (Kappelman et al. 2017, p. 49).

Against this backdrop, we pose the following research question: How does focus on IT–business alignment at the early stage (i.e., IT investment planning) versus later stages (i.e., IT delivery and IT change) compare in helping firms to leverage IT investments for firm performance? We propose that the strength of the effect of IT investment (i.e., the amount of money a firm spends on IT) on firm performance varies depending on the stage of the IT lifecycle at which IT–business alignment is focused: IT investment planning, IT delivery, or IT change. We view IT–business alignment as a dynamic capability to argue that IT–business alignments during the later stages of the IT lifecycle are more effective dynamic capabilities than IT– business alignment at the earlier stage. Therefore, we hypothesize that firms focused on alignment at the IT delivery or IT change stage of the IT lifecycle are better able to leverage IT investment for generating revenue than firms focused on alignment at the IT investment planning stage. We theorize that differences in the effectiveness of IT–business alignment at the IT delivery and IT change stages relative to the IT investment planning stage emanate from differences in the effectiveness of alignment as a dynamic capability that acts upon different routines at these stages and helps firms to adapt to changes in business and IT. We test our hypotheses using secondary data from over 120 firms in India that report IT–business alignment but differ in terms of the stage of the IT lifecycle where they focused on alignment as the key priority.

Our main contributions lie in theorizing and empirically showing that IT–business alignment during later stages of the IT lifecycle is more critical for firms to leverage IT investment for performance than IT–business alignment during the earlier stage. We also underscore that the effectiveness of IT–business alignment as a dynamic capability depends on where in the IT lifecycle IT–business alignment is focused Our work responds to calls to examine differences in strengths of various IT–business alignment approaches in influencing firm performance (Gerow et al. 2014) and calls to better understand how IT–business alignment “may matter in new and different ways” (Coltman et al. 2015, p. 92). Our findings have managerial implications for devising an approach to IT–business alignment based on the relative importance of IT–business alignment at different stages of the IT lifecycle.

## 2. Background and Theoretical Framework

## 2.1. Background

Prior literature on the effects of IT–business alignment on firm performance is largely rooted in contingency theory, which posits that alignment between patterns of relevant contextual, structural, and strategic factors leads to superior firm performance, whereas misalignment results in lower performance (Weill and Olson 1989). Firms with strategic alignment between IT and business extract strategic value from IT investments, thus leading to higher performance (e.g., Chan and Reich 2007, Oh and Pinsonneault 2007, Wu et al. 2015, Sabherwal et al. 2019).

One theme in this literature is that IT strategy and business strategy affect firm performance in unison by making the firm more adaptable to change (e.g., Sabherwal and Chan 2001, Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011, Liang et al. 2017). These findings resonate with IT business value research that finds that IT strategic emphasis in terms of revenue focus, cost focus, or dual focus affects the relationship between IT investment and firm performance (e.g., Mithas and Rust 2016). Furthermore, prior research uses various lenses to characterize IT–business alignment such as intellectual and social alignment (Reich and Benbasat 2000, Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011); strategic, operational, and cross-domain alignment (Sabherwal and Chan 2001, Gerow et al. 2015); alignment at different levels (Tallon 2012); and internal and external alignment (Chan and Reich 2007).

Notwithstanding the rich literatures on IT–business alignment (e.g., Gerow et al. 2014) and IT business value (e.g., Dedrick et al. 2003, Melville et al. 2004), few studies theorize or empirically test the stage in the IT lifecycle (i.e., IT investment planning, IT delivery, or IT change stage) where IT–business alignment is more influential in impacting IT investment’s effect on firm performance.<sup>4</sup> Because IT–business alignment is a moving target, and prior work calls for research to examine how alignment matters in different ways (Coltman et al. 2015), and how some types of alignment have a stronger or weaker impact than others on firm performance (Gerow et al. 2014), our study offers a new way to characterize alignment that enriches knowledge of how alignment at different stages of the IT lifecycle differentially impacts IT value.

## 2.2. A Dynamic Capabilities Perspective of IT–Business Alignment

We view IT–business alignment as a dynamic capability and focus on where in the IT lifecycle it has a greater impact, consistent with the call for studying the points in time when dynamic capabilities exert their effects (Schilke et al. 2018). Dynamic capabilities consist of strategic organizational processes that help firms to systematically generate and modify operat ing routines in pursuit of improved effectiveness (Zollo and Winter 2002). With dynamic capabilities, firms can achieve new resource configurations by integrating, reconfiguring, gaining, or releasing resources to match market changes (Eisenhardt and Martin 2000).

The dynamic capabilities perspective emphasizes the importance of reconfiguring resources and renewing competencies to achieve congruence with changing environments because dynamic capabilities act as a buffer between firm resources and the changing business environment (Teece et al. 1997). In changing business conditions, a firm’s ability to change direction quickly and reconfigure strategically is crucial to achieving superior performance (Hitt et al. 1998). More holistically, dynamic capabilities encapsulate “the firm’s potential to systematically solve problems, formed by its propensity to sense opportunities and threats, to make timely and market-oriented decisions, and to change its resource base” (Barreto 2010, p. 271). Empirical studies on dynamic capabilities in various streams of management research shed light on important forms of dynamic capabilities, such as replication and renewal strategies (Shamsie et al. 2009), marketing orientation and marketing capabilities (Morgan et al. 2009), organizational restructuring and reconfiguration (Girod and Whittington 2017), and alliance management and new product development (Schilke 2014).

Extant information systems literature recognizes that various IT capabilities are sources of or means to achieve dynamic capabilities.<sup>6</sup> At a fundamental level, IT infrastructure and IT capabilities enable firms to respond quickly to threats and opportunities (Bhatt and Grover 2005) and to develop adaptive business processes (Kim et al. 2011). IT capabilities in specific functional areas such as new product development (Sher and Lee 2004, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006, Pavlou and El Sawy 2010) and web-based customer management (Roberts and Grover 2012) enhance firms’ dynamic capabilities by facilitating knowledge assimilation and transformation. Other studies underscore that IT ambidexterity (Lee et al. 2015), managerial innovative uses of IT (Roberts et al. 2016), or remote patient monitoring (Singh et al. 2011) provide dynamic capabilities by boosting organizational agility and facilitating dynamic resource allocation.

Overall, this stream of literature recognizes that IT provides dynamic capabilities by enhancing firm agility, particularly in dynamic environments (Lu and Ramamurthy 2011, Nazir and Pinsonneault 2012, Chakravarty et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2015), thereby helping firms to be responsive to changing business environments (Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Overby et al. 2006).

We draw on prior research that suggests that IT– business alignment is a dynamic capability because it provides a firm with flexibility and agility to alter its IT resources and business resources to achieve organizational advantage (Schwarz et al. 2010, Baker et al. 2011, Liang et al. 2017). IT–business alignment is an organizational competency that helps a firm to coordinate its responses to changes in the business environment (Wade and Hulland 2004). IT–business alignment is embedded in an organizational process where firm-specific IT assets and business assets can be assembled and reconfigured (Yeow et al. 2018) to enable distinctive organizational routines (Teece et al. 1997). For example, by aligning IT and business, managers can ensure that IT systems are in congruence with the changing business environment. IT– business alignment can thus be viewed as a dynamic capability that leverages IT to shape and support the firm’s business strategies (Chen et al. 2008). As Baker et al. (2011, p. 303) note, “strategic alignment can be conceptualized as a management capability, one that fits the description of capabilities from the perspective of the Dynamic Capabilities Framework.” Alignment is thus “a firm-specific [dynamic] capability, fostering resource complementarity between IT and business resources” (Schwarz et al. 2010, p. 63). Overall, the ability of IT–business alignment to be a source of dynamic capabilities explains why IT–business alignment helps firms to sense and respond with agility to changing business needs (Tallon 2008, Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011, Yeow et al. 2018).

## 2.3. Conceptualizing IT–Business Alignment at Different Stages of the IT Lifecycle

Our core argument is that the extent to which firms benefit from IT investment depends on the stage of the IT lifecycle where IT–business alignment is most focused as the key priority: the IT investment planning stage, IT delivery stage, or IT change stage. We focus on these three stages for three reasons. First, although the IT lifecycle (or software development lifecycle) typically refers to its five stages as planning, analysis, design, implementation (delivery), and maintenance (change; Hoffer et al. 2012), IT delivery can be considered to subsume the analysis, design, and implementation stages. This is because “current practice combines the activities traditionally thought of as belonging to analysis, design, and implementation into a single process” (Hoffer et al. 2012, p. 13). Second, prior research recognizes that the analysis and design stages of the IT lifecycle are more iterative in nature.<sup>7</sup> Using three stages facilitates a relatively cleaner separation of the stages, enabling us to feasibly detect whether IT–business alignment at earlier or later stages is more beneficial. Third, from the perspective of organizations implementing IT, a system typically goes through three stages in its lifecycle: the IT investment planning stage (where investments in the system are planned), the IT delivery stage (where the system is delivered to end users), and the IT change stage (where the system is changed in accordance with evolving business needs). For the above reasons, our characterization of align ment at different stages of the IT lifecycle (IT investment planning, IT delivery, and IT changes) offers a novel perspective that complements prior character-

We next elaborate our conceptualization of IT– business alignment at the above three stages. First, consistent with a large body of research (e.g., Oh and Pinsonneault 2007), an early stage where IT–business alignment can be focused is the stage of IT investment planning. We refer to IT–business alignment at the IT investment stage as IT Investment Planning Alignment (ITPA). IT Investment Planning Alignment focuses on plans to invest in IT assets according to business strategies (Reich and Benbasat 1996, Oh and Pinsonneault 2007, Drnevich and Croson 2013). For example, in order to align IT with its business strategy of cost reduction, UPS planned to invest in information acquisition devices and web-based postsales order management systems globally while outsourcing some deliveries in rural areas to U.S. Postal Service to lower its overall costs (Kohli 2007). Prior studies suggest that misalignment between IT investment planning and business strategies is a key reason why organizations fail to reap returns from IT investments (Chan et al. 1997, Henderson and Venkatraman 1999) Strategic alignment between IT and business strategy at the IT investment planning stage helps spur business transformation (Ray et al. 2007); hence, firms that focus on IT Investment Planning Alignment are well placed to realize the business value of IT (Oh and Pinsonneault 2007).

A second potential stage of the IT lifecycle where IT–business alignment can be focused is IT delivery. IT delivery plays a fundamental role in IT’s ability to drive business value (Smith and McKeen 2005) because firm performance is influenced more by actual IT applications delivered than by the amount of IT investments (Weill 1992). Alignment at the IT delivery stage is important because a key challenge in leveraging IT investments is to establish and sustain a system priority set that is consistent with business priorities during IT system delivery (Luftman et al. 1993, 1999). We refer to IT–business alignment at the IT delivery stage as IT Delivery Alignment (ITDA).

IT Delivery Alignment is focused on aligning IT system delivery with the business priorities of the firm. IT Delivery Alignment is distinct from IT Investment Planning Alignment in that IT Delivery Alignment pertains to alignment at the stage of delivery of IT systems, whereas IT Investment Planning Alignment pertains to alignment at the stage of planning of IT investment. For instance, IT investment planning decisions are often made in an authoritative fashion by top management, but IT delivery decisions are made in a collaborative fashion by IT managers (Karhade et al. 2015). IT managers and business managers often face contrasting priorities. For example, business managers may focus on innovation, whereas IT managers in charge of IT delivery may focus on efficiency of the IT architecture. As a result, there is often a disparity between the IT organization’s delivery of systems and the business priorities in the firm. Moreover, because it often takes time for plans to be implemented before systems can be delivered, business priorities may change in the interim (Chan and Reich 2007); hence, the level of alignment at the IT delivery stage may be different from that at the IT investment planning stage (Ravishankar et al. 2011). Similar to the notion of alignment of IT project deliverables in congruence with a firm’s IT strategy (Jenkin and Chan 2010), IT Delivery Alignment ensures that IT understands business priorities, pursues projects, and delivers systems consistent with business priorities at the delivery stage (Shpilberg et al. 2007).

Finally, IT–business alignment during the IT change stage recognizes the criticality of IT changes to be synchronized and aligned with business changes. We refer to IT–business alignment at the stage of IT changes as IT Change Alignment (ITCA). In a turbulent business environment, rigid plans have negative outcomes because such interlocking plans react slowly when adjusting to changing environments (Chan and Reich 2007, Guillemette and Pare 2012). The importance of IT Change Alignment is undergirded by a need to keep business changes and technology changes in synchronization, necessitating a dynamic approach to alignment (Sabherwal and Chan 2001, Ravishankar et al. 2011).

## 2.4. Hypothesis Development

Consistent with prior studies on IT strategic alignment that underscore the importance of alignment of IT plans with business plans (Reich and Benbasat 2000, Sabherwal and Chan 2001), we expect that IT investment leads to superior firm performance when IT–business alignment is focused at the IT investment planning stage (Sabherwal et al. 2019). However, because IT–business strategy alignment (which maps most closely to IT Investment Planning Alignment) is well studied in prior work, our study focuses on how the strength of the IT investment–revenue relationship differs for firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment relative to firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment.

2.4.1. IT Delivery Alignment. We argue that firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment enjoy higher revenue from IT investment relative to firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment for three reasons. First, IT Delivery Alignment helps the IT organization in key routines such as setting systems’ delivery policies and schedules and making system delivery decisions so that IT delivery is in accordance with business priorities (Luftman et al. 1999). Often, “unplanned” systems need to be delivered, “either because the IT strategic planning process did not take them into account or because operational considerations” (Tarafdar and Qrunfleh 2009, p. 343) may require them to be implemented and delivered. IT Delivery Alignment ensures that system delivery responds to such changes in business priorities (Jenkin and Chan 2010). Considering that IT systems and assets are resources, IT Delivery Alignment enables the firm to reconfigure its IT systems resources (Schwarz et al. 2010), thereby helping the firm to more effectively seize opportunities presented by its IT investment (Yeow et al. 2018). Thus, IT Delivery Alignment provides a dynamic capability by enhanc ing a firm’s ability to adapt its system delivery to the changing business conditions (Bharadwaj et al. 2009). In contrast to firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment place less emphasis on accounting for changes in business priorities that necessitate different systems to be implemented and delivered. Firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment are therefore more likely to have a mismatch between the systems delivered and the business priorities of the firm (Luftman et al. 1993, Jenkin and Chan 2010).

Second, IT Delivery Alignment enables firms to dynamically allocate resources to IT projects that are important from a business standpoint and to deliver systems with the buy-in of business functions (Tarafdar and Qrunfleh 2009). By facilitating dynamic allocation of resources, IT Delivery Alignment allows the firm to reconfigure its routines of system delivery in line with its business priorities at the time of delivery. Because of the firm’s ability to suitably reallocate resources at the delivery stage, the firm’s IT investments will be more effective. Thus, IT Delivery Alignment is an effective dynamic capability because it allows the firm to account for changes that occur after the planning stage and to dynamically allocate resources (to IT projects) and reconfigure IT systems in accordance with business priorities that may have changed. In contrast, compared with firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment do not emphasize reallocation of resources or adaptation of systems in congruence with varying business priorities that manifest beyond the initial investment planning stage (Luftman et al. 1993, Chan and Reich 2007) Thus, IT Investment Planning Alignment creates resource rigidities and routine rigidities, thereby hindering the firm’s agility and increasing organizational inertia (Sabherwal et al. 2001, Zollo and Winter 2002, Liang et al. 2017).<sup>10</sup>

Third, IT Delivery Alignment is more beneficial than IT Investment Planning Alignment because it helps the firm to adapt the routines of system customizations and user training that are effected at the stage of system delivery. Such routines have a direct impact on the effectiveness of IT investments. For instance, customizations of systems (effected at the IT delivery stage) will be more appropriate when alignment is focused at the stage of delivery because system customizations will be more congru ent with changes in business priorities, processes, and procedures (Velcu 2010). IT Delivery Alignment also has a ripple effect on increasing the business relevance of training imparted to end users of systems at the delivery stage. More relevant training increases the extent to which IT investment ultimately translates into revenue. This is because IT investments may result in systems that are underused or used inappropriately if employees are not trained with relevant skills to use them (Soto-Acosta et al. 2010). In contrast, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment devote more resources to strategic planning and devote less attention to system customization and training (Benbya et al. 2019). Therefore, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment are less likely than firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment to ensure that system customizations and training are aligned with the business priorities at the stage of delivery.

In sum, we argue that IT Delivery Alignment is more effective as a dynamic capability than IT Investment Planning Alignment because it helps the firm to reconfigure its IT resources and business resources by (a) adapting system delivery to the changing conditions, (b) dynamically allocating resources to IT projects in line with the changing business priorities, and (c) adapting the key routines of system customizations and user training that are effected at the stage of system delivery. Thus, firms that focus on IT Delivery Alignment are better able to translate their IT investment into revenue than firms that focus on IT Investment Planning Alignment. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. The IT investment–firm revenue relationship is stronger for firms that focus on IT Delivery Alignment relative to firms that focus on IT Investment Planning Alignment.

2.4.2. IT Change Alignment. We argue that firms focused on IT Change Alignment enjoy higher revenue from IT investment relative to firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment, for two reasons. First, a key routine at the stage of IT changes is the evaluation of new features and their implementation into systems. With IT Change Alignment, the changes implemented in system features account for the changes in the firm’s business context (Chapin 2005). Hence, the system functionalities are more effective in generating revenue for the firm because the changes and instability in the firm’s operating business context are addressed by IT Change Alignment (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011). IT Change Alignment thus provides the agility to help organizations tackle changing business needs (Overby et al. 2006, Chan and Reich 2007, Liang et al. 2017). In other words, IT Change Alignment is an effective dynamic capability because it enables the firm to sense changes in the business environment and to seize unexpected opportunities by making changes to systems (Eisenhardt and Martin 2000).

In contrast, IT Investment Planning Alignment is less effective than IT Change Alignment in keeping up with changes in business and IT and adjusting the systems to accommodate the changes (Sabherwal et al. 2001). This is because IT Investment Planning Alignment results in tightly bundled resources that are difficult to mobilize when changing business conditions call for change (Liang et al. 2017). IT Investment Planning Alignment results in “reduced strategic flexibility and an inability to quickly respond to changing market conditions” (Liang et al. 2017, p. 863; Shpilberg et al. 2007, Chen et al. 2010). Because business and technology environments change quickly, once an IT plan is enacted, there is a high probability that the plan and technology are already obsolete (Chan and Reich 2007). Because of the inertia resulting from IT Investment Planning Alignment (Liang et al. 2017), IT Investment Planning Alignment is not as effective a dynamic capability as IT Change Alignment to make relevant changes in systems to keep up with changing business needs. These arguments explain why, compared with IT Investment Planning Alignment, IT Change Alignment is more helpful to the firm in addressing uncertainty and addressing the changing needs of IT and business, thereby deriving more benefit from IT investment (Sabherwal and Kirs 1994).

Second, IT change often involves the routine of discontinuing existing systems. At the IT change stage, managers are more aware of why existing IT systems are not working effectively and are also aware of business changes (Chapin 2005). With this knowledge, IT Change Alignment more effectively helps managers to adapt routines to ensure that decisions made regarding discontinuation of systems are consistent with business changes. Thus, IT Change Alignment ensures that systems that do not match with the current business environment are discontinued, so that the firm’s systems are more effective in translating IT investment into revenue. In contrast, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment continue with their existing portfolio of systems even when IT or business conditions have changed, and the strategies and existing systems are no longer appropriate (Baker et al. 2011).

In sum, we argue that the improvisation that IT Change Alignment facilitates via (a) appropriate changes in system features and functionalities and (b) effective decisions related to discontinuation of systems is more beneficial to firms in translating IT investment into revenue than the deliberative planning that characterizes IT Investment Planning Alignment (Baker et al. 2011). In other words, relative to firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment, firms focused on IT Change Alignment are equipped with more effective dynamic capabilities to adapt systems to the changes in business and translate IT investment into revenue (Teece et al. 1997, Sambamurthy et al. 2003). Hence, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 2. The IT investment–firm revenue relationship is stronger for firms that focus on IT Change Alignment relative to firms that focus on IT Investment Planning Alignment.

## 3. Method

We use archival data collected by a large international research firm known for its IT data and research services. The research firm collected IT investment data and IT-related information in its survey of Indian firms in 2008. India is a suitable empirical context for our study because alignment is particularly salient in emerging economies (e.g., India) undergoing fast growth amidst volatility. The environmental volatility in emerging economies due to financial market uncertainty, volatile consumer demand, competition, shrinking product cycles, and rapidly changing IT paradigms has led firms to consider their ability to respond to change (Xue et al. 2011). The dynamic nature of IT and the competitive environment in turbulent markets necessitate the evolution of IT to achieve alignment to keep up with changes in the business (Pavlou and El Sawy 2006).

The research firm conducted the survey to assess the IT investment patterns of Indian firms across sectors and cities in India. The survey respondents included large firms across industries in India, and the survey was administrated via face-to-face interviews of chief information officers (CIOs) and senior IT executives to collect objective metrics related to IT investments. Similar to prior research, data collection from CIOs and senior IT executives is important, as they are in good positions to be knowledgeable of the firm’s IT practices (Aral and Weill 2007, Tallon and Kraemer 2007) and are well positioned to respond to questions at the intersection of IT and corporate strategy (Palmer and Markus 2000). Our final data set comprises 125 firms in India in sectors including automobiles, consumer goods, textiles, pharmaceuticals, chemicals and fertilizers, banking, construction, petroleum, IT services, software, telecom services, and communication and media.

Table 2 presents the definitions and measurement of the variables. We measure firm revenue (REVENUE) as the dependent variable for firm performance, consistent with prior work (e.g., Mehra et al. 2014), and consistent with research that suggests that a significant portion of the impact of IT on firm performance is accounted for by IT’s impact on revenue, whereas the impact of IT on cost reduction is not significant (Mithas et al. 2012). We measure a firm’s total IT investment (ITINVEST), which includes IT investment in hardware, software, and services, and is consistent with similar measurement of aggregate IT investment in prior studies (e.g., Ho et al. 2017, Mithas et al. 2017, Havakhor et al. 2019).

Because we are interested in the relative strength of IT–business alignment at the IT investment planning, IT delivery, and IT change stages, we consider only firms that report IT–business alignment and have clearly indicated the stage of the IT lifecycle at which IT–business alignment is focused in the firm. Specifically, each firm indicated how IT is aligned with the business direction as the key priority, by selecting one of the following responses: (1) IT investment is aligned with business strategy (ITPA), (2) IT delivery is aligned with business priorities (ITDA), or (3) IT change is aligned with business change (ITCA).<sup>11</sup>

Our categorization is analogous in two ways to similar categorization approaches used in prior research. First, our study uses discrete categories to identify where the firm focuses on IT–business alignment. This is similar in principle to prior studies that use discrete categories to categorize IT systems (e.g., Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1997, Oh and Pinsonneault 2007), IT investments (Weill 1992), or IT’s role in industries (Dehning et al. 2003). For example, Oh and Pinsonneault (2007) categorize IT systems into revenue-growth and cost-reduction categories, based on whether the strategic thrust of the system had an overarching objective centered on efficiency or effectiveness. Second, our measures capture the stage of the IT lifecycle where the firm focuses its IT–business alignment as the key priority. Our measures are similar to those in prior studies that categorize IT systems according to whether the system predominantly falls into one category based on some criterion, such as strategic thrust of IT (Oh and Pinsonneault 2007), orientation of the impact of IT (Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1997), main role of IT in industries (Dehning et al. 2003), or main type of IT investments (Weill 1992). For example, an underlying logic for Weill’s (1992) categorization of IT investments as informational, transactional, or strategic is that although IT may be performing all roles in an industry, the categorization captures the key role of IT in an industry.

We include control variables to account for firm and industry heterogeneity. We control for firm size (SIZE, number of employees), firm legal status (PRIVATE, private or public), and sector differences (MFG, manufacturing or services) to account for firm or industry characteristics that may impact firm performance. Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics and correlations among the variables. As expected, IT investment is correlated positively and significantly with revenue.

Because our hypotheses pertain to comparing the strength of the relationship between IT investment and revenue for firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment relative to firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment, we use firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment as a reference group. We estimate the following cross-sectional equation to test our hypotheses:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\ln (R E V E N U E) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln (I T I N V E S T)} \\ & {\qquad + \beta_ {2} I T D A + \beta_ {3} I T C A} \\ & {\qquad + \beta_ {4} I T D A \times \ln (I T I N V E S T)} \\ & {\qquad + \beta_ {5} I T C A \times \ln (I T I N V E S T)} \\ & {\qquad + \beta_ {6} \ln (S I Z E) + \beta_ {7} M F G} \\ & {\qquad + \beta_ {8} P R I V A T E + \varepsilon ,} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where β’s are the parameters to be estimated, and ε is the error term.

We begin by using ordinary least squares (OLS) to estimate Equation (1), in which the focal explanatory variables (IT investment and alignment variables) are considered exogenous in an econometric sense (Wooldridge 2002). From a theoretical perspective, one can justify the alignment variables as exogenous to firm revenue but determined by the firm’s overall strategy, which is usually assumed to endure over long periods of time and hence not lead to any simultaneity between firm revenue and alignment focus. IT investment is based on the IT budget, which was planned in a previous year. Thus, IT investment is a predetermined variable and can be considered exogenous in our analysis.

We assess the robustness of our findings to endogeneity. First, we account for the possible endogeneity of IT investment by using three approaches: two-stage least squares (2SLS), limited information maximum likelihood (LIML), and generalized method of moments (GMM). Second, we account for the possibility that ITDA and ITCA may be endogenous, and we use three approaches to address this concern. The first approach is the method suggested by Heckman (1979) and used in prior research in information systems (Bharadwaj et al. 2007, Saldanha et al. 2017). The second approach is the estimation of a three-stage least squares model that accounts for the endogenous nature of ITDA, ITCA, and ITINVEST. As a third exploratory approach, we apply the propensity score matching method to address firms’ potential selfselection of the IT–business alignment stage (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). We discuss further details in the results section.

## 4.1. Accounting for Endogeneity

To account for the potential endogeneity of IT investment, we use 2SLS, which is an instrumental variable approach to address endogeneity. We use hardware investment (HWINVEST) as an instrumental variable for IT investment because hardware investment is highly correlated with IT investment and does not directly influence firm performance (Rai et al. 1997), except through the effect of IT investments on firm performance. We use industry average IT investment per employee (IndustryAvgIT) as another instrumental variable. Industry-level IT investment is known to influence firm IT investment and is considered exogenous to firm performance (Hitt 1999, Kleis et al. 2012). The criterion for a good instrument is high correlation with the endogenous independent variable but no correlation with the error term in the structural equation. The correlation between IT investment and hardware investment and the correlation between IT investment and industry average IT investment per employee are both positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that these instruments are appropriate. The coefficients of these variables in the first stage are significant (F-statistics significant at $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), further supporting the use of these variables as instruments and suggesting no problem of weak identification.

If IT investment is endogenous, the two interaction terms are also rendered endogenous (Maddala 1983), which can lead to biased OLS coefficients. Following the suggestions of Wooldridge (2002), we devise another set of instruments by multiplying the instruments for IT investment with the exogenous moderator variables ITDA and ITCA. Together, these instruments are used to identify the effects of the interaction terms on revenue. Equation (1) can then be estimated using 2SLS. Column (4) of Table 3 shows the 2SLS results. The F-statistic of the excluded instrumental vari able in the first stage is larger than 10 $( F = 2 2 3 . 8 2 )$ suggesting that we can reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are weak (Staiger and Stock 1997) Because we have more instruments than endogenous variables, the equation is overidentified. The Sargan (1958) test yields a p-value of 0.895, and the Basmann (1960) test yields a p-value of 0.908, both of which are much larger than 0.10, implying that the overidentifying restriction tests support the validity of the instruments (Wooldridge 2002). The interaction of IT investment and ITDA is positive and statistically significant $( \beta = 0 . 4 3 5 , p < 0 . { \bar { 0 } } 5 )$ , consistent with Hypothesis 1. The interaction of IT investment and ITCA is positive and statistically significant $( \beta = 0 . 6 6 3 , p <$ 0.05), consistent with Hypothesis 2. In sum, the 2SLS results are consistent with the OLS results.

Although the analyses so far suggest that our instruments are appropriate, for robustness, we use LIML, which is superior to 2SLS in the presence of weak instruments (Stock and Yogo 2002). The secondstage results of the LIML estimation are shown in column (5) of Table 3. The Cragg and Donald (1993) identification test F-statistic (205.87) is larger than the Stock and Yogo (2002) critical value for 10% maximal LIML size, implying that we can reject the null hypothesis of weak instruments. Also, the LIML results (column (5)) are almost identical to the 2SLS results (column (4)). This is expected, because when the instruments are strong, 2SLS and LIML should produce similar results (Stock and Yogo 2002).

To address the potential endogeneity of ITDA and ITCA, we use three approaches. As a first approach, we follow the two-step error-correction method suggested by Heckman (1979). We create a variable (ITDCA) that is 1 for firms with ITDA = 1 or ITCA = 1, and 0 otherwise. In the first step, we estimate a probit model to regress ITDCA on the variables that are likely to affect where the firm focuses its alignment.

In this first step, to facilitate model identification, we include four additional variables that may influence the firm’s likelihood of focusing on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment but are unlikely to directly influence firm revenue except via their influences on ITDA or ITCA. First, we include a variable that indicates whether the firm refers to in dustry benchmarks before making major IT investments (IndBenchBefore). The logic for the inclusion of this variable is that because the firm performs this action before making IT investments, it may also be more likely to focus on IT–business alignment earlier in the IT lifecycle (i.e., at stage of IT investment planning), and less likely to focus on alignment at the later stages of the IT lifecycle (i.e., IT delivery or IT change). Simply referring to industry benchmarks is unlikely to impact revenue directly, except via its influence on the firm’s actions to align IT with business at the earlier (or later) stage of IT lifecycle. Second, we include a variable that indicates whether the firm does return-on-investment (ROI) calculations before IT investment (ROICalcBefore). We include this variable because if the firm performs this action before making IT investments, it may also be more likely to focus its alignment earlier in the IT lifecycle. Note that performing an ROI calculation before IT investment is unlikely to influence revenue directly, except via its influence on a firm’s actions to align IT with the business. Third, we include a variable that indicates whether the firm views IT investment as operational or strategic (ITInvtOperational). The argument for including this variable is that if a firm has an operational view of IT investment, it may be more likely to focus on IT–business alignment at later (operational) stages of the IT lifecycle, rather than at the earlier (planning) stage. Simply viewing IT as operational is unlikely to directly impact revenue, except via its influence on the firm’s actions to align IT with the business. Fourth, we include a variable that indicates how frequently the firm updates its IT strategy plan (ITPlanUpdateFreq). More frequent updates of the IT strategy plan suggest that the firm may also focus on alignment at later stages of the IT lifecycle beyond investment planning. Also, simply updating the IT plan is unlikely to influence revenue, unless the firm takes action to align IT investment with the business at the later stages.

Based on our theoretical arguments above, the four variables satisfy the conditions of being good exclusion restrictions in our study; that is, they are correlated with alignment but uncorrelated with revenue except via their influence on alignment. The signs and significances of these variables in the first step (see Online Appendix D, Table D1) are broadly in the expected directions discussed above, further supporting the use of these variables in the first step. We compute the inverse Mills ratio from the first-step estimates and include the inverse Mills ratio as a control variable in the second-step estimation to address endogeneity (Heckman 1979, Shaver 1998, Bharadwaj et al. 2007). Table D2 in Online Appendix D shows the second-step results accounting for endogeneity. The findings are qualitatively unchanged, with positive and significant coefficients for the interaction terms of ITDA with IT investment (Online Appendix, Table D2, column (3), $\beta = 0 . 5 5 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ) and ITCA with IT investment (column (3), $\beta = 0 . 6 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

As a second approach to account for endogeneity of ITDA and ITCA, we estimate a three-stage least squares model (Zellner and Theil 1962) that treats

ITDA, ITCA, and IT investment as endogenous. To estimate this model, we made identifying assumptions by including some variables in the equations for ITDA and ITCA but not in the revenue equation, that is, we treat these variables as instruments. These vari ables are industry benchmarking before IT investment (IndBenchBefore), update of IT plan (ITPlanUpdateFreq), ROI calculation before IT investment (ROICalcBefore), and whether IT is seen as operational or strategic (ITInvtOperational). As discussed earlier, these four variables are good instruments because they are expected to influence the stage at which a firm aligns IT with business (ITDA and ITCA), but they are not likely to influence revenue directly other than via their influence on ITDA and ITCA. For the IT investment equation, as discussed earlier, we use hardware investment (HWINVEST) and industry average IT investment (IndustryAvgIT) as instruments.

## 4.2. Additional Robustness Tests

We perform additional robustness tests. First, if heteroskedasticity is present, the GMM estimator is more efficient (Baum et al. 2003). A heteroskedasticity test for 2SLS (White test) yields a p-value of 0.08, and Breusch–Pagan test yields a p-value of 0.137. As the

White statistic marginally rejects the null at the 10% level, we use robust heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors for all estimations. The second-stage results of robust covariance GMM estimation are shown in col umn (6) of Table 3 and are very similar to the 2SLS and LIML results.<sup>13</sup>

Second, the variance inflation factors are well below recommended limits, suggesting that multicollinearity is not problematic (Greene 2011). Third, in Harman’s one-factor analysis, no single major factor emerges, and the first factor accounts for only 30.2% of the total variance, implying that common method bias is not a significant concern. Moreover, because our core theory pertains to interactions, common method variance is less concerning, as such variance reduces the likelihood of detecting interaction effects (Siemsen et al. 2010). Fourth, we repeat the analyses by centering the variables before creating the interaction variables and get similar results to those reported here.

Fifth, we assess whether any statistically significant differences exist between the 125 firms in our final sample and the 86 firms that were included in the survey but got dropped from the final sample because of missing variables (the initial data set contained 211 firms). Using two-tailed t-tests, we compare the two sets of firms on relevant variables such as IT investment, revenue, and firm size. These tests show no significant differences between the two groups, suggesting that the variables were missing at random.

Finally, we conduct an exploratory sensitivity analysis to mitigate concerns of the potential impact of unobserved confounding variables. We compute the thresholds at which the impact of an unobservable confounding variable would render the effects of our focal variables (the two interaction terms) insignificant. Similar to prior studies (Kim et al. 2014, Huang et al. 2018, Whitaker et al. 2019), and following Frank (2000), we compute the impact threshold for a confounding variable (ITCV) to quantify the impact of a confounding variable on the inference of a regression coefficient. The ITCV determines the minimum correlation required between a confounding variable and the focal variables to alter the effect from statistically significant to insignificant. The ITCVs for the IT Investment and ITDA interaction term and for the IT investment and ITCA interaction term are <sup>−</sup>0.024 and <sup>−</sup>0.054, respectively. To put these numbers in perspective, the highest ITCVs based on our control variables (PRIVATE, SIZE, and MFG) are 0.014 (for PRIVATE) for IT Investment and ITDA interaction and 0.044 (for PRIVATE) for the IT Investment and ITCA interaction. Thus, we need a confounding variable with a stronger impact than PRIVATE to nullify our findings; that is, an unobserved confounding variable must be more highly correlated with revenue, the interaction between IT investment and ITDA, and the interaction between IT investment and ITCA than PRIVATE. A confounding variable that satisfies the above conditions is unlikely because we already have strong controls such as PRIVATE, SIZE, and MFG in our models. Therefore, a confounding variable that nullifies the moderating effects of ITDA and ITCA on the relationship between IT investment and revenue is unlikely. Together, these tests suggest that the findings are robust.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Findings

Our study has three key findings. First, the relationship between IT investment and firm revenue is stronger for firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment than for firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment. This finding suggests that focus on IT Delivery Alignment helps firms to be better equipped with dynamic capabilities to cope with the conflicting priorities of IT delivery and business that may hinder firms from leveraging IT investments. In contrast, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment may not enjoy as much benefit of alignment because system delivery and business priorities may be misaligned, although they may have been initially aligned at the stage of IT investment planning.

Second, the relationship between IT investment and revenue is stronger for firms focused on IT Change Alignment than for firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment. This finding suggests that firms focused on IT Change Alignment are better equipped with dynamic capabilities to handle changes in business and IT. In contrast, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment may be relatively hampered by a lack of dynamic capability because of not realigning IT with the business at the time of IT change.

Third, our analyses suggest that high IT investment results in increased revenue when combined with IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment. This finding highlights a need to consider the levels of IT investment when assessing how IT–business align ment affects firm performance. It implies that to benefit from high IT investment, firms need the dynamic capabilities provided by IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment. Simply aligning business strategy with IT investment planning may not be enough. Conversely, at low levels of IT investment, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment have higher revenue than firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment. This suggests that the benefits of IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment may not be fully realized if alignment is not supported by adequate levels of IT investment. Specifically, because IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment necessitate that the routines at the IT delivery and IT change stages of the IT lifecycle be adapted to the business change and organizational changes, such efforts may require a high level of IT investment that justifies the time and effort needed to adapt these routines to broader business changes at the later stages. In contrast, IT Investment Planning Alignment may require far more up-front effort and anticipation of likely changes in business plans, which could be achieved with lower levels of IT investment.

Our supplementary results using software and service investments are qualitatively similar to our results for all IT investments. These results suggest that firm performance using IT accrues from investments in software and services rather than just hardware (Dos Santos et al. 2012).

## 5.2. Contributions and Research Implications

Our study offers two contributions with several research implications. First, departing from prior alignment studies, our findings suggest that the effect of IT investments on firm revenue depends on the stage in the IT lifecycle during which alignment is focused on by a firm. Specifically, we theorize and empirically document that the strength of the relationship between IT investment and firm revenue is different for firms focused on alignment at later stages of the IT lifecycle relative to firms focused on alignment at the early stage. We contribute to the dynamic capabilities literature by enriching our understanding of the “kinds of environments [or conditions or situations] in which the dynamic capabilities concept [of IT–business alignment] is most relevant” (Barreto 2010, p. 276) and highlighting “how managers [can] use their dynamic capabilities” (p. 274) of IT–business alignment more effectively at later stages of the IT lifecycle. Also, we document the different effects of IT–business alignment, based on different “points in time at which dynamic capabilities [i.e., IT–business alignment, in our study] are developed or exert their effects” (Schilke et al. 2018, p. 399).

Our findings are counterintuitive because much of the prior alignment research emphasizes the need for IT strategy and business strategy to be aligned without making a distinction across different stages of IT implementation. Our findings suggest that IT–business alignment is more crucial at the later (i.e., IT delivery and IT change) stages than at the early (i.e., IT investment planning) stage of the IT lifecycle.<sup>14</sup>

The reasons for our findings become clear when we consider the routines that typically occur at the three stages of the IT lifecycle discussed in Table 1. At the IT delivery stage, the key routines are resource allocation for system delivery, system customization, and end-user training. Alignment focused at these routines provides dynamic capability and is more effective in deriving value from IT investment because the alignment is closer to the point when systems are implemented and delivered. At the IT change stage, the routines of evaluating and implementing new features and changes in systems account for recent changes in IT and business. In contrast, at the IT investment planning stage, the routines of planning and securing top management commitment, albeit important, do not account for business and technology changes after the planning stage (Chan and Reich 2007), thus resulting in lower performance benefit from IT investment (Benbya et al. 2019).

Second, our findings suggest that no single approach to IT–business alignment unconditionally dominates others in terms of revenue generation, at all levels of IT investment. Similar to contingency theory and studies that suggest the existence of “profile misalignment” in the context of IT innovation (Fichman and Melville 2014, p. 205), this finding suggests that firms with low levels of IT investment may not have an appropriate IT profile to benefit from IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment. This finding is consistent with the view that “having dynamic capabilities [in our study, ITDA or ITCA] per se does not lead to superior firm performance” (Zahra et al. 2006, p. 942; Eisenhardt and Martin 2000); instead “focal firms must, in the first place, possess the resources [in our study, IT investment or IT resources] on which dynamic capabilities can act” (Barreto 2010, p. 263). This finding also resonates with research that considers configura tions of elements to assess the performance implications of IT (Aljazzaf et al. 2019, Park and Mithas 2020).

Additionally, this study provides insights on IT– business alignment for firms in India, an emerging economy. Most prior studies in IT–business alignment were conducted in a developed economy context, barring a few exceptions (e.g., Yayla and Hu 2012, Wu et al. 2015). Given the relatively turbulent environments of emerging markets, our findings may be more critical for firms in growing markets than for firms in mature markets in pursuing alignment to drive revenue generation.

## 5.3. Implications for Practice

According to a survey of more than 500 senior executives conducted by Bain & Company, despite top management’s efforts to devote resources to align IT investments with their most important businesses, less than 20% believed that their efforts were succeeding (Lochan and Shah 2010). Our study has several implications for managers who seek to im prove the success of their efforts at IT–business alignment. First, a key insight for managers is that alignment at the later stages (IT delivery and IT change) of the IT lifecycle is a more effective source of dynamic capabilities that helps firms to adapt to changing business environments. Many firms take a front-end approach to alignment, where IT and business get aligned at the planning stage of IT investment, but alignment tapers off or is neglected after the initial investment planning. Our results suggest that such an approach is not very beneficial, as exemplified in the failure of ITS (a pseudonym for an IT services firm) to reap the benefits from its knowledge management system because of its focus on alignment at the initial investment planning stage and its inability to maintain the strength of alignment beyond the investment planning stage of the system (Ravishankar et al. 2011). Our findings imply that constant recalibration of IT–business alignment is more beneficial.

A second managerial implication is that firms focused on alignment during the IT change stage generate more revenue if they invest more in IT. In contrast, at a lower level of IT investment, it may be better to place emphasis on alignment at the early stage of IT investment planning. These insights offer managers guidance on where they may be better off focusing their alignment efforts depending on the level of their IT investments.

Third, the finding from our supplementary analyses related to software and IT service investments can assist managers in formulating IT–business alignment plans and allocating budgets to their IT portfolios. This finding can help managers to enhance the impacts of categories of IT investments (hardware, software, and services) on revenue by focusing on alignment during the relevant stages.

Finally, given our study’s empirical context of India, and realizing that managers in emerging markets usually have limited access to resources and face high uncertainty in the economic environment, our findings suggest that firms in emerging markets can use IT–business alignment as a lever at appropriate stages of the IT lifecycle to realize revenue benefits from their IT investments.

## 5.4. Limitations, Future Research, and Conclusion

Our study has limitations that can be starting points for future work. First, despite our careful analyses and robustness checks, it is difficult to prove causality. We call for studies to assess causality and generalizability, using methods such as structural models, case studies, or Bayesian networks (Zheng and Pavlou 2010). Second, whereas our empirical context of one country (India) enhances internal validity, future work could extend our study to other nations for more generalizability.

Third, although our archival data were collected from chief information officers and senior IT executives by an independent research firm, the selfreported nature of the data may still be viewed as a limitation. However, because the data used for our key variables are accounting measures (investment and revenue) and not perceptual data, and because the alignment variables are generally unambiguous (planning, delivery, or change), the concern of common method bias is substantially mitigated (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Moreover, self-reported measures also have many advantages because they are the perspective of key informants, are likely to reflect the firm’s current state (Kearns and Lederer 2003), and are often highly correlated with archival or internal objective data (Tallon et al. 2000, Sabherwal and Chan 2001).

Furthermore, although single-item measures are often viewed as a limitation, single-item measures for alignment are appropriate for our study because they allow us to capture the stage of the IT lifecycle at which the firm focused most on alignment as the key priority. This enables us to address our research ob jective to assess how firms that differ in their approach to alignment in terms of the stage (IT investment planning, IT delivery, or IT change) at which they focus on alignment differ in the extent to which they leverage IT investment for revenue. Also, singleitem measures are concrete, straightforward, narrow, and unambiguous as interpreted by informative respondents (Drolet and Morrison 2001). Nevertheless, future research with alternate operationalizations using multi-item scales can extend our findings. Moreover, although our categorization of the three stages is theoretically justifiable and meaningful to address our research questions, future work could build on our study by further extending our categorization.

Finally, we call for research to explore the governance mechanisms that are needed to achieve align ment during IT investment planning, IT delivery, and IT change (Wu et al. 2015) and to explore the extent to which various governance mechanisms during the three stages help firms to achieve synergies between IT and business (Kude et al. 2018). Our research also offers a foundation to test the mechanisms underlying the effects of alignment at various stages of the IT lifecycle. For example, studies using multiple theoretical perspectives can test the explanatory power and boundary conditions of theories to help us understand how firms achieve alignment at the various stages (Burton-Jones et al. 2013).

To conclude, this study tests the relative strengths of the effects of IT investment on firm revenue for firms focused on alignment at the early (investment planning) versus later (delivery and change) stages of the IT lifecycle. Analyzing archival data from Indian firms, we found that the relationship between IT investment and firm revenue was stronger for firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment than for firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment. The findings suggest that at high levels of IT investment, firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment outperform firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment whereas at low levels of IT investment, firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment outperform firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment. These findings provide new insights to spur further research on IT–business alignment, and for managers to improve firm performance by sharpening their attention to alignment beyond the planning stage.

This suggests that for alignment to be effective, IT investment needs to be substantial. These findings are consistent with the recognition in the dynamic capabilities literature that “dynamic capabilities may damage rather than improve a firm’s performance if dynamic capabilities are used when there is no need for them” (Barreto 2010, p. 264). Without the interaction terms included in the model, the coefficients of IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment are nonsignificant (column (2) in Table 3), implying that IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment have nonsignificant unconditional effects on revenue, but help firms to translate IT investment into revenue, consistent with the view in dynamic capabilities literature that “having dynamic capabilities per se does not lead to superior firm performance” (Zahra et al. 2006, p. 942; Eisenhardt and Martin 2000).

<sup>14</sup> As discussed earlier, IT Investment Planning Alignment can be considered a type of strategic (intellectual) alignment, whereas both IT Delivery Alignment and IT Change Alignment can be considered as cross-domain alignments. In this regard, it is interesting to compare our findings with the findings from a meta-analysis of IT– business alignment (Gerow et al. 2014). Although it is not a direct apples-to-apples comparison (because we take a perspective of IT lifecycle stages that differs from the approach of Gerow et al. (2014), and ours is a firm-level study, whereas the study by Gerow et al. (2014) is a meta-analysis), our finding that firms focused on IT Delivery Alignment or IT Change Alignment benefit more from IT investment than firms focused on IT Investment Planning Alignment is along the lines of their finding that cross-domain alignment had a slightly higher population correlation point estimate with financial performance (0.4464) than intellectual alignment (0.4192; Gerow et al. 2014, p. 1168).
