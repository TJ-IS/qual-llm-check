# Gamification in the Workplace: The Central Role of the Aesthetic Experience

ABSTRACT: Although gamification in the workplace is burgeoning, organizations frequently have difficulty sustaining user engagement with a gamified information system (IS). The focus of this study is how a gamified IS in the workplace engages users and encourages them to continue system use. By proposing the concepts of flow experience (FE) and aesthetic experience (AE) as different ways to provide deep and meaningful user engagement, this study develops a model that explores the antecedents of FE and AE and their roles in explaining an individual’s continuance intention to use of a gamified IS. The model is tested using data collected from 178 users of a gamified IS in a global consulting company. The results demonstrate that although FE and AE are complementary forces, AE is more salient than FE for explaining continuance intention. The research proposes AE as a parsimonious yet powerful construct that extends the research on user engagement. The findings contribute to research on gamification by shifting scholarly attention from deep engagement characterized by FE to meaningful engagement characterized by AE.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: aesthetic experience, affordances, continuance of IT use, flow experience, gamification, user engagement, workplace gamification aesthetics in IT.

In recent years, researchers in the information system (IS) field have expanded their focus from the utilitarian value of IS use (e.g., usefulness, effectiveness, and productivity) to include IS hedonic value (e.g., enjoyment, fun, playfulness) [11, 54]. One strand in this broad shift, the study of gamification—the use of game design elements in nongame contexts to engage users—has gained attention in the academic and industrial fields [19]. Many organizations have introduced gamification into IS for existing business activities [45, 78]. For example, employees receive rewards such as points, badges, and trophies when they reach milestones, join in challenges, set new goals, or win competitions in work-related activities (e.g., knowledge sharing, idea creation, and sales performance).

Despite widespread belief in the benefits of gamification, studies have highlighted the difficulty of sustaining user engagement because the effects of game elements are often short-lived [40, 66, 79]. Many gamified IS users reportedly discontinue their engagement within a few months after their initial system use [31, 70]. Hence, it is crucial to understand the mechanisms that explain why users would continue to use gamified IS. Without a precise, evidence-based understanding of how a gamified IS engages users and encourages them toward continued system use, organizations will fail to achieve a return on their investment in gamifying IS, and the opportunity to harness emerging technologies with a view to engaging users will be lost.

In order to predict a user’s continuance intention, gamification research has offered a number of explanations, including attitude toward a gamified IS [28], social influence [27], satisfaction, and self-efficacy [12], chief among them being flow experience (FE) as a construct [29, 32, 42]. FE describes a user’s state of mind when absorbed completely by the experienced activity together with heightened enjoyment [77]. Researchers have found that when a user becomes cognitively absorbed and feels a sense of merging with the gamified IS during the interaction, his or her continuance intention increases [34].

FE has been adopted widely in IS research as a construct that reflects the depth of engagement from a hedonic perspective. Yet, researchers currently stress that a gamified IS should not rely merely on hedonic user experience that is based on deep engagement; rather, it should also create an avenue that enables individuals to find meaningful connections between their own interests and system use [66, 67, 79]. Correspondingly, users’ meaningful engagement in the interaction with the gamified IS has been suggested as a key determinant for the continued use of the system [9]. Nevertheless, a systematic and rigorous approach to developing a research construct that reflects meaningful engagement is lacking in the IS literature, despite the increase in scholarly and practical attention devoted to meaningful engagement. One promising prior approach toward conceptualizing meaningful engagement is provided by the theory of aesthetic experience (AE). AE, in the interpretation used here, was initially proposed by Dewey [21]. According to Dewey, AE refers to a state of mind in which a person feels a sense of meaning and more deeply understands the essence of the experienced events. This interpretation of AE thus differs from common uses of the term in regard to beauty or visual appeal (cf. [8, 39, 84]). As explained by McCarthy and Wright [59] and Nardi [65], AE derives its theoretical basis from the meaningfulness of engagement with a technology. The present study thus proposes the concept of AE to characterize meaningful engagement with a gamified IS and posits AE as a key construct that is expected to account for a user’s continuance intention in the context of gamification. However, unlike FE, the concept of AE has not been well-established in the IS literature. Consequently, we seek to determine the precise nature of AE in the context of gamification. We further aim to identify the factors that influence AE, its relationship with the well-understood dynamics of FE, as well as the different roles of AE and FE in explaining an individual’s continuance intention to use a gamified IS.

Toward that end, we theoretically conceptualize AE, describe its dimensions and interrelationships, and distinguish it from FE. Then, we integrate this concept of AE with the notion of affective affordances [85], which proposes that it is essential for continued use to create and design an IS that generates specific technological affordances to engage users. Using this affordance lens, we explore the antecedents of AE and FE. An affordance lens is appropriate because it allows researchers to assess how users perceive the actions they can perform while interacting with technological features. Finally, by developing and testing a nomological network that includes gamification affordances as antecedents of FE and AE, this study verifies the AE’s validity as a construct for predicting an individual’s continuance intention.

The study contributes to IS literature by drawing scholarly attention to AE as a parsimonious yet powerful construct that complements the notion of deep engagement based on FE with meaningful engagement based on AE. In addition, by demonstrating how the technological affordances of a gamified IS can enhance meaningful engagement, the study provides both a theoretical explanation and empirical justification of how and why gamification engages users with an IS and keeps them continually using the system. For practice, the study has the potential to inform future design strategies because it identifies effective mechanisms for successful gamification that increase employee engagement with an IS.

## Research Background

Gamification is increasingly being employed as a paradigm intended to enhance user engagement by adding playfulness and fun to existing ISs. Application areas include education [74], training [77], marketing [34, 37], crowdsourcing [18], and health care [42]. User engagement is considered “a desirable—even essential—human response to computer-mediated activities” [46, p. 112]. Research has suggested two divergent views, each with its own emphasis, in conceptualizing user engagement: deep engagement and meaningful engagement. The two views are not mutually exclusive, but they nevertheless differ substantitively. The former, which focuses on the depth of engagement, argues that a gamified IS should be designed in such a way that users can become totally involved with the system, thereby experiencing a sense of heightened enjoyment, immersive concentration, and time distortion [54, 77]. The latter view, which addresses the meaningfulness of engagement, argues that a gamified IS should provide users with a means to explore and to reflect on the information obtained from their activities, thus helping them to experience a sense of meaning [49, 66]. According to the literature on meaning [51, 52, 61], people derive meaning when their activities are consistent with core aspects of the self (e.g., beliefs, values, traits, personal contexts, and competences). People also perceive their activity to be meaningful when the activity facilitates progress on personal goals [61]. Therefore, in this study, meaning refers to the personal connection between a user and the IS use. Given that a gamified IS provides information on a user’s personal records of their activities as feedback, encourages new challenges, and facilitates self-progress, providing users with meaning is essential to engage users. In the gamification literature, researchers increasingly concur about the significance of meaningful engagement as an important boundary condition for the validity of the gamification model [9, 20, 67, 79]. However, the question of how to capture the extent to which a user feels that he or she is meaningfully engaged with a gamified IS has yet to be answered.

## Conceptualizing Aesthetic Experience as Meaningful Engagement

Developed by Dewey [21], the theory of AE suggests AE as an avenue to finding meaningful connections between an individual’s own interests and behavior—one that should not be confined to the experience of viewing the highly revered and distant art of museums [65]. AE can emerge in day-to-day life and should be understood as more than a sensory experience, such as visual beauty or sensory stimuli [6]. Sandelands and Buckner [76] argue that work can have properties similar to art and that these properties can function similarly to encourage an AE. Taylor [81] argues that management practices in organizations should consider employees AE in a way that allows managers to create and bring meaning to employees personal lives through work-related activities. Although the concept of AE has been developed in the fields of arts and philosophy, its scientific value in explaining the mechanisms that cause people to become captivated and/or engaged with IS has been reported in diverse disciplines, such as game studies [65], human–computer interactions [38], and marketing [69]. These studies have acknowledged that AE is essential for positive computer–human interactions and that it reflects a user’s state of mind in which he or she understands the meaning of his or her interactions with an IS [43, 59]. In the present study, we introduce AE as an aspect of user engagement distinct from FE, which will help to enrich our understanding of user engagement with an IS.

Some scholars suggest that FE and AE partially overlap in that they commonly reflect a psychological state in which a person strongly concentrates on the task at hand, leading to optimal experiences [13]. However, FE and AE differ from each other in a number of important respects. First, FE is strongly task-focused. It reflects a state of mind in which one’s attention is limited to a single action or narrowly defined set of tasks [13, p. 40]. By contrast, AE reflects a state of mind in which one’s focus is given to an intentional field of actions in order to accomplish a desired outcome [6, 38]. As opposed to the task-focus of FE, AE is defined by awareness of the relationship between the task environment and self. In an AE state, people are continually aware of the contextual situation in which given tasks are performed. Second, FE refers to a state of immersion in one moment, whereas AE expands beyond the specific moment in time, reflecting a sense of fulfillment that lingers after the experience has ended. Hence, FE is manifested by a temporary immersion in a specific moment [13, p. 50], whereas AE is manifested by a continuing process of development of the experience [4, p. 10]. Third, FE is manifested by loss of selfconsciousness and a distortion of time, whereas AE is manifested by awareness of the self in a process brought to fulfillment through a medium of activity [38, p. 79]. Lowry et al. [54, p. 622] describe FE as “the inability to register the passage of time while engaged in an interaction.” In a flow state, people feel they are in control over the interaction with an object when the challenges of the task fit their skills [25]. By contrast, in an AE state, people actively discover new paths to fulfilling their activities and feel that they are exercising power to meet environmental challenges [38]. Thus, AE reflects the users’ awareness of how to proceed and of what counts as the fulfillment of the purposes and objectives being pursued within an IS.

Drawing on the existing literature on AE, we identify three dimensions that define AE: meaning, self-expansion, and active discovery. The three dimensions of AE capture the extent of meaningful engagement a user experiences while interacting with an IS. The first dimension, meaning, refers to the extent to which an individual comes to understand the meaning of an activity [6, 21, 59]. Given that a gamified IS provides users with information on themselves, users can reflect on their activities by using the IS, thereby facilitating a personal connection between users and the system use. The second dimension, self-expansion, refers to the extent to which an individual’s sense of self has expanded by knowing things or broadening his or her perspective [6, 21, 38]. Self-expansion is related to self-growth [4, 5, 59]. Within a gamified IS, users track their performance and achieve milestones, which enable the users to experience personal growth [20]. The third dimension, active discovery, refers to the extent to which an individual feels he or she is actively seeking answers or resolutions to cognitive challenges to achieve his or her personal goals. A gamified IS enables users to set new goals and level up their status when achieving their personal goals, during which users experience self-progress. Together, these three dimensions create the overall extent to which a user feels AE while interacting with a gamified IS. Therefore, the absence of any one of these three dimensions will deflate, although not completely eliminate, the overall extent of AE a user feels. Some additional general assumptions regarding this definition of AE should be stated. First, AE is not an enduring personality trait generalizable across situations, but rather a set of cognitions shaped by the technological features of the IS [59]. Thus, AE reflects different degrees of the users’ perceptions of themselves in relation to their use of an IS. Second, AE is a continuous—rather than dichotomous—variable, which emerges more or less while using an IS. Third, AE is not a global construct that is generalizable across different types of IS. Instead, it is specific to particular types of IS. This is because AE typically emerges when people are involved with volitional and self-determining activities [21, 38]. In this sense, we posit that AE might be more salient in the context of gamification in which employees use an IS voluntarily to perform task-related activities for self-fulfillment, rather than in the context where employees’ IS use is mandated to enhance the productivity of their work toward task completion. The idea of gamification seeks to increase self-awareness and behavioral change, enabling employees to monitor and track their activities on a real-time basis. While many enterprise technologies have been deployed to inform employees about their company, a gamified IS provides employees with information about themselves, which enables them to explore their own behaviors and experience more meaningful engagement with the IS.

## Antecedents of User Engagement

One of the main purposes of gamification is to make an activity more engaging [77]. To identify the antecedents of AE and FE in the context of gamification, we employ the theory of affordance, which provides an analytical link between technological features and user experiences [85]. An affordance is defined as a combination of actual and perceived properties of a thing, primarily those that determine how the thing can be used [68]. The central tenet of affordance theory is that technological capabilities do not exist in a specific technological function or feature, but rather exist as part of the relationship between a user and technological artifacts in a specific situation [48]. That is, the usability of a technological feature is determined by what a technology affords and whether the affordances fit the user’s goals and allow the relevant actions to be performed [68]. It is important therefore to assess users’ perceived affordances, so as to diagnose whether an IS has the appropriate functions for users to perform their desired actions and to achieve their goals [62]. In this sense, researchers argue that perceived affordances actually determine user engagement [30].

Actions possible within a gamified IS include physical interaction with game features such as points, levels, badges, trophies, and leaderboards. According to affordance theory, different users can use a technological feature of an IS in different ways; an individual chooses the features to achieve the same objective from different possibilities. For example, points are among the most commonly used technological features in gamification and granular units of measurement that the system employs to keep track of users’ actions in relation to specific targets. While one person may use the points feature to obtain more rewards, someone else may use the same feature to track personal performance or to compare the personal performance with that of others. Likewise, levels are used as a game feature in gamification, but how levels are perceived depends on the individual users. One person may view the levels as a challenge to achieve increasingly difficult tasks, thereby stimulating the person’s desire to increase their status. In contrast, others may consider levels as a kind of reward for their activities. Digital badges and trophies are a symbolic representation of users’ achievement, which enable some users to feel a sense of achievement and progress; they allow other users to feel that they confer on them a unique identity by showing their expertise or excellence in specific fields. While leaderboards are used to illustrate the results of games involving points and badges to a group as a whole, they also enhance the social aspects of IS. Leaderboards display participants’ names on a list, with the individuals typically ranked in descending order (i.e., with the highest number of points at the top). Some users may view leaderboards as an opportunity to compete with others, whereas others may feel that leaderboards facilitate goal setting or progress tracking. These game elements provide certain affordances within a gamified IS. The affordance lens is relevant to the present study because it can shed light on why technological features (game elements in this study) do not create the same effects in all gamified IS.

A literature review identified the following four gamification affordances commonly implemented in an IS that uses game elements: rewards, status, competition, and self-expression. Rewards refer to an affordance that enables users to perceive that the gamified IS affords an opportunity to receive rewards as a payoff when they complete predesigned tasks [7, 30]. Status is an affordance that enables users to improve their standing by achieving predefined goals or reaching milestones within a gamified IS [78]. The users’ status increases in accordance with the number of levels passed. Competition is an affordance that enables users to compare their performances with those of others [77, 78]. Self-expression is an affordance that enables users to create unique identities within a gamified IS [30, 80]. Badges or trophies signify users’ expertise or personal characteristics and thus enable users to express their self that is distinct from that of others.

## User Engagement and Continuance Intention to Use

Since Davis et al. [15] highlighted the importance of users’ hedonic experiences in ensuring IS continuance in the workplace, the concept of FE has been widely adopted in the IS literature by researchers on the basis of the assumption that the experiences of total immersion and absorption with an activity engender heightened enjoyment, which in turn leads to users’ continuance intention [1, 17]. The theoretical and practical implications of understanding the effect of FE on IS continuance have been promising in the IS field [57]. Drawing on the flow theory [13], previous studies have argued that FE reflects a state in which users are deeply involved and engaged with an IS; furthermore, these studies have provided evidence that FE is essential for repeated use [23], intention to use [44, 86], and continuance intention [17, 58]. In this line, gamification researchers suggest that FE is an important psychological state in which a user is deeply engaged with a gamified IS, which increases a user’s continuance intention [29, 32]. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 1: FE will positively influence continuance intention to use.

Researchers argue that users feel they are meaningfully engaged with an IS when they explore and reflect on the information about themselves while using the IS [49]. Given that gamified IS enable users to track and reflect on their records and constantly monitor their performance, users are able to derive a meaning of their activities and experience a sense of self-progress and self-growth [73]. According to the theory of AE [21], people continuously engage with an activity or object when they enter a state of AE because they feel their needs for self-progress or self-growth are fulfilled [38]. In this sense, Nardi [65] argues that AE is a core driver to make users continuously engage with a technology. Based on the discussion above, we infer that AE is a valid antecedent of continuance intention to use a gamified IS, and we formulate the following hypothesis:

## Gamification Affordances and User Engagement

Researchers suggest that successfully conveying affordances (the possibilities for action offered by a technology) induced by game elements is necessary for designing an engaging IS [85]. Therefore, the extent to which users perceive of a certain affordance needs to be considered when trying to evaluate and improve their engagement with an IS [61]. Through an extensive literature review, we identified four gamification affordances (rewards, status, competition, and self-expression) that may elevate user engagement in the context of gamification [7, 30, 77, 78].

The rewards affordance refers to the extent to which a user perceives that the gamified IS affords an opportunity to receive rewards as a payoff when they complete predesigned tasks. A greater rewards affordance can be perceived by users when a point system of a gamified IS provides granular and timely feedback [7, 72]. Obtaining short-term rewards leads to dynamic interactivity among users within a gamified IS, because—as the theory of FE suggests—prompt feedback and clear goals enable users to focus on the essentials of an activity [72], leading to immersion in it; thus, people often experience FE while using an IS [25]. In other words, the greater a user perceives the rewards affordance within a gamified IS, the deeper the engagement he or she will experience. Therefore, we state the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 3a: Rewards affordance will positively influence FE.

A greater rewards affordance can be achieved when a gamified IS provides users with a sense of meaning regarding the rewards users attain within a gamified system [66]. A well-designed reward structure of a gamified IS enables users to become aware of their performance and thus provides them with a greater sense of self [79]. It has been found that users who obtained rewards upon reaching milestones experienced a continued sense of accomplishment and higher self-worth, leading to a positive state of mind [16, 36]. That is, beyond offering points, levels, and badges as an immediate payoff for users’ activities, when users understand why they receive a certain amount of points or why they achieve badges or trophies within a gamified IS, they perceive their interaction with the IS to be meaningful [79]. The greater a user perceives the rewards affordance, the more meaningful engagement he or she will experience. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 3b: Rewards affordance will positively influence AE.

The status affordance refers to the extent to which a gamified IS enables users to increase their levels by achieving predefined goals or achieving milestones. A user perceives a greater status affordance when a well-structured tiered levels of the gamified IS can enhance gaming aspects so that users wish to challenge themselves by reaching higher levels [7]. A greater status affordance can be achieved when people believe that their skills are adequate to meet the challenges. If a gamified IS is designed in a manner in which each user can customize tasks and adjust his or her skills to the levels of the given challenges, users perceive greater status affordance.

Otherwise, users easily become bored with activities that do not require new challenges [72]. In this sense, Santhanam et al. [77] argue that different levels of challenges are critical to incurring deep engagement. Accordingly, the extent to which a user perceives the status affordance is an important antecedent of FE. We therefore propose the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 4a: Status affordance will positively influence FE.

A greater status affordance can be achieved when users perceive that they can effectively track their performance and upgrade their levels when they reach milestones. Rapp and Cena [73] argue that when policies and rules for upgrading users’ levels are designed in light of the magnitude and difficulty levels of tasks, users perceive a greater status affordance within a gamified IS. Accordingly, status affordance encourages users to set new goals and encourages them to challenge harder tasks through which users feels a sense of self-progress [80]. The theory of AE suggests that a strong sense of self-progress that balances the self and the object is essential to AE [6, 38]. Accordingly, the greater the status affordance, the more meaningful the engagement will be. We thus hypothesize:

## Hypothesis 4b: Status affordance will positively influence AE.

Competition affordance refers to the extent to which a gamified IS enables users to compare their performance with that of others and to strive to surpass others’ performance. Leaderboards are the main mechanisms that afford users opportunities for competition, which drives users to challenge each other to achieve the highest score in activities [19]. While points, levels, badges, and trophies induce competition among participants, leaderboards more directly encourage competition by displaying results and celebrating winners [82]. In the gaming literature, competition is considered to be one of the most important characteristics of gaming that leads to FE [53], as the suspense of not knowing the final outcome and the challenge posed by competitors will keep the players immersed [77]. In this study, we also expect that the sense of competition allows users to experience FE within a gamified IS. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 5a: Competition affordance will positively influence FE

Studies have suggested that well-designed competition motivates individuals to improve their performance, increasing the feeling of competence in a gamified context [53, 77, 79] because competition allows users to interact and develop their own strategies without external control [75]. Although competitions might have a negative effect on users’ psychological states when they are excessive or poorly designed without considering users’ characteristics [45], the empirical evidence from gamification research shows that in a gamified context in which participants are resilient to the experience of failure, competition supports their sense of self-efficacy by stimulating them to achieve high scores [80]. Nardi [65] argues that competition in a gameful context enables people to learn from themselves by comparing performances with peers and come to understand the meaning of competition, which strengthens their personal connection with the activities. Therefore, the greater a user perceives a competition affordance within a gamified IS, the more meaningful engagement he or she will experience. Accordingly, we propose:

Hypothesis 5b: Competition affordance will positively influence AE

Self-expression affordance refers to the extent to which individuals create unique self-identities [14]. Game elements, such as points, levels, badges, and trophies, used in a gamified IS afford users opportunities to develop their own identities by allowing them to be distinguished from others [7]. In particular, badges or trophies often serve as rich focal points for identity expression, demonstrating group affiliations or stimulating a user’s identity. Research suggests that the ability to express a unique self in an online environment increases the levels of perceived copresence with other participants, which leads to an immersed focus on the given tasks and heightened enjoyment while using an IS [44]. Thus we propose:

Hypothesis 6a: Self-expression affordance will positively influence FE.

Self-expression in the workplace provides employees with a mechanism to shape their own identities and express who they are and what they know [63]. Within a gamified IS, providing users with opportunities to express self-identity through their achievement (e.g., badges or trophies) increases their sense of competence, agency, control, and awareness of personal identity [20], which leads to a greater AE [21]. Accordingly, the self-expression affordance can lead to a more meaningful engagement by allowing users to feel self-fulfillment and self-expansion. We thus offer the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6b: Self-expression affordance will positively influence AE.

## Research Methods

## Data Collection

The model was tested using data collected from a survey of 178 employees of a global consulting company that applied gamification ideas in an IS. The gamified IS we examined is based on Jive Software’s enterprise applications. Jive was recognized as a Leader in the 2015 Gartner Magic Quadrant, positioning well in its market segment. The system was introduced in the company in 2012 for the purpose of enhancing employees’ knowledge sharing and collaboration. The gamified IS incorporates several game elements, such as points, levels, badges, trophies, and leaderboards. Employees earn points and have levels according to their activities (e.g., creating new documents, correctly answering discussion questions, writing new blogposts, completing project tasks). The gamified IS also incorporates leaderboards to make users’ activities visible and spur competition. The system use is voluntary, not mandatory.

We obtained e-mail addresses of 300 employees who have used the gamified IS by contacting several company executives and soliciting their participation in this study. An online questionnaire was created, and a link e-mailed to the identified employees, who were informed that their involvement in the study was voluntary and anonymous. The survey was ended after 200 valid responses were gathered. Excluding responses with missing values left 178 valid questionnaires. The respondents’ demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Early and late respondents were compared to test for possible nonresponse bias. No statistical differences were found between the groups, indicating that the problem of nonresponse bias is not detected in the study.

## Measures

AE is a relatively new concept without readily available measures. To develop measures of AE, the approach proposed by Hinkin [35] was used. First, based on earlier conceptualizations [4, 5, 6, 21, 38, 59], three reflective measures of AE were identified: self-expansion, meaning, and active discovery. The items for self-expansion were drawn from the Individual Self-Expansion Questionnaire [58], while the items for meaning and active discovery were developed based on the conceptualization of AE (see Table 1), resulting in an initial pool of nine items intended for the three measures of AE. Five experienced researchers were invited to evaluate the content validity of each item with respect to our conceptual definitions of the three dimensions of AE; they were asked to classify the items into the corresponding dimensions of AE. Cohen’s kappa and item placement ratio were assessed to test the validity of the scale. The kappa values for all items exceeded 0.65, a value typically considered as threshold for validity [83]. Prior to the main study, the measurement items were further refined, and their validity pilot tested with 60 employees of a partner company. The 60 participants in the pilot, all users of a gamified IS, had not been involved in the previous stages of scale development, review, and refinement. The data from the pilot study were not included in the final analysis. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for assessing the validity and reliability of the scale.

## Measurement Model

The measurement model was tested by assessing both the convergent and discriminant validity. Three criteria were used to determine convergent validity [22, 26]: (1) Composite reliability (CR) should be at least 0.70; (2) the average variance extracted (AVE) should be at least 0.50; and (3) all item loadings should be greater than 0.70 and statistically significant (i.e., more than twice the standard error). Because we view AE and FE as superordinate, second-order constructs composed of multiple reflective, first-order dimensions, we assessed the reliability and validity of the reflective measures (four dimensions of FE and three dimensions of FE). The results show that all items passed the reliability and validity tests. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, all the criteria for convergent validity were met, with CR values ranging from 0.884 to 0.923, AVE values of 0.667–0.783, and item loadings being higher than 0.70.

To assess discriminant validity, the AVE value was examined. For satisfactory discriminant validity, the AVE of a construct should be greater than the variance shared between the construct and other constructs in the model. For each construct, the square root of its AVE has to exceed its correlation with every other construct [22]. The results indicate that the AVE of each construct is larger than its correlation with all the other constructs, confirming their discriminant validity (see Tables 3 and 4). To ensure that multicollinearity did not pose a problem, the index of variance inflation factor (VIF) was checked. VIF scores ranged from 1.00 to 2.473, and were therefore well below the recommended threshold value of 10 [33]. Thus, the results indicate that multicollinearity was unlikely to be a serious issue in this study.

Harman’s one-factor analysis was used to check if the variance found in the data was a result of using a common source in data collection. When a single factor emerges from the factor analysis or one general factor accounts for the majority of the covariance in the independent and criterion variables [71], common method variance may pose some threat to the quality of the data set. All the measures used in this study were subjected to a principal component analysis. We found that the largest single component accounted for 38 percent of the variance in the data. In other words, no single factor dominates the variance explained. We further tested the common method variance by adding a common method factor in the research model and calculated the variance of each indicator using its principal construct and method factor [50]. The average variance of the indicators explained by the construct was 0.832, and the average variance explained by the method was 0.024. ## Structural Model

Figure 2 presents the results of the structural model. The model explained around 48 percent of the variance in continuance intention to use. As hypothesized, the results showed that both AE and FE were positively associated with continuance intention to use a gamified IS. Notably, the results indicated that AE was more strongly associated with continuance intention to use than FE (AE: β = 0.557, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; FE: $\beta = 0 . 1 5 9 , \ p \ < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The results also revealed that two variables, status and competition affordances, were positively associated with FE $( \beta \ = \ 0 . 3 3 8 , \ p \ <$ 0.001; $\beta = 0 . 5 7 0$ $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The same two variables, status and competition affordances, were also positively associated with AE, as was self-expression (β = 0.326, p < 0.01; β = 0.252, p < 0.05; β = 0.349, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In contrast, rewards affordance did not impact either FE or AE. Taken together, the results supported all hypotheses except H3a, H3b, and H6a.

To test for mediating effects of FE and AE in the research model, we followed the approach of Baron and Kenny [3]. This involved three main steps. First, we tested whether the independent variables were correlated with the mediators (FE and AE). Second, we verified whether the independent variables were correlated with the dependent variable. Finally, we determined whether the independent variables affected the dependent variable when the mediator was controlled. Full mediation occurs when the independent variables no longer exert a significant effect when the mediators are included. Partial mediation occurs when the independent variables continue to exert a significant but diminished effect.

Step 1 showed that the status and competition affordances were significant predictors of FE, and that the status, competition, and self-expression affordances were significant predictors of AE (see Table 5). Step 2 revealed that two of the gamification affordances (status and self-expression) significantly influenced continuance intention to use. Step 3 of the analysis uncovered that when we controlled the mediator FE by entering it into the regression, status had a significant effect on continuance intention to use. The magnitude of status affordance on continuance intention to use was less in the third regression than in the second, indicating that the effect of status affordance on continuance intention to use was partially mediated by FE. In contrast, when we controlled AE in the third regression, the results showed that the effect of status affordance on continuance intention to use was significant, whereas the effect of self-expression affordance on continuance intention was not significant, indicating that status was partially mediated by AE and that self-expression affordance was fully mediated by AE. The revised model is presented in Appendix C (Figure C1).

## Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine how a gamified IS engages users and keeps them continually using the system. Thus, we elaborated on the concept of user engagement by distinguishing deep engagement from meaningful engagement.

Motivated by the need to conceptualize meaningful engagement, we examined (1) the nature of AE, (2) the factors influencing AE, and (3) its relationship with FE, and (4) the different roles of AE and FE in explaining an individual’s continuance intention to use a gamified IS. In this section, we discuss our findings in relation to these inquiry objectives.

First, drawing on the theory of AE [21], we introduced the concept of AE and empirically showed that this newly introduced concept is manifested by three subdimensions (self-expansion, meaning, and active discovery). Following a rigorous approach, we developed a nine-item scale for AE. The results showed that the three subdimensions of AE had convergent and discriminant validity and indicated that AE has sufficient psychometric properties that are distinct from FE.

Second, to understand how AE arises in the context of gamification, we built on the theory of affordance and tested how four gamification affordances—rewards, status, competition, and self-expression—influenced AE. We found that status, competition, and self-expression affordances positively influence AE, accounting for 52 percent of its variance, whereas rewards affordances do not significantly influence AE. The results indicate that designing proper gamification affordances significantly enhances AE. In addition, the results also demonstrate that enterprise gamification is not all about rewards, but that it should be viewed as a means to reflect on user performance, progress tracking, and expression of self-identity.

Third, we found that, while competition and status affordances positively influence both FE and AE, the magnitude of their influence is stronger for FE than for AE. We speculate that employees who use game elements to enhance their performance and status are more likely to feel FE because immediate feedback and visibility of outcomes provided by points, levels, badges, and leaderboards keep users focused and tactically immersed without necessarily providing a strong sense of meaning. The results also suggest that self-expression is the strongest force to incur AE within a gamified IS. In other words, people who use a gamified IS to express themselves are more likely to have an AE. Contrary to expectations, rewards did not influence FE or AE. Although users may recognize that the gamified IS offers rewards, they apparently are not engaged with the system because of the rewards. The results imply that the provision of game elements (e.g., points, levels, and badges) simply as payoffs for particular actions does not foster user engagement with a gamified IS.

Finally, the results show that both AE and FE are important in explaining the continuance intention to use, with AE having a stronger influence than FE. Users’ sense of meaningful engagement with the gamified IS, which comes with AE, appears to be more persistently felt than the sense of flow arising during the experience. This result is noteworthy because FE has been regarded as a promising concept for explaining continued IS use across various types of IS [10, 53]. A post hoc analysis revealed the relevance of that earlier insight, indicating that FE has a strong influence on continuance intention to use in the absence of AE (see Figure B1 in Appendix B). ## Implications for Research

This study provides several important key contributions to academia. First, the critical research gap we sought to address in this present study of gamification was that the very nature of meaningful engagement was not clearly understood and, thus, its role in explaining continuance intention could not be modeled. To address this, we explicitly positioned AE as a central concept in the study of gamification; we demonstrated how AE and FE differ in influencing individuals’ continuance intention. While FE represents an important holistic aspect of user experience and is a critical predictor of IS continuance [57], our work shows that AE explains additional variance in continuance intention. Omitting AE may produce potential biases in research toward user engagement, and appreciating the role of AE can further our understanding of how a gamified IS successfully engages users and keeps them using the system.

As a research context, gamification in the workplace presents a unique sociotechnical environment where game elements are incorporated into an existing IS to enhance user engagement and to keep them using the IS and where users are prone to discontinue the use of the IS as the novelty of game elements diminishes over time. The present study demonstrates that fostering AE is essential to meaningful engagement, which goes beyond merely providing hedonic enjoyment derived from deep engagement.

This study confirmed the applicability of the theory of AE in IS research to explain users’ psychological and behavioral reactions to the technological artifacts of an IS. Although the theory of AE highlights the meaningful engagement with an object (i.e., artwork, technology, or an activity), the user experience from the aesthetic perspective has been ignored in IS research. Our research breaks new ground in the understanding of user engagement from an AE perspective, which differs from prior studies that limited views to the visual appeal or stimuli of an IS. This study represents one of the first attempts to systematically examine the role of AE in the gamification context and incorporate the construct, AE, into the IS continuance model. By empirically showing that AE can serve as a reliable theoretical concept to explain continuance intention, this study complements existing concepts aimed at facilitating IS continuance.

Finally, the proposed model contributes to creating theory-based knowledge for designing an engaging IS. The model, which links gamification affordances and user engagement (FE and AE), serves as a theoretical platform to examine, verify, and advance understanding of how to design an engaging IS. Our work indicates that both AE and FE seem to be elevated by the creation of desirable affordances and the level of user awareness of these affordances. Our measurements of gamification affordances can be used to assess and quantify how users react to these features differently, thereby determining how to foster desirable affordances that promote sustainable use.

## Implications for Practice

This study provides practical implications for system designers and managers who seek new ways to promote user engagement with an IS through gamification. It is noteworthy that AE plays a more critical role than FE in predicting continuance intention to use. According to the IS literature, the positive effects of hedonic elements on technology usage tend to decrease because of a decline over time in the users’ sense of novelty and curiosity [56]. We conjecture that a gamified IS may initially attract users by increasing the depth of engagement, but that it may not ensure that the users continually use the system unless they feel that they are meaningfully engaged by the system. We do not mean that FE can be ignored when designing a gamified IS; we argue that AE is a complementary force with FE for increasing users’ continuance intention. Therefore, we highlight that a gamified IS should not be limited to increasing FE; it should also create AE in a way that helps users to fulfill their needs for sense of meaning, self-expansion, and active discovery.

Our work also helps IS developers to understand how FE and AE can be enhanced by gamification affordances. We found that the status and competition affordances account for more than 65 percent of the variance in FE, whereas the status, competition, and self-expression affordances account for 52 percent of the variance in AE. These findings indicate that the gamified IS we examined was designed to increase FE more than AE. Considering the critical role of AE in explaining continuance intention, we suggest that IT designers and developers consider incorporating affordances in gamified ISs that can increase AE. According to the findings of this study, self-expression seems to be the strongest antecedent of AE among the four gamification affordances. Self-expression is a way of constructing self-identity. Managerial interventions through promotion or training that help users better understand the affordances of gamification may be useful for employees to experience aesthetic pleasure. Tools or functions that support tracking performance, visualizing expertise, and competing with others may lead to greater AE.

The finding that the competition affordance positively influences both FE and AE indicates that competition is an important mechanism that facilitates users’ deep and meaningful engagement within a gamified IS. Greater competition affordance does not mean incorporating leaderboards that let users view the performance of others; instead it means how to develop competition structures that can encourage friendly competition and motivate performance. It has been reported that excessive competitiveness discourages individuals who are less competitive [45]. Possible methods for discouraging these countereffects include diversifying events to include individual and group activities so that users can choose something that interests them and try diverse competitions. Santhanam et al. [77] suggest that a well-designed competition structure that considers user characteristics leads to positive user experiences within a gamified IS. Our findings suggest that a gamified IS should be designed in such a way that users are willing to participate in competitions, and should encourage them to view the competition as a tool for measuring their progress and for displaying and celebrating those who make significant progress.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our current study’s contributions need to be evaluated in the light of its limitations, which point to future research efforts. Although we believe our study contributes to a better understanding of user engagement by validating AE as a construct to predict continuance intention, the magnitude of AE’s effect may vary depending on the purposes of gamified IS. We call on researchers to examine our model in different gamification contexts in order to ensure the generalizability of the proposed model. While we proposed AE as a key construct to explain continuance intention to use a gamified IS, AE can occur in the use of any other type of IS, such as those designed for productivity. Further research might benefit from examining the role of AE in different types of IS use.

Second, our data were collected from a single source and all research constructs were measured by respondent perceptions. To alleviate the concern of common method bias (CMB), objective data on users’ actual behavior would provide insights into how FE and AE affect actual IS usage behavior over time. Although our data analyses revealed that CMB was not a serious concern for the current study, we encourage future studies to use objective data to better address the CMB issue.

## Conclusion

Recent IS research has highlighted the role of FE in the user experience of IS, the belief that the hedonic value of an IS (e.g., enjoyment, fun, and playfulness) is key to sustaining user engagement. The framework developed in this study recognizes that gamified IS provide users with unique sociotechnological environments that feature both FE and AE. We conclude from our empirical tests of the framework, that although FE and AE are complementary forces within a gamified IS, employees primarily contribute to and keep using a gamified IS because of the AE. Hence, we suggest that affordances that can facilitate AE should be designed and implemented to harness the benefits of the gamified IS in the workplace.
