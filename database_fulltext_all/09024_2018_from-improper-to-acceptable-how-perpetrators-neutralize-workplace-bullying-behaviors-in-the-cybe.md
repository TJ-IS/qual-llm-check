2. Publicity negatively moderates the relationship between neutralization and cyberbullying intention.

3. Invisibility positively moderates the relationship between neutralization and cyberbullying intention.

4. Anonymity positively moderates the relationship between neutralization and cyberbullying intention.

## Abstract

Workplace cyberbullying is a growing phenomenon with important consequences ranging from employee mental strain and low job satisfaction to hostile organizational climates. In this paper, we examine how workplace cyberbullies justify their bullying behaviors and how cyber communication features influence workplace cyberbullying behaviors. Drawing from the neutralization theory and the social presence theory, we develop a research model that incorporates cyber communication features that explain how perpetrators legitimize their workplace cyberbullying acts. To empirically test our model, we collected 473 responses from employees across a variety of job positions, companies, and industries. Our results highlight three denial neutralization techniques used by perpetrators to justify their workplace cyberbullying behaviors and demonstrate the moderating effects of cyber communication features.

Keywords: Workplace Cyberbullying, Neutralization Theory, Social Presence Theory, Cyber Communication Features

## 1. Introduction

In 1995, Tammy Blakey, the first female pilot to fly the Airbus A300, filed a lawsuit after finding multiple derogatory and contemptuous comments criticizing her professional competence in the company’s electronic bulletin board. In 2012, Ralph Espinoza won \$820,000 from a jury because of his co-workers’ blogged insults about his disfigured hand. Kevin Morrissey, a 52- year-old managing editor of the Virginia Quarterly Review who purportedly suffered depression stemming from excessive workplace cyberbullying, committed suicide on July 30, 2010. The widespread use of mobile technology and social media networks has provided various new avenues for workplace cyberbullying [1]. The recent rise of IT consumerization has further exacerbated cyberbullying behaviors in the workplace in that employees are familiar with the devices and applications they bring to work and may bring with them bad habits that they have seen or witnessed in their private use [2]. Statistics from a recent study on workplace cyberbullying provided by Economic and Social Research Council show that nearly 80% of respondents in the study had experienced at least one cyberbullying act in the previous six months and 70% had been cyberbullied on at least a weekly basis – a ratio similar to that of traditional bullying [3].

As a universal phenomenon, cyberbullying brings various negative consequences on both targets and witnesses. In terms of workplace cyberbullying, such negative impacts include mental strain of the employee and low job satisfaction for targets and witnesses and high turnover rates for the organization [4]. These serious consequences make workplace cyberbullying a challenge to organizations that wish to maintain quality work environments. To address the issue of workplace cyberbullying, practitioners have applied various strategies such as establishing a clear and well communicated anticyberbullying policy, raising awareness of the negative influences of workplace cyberbullying during organizational training, and promoting a workplace culture where any form of cyberbullying act is not tolerated [5,6]. Despite such strategies, the occurrence of workplace cyberbullying has been rising as reported in both public reports and previous literature [7,8]. This increase suggests that either workplace cyberbullies are unaware of their cyberbullying behavior, or, more plausibly, that they are aware of their cyberbullying behavior but nevertheless believe the behavior is justified. The latter is referred to as neutralization.

Recent research on cyberbullying of juveniles and college students proposes that cyberbullies recognize the immorality of their deviant behaviors but use certain neutralization techniques to justify those behaviors [9,10]. Yet these findings are not directly transferable to the context of workplace cyberbullying because the workplace comprises a structure, control, punishment, and reward system substantially different from those of the social settings within which most studies

#

on cyberbullying of the juveniles and young adults have taken place. To date, little is known about how employees who engage in cyberbullying justify these behaviors in the workplace. Workplace cyberbullying encompasses a variety of avenues (i.e., email, text message, and social network) and organizational factors (i.e., culture, environment, and norms) that might influence how perpetrators justify their bullying behaviors. Hence, to advance both practice and research in the field of workplace cyberbullying, this study first aims to explore the question: How do workplace cyberbullies justify their bullying behaviors?

While workplace cyberbullying may differ from the cyberbullying of juveniles and college students, workplace cyberbullying also differs from traditional workplace bullying. Several aspects of the cyberbullying context distinguish it from traditional bullying. In a traditional faceto-face bullying context, the perpetrator sees the injury or humiliation that he/she inflicts as it occurs. Workplace cyberbullies can inflict the damage without being copresent [11,12]. In addition, unlike the face-to-face context, the cyber context provides avenues that allow cyberbullies to conceal their real identities [13]. Moreover, some of the cyberbullying behaviors inappropriate picture of someone in enterprise social media (ESM) or spreading rumors about some colleague in the company forum [14]. The potential for lack of copresence, identity concealment, and broad reach indicates that cyber features such as invisibility, asynchrony, anonymity, and publicity might foster different bullying behaviors in the cyber context than those found in the traditional context. More importantly, cyber features might play important roles in interacting with bullies’ justification processes because these features may psychologically distance perpetrators from the victims and injury inflicted [15].

Although considerable research has been conducted in the field of workplace bullying [16], research on workplace cyberbullying is still nascent [4]. Of the few studies that examine workplace cyberbullying, little attention has been paid to the role of cyber features in influencing the bullying behaviors. There is a need for research on workplace cyberbullying that specifically accounts for aspects of the cyber context that are not present in traditional workplace bullying. Such research will help advance theory in the area of cyberbullying and help inform organizational policies surrounding the use of various communication and information systems (IS). Therefore, our second research question is: How do cyber communication features influence workplace cyberbullying behaviors?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We depict our theoretical background and hypotheses in the next section. Then, we detail the methodology and present the results of data analysis. Finally, we discuss the implications, limitations, and recommendations for future research.

## 2. Theoretical development

In this section, we first define workplace cyberbullying. Following that, we develop our research model and associated hypotheses.

## 2.1. Definition of workplace cyberbullying

Perhaps because of the complexity of this phenomenon or its relative newness, there is no agreed-upon definition of workplace cyberbullying in the literature. We base our definition of workplace cyberbullying on the definitions of general cyberbullying and workplace bullying. Workplace bullying is defined as a situation in which an employee is exposed to negative treatment from supervisors (downward bullying), colleagues (horizontal bullying), or

#

subordinates (upward bullying) [16, 17, 18]. Cyberbullying is defined as “deliberate and hostile behavior intended to harm people using the Internet by leveraging the imbalance of power between bullies and victims” [9, p. 964]. Drawing from these definitions, we define workplace cyberbullying as the exposure of an employee to negative treatment from supervisors, colleagues, or subordinates by electronic forms of contact (i.e., text messaging, email, and ESM) in a situation in which the perpetrator has more power than the target in either formal or informal ways [9, 16–20]. Examples of workplace cyberbullying include insulting/offensive remarks through text message, repeated criticisms of one’s errors and mistakes at work through email, and gossip and rumors about someone being spread through ESM.

## 2.2.1. Neutralization theory

The neutralization theory was originally developed to trace the roots of juvenile delinquency [22]. It was subsequently adopted to predict various criminal behaviors and rule-breaking actions. The theory opines that, in general, individuals who break a law believe in the values held by lawabiding society [25]. However, the theory also proposes the existence of a delinquent sub-system within a law-abiding society composed of values that stand for the inversion of values held by the general community [26]. It is the existence of this subsystem that allows lawbreakers to define their immoral behaviors as “right” and to assuage their guilt and shame even before engaging in the criminal behavior [27].

We choose the neutralization theory as our theoretical base for several reasons. First, research on cyberbullying of juveniles and young adults suggests that bullies may adopt certain

#

neutralization techniques to justify their deviant behaviors, indicating that the neutralization theory might also be effective in predicting how cyberbullies in the workplace justify their bulling behaviors [9, 10, 28, 29]. Second, workplace cyberbullying provides a rich and complex context to advance the neutralization theory. Scholars call for research that explores the conditions affecting the relationship between neutralization and deviance [30, 31]. However, in previous studies that adopted the neutralization theory to predict deviant acts, little attention has been paid to such conditional factors. Compared to workplace bullying, cyberbullying involves certain interactions between cyber features and neutralization techniques, enabling us to explore the conditioning effects between neutralizations and deviant acts. In addition, unlike cyberbullying of juveniles and young adults, workplace cyberbullying encompasses various organizational factors and communication avenues that might influence how bullies neutralize their deviant behaviors. Third, the neutralization theory is particularly useful for understanding a phenomenon in which individuals are aware of deviant behavior but nevertheless commit it. Given our interest in the workplace where employees are expected to recognize potential bullying behaviors and their potential negative consequences, the neutralization theory can provide a lens for understanding why employees justify or ignore such behaviors when committed either by themselves or others.

In their seminal article dating back to 1957, Skype and Matza explicated five neutralization techniques: denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, and appeal to higher loyalties [22]. Other scholars subsequently introduced seven other neutralization techniques: the metaphor of the ledger [32, 33], the defense of necessity [33], claims of individuality [34], claims of relative acceptability [34], claims of entitlement [35], claims of normality [36], and postponement [36]. Consistent with most studies adopting the neutralization theory, our study employs a subset of the above-mentioned neutralization

techniques [37]. Specifically, we adopt the following three traditional neutralization techniques:

denial of responsibility, denial of injury, and denial of the victim. The three techniques are

chosen because each of them is connected with an object (e.g., responsibility, injury, and victim)

that might be ignored [12] or amplified [14] in the cyber environment. Although some of the

other neutralization techniques (i.e., appeal to higher loyalties, the metaphor of the ledger, and

condemnation of the condemners) have been shown to be effective in explaining various

offline/online deviant behaviors in IS research (e.g., Ref. [27, 38, 39, 40]), we do not have a

theoretical reason to expect them to be useful in explaining how cyber communication features

affect workplace cyberbullying, a main focus of our study.

Research in the criminology field shows that the neutralization theory has been used in

explaining illicit acts such as poaching behaviors [41], tax evasion [42], and illegal drug use [25,

43]. In the area of IS, the neutralization theory has also been widely used to predict deviant acts.

Various neutralization techniques have been found to predict a range of deviant acts (see Table 1

in the Appendix for a summary of the literature). For example, researchers have found that denial

of responsibility had a direct effect on individuals’ computer abuse judgment and intention to

perpetrate computer abuse [44]; defense of necessity, denial of injury, and denial of

responsibility significantly influenced whistleblowing intentions [38]; and appeal to higher

loyalties and condemnation of the condemners were used to justify software piracy [45]. Other

studies on cyberloafing, and more generally, Internet abuses found that the metaphor of the

ledger invoked by employees’ perception of organizational justice legitimized their subsequent

cyberloafing acts [46] and that neutralization techniques explained employees’ Internet abuse

intentions [40]. A recent work had investigated cyberbullying on social networks and reported that neutralization techniques invoked by anonymity predicted adult cyberbullying frequency on social media [9] and that neutralization is positively associated with college students’ intention to cyberbully in Facebook [10]. Briefly, previous studies of the neutralization theory in IS research have offered empirical support for neutralization techniques as strong predictors for various we discuss the specific neutralization techniques examined in this study.

2.2.1.1. Denial of responsibility. According to the denial of responsibility neutralization technique, the delinquent justifies his action in question by perceiving himself as the victim of the environment [36]. The delinquent defines himself as a “billiard ball” helplessly propelled into an environment with factors such as high workloads or bad supervisors that force him to commit a crime [22]. In the case of organizations, organizational policy breakers may rationalize unethical behaviors by claiming that the company guidelines or policies are unclear or that the situation leaves them no choice [44, 47]. In the context of workplace cyberbullying, an organization’s climate may inadvertently foster cyberbullying through, for example, emphasizing individual productivity over collegiality, or performance at any cost [48]. In this situation, the bully may assuage anticipated guilt by shifting accountability for his negative behavior to his company.

2.2.1.2. Denial of injury. The denial of injury neutralization technique allows the delinquent to define his harmful acts as having little negative consequence [36, 40] or to brush away the negative consequences as something with which the victim should easily cope [22]. Thus, the delinquent admits his responsibility for the bad behavior but fails to acknowledge the connection between his own delinquent behavior and the possible damage done to the victim. Moreover, because the delinquent is capable of using the technology to perform potentially negative acts, he

#

may well assume that others should similarly be able to use the technology to defend themselves. This latter assumption is often false. Indeed, studies indicate that a digital divide separates technology users into different categories (i.e., experienced users/novices and digital natives/migrants) according to their self-efficacy beliefs and skills [49]. A potential cyberbully might claim that it is easy for experienced technology users to defend themselves online and, in the absence of vigorous defense, deny the injury inflicted. However, victims may not have the requisite skill to defend themselves from the cyberbully. In fact, research shows that employees being bullied a few times usually choose to cope passively, either by keeping silent or by distancing themselves from the work situation [48, 50]. Such responses may be counterproductive in that the perpetrators may deny the harm or injury caused by their bullying and continue to bully the same targets, convincing themselves that the targets are not being harmed by their behavior because the victims are not defending themselves.

2.2.1.3. Denial of victim. The denial of victim neutralization technique asserts that even if the delinquent takes responsibility for his harmful behavior and acknowledges the injury caused by such behavior, he nevertheless justifies his delinquency as retaliation upon a deserving target [22, 36]. By transforming a victim into a person deserving of injury, the delinquent minimizes his feeling of shame or guilt for his bad act. There may be several rationales for cyberbullies viewing the target as deserving of the cyberbullying. Some perpetrators may treat victims as deserving targets when victims disclose personal information on social network sites [51]. Other perpetrators may use cyberbullying as an act of revenge in an online or offline rivalry suffering from interpersonal conflict [48]. Employees with poor conflict management skills may escalate the conflict through cyberbullying [16] or use cyberbullying as retaliation in a conflict. In fact, 25 percent of those who engaged in cyberbullying reported that they did it as an act of revenge [52, 53]. In these cases, perpetrators view the target as someone who “had it coming” and thereby deny the victim.

On the basis of the theoretical lenses of the neutralization theory, we argue that employees are aware of the impropriety of cyberbullying behaviors at work. However, they may still conduct such behaviors after they use neutralization techniques (denial of responsibility, denial of injury, and denial of the victim) to minimize guilt and shame from those bullying behaviors. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H1. Neutralization of workplace cyberbullying (through denial of responsibility, denial of injury, and denial of the victim) is positively associated with the intention to cyberbully in the workplace.

## 2.2.2. Social presence theory and cyber features

Next, we consider differences between workplace cyberbullying and face-to-face bullying. There are considerable overlaps between the antecedents of cyberbullying and face-to-face bullying and between consequences to cyber victims and traditional victims [54, 55, 56], prompting some to argue that this overlap provides evidence that the media through which bullying behaviors take place are insignificant in influencing bullying incidents [55]. However, we suggest otherwise. Cyber phenomena such as reduced social cues [57], toxic disinhibitions [58], and depersonalization [59] are important features of electronic media that differentiate cyberbullying from conventional bullying acts. Research indicates that when social cues are strong, behaviors tend to be relatively others-focused and regulated. By contrast, when social cues are weak, behaviors tend to be relatively self-centered and unregulated [57]. In the context of workplace cyberbullying, communication media such as emails, text messages, and ESM might reduce perpetrators’ perceptions of social cues and amplify the online disinhibition effect [15]. Instead of examining the cyber media, we follow the convention in previous cyberbullying studies of examining cyber communication features (e.g., Ref. [14, 58, 60]) because it is the features of the media, rather than the media per se, that are expected to influence the cyberbullying behaviors [55].

Cyber communication features are embedded with a certain degree, or lack therefore, of social presence. Social presence is defined as “the moment-to-moment awareness of co-presence of a mediated body and the sense of accessibility of the other being’s psychological, emotional, and intentional states” [23, p. 14], and it features prominently in conceptions of communication media, such as the media richness theory [24, 61] and the media naturalness theory [62]. Social presence reflects the degree to which an individual is perceived as a real person in a computeral. [64] suggest that the social presence of a medium is associated with individuals’ sense of intimacy and immediacy when using the medium. While intimacy is a measurement of a communicator’s sense of physical distance between him and the object of his communication, immediacy reflects a communicator’s feeling of psychological distance between the two

The social presence theory has been widely used to examine various online communication phenomena such as collaborative learning in computer conferences [63], trust building in ecommerce environment [65], and social interaction within virtual environments [66]. In addition, a few studies have also applied the lens of the social presence theory to examine cyber deviance (for a summary, see Table 2 in the Appendix). For example, McLeod et al. [67] reported that the reduction of social presence stimulates antinormative behaviors in anonymous computer-

#

supported groups. Lapidot-Lefler and Barak [58] indicated that social presence implicit in cyber features such as anonymity and invisibility explained online flaming behaviors. Lea and Spears [68] found that computer-mediated communications low in social presence may cause deindividuation effects, which lead to uninhibited online behaviors. Lowry et al. [69] found that social presence awareness promoted by cyber features affected online social media deviance through accountability and deindividuation. Briefly, the above-mentioned studies on cyber communication suggest that social presence implicit in cyber features explains cyber deviance and hence might also be effective in predicting cyberbullying in the workplace.

We previously argued that the greater the degree of neutralization, the greater the cyberbullying. Nevertheless, not all individuals inclined to neutralization will ultimately engage in cyberbullying behavior. Despite neutralization, some individuals will refrain from cyberbullying behavior out of a concern of being observed. We thus suggest that the degree of social presence implicit in cyber features will moderate the association of neutralization with cyberbullying intention. Specifically, in a mediated communication, the degree of social presence is associated association, which may affect the way individuals interact through the medium [70]. When a perpetrator adopts the denial techniques to insulate himself from a feeling of shame or guilt, a high degree of social presence decreases his sense of psychological distance between himself and the victims [70], thereby weakening the effects of the neutralization techniques on bullying intentions. Hence, cyber communication features that amplify the psychological distance by lowering the degree of social presence should intensify the association of neutralization with the intention to cyberbully. As we aim to explore the interaction between cyber features and the neutralization process, we treat the social presence construct as embedded in the cyber communication features. It is thus an underlying construct that informs the relationships between the cyber communication features and the cyberbullying outcome.

Four cyber communication features in particular (invisibility, asynchrony, anonymity, and negatively, to individuals’ sense of social presence during electronic communications [63, 64, 71, 72, 73]. The present study explores perceptions of these cyber features as measures of the extent to which employees feel they are in an invisible, asynchronous, anonymous, or public environment when using certain electronic media. In the next section, we will discuss how each perception moderates the neutralization–intention relationship.

2.2.2.1. Invisibility. The invisibility of the cyber world inhibits the ability of individuals to read standard social cues such as facial expression, vocal tone, and body language [58], all of which have specific connotations in face-to-face interaction. The social presence theory associates a lack of social cues with a decreased degree of social presence in a communication medium [63]. In the context of workplace cyberbullying, employees who have a strong perception of being invisible may be unable to read social cues, or may feel free to ignore reading social cues, and may be able to hide their own social presence, thus distancing themselves from the victims [1, 15]. Consequently, when adopted, the denial neutralization techniques might be more likely to lead to workplace cyberbullying intention when employees have a strong perception of being invisible than in situations when such perception is weak. Formally stated:

H2a. Perceived invisibility positively moderates the effect of neutralization on intention to cyberbully in the workplace

##

2.2.2.2. Asynchrony. Asynchrony refers to a specific communication mode provided by media such as email and ESM whereby participants do not have to communicate at the same time [74]. Unlike bullying in a face-to-face environment where feedback is certain and immediate, in an asynchronous environment, there is delay in the feedback [75]. Communication scholars have argued that the asynchrony feature of cyber media may hide the nonverbal and vocal cues that convey social presence and block the immediacy behaviors that increase the degree of social presence [57, 64, 71]. Thus, the psychological distance between two communicating parties is greater in the case of asynchrony as in the case of synchrony. In the context of workplace cyberbullying, this psychological distance implicit in asynchrony enables perpetrators to ignore victims’ immediate reaction to a personal, aggressive, or hostile message [15]. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H2b. Perceived asynchrony positively moderates the effect of neutralization on intention to cyberbully in the workplace.

2.2.2.3. Anonymity. Anonymity is defined as the condition of being unidentified [58]. In the cyber world, people can hide or even alter their identities, allowing them to distance themselves physically and psychologically from their aggressive behaviors [76, 77]. For example, by using a contrived username or email address, people can separate their online deviant behaviors such as cyberbullying from their real-life identities [15]. Previous studies have shown that anonymity might lower the degree of social presence by restricting the exchange of social cues and reducing the immediacy of others in a communication setting [64, 73, 78]. In this study, we argue that, when utilizing the denial techniques to justify their workplace cyberbullying behaviors, employees with a high level of perceived anonymity are able to distance themselves from certain social cues that direct their attentions to the victims [79]. Hence, such a perception may result in an increased effect of the denial neutralization techniques on bullying intention. In other words, a high level of perceived anonymity strengthens the relationship between neutralization and cyberbullying intention in the workplace, as hypothesized:

H2c. Perceived anonymity positively moderates the effect of neutralization on intention to cyberbully in the workplace.

2.2.2.4. Publicity. Contrary to private communications between two parties, publicity characterizes a situation in which the exchange behavior is observed by a large audience [80]. With a large audience witnessing the mediated interaction between two parties, the witnesses’ responses toward the interaction (i.e., critical comments to the perpetrators, defensive acts for the victims) might reduce the psychological distance between the perpetrator and victim as perceived by the perpetrator [14, 72]. As ESM platforms have become more popular in organizations, cyberbullying through ESM has become a topic of concern for practitioners. One distinctive feature of ESM cyberbullying is that the bullying behaviors are public and can potentially reach a large audience [56, 81]. Hence, when utilizing the denial techniques to justify their workplace cyberbullying behaviors, workplace bullies who recognize the publicity of their cyberbullying behavior may realize that their bullying acts will attract negative reaction from the broader audience. This awareness of publicity would serve as a deterrent and therefore weaken the impact of the adopted neutralization techniques on intention to cyberbully in the workplace, as hypothesized:

H2d. Perceived publicity negatively moderates the effect of neutralization on intention to cyberbully in the workplace.

## 3. Research design

Generally, people tend to respond to a controversial topic in a socially acceptable manner, even if their responses are remote from their true feelings. This tendency leads to social desirability biases [82]. Social desirability biases may occur in measuring an individual’s intention to cyberbully in the workplace because a respondent may wish to hide his intention to conduct harmful behaviors and thus present himself in a socially acceptable manner. To avoid such biases, we used a scenario-based method [83] to measure our dependent variable (intention to cyberbully in the workplace).

## 3.1. Scenario-based method

A scenario-based method allows respondents to make decisions based on true-to-life vignettes that describe a fictitious character’s behaviors in hypothetical terms [83]. The respondents then are asked to report the likelihood that they would conduct a behavior same as that of the character in the vignette [84]. Scenario-based methods are a common means for controlling social desirability bias [39]. The respondent may feel comfortable reporting an unethical behavior similar to that of the scenario character in a specific context designed by investigators [44]. Furthermore, rather than measuring intention in general terms by using normal survey questions, a scenario-based method provides methodological advantage in that it distributes uniform vignettes across respondents and therefore avoids respondents having to imagine situations that are different from where the delinquent incidents actually happen [85]. In previous cyberbullying studies, the scenario-based method has been widely used to measure individuals’ behavioral intentions such as bystanders’ intervention intention, perpetrators’ cyberbullying intention, and victims’ response intention (e.g., Ref. [10, 86–90]).

Appendix Table 3 illustrates the three scenarios in the current study. Each scenario represents a typical type of workplace cyberbullying (Scenario 1 – horizontal bullying; scenario 2 – upward bullying; and scenario 3 – downward bullying). In addition, each scenario is designed with a typical workplace cyberbullying medium – email, text messages, and ESM. The three media are chosen according to the list of electronic forms of cyberbullying summarized by Smith et al. [20]. The three media are also reported as the most frequently used media in previous cyberbullying studies [5, 20].

Although we included four cyber communication features in our model, none of these cyber features can simultaneously be applied to all the mediums. For example, email bullying is generally not anonymous in the organizational context. In addition, the applicability of certain cyber communication features to the specific medium is largely relying on the specific context where the cyberbullying acts happen. For example, email bullying is generally not public unless the perpetrators copy the bullying message to a large audience. Therefore, as illustrated by the check marks in Table 1, in each scenario, we choose to test a subset of the four cyber communication features based on the corresponding medium and specific context associated with that scenario.

Previous literature also suggests that the situations in the scenarios must be realistic to the respondents [39]. Thus, we conducted three rounds of scenario validation. In the first round, we invited four Ph.D. students to join the instrument matter expert panel and asked them to compare the definition of workplace cyberbullying with the content of our scenarios. We also invited two employees with over 10 years of working experience to join the subject matter expert panel and asked them to judge the relevance of the scenarios to the realistic situation in the workplace. In the second round, we invited two faculty members and five Ph.D. students to form a combined expert panel to evaluate the scenarios. In the third round, we asked another two employees with over 20 years of working experience to join the subject matter expert panel and asked them to judge the relevance of the scenarios to the workplace cyberbullying. Revisions were made based on suggestions.

## 3.2. Instrumentation

Most of the construct items were measured on a 7-point Likert-type scale. The scales for the neutralization techniques were adopted from Siponen and Vance [39] and adapted to the workplace cyberbullying context. Neutralization was modeled as a formative second-order construct with denial of responsibility, denial of injury, and denial of victim being modeled as three first-order sub-dimensions. Although all these neutralization techniques are conceptually different, at a higher level, each reflects a distinct facet of the neutralization construct and can be used by the workplace cyberbullies to justify their harmful behaviors [91]. Thus, each first-order construct measures a different aspect of the unobservable higher level latent variable (neutralization) [92].

#

The measurement for anonymity was taken from Dinev et al. [93]. Items for invisibility and asynchrony were developed based on the definitions and descriptions from Suler [15]. We also developed items for publicity based on the descriptions from Smith and Slonje [56] and Sticca and Perren [14]. To ensure content validity, we invited three Ph.D. students to evaluate the face validity of each item. Revisions were made according to their recommendations. To access construct validity, we conducted two rounds of sorting with two different sets of judges [94]. Averaged scores for both raw agreement and Cohen’s Kappa were above 0.90.

Scenarios from previous studies, which have been widely replicated [95, 96], and studies on corporate policy violations [39, 97] generally ask the respondents to report their intention to conduct a behavior same as that of the scenario character through a single-item measure immediately following the vignette. However, the use of single-item measures has been criticized for mono-operationalization bias in the IS field [98, 99]. Using single-item measures may also lead to lower computable reliability because they do not allow for adjustment of measurement errors [100]. Therefore, we used two items to measure the dependent variable in the current study. Table 4 in the Appendix summarizes the items for the constructs.

## 4. Model analysis

As a Partial Least Square (PLS) Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) tool, SmartPLS [101] was used to analyze our research model and hypotheses. We chose a PLS-SEM tool to test our research model for the following reasons: (1) Neutralization is conceptualized as a multidimensional formative second-order construct which is difficult to identify when using covariance-based methods [102]; (2) PLS-SEM works well on predicting key target constructs and aiding theory development for research such as this study in which the phenomenon of interest is still in the exploratory stage; and (3) although we use a scenario-based approach to minimize social desirability biases, we still expect that our dependent variable (intention to cyberbullying in the workplace) involves some level of skewness. PLS-SEM can handle extremely non-normal data efficiently [100].

## 4.1. Measurement Model

A 5000 bootstrap resampling of the data was performed as recommended by Hair et al. [100]. Table 5 in the Appendix shows the internal consistency reliability and convergent validity of all the latent variables across our three surveys. The results indicate a high level of internal consistency reliability as evidenced by the constructs’ composite reliability that exceeds the threshold value of 0.70 [100]. Additionally, AVE values for all the constructs exceed 0.50. Thus, convergent validity is established.

Appendix Table 6 shows the loadings and cross-loadings of all the measurement items. Two items from asynchrony load had threshold values of 0.708 lower than those in scenario 1. However, the difference between the loadings and cross-loadings for those two items is more than 0.5, which exceeds the threshold value of 0.1 and demonstrates an order of magnitude larger than any other loading [103]. All the other items from the first-level constructs load highly on their respective construct and exceed the threshold values of 0.708, thus establishing the reliability. In addition, all the items load on their respective construct more highly than any other construct, providing evidence of discriminant validity. We also used Fornell–Larcker criterion to assess discriminant validity. As shown in Appendix Table 7, the elements bolded in the matrix elements in their corresponding row and column, supporting discriminant validity.

Neutralization is modeled as a formative second-order construct with the three neutralization techniques as first-order sub-dimensions. The correlations in Appendix Table 7 show that some of the lower order constructs of neutralization are positively correlated with each other, indicating some level of collinearity. Thus, a variance inflation test (VIF) was performed to check for lower order construct collinearity. The VIF statistics range from 1.233 to 2.740 and are below the recommended threshold value of 3.33 [104]. Thus, collinearity is not considered as a threat to the estimation of the constructs. In addition, Appendix Table 8 illustrates the outer weights and the out loadings of the lower order constructs on the higher order construct, indicating a high level of significance and relevance of the lower order formative constructs to the “Neutralization” construct.

Common method bias is a potential threat for research using self-reported data. Although we common method bias may still exist because we collected data for independent and dependent variables from the same data source [82]. To assess the effects of common method bias, we followed the Latent Common Method Variable (LCMV) approach recommended by Liang et al. [105]. In all the scenarios, the average percentages of indicator variance explained by the constructs in our model were more than 60%, while the average percentages of indicator variance attributed to LCMV were less than 1%. In addition, loadings of LCMV were insignificant in all the scenarios. The small explained variance and insignificant loadings of LCMY indicate that substantial common method bias is unlikely to be present in our study.

## 5. Discussion

The neutralization theory is widely used in the criminology field and has been used to explain illicit acts such as poaching behaviors [41], tax evasion [42], and illegal drug use [25, 43]. In the IS field, the theory has been employed to explain phenomena such as IS security policy violations [39], software piracy [45], employees’ whistleblowing intention [38], cyberstalking [27], and Internet abuse in the workplace [40]. Consistent with these previous studies, our results demonstrate that neutralization is also an effective predictor of workplace cyberbullying behavior. Modeling neutralization as a second-order formative constructs enables us not only to build a parsimonious model but also to discuss our nomological framework at the construct level as opposed to the individual item level. We empirically tested three neutralization techniques in the context of workplace cyberbullying. Our results indicate that perpetrators are likely to use the following neutralization techniques to justify their cyberbullying behaviors as right and acceptable: denial of injury, denial of responsibility, and denial of victim.

#

In addition, we conducted certain post hoc analysis to calculate the effect sizes of the path coefficients between neutralization and cyberbullying intention across the three scenarios. While the effect sizes of the relationship between neutralization and intention to cyberbully in horizontal bullying through ESM are similar to those in upward bulling through text message, the effect size in downward bullying through email is much higher. Three features of downward bullying might explain such a large effect size. First, downward bullying is normally more aggressive and straightforward toward victims than horizontal or upward bullying, leading to a higher degree of perceived injury inflicted on the victims [106]. Second, downward bullying is the most common form of bullying in the workplace [107], leading to a conditioning effect in which managers have grown accustomed to justifying the behavior. Third, managers who bully their subordinates have often been the victims of bullying by their superiors [108, 109]. Hence, they are more likely to have experienced the pain of downward bullying than perpetrators of horizontal bullying and upward bullying, leading to a tendency to easily rationalize their own bullying behavior. These features of downward bullying indicate that evidence of injury, victim, and responsibility might be more salient for downward bullying than horizontal bullying and upward bullying. Hence, when injury, victim, or responsibility is denied before conducing bullying behaviors, the neutralization effect of downward bullying on bullying intention might be higher in downward bullying than in cases of horizontal and upward bullying.

Furthermore, our results show that individuals’ perceptions of invisibility and asynchrony are strong positive moderators in horizontal bullying through ESM and downward bullying through email. In upward bulling through text message, although perceived anonymity is verified as a strong positive moderator, the moderation effects for perceived invisibility and perceived asynchrony are insignificant. This suggests that superiors who bully subordinates through email

#

and peers who bully each other through ESM are more influenced by perceived invisibility and perceived asynchrony than are subordinates engaging in upward bullying through text. There are several explanations for this finding. First, superiors exhibit less inhibition toward individuals lower in a hierarchy, and peers exhibit less inhibition toward one another than subordinates experience relative to superiors. With inhibition levels already lesser than in a horizontal or upward context, superiors or peers who perceive invisibility and asynchrony are more likely to rationalize their cyberbullying behavior than subordinates. Second, organizational members who are involved in upward bullying are at greater risk of repercussions than those who conduct downward or horizontal bullying because managers who are bullied by their subordinates might conduct disciplinary behaviors toward the perpetrators [110]. A subordinate’s ability to hide or even alter his identity would allow him to avoid disciplinary action and overcome the authoritative power of his supervisor, thereby distancing himself physically and psychologically from his supervisor. Hence, perceived anonymity is much more important in the context of upward bullying than are perceived invisibility and perceived asynchrony. Even if the subordinates feel that they could ignore victims’ immediate reaction and stay invisible, they remain aware that their supervisors might discover who they are and initiate disciplinary actions against them. The psychological distance between them and their potential victim thus remains very low. By contrast, perceived anonymity amplifies the psychological distance by enabling the subordinate to distance himself from his own identity and from his supervisor.

In the next section, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of this study, followed by the limitations and conclusion.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

Our study contributes to the two important theories. First, the current study contributes to the neutralization theory by identifying moderators between neutralization techniques and behavioral intention. The neutralization theory claims that individuals who tend to engage in deviant behaviors believe in dominant social order and admit the immorality of offending behaviors [22]. However, prior to committing deviance (e.g., workplace cyberbullying), they adopt neutralization techniques – “preemptive self-talk justifications and excuses” [26, p.798] – to assuage anticipated moral condemnation. Even though neutralization explains various deviant behaviors [33] and is widely used in IS studies (e.g., Ref. [27, 38, 39, 40, 45]), Matza [31] emphasizes that the adoption of neutralizations does not automatically result in delinquency. Rather, neutralization only leads to delinquent behaviors under certain conditions [31]. Agnew [30] also suggested that research on neutralizations and deviance should consider conditioning conditioning effects of four cyber communication features. Specifically, under conditions when perpetrators and targets are physically or temporally separated (invisibility and asynchrony), effects of neutralization techniques on harmful behaviors are strengthened. In situations when the offending behaviors may reach a large audience and be witnessed by many people (publicity), the effects are reduced. The four moderators found in our study can also be applied to other studies that attempt to use the neutralization theory to explain deviant behaviors.

Second, our study provides insights into the relationship of cyber communication features with behavioral intention. Previous studies on cyberbullying typically model cyber communication features as antecedents leading to neutralization techniques or behavioral intention (e.g., Ref. [9, 58, 93, 111, 112]). Our study takes a different perspective in that we examine the impacts of

#

cyber communication features on the neutralization–intention relationship. The fundamental logic of features as moderators is as follows: individuals have a tendency to neutralize their potential bad behaviors [33]. This tendency exists regardless of whether particular communication features are available or not. Whether an individual actually engages in the bad behavior depends largely on the strength of the individual’s neutralization [30]. Given the same level of neutralization, individuals who perceive greater invisibility, asynchrony, and anonymity in their communication will demonstrate a stronger relationship between neutralization and actual intent to cyberbully than individuals who perceive a lesser degree of invisibility, asynchrony, and anonymity. We realize that there are other logics that one can use to position communication technology features as either antecedents or independent variables, but we maintain that it is valuable to consider this logic of features as moderating. To argue that features are antecedents to the degree of neutralization is to suggest that features cause individuals to indulge in higher neutralization. However, we know that traditional bullying existed well before cyberbullying became possible and that neutralization is a significant predictor of traditional bullying [48, 113]. Hence, we suggest that neutralization is not directly influenced by communication media features but that the features make it easier/more likely for an individual to act on the neutralization.

## 5.2. Practical implications

In this study, neutralization is found to be significantly and positively related to cyberbullying intention in the workplace. We suggest several strategies to challenge the neutralization techniques adopted by employees. The denial of responsibility is the tendency of cyberbullies to place blame on their environment. Thus, it is important for managers to promote a work environment that refuses to nurture a cyberbully. The workplace also needs to establish a clear

#

written policy emphasizing perpetrators’ responsibilities for cyberbullying acts. The restraining impacts on cyberbullying behaviors are undermined when an injury is not acknowledged. As a result, employees who feel they are victims of cyberbullying behavior need to feel that the organization’s culture supports their reporting of such incidents. We suggest that organizations include sessions on workplace cyberbullying during employee training, emphasizing the harm such behavior inflicts on victims as well as observers. Employees should also be taught to recognize conflict and engage in effective conflict management. In addition to addressing potential cyberbullying through proper employee training, organizations might also consider technology tools to combat cyberbullying. Intelligence technology could be incorporated with daily communication tools such as email systems and text messaging applications. Such tools could be designed to detect inappropriate contents in image or text and provide an alert to the sender before the message is sent. Similarly, organizational electronic social media platforms should be programmed to distinguish offensive messages or pictures, caution senders that the content they send can be made public very quickly, and remind receivers not to forward those inappropriate contents.

Our results suggest that anonymity may have a particularly acute moderating effect. When anonymity was present, the effects of invisibility and asynchrony became insignificant. In the absence of anonymity, invisibility and asynchrony were significant. This suggests that even if an organization eliminates the possibility for individuals to engage in anonymous electronic communication at work, there will nevertheless remain a strong relationship between neutralization and intention to cyberbullying provided that employees perceive invisibility and asynchrony. However, the negative moderating effect that publicity has on the relationship of neutralization to cyberbullying intention suggests that organizations can reduce cyberbullying by

#

increasing the perception of the publicity of organizational communication. Organizations might increase the perception of publicity by informing employees that all emails, text messages, and ESM posts made using company phones are organizational property and will be randomly monitored by human resource executives. Perhaps the very threat of publicity will be sufficient to negatively moderate the neutralization–cyberbully intention relationship. We suggest that organizations that experience problems with workplace cyberbullying consider such a tactic.

## 5.3. Limitations and conclusion

This study, although insightful, is not free of limitations. The first limitation lies in the effects of social desirability bias triggered by the offensive nature of the behavior intention measured in the current study. Although a scenario-based method was used to control such bias, the process that respondents report their inclinations of conducting the aggressive actions by the fictitious figure in the scenario still raises the concern as to whether the reported intention reflects the true intention. Future research should attempt to use additional remedies for hindering such bias.

The second limitation is caused by our attempt to use intention as a proxy for the actual behavior. Although IS research that investigates the relationship between intention to cyberbully and actual behavior demonstrates intention as a strong indicator of behavior [114], the high R² of the cyberbullying intention reported in this study indicates that gaps in intention– actual behavior relationship might exist. Future research in the field is encouraged to measure actual cyberbullying behaviors at work to strengthen the model’s validity.

Third, although modeling neutralization as a second-order construct lends parsimony to our framework and makes our model easy to test, it may lead to the question as to whether all neutralization techniques are moderated by each cyber communication feature. Future research might try to test which neutralization technique effect is moderated by which cyber communication feature.

Fourth, in this study, we chose neutralization as the single predictor of workplace cyberbullying 117] and the rational choice theory [97] might also be effective in predicting workplace cyberbullying behaviors. Future research is recommended to integrate the neutralization theory with other theories to come up with comprehensive understanding of workplace cyberbullying.

Fifth, in this study, we assigned different surveys to our respondents. Each survey included a specific scenario followed by questions associated with a subset of the four cyber features. In other words, in each scenario, we chose to investigate a subset of the four features based on the corresponding medium and specific context associated with that scenario. The adoption of such a method results in several scenario-based studies instead of one study with all vignettes. Because a major purpose of scenario designs is to create variance across different conditions, future research utilizing the scenario-based method is encouraged to provide an overall model covering all types of bullying and all cyber features.

Finally, the vignettes created practical constraints in the administration of the survey that may have led to bias in measurement. We implemented recommended techniques to mitigate bias in measurement. However, some declared variables with identical lead phrases (e.g., “When you do what John did in the scenario”) were placed together in the survey instrument, preventing true randomization of the items. That might have allowed respondents to make similar choices for items that were placed together, resulting in a high level of internal consistency reliability in our measurement model.

#

Several directions for future research in this stream can be highlighted. As for antecedents of workplace cyberbullying, human factors such as personalities might be examined as predictors of workplace cyberbullying. Indeed, Baillien et al. [48] found that personalities such as “intolerant and being very strict” and “tendency to protect self-esteem” were likely to result in workplace bullying behaviors. In addition, following the current study, future research can investigate what makes some people prone to neutralize their cyberbullying behaviors at work. Future research might also examine the inhibition factors for cyberbullying at work. For example, deterrence constructs such as formal sanctions, shame, and moral inhibitions might be examined. Organizational factors such as culture and atmosphere that directly prohibit cyberbullying behaviors might also be tested. Furthermore, although we draw upon the theoretical lens of the social presence theory and adopt the social presence concept to develop the moderation hypotheses, we do not explicitly examine the social presence construct in our model. Therefore, future studies are recommended to explicitly investigate the impact of the social presence construct on workplace cyberbullying acts.

In conclusion, although previous research has paid considerable attention to workplace bullying and teenagers’ cyberbullying behaviors in schools or social network sites, this study focuses on workplace cyberbullying. Specifically, we examined the neutralization techniques of denial of victim, denial of responsibility, and denial of injury and found that neutralization is a strong predictor of the intention to cyberbully in a workplace context. We also noted that three technology features – invisibility, anonymity, and asynchrony – accentuate this effect while one feature – publicity – mitigates the effect. Workplace bullying, in general, and workplace cyberbullying, in particular, are behaviors that are unlikely to be eradicated completely in an organization. However, by understanding better the mentality of perpetrators, it is possible to develop policies and procedures that make potential cyberbullies aware of the potential impact of their online behavior on others, thereby reducing the effect of neutralization.

## About the Authors

Sixuan Zhang is a faculty member at the School of Economics and Management, Beihang University. Sixuan earned his PhD and MS degrees in Information Systems from Baylor University (Waco, TX, U.S.). He received his BMS degree in Project Management from the Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications (Beijing, China). Following that, he worked as an IT consultant for about 3 years. His research interests include the business use of social media, workplace cyberbullying, IT consumerization, and hybrid organizations.

Dorothy E. Leidner, PhD, is the Ferguson Professor of Information Systems at Baylor University and a visiting professor at the University of Lund, Sweden. Since 2005, she has had a regular visiting professor appointment at the University of Mannheim, Germany, where she teaches during the summer. She previously served as an associate professor at INSEAD (Fontainebleau, France) and TCU (Ft Worth, TX) and has held visiting appointments at ETH-Zurich, SMU (Dallas, TX), the University of Caen (France), and ITESM (Monterrey, Mexico). Dorothy received her BA, MBA, and PhD degrees from the University of Texas at Austin. She has more than 50 refereed publications in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Organization Science, and the Journal of Management Information Systems, among others. Dorothy serves as the Editor-in-Chief for MIS Quarterly Executive and as Senior Editor for Information Systems Research and for the Journal of the Association of Information Systems. Previously, she has served various editorial roles including co-Editor-in-Chief of Data Base for Advances in IS, associate editor and senior editor for MIS Quarterly, associate editor for Decision Sciences Journal, and senior editor for the Journal of Strategic Information Systems.
