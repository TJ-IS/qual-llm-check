# Do visitors’ interest level and perceived quantity of web page content matter in shaping the attitude toward a web site?

Yong-Soon Kang <sup>1</sup>, Yong Jin Kim

School of Management, Binghamton University (SUNY), Binghamton, NY 13902, United States

Received 21 January 2003; received in revised form 27 September 2005; accepted 17 October 2005 Available online 28 November 2005

## Abstract

Understanding the factors that affect consumer attitude toward web sites is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. Literature suggests that how much a consumer likes a web site after visiting it depends primarily on the <sup>b</sup>quality<sup>Q</sup> of the web site; for example, perceived informativeness of the content, entertainment quality of the content, and ease of navigation. The current research investigates the role of the perceived amount of web site content (perceived quantity) in determining the consumer’s postvisit attitude toward web sites. In particular, this study examines the attitudinal impacts of the perceived quantity in relation with the visitors’ interest level. This research consists of three consecutive studies (two preliminary studies and a main study). In the two preliminary studies, the effects of informativeness, entertainment, and ease of navigation were tested and verified. The main study was conducted to test primarily the hypotheses regarding perceived quantity and the effect of consumer interest level on web quality perception and visitor attitude. The results indicate that, consistent with our predictions derived from dual-process models of informational influence, the perceived quantity has a positive attitudinal impact among low-interest visitors but not among highinterest visitors.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid growth of the Internet, web sites have played important roles as a medium to communicate with both customers and business partners [45] and as a business enabler that allows firms to sell products and services [52,64]. Given the importance of web sites, managers and researchers have examined various issues that include consumer experience during visits to corporate web sites [2] and how to develop web sites to be responsive to user needs [57].

Among these issues, web site design has been considered to be a catalyst in attracting visitors and convincing customers to purchase offerings from a web site [51]. To make a web site design salient, two considerations are required: giving attention to technical aspects of web site design and taking into account customer needs [41]. The considerations indicate that understanding the way consumers evaluate web sites is essential to the success of electronic commerce.

Previous studies of electronic commerce have investigated the factors affecting customers’ attitude toward web sites such as the number of pages accessed, the time spent at a site, web page organization and navigability, content quality, and shopping related functionality [17,19,28,41]. However, there has been little research done on the impact of customer interest on choosing a web site, and the proper amount of content displayed in a web page. This has been neglected, despite the fact that recent studies emphasize the importance of information packaging and amount of content in explaining user attitude toward web sites [27,51,52]. The current study incorporates examining previous observations in the field, along with a new, innovative technique to further our knowledge of web site design. This discourse analyzes both the impact of the amount of web site content on perceived information quality, and to what extent ease of navigation influences the attitude of consumers toward a web site [44,52,58]. Another area of focus in this study will be the potential effect of a web site visitors’ interest level in determining the interrelationships among the research constructs.

The contributions of the current study are twofold. First, the current study investigates the role of consumer interest level in evaluating a web site. The interest level of consumers is important to predict their behavior, in particular in the context of electronic commerce where customers are quite different from traditional shoppers. The finding of this study will help managers and developers design flexible and responsive web sites by identifying contingencies in terms of customer interest. Second, this paper investigates the effect of the perceived amount of web site content on the precursors of the attitude toward a web site. The results of this study will provide practitioners with insights in which factor is important in making a web site successful. The results will also suggest how to design an effective web site from consumers’ views, by explaining both the main factors and the interaction effect between consumer interest level and key constructs.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Literature review

Previous research on web sites indicates that background, image size, sound file display, and celebrity endorsement influence the number of pages accessed or the amount of time spent at a site [17]. Some researchers suggest that consumers enjoy web sites that are interactive, informative, entertaining, and orderly [19,28,41]. Others believe factors such as download delay, interactivity, navigability, responsiveness, and content quality are the determinants of web site success [2,3,38,44,52,58]. Studies on the commercial use of the web suggest that factors such as online payment, product choice, shopping convenience, product value [21,64], amount of information, variety of information, and word count [52] influence the success of electronic commerce.

The major concepts of web site success in previous studies involve information quality and ease of use. Information quality, one of the major dependent variables in information systems research [15], is represented by a multi-attribute measure of information value such as informativeness, accessibility and adaptability [37]. Informativeness includes the concepts of accuracy, relevance, comprehensiveness, recentness, and credibility. Accessibility, on the other hand, consists of timeliness, convenience, and interpretability. Shapiro and Varian [61] emphasize comprehensiveness and completeness of information as key web site capabilities. Liu and Arnett [44] also argue that information quality and playfulness contribute to the success of a web site.

Along with informativeness, navigability of a web site has been considered critical to its success [2] because the use of the web site is non-linear. Interface features such as navigability and organization of the provided information are potential key variables related to usability [51,62] and are shown to explain sales for Internet stores $( R ^ { 2 } = . 6 1 )$ [46].

Although comprehensive, previous literature does not pay attention to the impact of amount of content, one of the most basic online communication decisions, and customer interest level that is very important in designing web sites [16,41,54]. The underlying reasoning to take into account users’ interest level is that Internet shoppers are quite different from traditional retail shoppers [16,54]. Internet shoppers consider convenience to be a very important aspect of web site use. They are also highly innovative, have propensity to variety-seeking and impulsiveness, and possess positive attitudes toward advertising [16]. The next section further discusses the extent to which information quantity affects consumers’ site evaluation, along with consumer interest levels in conjunction with dual-process models of informational influence including the Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion and Heuristic–Systematic Model [9].

## 2.2. The impact of perceived amount of content on informativeness and attitude

To be successful, web sites should offer both information and entertainment [19,41,44,59]. Followers of this advice, however, are likely to develop web sites that are too complex. This runs the risk of making them less attractive or less user-friendly. In this particular context, two questions are asked: First, all else being equal, is it a good idea to pack a web site with an overabundance of content? Second and more importantly, does the relationship between content quantity and site evaluation depend on a consumer’s level of interest? Note that in this study, the term <sup>d</sup>interest<sup>T</sup> is used as a motivational variable that provokes elaborative information processing [40]. This is mainly because interest is a major criterion to evaluate a person’s perceived relevance of an object [68]. Moreover, interest is the most common element in the definition of involvement as a general motivational variable [5] that affects the level of efforts on information processing [40].

The Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion (ELM) explains how consumer interest level correlates to the effort of elaborating a given message, and how this results in quality perception and attitude toward a brand [56]. According to the model, individuals with high interest in a site will process central message elements in a purposeful and rational way when they are exposed to a persuasive message. Low interest consumers, however, have less processing motivation, and evaluate messages based on their liking or disliking of executional or peripheral cues of a message. For instance, low interest consumers are likely to take their perceived quantity of content as a heuristic cue indicative of quality. In case large quantity is being perceived, the ELM predicts the perceived quality of information and attitude formed by consumers is likely to be favorable. Here, quantity plays a role as the peripheral cue that renders consumers have a beneficial opinion toward a web site, even without reading any information provided. In other words, sheer volume of the content can impress casual surfers and make them believe a web site is good in terms of its quality.

These predictions can also be derived from a number of <sup>b</sup>dual process theories<sup>Q</sup> (see, Ref. [9]). For example, <sup>b</sup>Judgments formed on the basis of heuristic processing reflect easily processed judgment-relevant cues (e.g., message length. . .). . .. Relative to systematic processing, heuristic processing makes minimal cognitive demands on the perceiver.<sup>Q</sup> ([10], p. 74). Hence, low involvement consumers are likely to use heuristic cues such as the perceived quantity of web content. In the remainder of this paper, these hypotheses and others are presented, followed by methods and a general discussion.

## 3. Theoretical framework

The current research builds on previous studies (e.g., [15,44,52,65]) investigating major web site success factors including information quality, entertainment or playfulness, and navigational difficulty. Note that information quality in this study is specified as two dimensions: informativeness and entertainment (discussed later). This research then adds the perceived amount of web page content (perceived quantity) to the model, and investigates the role of interest levels play in consumer evaluations of web sites. Fig. 1 shows the research model.

The proposed model is an extension of technology acceptance model [13,14] in which user attitude toward a behavior is determined by the perceived usefulness belief and the perceived ease of use belief. In the proposed model, the perceived usefulness belief corresponds to informativeness and the perceived ease of use belief is compatible with the navigability construct. Finally, the entertainment construct can be regarded as a part of usefulness because it represents hedonic utility [4]. The technology acceptance model [13,14] assumes that the internal variables, such as beliefs and attitudes fully mediate the effects that all other external variables may have on the system usage of individuals. The effect of perceived quantity in the proposed model is then hypothesized to be carried through informativeness and navigability as in the technology acceptance model.

## 3.1. The characteristics of information quality

As discussed earlier, the recent studies on electronic commerce repeatedly point to information and entertainment as the key determinants of web site attractiveness or success. There are, however, more reasons to support a simple bidimensional attitude model. For example, Batra and Ray [4] propose that attitudes have cognitive and affective components called the <sup>b</sup>utilitarian affect<sup>Q</sup> and the <sup>b</sup>hedonic affect,<sup>Q</sup> respectively (see also Ref. [33]). In line with this bidimensional conceptualization of attitudes, consumer web behavior also is bifurcated. For instance, Hoffman and Novak [32] identify goal-directed behavior such as online shopping (i.e., information search) and experiential <sup>b</sup>netsurfing<sup>Q</sup> behavior as <sup>b</sup>two broad categories of online behavior<sup>Q</sup> (p. 44). Taken together, the literature indicates that practical utility (informativeness) and hedonic utility (entertainment) are the key dimensions of web site content quality perception, which influences post-visit attitudes. These two characteristics may have an impact on the attitude toward web sites regardless of the level of individual interest in the web sites visited. Therefore,

H1a. The more informative or practically useful the web site content is perceived to be, the more positive the post-visit attitude, regardless of the interest level.

H1b. The more entertaining or experientially pleasing the web site content is perceived to be, the more positive the post-visit attitude, regardless of the interest level.

The level of individual interest in the web site, however, may hold different effects on the attitude toward the web site, as inferred from Elaboration Likelihood Model. High interest consumers are much more likely to focus on the central message or content itself such that informativeness has a stronger effect on the consumers’ attitude than entertainment. On the contrary, in the case of low interest individuals, entertainment will affect the attitude as much as informativeness.

H1c. The effect of informativeness on the attitude toward the web site is greater than that of entertainment in the case of high interest individuals, but not in the case of low interest individuals.

## 3.2. Navigation difficulty

Regardless of the amount of content, some sites are better organized, while others are less organized and are thus more difficult to navigate. Sites that are more difficult to navigate will demand more mental resources to keep up several tasks at a time [12], therefore, raising the search cost [50] which individuals usually strive to minimize [34]. In addition, complex web sites tend to incur the disorientation problem [12], which leads to decreasing sales [46]. Accordingly, many studies concerning electronic commerce and information systems success [8,19,35,46,52] tend to regard the ease of navigation as a key success factor. Regardless of the interest level of individuals, it is evident that the more negative the perceived navigability is, the less the perceived informativeness and entertainment are. The negative perceptions of informativeness and entertainment, in turn, generate a negative attitude toward a web site.

H2a. The more difficult to navigate the web site is perceived to be, the more negative the perceived informativeness, regardless of the interest level.

H2b. The more difficult to navigate the web site is perceived to be, the more negative the perceived entertainment, regardless of the interest level.

H2c. The more difficult to navigate the web site is perceived to be, the more negative the post-visit attitude, regardless of the interest level.

## 3.3. Perceived quantity of content

In contrast to the predictions regarding the variables discussed so far, attitudinal impacts of the content quantity are not straightforward. The Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion posits that low interest consumers will take both a peripheral route to persuasion and the <sup>b</sup>number of arguments<sup>Q</sup> or <sup>b</sup>message length<sup>Q</sup> as a simple heuristic cue [55,56]. Web site content can be seen as the equivalent to <sup>b</sup>arguments<sup>Q</sup> in conventional persuasive communications. Thus, if low interest consumers use the perception of content quantity as an input for the common more-is-better heuristic, the resulting perceived web site quality, represented by informativeness and entertainment perception, will be an increasing function of the perceived content quantity. High interest consumers, by contrast, will not be affected by the perceived quantity. Instead, high interest consumers’ evaluation of web sites will be determined by the true merit or quality of the content [56]. In sum,

H3a. The perceived quantity will have a positive effect on informativeness among low interest visitors and no effect among high interest visitors.

H3b. The perceived quantity will have a positive effect on entertainment among low interest visitors and no effect among high interest visitors.

Note that the above hypotheses represent an extension of the ELM; predictions of the ELM involve the extent of persuasion, that is, the attitude toward the position advocated in a message, rather than the attitude toward the message (or web site itself). Such an extension is justified by both theory and empirical evidence. First, according to balance theory [29], when two attitudes are closely associated, change in one induces a concomitant change in the other. Second, there is a large body of evidence indicating a strong correlation between attitude toward an ad and attitude toward a particular brand [7].

According to the information-overload hypothesis and cognitive overhead [12,47], if the quantity of information is beyond the optimal point, increasing information quantity decreases the decision quality, causing anxiety [42] and stress [48]. Information overload can also be understood in the context of the bounded rationality situation and propensity to a reduction in effort to avoid cognitive overhead [6,39]. In particular, in the context of a non-linear information structure like the web, increases in information quantity may require a higher level of navigability to reduce cognitive overhead. Hence, when facing too much information, customers tend to avoid cognitive overhead by reducing their efforts, which results in unfavorable attitudes toward a web site, regardless of interest level. Therefore,

H3c. The perceived quantity will have a negative effect on navigability, regardless of the interest level.

## 4. Methods

Prior to the main study, two preliminary studies were conducted. The primary purpose of these studies was to investigate the effects of the three variables (informativeness, entertainment, and navigation difficulty), which have been verified by previous studies. Another purpose of the preliminary studies was to develop and refine measurements.

## 4.1. First preliminary study

Fifty-seven volunteering college students, enrolled in an introductory information systems course and who were promised extra course credit, rated ten different web sites chosen from five topical areas (two of each): movie, professional sports, automobile, book review, and local community information. Participants visited all ten sites in randomized orders using <sup>b</sup>hyperlinks<sup>Q</sup> provided on a word processing file. Ratings were obtained immediately after the subjects viewed each site. The sessions, conducted in small groups in a computer lab, lasted approximately 50 min.

## 4.1.1. Measures

The measures for this study were drawn from previous studies on web site adoption and use [25,43,44,52,58], information system success [15], and attitude structure [18]. They were, if necessary, adapted to capture the most common concept of the research variables. The attitude measure <sup>b</sup>Overall, I like this web site<sup>Q</sup> was adapted from Eagly and Chaiken [18]. Consumer perception measures including informativeness (<sup>b</sup>This site is very informative<sup>Q</sup>), entertainment (<sup>b</sup>. . . is very entertaining<sup>Q</sup>), and ease of navigation (<sup>b</sup>. . . is well organized<sup>Q</sup>) were rooted in web site success factors [25,43,44,52,58], and information system success [15]. There were also two behavioral intention measures [1]: <sup>b</sup>I would like to explore this site more<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>I would recommend this site to my friends.<sup>Q</sup> All of the ratings were obtained on a ten-point Likert scale anchored by strongly disagree (1) and strongly agree (10).

## 4.1.2. Results

This study yielded 526 usable observations in aggregate, which were subjected to a multiple regression analysis. The dependent variable was attitude toward the site and the independent variables were the three perception scores: informativeness, entertainment, and navigational difficulty (the raw scores were reversed). All three variables had directionally correct, significant effects $( p < . 0 0 5$ for all), and jointly explained a major portion of the variance in the dependent variable $( R ^ { 2 } = . 6 2 )$ . See Table 1 for the result summary. In addition, the overall liking score was highly correlated with behavioral intentions; i.e., .66 with the intention to explore further and .65 with the intention to recommend to friends $( p < . 0 0 0 1$ for both).

## 4.1.3. Discussion

These findings lend some preliminary support for the previous studies on the relationship between the attitude and information quality and navigability. The relatively high correlation between the overall liking score and the two intention items indicates that the single-item attitude measure has validity in the current context. In this analysis, we have learned that the relationship between attitude and behavioral intention was consistent with results from previous studies (e.g., [13,14,22]). The use of reversed scores of <sup>b</sup>well organized<sup>Q</sup> as a measure of navigation difficulty, however, seemed problematic because this measure was likely to tap into a site’s aesthetic aspects—not a variable of interest in this research.

## 4.2. Second preliminary study

Thirty-four volunteering undergraduate students (business major juniors and seniors) evaluated 12 typical web sites (none of which were used in the first study). The web sites chosen had to be at least of moderate interest to college students and needed to represent a wide spectrum of topics. The chosen web sites included three news providers, three entertainment companies, three product sellers, two financial firms, and a university web site. Participants who were promised extra course credit visited and rated the sites, from either residence or school, at their own pace (the mean self-reported time taken for the task was 55 min). For this study, two more items were added to informativeness and entertainment construct respectively to check the possibility of using single item scale for wellstudied construct. As discussed, the measure of navigation was changed to capture signs of navigational difficulty. A measurement item for information quantity was also added.

The results also revealed that p-value for the difficulty construct enlarged greatly, although still significant. In order to test the effect of difficulty more precisely, it was desirable to use a multiple-item scale. ## 4.3. The main study

The main study was conducted to primarily test the hypotheses regarding information quantity (H3a, H3b and H3c) and the effect of consumer interest level on web quality perception and visitors attitude. College students, recruited from various classes with a monetary incentive, visited and rated four chosen web sites including an online music store, an online map and travel information site, a book review site, and a consumer information site. All four web sites primarily focused on marketing information products such as music CD and/or providing information. At initial contact, the participants filled out a short questionnaire designed to check the level of participants’ interest in the web site topics. Upon returning it, they received a second questionnaire, to be completed at the participants’ own pace within the next two days. Prior to the survey, two experts examined the four web sites to give ranks based on the number of links, number of graphics, number of words, and number of banners in a homepage. The expert ranking was consistent with subjects’ perception of the web site content quantity. We also checked correlated errors among the four measures collected within subject. All the constructs except perceived quantity are significantly different from site to site (the difference in perceived quantity is marginal) (see Appendix 1).

## 4.3.3. Model assessment

Assessment of the research model was conducted using the Partial Least Square (PLS) method. PLS is a technique that can analyze structural equation models involving multiple-item or single-item constructs with direct and indirect paths. We used the PLS-Graph Version 2.91.03.04 to perform the analysis. The emphasis of PLS is on predicting the responses as well as understanding the underlying relationship between the variables [63]. The work using PLS, therefore, focuses on predictability of research models, while the work employing other structural equation modeling methods centers on explaining the data [11]. PLS is also a powerful approach for analyzing models and theory building because of the minimal demands on measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions [67]. In addition, the component-based PLS avoids two serious problems, inadmissible solutions, and factor indeterminancy [23].

PLS analysis involves two stages [11]: (1) the assessment of the measurement model, including the reliability and discriminant validity of the measures, and (2) the assessment of the structural model. The assessment of the measurement model is done by examining individual item loadings and internal consistency to test reliability. Individual item loadings and internal consistencies greater than 0.7 are considered adequate [24].

As shown in Table 4, loadings for all measurement items for both low and high interest groups are above 0.7, which indicates there is sound internal reliability. The weights are all statistically significant and show each item contributes to each construct almost equivalently.

## 4.4. Assessment of the structural model

The path coefficients in the PLS model represent standardized regression coefficients. The suggested lower limit of substantive significance for regression coefficients is 0.05 [53]. In a more conservative position, path coefficients of 0.10 and above are preferable. As shown in Fig. 2, all path coefficients except for those from perceived quantity to informativeness (for high interest individuals) and perceived quantity to entertainment satisfy both conservative criteria (over 0.1) and the suggested lower limit (over 0. 5).

The statistical significant test on the path coefficients was performed using the jackknifing technique. All the paths except those from perceived quantity to informativeness (for high interest individuals, not significant; for low interest individuals, significant $p { < } . 0 5 )$ and to entertainment (for both high and low interest individuals, not significant) are statistically significant at $p { < } . 0 0 1$ . This result indicates that hypotheses H1a– H3c are fully supported, while hypothesis H3b is not backed up by the data.

Given the strong relationship among the constructs, the link between perceived quantity and the subsequent constructs (informativeness and entertainment), shown in Fig. 2, appeared relatively to be weak. This weak link might be resulted from the slightly negative wording for the perceived quantity items. To closer look at the link between the factors and check the influence of the wording, we performed a quadratic regression analysis. Tables 7a and 7b show the results.

In general, perceived quantity turns out to affect informativeness and navigation difficulty in a quadratic manner, but not entertainment for both low and high interest group. This result slightly differs from the structural model test result for H3a where perceived quantity is posited to have a positive effect on informativeness among low interest visitors and no effect among high interest visitors. Although the quadratic effect is more clearly shown in the low interest group than in the high interest group, perceived quantity does affect informativeness in a concave form. This result indicates that the quadratic relationship between the constructs is probably a more realistic assumption than a linear relationship.

High $R ^ { 2 }$ for the dependent variable, attitude toward a web site, in the structural model shows that this model can be used to predict the behavior of customers within the web environment. ## 5. Discussion and concluding remarks

The web environment is in many respects qualitatively different from the traditional one. Many studies also have emphasized the importance of understanding user needs without showing empirical evidence [31]. Previous studies tested and supported the role of the variables including informativeness, entertainment, and navigation difficulty. This paper incorporates consumer interest level as a dimension of user needs and investigates the effects of the interest level on the web site success factors. Table 8 summarizes the results of hypotheses testing. As discussed in the results section, most hypotheses (seven out of nine) were supported by the data and one of the three hypotheses related to consumer interest level were supported (one is marginally supported). The findings of this study are quite interesting compared to those from a single structural model test in which we include all the constructs used in this study and use the full data set without separating low and high interest users (see Appendix 2). In the single structural model test, all paths from perceive quantity to the subsequent constructs are statistically significant.

High $R ^ { 2 }$ for the endogenous variables in the structural model (for informativeness $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 3 4$ for high interest users and 0.30 for low interest users; navigation difficulty $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . \ 2 3$ for high interest users and 0.25 for low interest users; entertainment $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 2 4$ for high interest users and 0.22 for low interest users; and attitude $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0$ 59 for high interest users and 0.74 for low interest users) suggest that this model can be used to explain the relationships between the constructs. This is mainly because the effectiveness of a model in PLS is determined by the $R ^ { 2 }$ values for the endogenous variables [36], while that in covariance-based structural equation modeling is determined by Goodness-of-Fit Indices [11].

In terms of the magnitude of variable influences, the path from perceived quantity to navigation difficulty to informativeness carries the effect of web site design on customers’ attitude toward a web site [2,8,25,43,44,52, 58]. With respect to the relationship between entertainment and informativeness, according to the results, although marginal, low interest customers seem to consider entertainment (path coefficient 0.402, p <sup>b</sup> .001) as important as informativeness (path coefficient 0.401, $p { < } . 0 0 1 )$ while high interest individuals appear to perceive informativeness (path coefficient 0.390, p <sup>b</sup> .001) to be more important than entertainment (path coefficient 0.265, $p { < } . 0 0 1 )$ . This indicates that providing affluent information is very important to appeal to high interest customers, while entertainment factors in a web site is essential to low interest individuals.

The impact of perceived quantity on navigation difficulty is very clear since the path coefficient is 0.477 for high interest group, and 0.496 for low interest group, at $p { < } . 0 0 1$ for both. In the context of the web, the use behavior is non-linear, which means increases in information quantity may require a higher level of system navigability and impose cognitive overload to users. This results in poor navigability perception. The impact of perceived quantity on informativeness (path coefficient 0.032 for high interest group, not significant; 0.206 for low interest group, $p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ also supports the argument based on the Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion. The ELM predicts that, when the visitor interest is low, having more content is a better idea. This is plausible because quantity can be taken as a heuristic cue for the quality of the information provided by the web site. H3b (i.e., the differential effect of information content perception on entertainment) was not supported for both high and low interest individuals. This result indicates that both high and low interest individuals do not relate web site content quantity to entertainment, but low interest individuals perceive the quantity as an indicator of informativeness.

The effect of navigation difficulty on informativeness (for low interest individuals, path coefficient 0.619 and for high, 0.596), entertainment (for low interest individuals, path coefficient 0.500 and for high, 0.512), and attitude toward a web site (for low interest individuals, path coefficient 0.265 and for high, 0.262) turned out to be as expected. There is no statistically significant difference between high and low interest individuals. As argued in previous studies [2,34,44,52,58], navigability plays an important role in developing consumers’ perception on a web site. Hence, regardless of the interest level, it seems that the participants were likely to only view a limited amount of content. Consequently, the greater the perceived quantity of content, the more difficult it was to navigate. The more complicated the navigation is, the less favorable the web site quality and the attitude.

To summarize, regardless of the level of consumer interest, perceived quantity negatively affects the navigability of a web site. In turn, the perceived navigation difficulty negatively influences informativeness, entertainment, and attitude toward a web site. On the other hand, both informativeness and entertainment have a positive effect on the attitude toward the web site. The perceived quantity of web content positively affects informativeness of a web site in the case of low interest visitors, but not in the case of high interest visitors. The Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion explains the impact of perceived quantity on informativeness. Low interest consumers take a peripheral route to persuasion and take the <sup>b</sup>number of arguments<sup>Q</sup> or <sup>b</sup>message length<sup>Q</sup> as a simple heuristic cue for evaluating information quality of a web site [55,56]. The impact of entertainment on attitude toward a web site appears to be larger in the case of low interest visitors than in the case of high interest individuals, although it is marginally supported. Hence, low interest individuals are more interested in the entertainment factor than high interest consumers.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research

With no known prior research on the moderating effect of visitors’ interest on key variables and perceived quantity and without independent observations, the current research was largely exploratory in nature. There are several shortcomings in this study, which can be addressed by future researchers on this topic. In this research, the quantity of web content was not experimentally manipulated; instead, the perception of quantity was used as the independent variable. However, as discussed earlier, the expert rankings based on various aspects of the quantity of web site content were consistent with the subjects’ perception of the quantity of web site content. This result validates the use of the measures for perceived quantity. In addition, the use of the corporate web site allows this study to investigate consumers’ perception of web sites within a real situation and gives researchers an opportunity to generalize the results. Another issue with regard to the perceived quantity that should be addressed by future research is its operationalization. In this study, the items of perceived quantity seemed to be worded in a slightly negative manner (<sup>b</sup>too all encompassing and overwhelming), which may result in the weak relationship between perceived quantity and informativeness and entertainment. Future study can address this problem by using neutral terms or letting respondents evaluate the amount of the quantity without providing scales. The third issue regarding perceived quantity is the comparison of the magnitude of its impact on informativeness and entertainment based on users interest level. For example, future research may ask a question like <sup>b</sup>which construct (entertainment or informativeness) is affected more by perceived quantity in case of low interest users,<sup>Q</sup> and vice versa. Answering this question will enhance our understanding the role of perceived quantity in a web site design. Finally, we did not investigate the antecedent variables to perceived quantity that will be important for a successful web site design. We suggest that future study examine various forms of determinants for perceived quantity.

Second, web user interest level was measured (prior to actual visitation) rather than manipulated. Future studies may attempt to manipulate this key construct. The third topic that needs to be addressed is that the quality of measures leaves room for refinement, despite the effort made through conducting the two preliminary studies. In particular, using multiple items for the constructs, informativeness, entertainment, and attitude may result in more reliable measures and hence a better understanding of the relationship among the constructs.

Third, like other self-report based studies, this study may be subject to the common method variance problem. However, considering the fact that we used different student samples in the three separate data collection, if the results of the three studies generated similar results, we may say the results of this study are valid. Moreover, the results of the current study for the path coefficients from entertainment, informativeness, and navigation difficulty to attitude are quite similar to the previous studies in information systems area (see Refs. [1] and [49] for more details). Future research needs to explicitly address the common method problem.

Finally, the use of student samples might limit the generalizability of findings, although it may be less inappropriate for Internet-related topics than for conventional topics [31]. According to Hoffman and Novak [30], the major users of the web are individuals who have a college degree and are 16 to 44 years old. At the same time, the use of older consumers, although not major users of the Internet, may find an interest-byquantity interaction effect that is far stronger than that found by the current study. This is concluded because older people are more susceptible to information overload [26].

## 5.2. Practical implications

For practitioners, this research offers an interesting set of recommendations. Consistent with results from previous studies [2,17,28,41,44,52,58,59], web sites should be informative (useful), entertaining (fun to explore), and easy to navigate. In particular, to design an attractive web site, interface consistency [60] and navigation clarity [46] should be taken into account.

With respect to the overall amount of content, this research advises designers and marketers to be aware of the level of visitors’ interest in the topic or the site. After figuring out the visitors’ actual interest level, they can package the required information in a way that is attractive to consumers [20,51]. For example, Amazon.- com’s web site is built on this principle. The web site shows items related to the product that users recently purchased, as soon as a user logs on to the web site. The assumption underlying this practice is that purchased products mostly represent consumer interest. In this way, Amazon.com may acceptably respond to the consumers’ needs. Web sites such as financial companies that have detailed customer information can easily customize their web sites based on identified customer interest.

Many consumers visit corporate web sites only to do simple tasks such as getting the customer service phone number or locating the nearest retail store. In addition, some topics and product categories are inherently low in consumer interest. In such cases, placing entertainment factors together with the content and FAQ (frequently asked questions) page may result in a better impression of the site; this in turn correlates with a higher likelihood of revisiting or recommending. On the other hand, if consumer interest in a topic or site is high, it is better to have precise and wellorganized content (that is useful and/or fun). This is true as long as it does not increase the perceived difficulty of the site.

## 5.3. Concluding comments

This research attempted to address a fundamental web site design issue and consumer communications question, which is newly presented by the World Wide Web environment. The findings of the current research show that consumers’ interest level partially moderates the effect of the quantity of web site content on the visitors’ perception of informativeness. They also indicate that the main effects of navigation difficulty, informativeness, and entertainment on the attitude toward web sites are still greater than the moderating effect, although the effects slightly vary according to the interest level. Future research is suggested to precisely look at the relationship between perceived quantity and informativeness, and that between perceived quantity and attitude toward web sites.
