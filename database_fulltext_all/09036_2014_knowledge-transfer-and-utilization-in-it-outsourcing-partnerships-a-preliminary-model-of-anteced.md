# Knowledge transfer and utilization in IT outsourcing partnerships: A preliminary model of antecedents and outcomes

## A B S T R A C T

We developed a nomological network of antecedents and outcomes of knowledge transfer and utilization in IT outsourcing relationships, and tested it using a survey of 146 IT outsourcing partnerships in Singapore.

Our findings showed that the characteristics of outsourcing clients, vendors, and knowledge transferred played important roles in facilitating knowledge transfer; the transferred knowledge in conjunction with the knowledge integration mechanisms affected knowledge utilization in client-firms, and that this generated significant operational and strategic performance gains in IT operations. Our findings can aid practitioners determine how to use outsourcing to improve knowledge management in their organization.

## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, firms are using IT outsourcing to external vendors to obtain returns far beyond cost savings and operating efficiencies. As Hanif Lalani, British Telecom’s Group Finance Director commented, ‘‘People have realized that . . . outsourcing vendor staff are very clever.’’ . . . They have many ‘‘ideas about how they could work with you and improve things’’. By outsourcing to India, Singapore, and China, the Bank of America is using its IT outsourcing to acquire valuable knowledge from its vendors, in addition to cost savings, time savings, and better quality programming. Barbara Dosoer, Chief Technology, Service and Fulfillment Executive commented, ‘‘There’s a lot that we’ve learned from our vendors that we’ve been able to bring back to the U.S.’’.

The above examples suggest that IT outsourcing partnerships are gradually evolving from simple cost reduction initiatives to knowledge management initiatives, and that some firms are using IT outsourcing as a strategic partnership to learn from their vendors. However, the process by which outsourcing clients acquire knowledge from vendors, as well as the key drivers and outcomes of learning, remain unclear. Although there is an extensive body of research on the predictors and outcomes of IT outsourcing [16], such work has focused on operational roles of outsourcing such as improving cost efficiencies or scale; relatively less attention has been devoted to understanding their potential ramifications for knowledge flows between vendor and client firms. Our research addressed this gap.

While some outsourcing research has emphasized the need for knowledge transfer from client to vendor (e.g., [7]), little research has examined knowledge transfer from vendor to client. Using an economic learning model, Cha et al. [5] found that the optimal outsourcing rate depended on the client’s ability to acquire production knowledge from the vendor. In cases where such knowledge transfer was inadequate, the client tended to adopt either total insourcing or total outsourcing. In contrast, if the transfer of such knowledge was substantial, the client tended to engage in selective outsourcing. The growing preference for selective outsourcing over total insourcing or outsourcing suggests that clients do see outsourcing vendors as a valuable source of external knowledge.

A useful lens to conceptualize knowledge flows is the notion of knowledge transfer and its utilization. While there is significant research on knowledge transfer, its subsequent utilization in IT outsourcing partnerships remains underdeveloped. Though recent IS work has explored knowledge transfer between clients and vendors [17] as well as knowledge transfer portfolios within the firm [30] during the systems development process, nomological network spanning knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization remains unclear. Even less work has examined whether knowledge transferred across firm boundaries can be effectively utilized in the internal activities of client firms. Furthermore, the literature that examined interorganizational knowledge transfer has identified a variety of antecedents in the broader context of international strategic alliances, rather than in the IT outsourcing context. The objective of our study was therefore to address the gaps in the literature, focusing specifically on the following research questions:

1. What are the key antecedents of knowledge transfer and utilization in IT outsourcing partnerships?

2. What contingent factors, if any, mediate the relationship between knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization in IT outsourcing partnerships?

3. What are the operational and strategic consequences of knowledge utilization for client firms with IT outsourcing partnerships?

To address these questions, we built on past research to identify key antecedents (client’s motivation, vendor’s willingness, knowledge codifiability, client’s prior experience with vendor) presumed to influence knowledge transfer. We also examine the role of knowledge integration mechanisms in facilitating the client’s utilization of such transferred knowledge to postulate the operational and strategic outcomes of the client’s IT function. The proposed model is tested using data on IT outsourcing partnerships from 146 organizations in Singapore.

## 2. Theoretical development

Previous research on knowledge management has examined its frameworks [2,4], factors affecting it [34,40], knowledge flows [35], and its value [33]. A different stream of research on IT outsourcing has examined factors affecting it [31,37], its benefits and risks [13,22], its relationships and value and success of outsourcing [12]. Detailed reviews of IT outsourcing research are available from, Gonzalez et al. [11] and King and Torkzadeh [15]. However, beyond a few case studies (e.g., [19,26]), relatively little research has attempted to integrate the two streams or examined IT outsourcing as a way for enhancing a firm’s knowledge. A summary of key themes in this research area is shown in Table 1.

We defined knowledge transfer as the degree to which knowledge flows from the vendor to the client and knowledge utilization as the degree to which such transferred knowledge is used by the client in its internal IT activities.

Fig. 1 provides a conceptual model of knowledge transfer and utilization from previous knowledge management research. According to this model, contextual antecedents influence knowledge transfer, which, in conjunction with knowledge integration mechanisms, impacts knowledge utilization, which then influences strategic and IT performance. Knowledge transfer is a necessary but not sufficient condition for knowledge utilization because of the effect of knowledge integration mechanisms.

## 2.1. Antecedents of knowledge transfer

The three key features of knowledge management contexts are the properties of organizational units, of knowledge, and of relationships between units. Characteristics of the donor and recipient firms, attributes of the knowledge transferred, and attributes of the knowledge transfer process (relationships between units) are central to organizational learning. Likewise, knowledge transfer is affected by its characteristics (of the source, the recipient, and the context in which the transfer occurs).

Consistent with these, we expected that characteristics of the vendor and client firms, attributes of the transferred knowledge, and the relationship between vendors and clients affected the extent of knowledge transferred.

We posited that the client’s motivation to absorb knowledge and the vendor’s willingness to share knowledge were important antecedents of knowledge transfer in IT outsourcing partnerships.

In addition, since knowledge transfer is influenced by the characteristics of the knowledge being transferred, we included knowledge codifiability as a third antecedent. Further, properties of the relationship between units can be defined in terms of a client’s prior experience with the vendor which we included as the fourth antecedent. Of course, prior experience is a proxy variable for the history of the relationship between client and vendor. These four antecedents are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Client motivation. Knowledge cannot be transferred effectively if the client is not receptive to the transfer, for instance, because of a not invented here feeling of the implementors and users or their lack of perceived value of a new idea. On the other hand, some firms may be motivated to accelerate knowledge transfer if their competitors have announced that they are in the process of acquiring a similar system or process. Hence, motivational factors have been identified as essential for knowledge transfer or a key trigger for knowledge flow from external sources.

In IT outsourcing partnerships, motivated client firms are more likely to seek knowledge transfer. Rottman reported a case study of a manufacturer whose initial attempts at IT offshoring was not successful (project failed to deliver its objectives, was behind schedule and over budget) because knowledge transfer was an afterthought. The firm eventually learned from its mistakes and made knowledge transfer an important objective of IT offshoring. In another study, Koh, Ang and Straub reported that client’s expectation that a vendor will transfer knowledge effectively will positively influence outsourcing success. Weeks and Feeny [42] also stated that while firms traditionally outsourced in order to lower costs, they also expected that such partnerships would be a source of new ideas.

We therefore hypothesized:

H1. Client motivation is positively related to the extent of knowledge transfer from the vendor.

Vendor willingness. In addition to motivated clients, vendors must be willing to share their knowledge with them. In knowledge-based industries such as IT consulting and software development, many vendors deal with knowledge products and may want to protect their proprietary knowledge to maintain their competitive edge. Further, vendors may be unwilling to transfer knowledge. While some knowledge flow can occur due to contractual requirements, tacit knowledge is transferred through interactions between client and vendor organizations. Hence, vendor’s willingness to share knowledge with client firms will affect the degree of knowledge transfer.

Thus:

H2. Vendor willingness is positively related to the extent of knowledge transfer from the vendor.

Knowledge codifiability. Certain types of knowledge may be easier to transfer across the client–vendor boundary, while other transfers may be relatively difficult. While explicit knowledge can easily be codified and therefore be easily transferred from vendor to client, tacit knowledge is difficult to codify and transfer [29]. Codifying knowledge is also difficult and costly. Further, since tacit knowledge is not documented, often being knowledge gained in doing a job, a client is less likely to recognize its availability or even be aware of its existence. In the context of strategic alliances, researchers have found that knowledge tacitness is a significant determinant of knowledge transfer and knowledge ambiguity (associated with tacit knowledge) is directly and negatively associated with knowledge transfer.

In a case study of automotive manufacturing outsourcing, Leonardi and Bailey found that although both client and vendor used the same CAE tools, the use of such tools did not make engineering judgment and assumptions (tacit knowledge) transparent in the design. In order to encourage knowledge transfer, client and vendor had to develop new work practices to compensate for such technological deficiencies. Hence, we hypothesized:

H3. Knowledge codifiability is positively related to the extent of knowledge transfer from the vendor.

Client’s prior experience with the vendor. Previous research has suggested that prior experience plays a critical role in firms’ ability to identify and assimilate new knowledge. Experience through repeated interactions allows knowledge transfer by formalizing the processes and procedures of knowledge transfer, creating formal and informal social ties, and building trust and relational capital between client and vendor firms.

In a case study of a manufacturing firm that was debating between onshore and offshore IT outsourcing, the client realized that prior experience with the onshore vendor could help knowledge transfer. Prior relationships play an important role in vendor choice regardless of whether the vendor is domestic or offshore [10]. Hence we hypothesized:

H4. Client’s prior experience with the vendor is positively related to the extent of knowledge transfer from the vendor.

## 2.2. From knowledge transfer to knowledge utilization

Although the link between knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization appears tenuous, it is clear that knowledge transfer from IT outsourcing vendors is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for knowledge exploitation by client firms, and the greater the amount of knowledge sharing, the greater the knowledge utilization by the client. Hence, we posited a direct positive association between knowledge transfer and its utilization:

H5. Knowledge transfer is positively related to knowledge utilization.

Knowledge integration mechanisms. New knowledge must be integrated within the recipient organization’s internal processes and activities in order to be able to utilize it. Organizations lacking effective knowledge integration mechanisms may absorb external knowledge but fail to realize its value or how to utilize it. Studies have found that knowledge integration is a key predictor of organizational performance at the project level [24,38]. Formal and informal mechanisms for integrating tacit and explicit knowledge in organizations may include team meetings and organizational norms and culture facilitating knowledge exploitation Ramasubbu et al. [25] observed that key process areas of the capability maturity model (CMM) can facilitate learning in outsourced software development processes by facilitating social interaction to disseminate and institutionalize knowledge.

We therefore posited that:

H6. The relationship between knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization is positively moderated by the availability of knowledge integration mechanisms.

## 2.3. Impact of knowledge utilization on IT performance

We suggested that IT performance in firms can be conceptualized in terms of two dimensions: operational and strategic performance. Strategic performance is the extent to which the IT department meets the strategic needs of the firm. Thus we view strategic IT performance as the ability of IT to make the firm more agile relative to its competitors in adapting to external pressures.

IT outsourcing relationships should contribute to firm performance by enhancing the firm’s functional activities. Greater knowledge utilization should improve operational performance by making its operations more efficient and effective and also increase the responsiveness of operations to the client firm’s business needs, thereby enhancing the strategic dimension of IT performance.

Previous research found mixed impact of outsourcing on the client firm’s performance, with some studies reporting no relationship [3], some reporting positive relationship at the process rather than firm level [41], and some finding positive relationship only at the firm level [39,43]. However, studies on knowledge management generally found positive relationships with firm performance [32]. Although apparently no one has yet examined the relationship between knowledge utilization and IT performance within the IT sourcing context.

We therefore hypothesized:

H7a. Knowledge utilization is positively related to the client’s operational performance.

H7b. Knowledge utilization is positively related to the client’s strategic performance.

## 3. Method

We tested our hypotheses using a field survey of senior IT managers in the largest firms of Singapore.

## 3.1. Instrument

A survey instrument (see the Appendix) was developed to measure the various constructs of interest in this study. Most measures employed previously validated 7-point Likert scales, adapted from prior research studies to fit our specific context.

Knowledge transfer was measured using four items that determined the extent to which the client firm acquired knowledge from the vendor in various aspects of software development (applications architecture and software design, business process design, programming, and testing). Knowledge utilization was measured using five items that examined the extent to which the knowledge acquired from the vendor was utilized by the client to improve business processes, build innovative IT applications, enhance IT infrastructure/capabilities, improve project management practices, and increase its ability to partner with other vendors, consistent with our definition.

Among the antecedents of knowledge transfer, client motivation was measured using four items that measured the degree of interest in the client firm in acquiring knowledge about related technologies, best practices, technical expertise, and project management skills. Vendor willingness used three items to discern the client’s perceptions of the vendor’s willingness to share technical expertise and best practices with the client. Knowledge codifiability used three items to assess the degree to which the knowledge used by the vendor was formalized and documented, the knowledge received from the vendor was documented, and the knowledge relevant in the partnership was readily transferable. Client’s prior experience with the vendor was measured as number of years the client had worked with the vendor.

Knowledge integration mechanisms used seven items that measured the communication among team members (documentation practices, project meetings, attendance at vendor/industry conferences), openness to experimentation, and availability of resources to explore new ideas.

IT operational performance was measured using five items that assessed the effectiveness of various facets of IT operations, such as cost-efficiency and effectiveness of IT solutions, reduction in operational cost, technology obsolescence, and access to technologies. IT strategic performance was measured using four items that determined the ease with which the client firm’s IT infrastructure was adaptable to changes in business processes and in technology relative to their competitors.

We controlled for client’s existing stock of knowledge and partnership type on knowledge transfer [9]. Existing knowledge was measured using two items that captured preexisting skills and experience in the client firm. Partnership type was measured with two constructs: scale dependence, which assessed whether the client’s dependence on the vendor was primarily for scale of operations and partnership scope, which examined whether the scope of the partnership was IT operations vis-a\`-vis other IT activities. We measured scale dependence using two items that captured whether the IT partnership was primarily employed by the client firm for IT operations. Partnership scope was measured using a dummy variable coded as 1 if the client relied on the vendor for IT operations services and as 0 if the partnership encompassed other IT activities (such as applications development, consulting, and systems integration.

We also controlled for the effects of industry type, firm size, IT department size, and IT outsourcing intensity on IT performance [1,28]. Since manufacturing was the largest industry in our sample, we controlled for industry type by using a dummy variable (manufacturing versus non-manufacturing firms) [36]. Firm size (annual revenue), IT department size (employee count), and IT outsourcing intensity (proportion of IT budget allocated to work by outside vendor) were captured as items in our survey questionnaire.

All survey items were pretested with IT managers in four organizations with varying levels of IT partnerships, organizational size, IT department size, and IT budgets, in the financial services, manufacturing, transportation, and industrial industries. Pretesting allowed us to refine our measurement items to ensure that they were clear and unambiguously interpreted by respondents.

## 3.2. Data collection

Our sampling frame included firms in the Singapore 1000 directory (the top 1000 firms in Singapore based on their sales revenue). We employed the key informant approach in our survey, soliciting the person who was most capable of answering questions. Our chosen respondents were senior level IT executives (CIO or equivalent). Phone calls were made to identify the desired executive in each firm.

The survey questionnaire was mailed to IT executive in 732 randomly selected firms. Respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity. Two rounds of survey mailing were conducted, spaced about three weeks apart. We received a total of 146 usable responses, for an overall response rate of 20%, which was comparable to that of many prior field surveys. Our respondent pool consisted of 55.7% CIO/IT Manager/IT Director and 15.7% CEO/Managing Director/Senior Manager, indicating that we did reach our targeted.

In addition to asking our respondent to complete the survey questionnaire, we also asked them to identify the name of their vendor and a contact person, and permission to contact them to complete a shorter survey. This was used to cross-validate clients responses with those of vendors, since knowledge transfer involves a dyad. Unfortunately, only 30 of 146 respondents identified their vendors. Reasons cited for not identifying their vendors included confidentiality agreements, unwillingness to disturb their future relationship with the vendor, and unwillingness to check if their vendors would like to fill in a survey. We mailed a short one-page survey to the 30 identified vendors, soliciting their responses on a subset of items from the client questionnaire, and only 17 vendors responded to our request. Though this response was not large enough for statistical analysis, a comparison of the 17 matched responses did not reveal any significant differences between clients’ and vendors’ perceptions of their outsourcing relationships, thereby providing some evidence of data quality. To further assess whether non-identification of vendors systematically biased our results, we conducted t-tests comparing the 30 firms that disclosed vendor names with the remaining firms that did not. We did not find any statistically significant differences in client revenue (t = 1.05), number of employees (t = 1.48), industry sector classification (t = 0.86), scale dependence (t = 0.07), partnership scope (t = 0.01), prior experience with vendor (t = 0.97), the percentage of IT budget that is used for vendor contracts (t = 9.07), or the tenure of the responding manager (t = 0.18).

To assess non-response bias, we compared the sample characteristics of early respondents (first round) with that of late respondents (second round) as a proxy for non-respondents. No significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of sales revenue, number of employees, number of IT employees, industry sector, proportion of IT budget for outsourcing, and prior experience with the vendor, suggesting that non-response bias did not significantly influence our observed data.

Despite all the above checks, single informant responses are always susceptible to common method bias. To assess the extent of this problem, we conducted Harmon’s one-factor test for common method bias and the marker variable technique. Exploratory factor analysis revealed eight factors explaining 75% of the total variance, with the first factor accounting for the highest variance of 13.8%, and a distribution of variance among the other factors ranging from 11.2% to 7.5%. This suggested a lack of common method bias. We also used a marker variable to adjust partial correlation to control for this bias [20]. We chose tenure of respondents in the firm as the marker variable since it is theoretically unrelated to the constructs of interest to our study (average correlation = 0.07). After partial correlation adjustments, all significant zero order correlations remained significant, providing further evidence that common method bias was not a serious problem in our study.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Sample characteristics

The demographics of firms in our sample are shown in Table 2. The sample consisted of a mix of small and medium enterprises (SMEs) and large firms, as evident from employee count and annual revenue. Though 66% of these firms had less than 20 IT staff, this was not surprising since many of these firms had outsourced some or most of their IT operations. Respondents worked an average of 7.6 years in their firm and 11.9 years in the industry, providing some assurance that respondents were indeed experienced professionals.

On average, respondents had worked 7.4 years with the vendors that they had chosen. The vendors provided various services including applications development (54.1%), systems integration (41.8%), consulting services (35.6%), IT operations (27.4%), and others (7.5%). These percentages totalled more than 100% as some vendors provided multiple services to clients. The majority of respondents (56.9%) reported moderate growth in their relationship with the chosen vendor over the last 1–2 years. Overall, the proportion of the responding firm’s IT budget allocated to outside vendors varied from under 5% to over 60%, suggesting that there is scope for improving their IT outsourcing partnerships.

## 4.2. Validation of measures

We tested our constructs for convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability using the PLS technique. Compared to covariance-based structural modeling approaches such as LISREL, the PLS approach imposes minimal sample size restrictions and does not require the underlying data to be multivariate normal. In our PLS model, all scale items were modeled as reflective indicators of their underlying latent constructs, and a bootstrap algorithm with 500 resamples was used to estimate significance levels.

Convergent validity was assessed using three criteria: (1) all item factor loadings should be significant and exceed 0.70, (2) composite construct reliabilities should exceed 0.80, and (3) AVE for each construct should exceed the variance attributable to measurement error (i.e., 0.5). As shown in Table 3, all three conditions were met. Discriminant validity was assured because the AVE for each construct exceeded the squared correlations between that and all other constructs. Table 4 shows that the square root of AVE values (on the diagonal) for each construct were higher than the corresponding correlations. Thus, our measures were psychometrically adequate.

## 4.3. Hypotheses testing

PLS analysis, including path coefficients, path significance, and variance explained for each dependent variable, are shown in Fig. 3, and summarized in Table 5. The moderating effect was modeled as an interaction variable consisting of multiplicative terms of each item in the two interacting constructs (knowledge transfer and knowledge integration mechanisms). To reduce the possibility of multi-colinearity with the parent constructs, we used standardized values for all items.

Three out of four hypothesized antecedent variables of knowl edge transfer (client motivation, vendor willingness, and knowledge codifiability) had significant effects (p < 0.001) on the dependent variable, supporting H1, H2, and H3 respectively. Vendor willingness had the largest effect (b = 0.35), followed by client motivation (b = 0.27), and knowledge codifiability (b = 0.25). Client’s prior experience with the vendor did not have significant relationship with knowledge transfer (b = -0.02), thereby failing to support H4. Jointly, these variables explained 41.5% of the variance in knowledge transfer. The path coefficient from knowledge transfer to knowledge utilization was significant (b = 0.52, p < 0.001), supporting H5. We found that knowledge integration mechanisms negatively moderated the relationship between knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization (b = -0.11), though we expected a positive moderation effect from H6. However, knowledge integration mechanisms had a significant direct positive effect on knowledge utilization $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ). Knowledge transfer and knowledge integration mechanism jointly explained 53% of the variance in knowledge utilization. Knowledge utilization had significant effects on IT operational performance $( \beta = 0 . 6 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and IT strategic performance $( \beta = 0 . 3 9 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and explained 48% and 23.5% of variance respectively, supporting H7a and H7b. The control effects were mostly non-significant, with the exception of that of firm size on strategic performance.

While our unexpected findings may be partially attributed to our small sample size, these findings deserve a more careful examination. Our first unexpected finding is the lack of a significant relationship between client’s prior experience with the vendor and knowledge transfer. Maybe prior experience matters in initial stages of an IT partnership, and becomes less important as the relationship matures or the client firms examined in our study (IT units) are highly specialized and it is unclear whether the length of prior relationship have any impact on a client’s ability to acquire specialized knowledge.

Our second unexpected finding was the negative moderating effect of knowledge integration mechanisms on the relationship between knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization. One explanation is that because client firms integrate knowledge from various sources, client employees may not readily realize what knowledge has been acquired from a given vendor, resulting in a systemic underestimation of the value of knowledge acquired from a vendor.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Implications for research

We developed a theoretical model of knowledge transfer and utilization in outsourcing relationships by drawing on prior expectations and findings and from the IT outsourcing and knowledge management literatures. Empirical analysis of our model, using survey data from 146 client firms in Singapore validated most of our hypothesized relationships.

The findings of our study have at least three important implications. First, prior research has viewed IT outsourcing as transactional relationships, intended to increase efficiency and reduce costs of IT operations. However, our findings suggest that partnerships also serve a strategic role, in improving client firms’ knowledge assets critical to their long-term survival. IT outsourcing partnerships can serve as valuable conduits for the transfer of specialized knowledge. Knowledge gained from outsourcing can enhance a firm’s IT flexibility. Second, our study serves to bridge IT outsourcing and knowledge management research that have historically followed different trajectories. Our proposed model provides a nomological network of the different antecedents and outcomes of knowledge transfer and utilization, elaborates a systematic set of factors (vendor, client, and knowledge) that enable knowledge transfer, elaborates why knowledge transfer may not necessarily lead to knowledge utilization, and what operational and strategic outcomes can be gained from knowledge utilization.

Third, regarding the antecedents of knowledge transfer in outsourcing relationships, although prior studies have demonstrated the independent and isolated effects of vendor, client, and knowledge characteristics on a firm’s knowledge transfer, our study may be the first to integrate these diverse set of factors and examine their joint effects on knowledge transfer.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

First, IT outsourcing partnerships offer client firms the opportunity to acquire specialized technical knowledge from their partners. Just as the learning benefits of IT outsourcing can turn a new page in outsourcing research, it can also transform outsourcing practice by enabling clients to derive greater value from their outsourcing initiatives.

Second, our study elaborates the forces shaping knowledge transfer in outsourcing relationships. Such knowledge transfer is influenced both by the willingness and motivation of both client and vendor firms in the partnership. Further, the codifiability of knowledge is a key constraint influencing the extent of knowledge transfer. Hence, not all types of knowledge are conducive to transfer.

Third, we showed that knowledge transfer is necessary but not sufficient to impact significant performance gains in client firms. A key barrier that may hinder a firm’s ability to utilize acquired knowledge is the lack of knowledge integration mechanisms. Although we did not find statistical evidence of the expected moderating effects of these mechanisms on knowledge utilization, the main effects observed in our study is testimony to the importance of knowledge integration mechanisms in helping knowledge utilization in client firms.

## 5.3. Limitations

Six limitations of the study must be considered while generalizing its results. The first limitation is our cross-sectional design. Presumably, knowledge transfer and knowledge utilization are temporal processes that unfold over time, and are therefore best studied using a longitudinal design. Access and resource constraints precluded our ability to perform a longitudinal survey. We attempted to overcome this limitation by asking each respondent to answer each survey question with respect to one of their current or recent IT vendor partners, to minimize the potential of recall bias.

Second, although we took several steps to mitigate common methods bias, no single respondent study can be immune to this bias.

Third, our response rate of 20% may raise the concern of sampling bias. An additional generalizability concern is that findings derived from our sample of Singaporean firms may not generalize to other cultures, especially because knowledge sharing may depend on culture.

Fourth, our research model was purposely kept parsimonious in view of our limited sample size. However, there may be other antecedent variables that should be examined in order to develop a richer and more comprehensive understanding of knowledge transfer and utilization in IT outsourcing relationships.

Fifth, our research design relied on the key informant approach wherein the primary respondents were IT managers in the client firm. Though these managers had over seven years of experience in their firms and were presumably well qualified to answer questions regarding IT partnerships, their perceptions may not match that of end-users or the organization.

Lastly, our study focused on knowledge transfer from IT outsourcing vendor to client. However, knowledge transfer may also occur from client to vendor.

In closing, this paper theorized and empirically validated a model of the knowledge transfer and utilization in IT outsourcing partnerships.

## Appendix. Measurement items

All items were measured on a seven point Likert type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

## Client motivation (CMOT)

In our partnership with this vendor . . .

1. We were interested in obtaining technical knowledge on various technologies.

2. We were interested in obtaining information on business best practices.

3. We were interested in obtaining technical expertise (e.g., design, methods).

4. We were interested in acquiring IT and/or project management skills.

In our partnership with this vendor . . .

3. Vendor discourages their employees from sharing their expertise.

In our partnership with this vendor . . .
