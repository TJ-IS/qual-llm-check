## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cooperative sales promotion Independent sales promotion Evaluation mode Product evaluation Misprediction Acquisition-transaction utility

## A B S T R A C T

Inspired by the evaluation mode theory, we show online vendors mispredict consumers’ responses to diferen types of sales displays. While vendors predict that consumers evaluate a featured product more positively in a cooperative sales promotion (CSP; i.e., multiple stores promoting synchronously) than in an independent sales promotion (ISP; i.e., a single store promoting independently), consumers actually do the opposite. The reason is that vendors compare CSP with ISP and are able to evaluate depending on perceived acquisition utility; consumers, however, see either CSP or ISP, resulting in dificulty in accessing acquisition utility. Thus, they evaluate according to perceived transaction utility.

## 1. Introduction

In the present highly competitive e-commerce market, online vendors usually adopt promotions to attract consumers and increase sales [1]. In e-commerce practice, there are two types of sales displays—traditional independent sales promotion (ISP), in which a vendor/brand provides the sales promotion on its own [2,3], and cooperative sales promotion (CSP), in which multiple vendors/brands ofer price discounts in a commerce activity, simultaneously [4,5]. Participation in CSPs, such as Cyber Monday Sales in the US and Tmall 11/11 shopping festival in China, is becoming more and more popular among online vendors. For example, more than 140,000 vendors/ brands joined the Tmall 11/11 shopping festival in 2017, synchronously providing steep discounts and multiform promotions [6]. On the other hand, consumers’ responses to CSP are ambiguous. While daily sales in the Tmall 11/11 shopping festival grew from \$14.3 billion to \$25.4 billion from 2015 to 2017 [7], anecdotal evidence suggests that the 11/11 efect provides an illusion of prosperity, as the refund rate of some top brands has reached as high as 69% [8]. These seemingly inconsistent observations raise the following questions: Why are vendors obsessive about CSP? Do they believe that consumers evaluate products in CSP more positively than those in ISP? If so, are consumers’ actual product evaluations in line with this forecast?

The answers to these questions are substantially important. From a theoretical perspective, they would extend our understanding of the relatively underexamined CSP. Previous research has demonstrated that vendors’ promotional decisions, such as promotion type [9,10], discount framing [11,12], and visual design of price information [13,14], can alter consumer behaviors. However, these studies focus mainly on ISP and, to date, few have paid attention to CSP. The handful of exceptions are mostly qualitative [5,15,16] or focus predominantly on channel management [4,17]. Despite CSP’s popularity in practice, whether vendors’ enthusiasm about CSP is reasonable and whether CSP actually results in more positive product evaluation among consumers than ISP remain unexamined. Practically, it is also imperative to examine these issues because vendors’ possible misprediction of consumers’ preference may prevent their choosing the more efective sales campaign and hurt firms’ financial performance in the long run.

Motivated by the growing interest in online CSP and the lack of empirical examination, the current research investigates if consumers evaluate featured products in CSP (vs. ISP) more favorably. Moreover, our approach allows us to compare vendors’ prediction about consumers’ preference and consumers’ actual preference. We propose that vendors mispredict consumer preference. Here, a featured product refers to the major product in a promotional advertisement, one which has more descriptions than other also-mentioned products. We focus only on the key diferentiator between CSP and ISP, namely the number of stores promoting in a promotion activity, keeping other dimensions

(e.g., discount levels) constant.

To elaborate, transaction utility theory (TUT; [18–20]) suggests that consumers’ product evaluation depends on either their perceived acquisition utility (i.e., the benefits consumers believe that they are getting by acquiring and using a product relative to its price), their perceived transaction utility (i.e., the perception of psychological satisfaction or pleasure obtained from taking advantage of the financial terms of the price deal), or both. Evaluation mode theory [21,22] further suggests that it is easier for people to judge the amount of benefits one could get from a product, which by definition is associated with acquisition utility, when comparing multiple objects jointly than when evaluating one object separately without knowing the characteristics of other comparative objects.

Regarding the situations we are interested in, vendors compare CSP and ISP directly so as to choose between the two. The comparison leads vendors to think that a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) outperforms more products, resulting in consumers’ higher perceived acquisition utility. Given that most people have the lay belief that consumers’ main goal during shopping is to get high value products at a low price [23], it is likely that vendors draw on acquisition utility and predict that consumers would evaluate the featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) more positively. In contrast, consumers see either CSP or ISP, according to a vendor’s promotion decision. Without considering the other possible type of sales display, they would have dificulty in judging acquisition utility and, thus, have no choice but to draw on transaction utility. Buying a product in CSP results in high opportunity cost of being unable to purchase other discounted comparative products and, thus, reduces the pleasure of taking advantage of the discount (i.e., perceived transaction utility). As a result, consumers’ evaluation of featured products in CSP (vs. ISP) would be less positive. Moreover, if consumers do base their judgment on transaction utility because of the dificulty accessing acquisition utility, their preference for ISP over CSP should diminish when they are high in product knowledge, which would enable them to evaluate the product benefit based on their previously acquired internal knowledge without being influenced by external promotional cues [24].

This paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on diferent types of sales displays (ISP vs. CSP), TUT, and evaluation mode theory. Then, we develop five hypotheses about vendors’ prediction of consumers’ preference (H1), the corresponding mechanism of acquisition utility (H2), consumers’ actual preference (H3), the corresponding mechanism of transaction utility (H4), and the moderating role of product knowledge (H5) as supporting evidence for the mechanism of consumers’ actual preference. After that we report four studies supporting the hypotheses. Finally, we discuss the findings, contributions. limitations. and future directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The two sales displays: ISP versus CSP

In the e-commerce arena, there is a growing trend of pursuing new promotion strategies to improve sales and market share. Recently, IS scholars have paid attention to these newly developed online promotional vehicles, such as cash-back promotion [1], location-based mobile promotion [25], and even the interaction between promotional marketing (e.g., ofering coupons) and word-of-mouth [26]. However, all of the above promotional strategies are diferent manifestations of ISP, in which a single brand ofers a sales promotion independently. In contrast to ISP, the strategy CSP has become popular in recent years but is relatively underexamined in academic research.

CSP refers to any sales promotion scheme in which more than one brand participates; it is also called umbrella sales promotion [5,16]. In CSP, multiple brands ofer sales promotions simultaneously for a specific event, such as an anniversary promotion or trade fair [27]. Traditionally, researchers have studied CSP in a descriptive way [5,15,16]. For example, Crawford [15] defined the nature and purpose of cooperation and ofered six specific ways of operationalizing CSP. Recently, researchers have also tried to better understand CSP from the perspective of channel management. For example, Gu et al. [17] examined how to motivate distributors to participate in CSP using information and peer distributors’ influence. Karray [4] investigated the efectiveness of CSP under diferent channel structures $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ centralized vs. decentralized).

However, how consumers respond to CSP as compared to ISP has never been examined empirically, and the answer is not apparent. On the one hand, CSP ofers positive aspects such as cost eficiency, promotion efectiveness, and more product choices for consumers [16]; on the other hand, it may also lead to negative aspects, such as fierce competition among diferent brands [27]. Given that vendors usually try to increase “store trafic” through attractive featured products [28,29] in both CSP and ISP, the current research focuses on how consumers evaluate featured products in CSP and ISP, from the perspectives of both vendors and consumers.

Previous research has found that consumers’ product evaluation is determined mainly by perceived utility [30], which refers to “con sumers’ overall assessment of the utility of a product or service based on perceptions of what is received and what is given” [31]. In the next section, we introduce TUT [19,20] and discuss how product evaluation is determined by diferent types of utilities.

## 2.2. Transaction utility theory and product evaluation

Marketing and e-commerce literature suggest that utility is an important predictor of consumers’ product evaluation (e.g., [32–34]). Product evaluation is a function of multiple utility dimensions that make contributions in diferent situations [35], and researchers have developed frameworks that incorporate diferent dimensions of utility (e.g. [19,20,36,37]).<sup>1</sup> Among them, TUT [19,20] is the most widely adopted for explaining consumer behaviors when sales promotions such as coupons or price discounts are involved (e.g. [32,38]). This explanatory preference reflects the view that the two types of utility that it focuses on, namely acquisition utility and transaction utility [38,39], dominate during and immediately following purchase [36]. Because of TUT’s frequent use in explaining consumer product evaluation in sales promotion activities, we draw on it to develop our theoretical rationale.

According to the TUT, acquisition utility refers to the net gains associated with the product acquired and can be calculated by subtracting the purchase price from the consumption benefit of the purchased product [20,32]. It is positively influenced by the benefits of getting the product and negatively influenced by the advertised selling price. Thus, at a given purchase price, perceived acquisition utility is higher when consumers perceive higher product quality [18]. Transaction utility refers to the emotional pleasure and perceived merits of the deal, and it can be calculated by subtracting the purchase price from consumers reference price [20,32]. At a given purchase price, perceived transaction utility increases with a higher advertised reference price [18], unconditional and no-cost return and refund [40], etc. Moreover, previous findings have suggested that consumers’ perceived transaction utility is likely to be altered by the presentation format of a deal even while the essentials of the deal (e.g., the savings) remain unchanged [41,42], a finding which indicates that perceived transaction utility is easily influenced by contextual cues.

Previous research has shown that both perceived acquisition and transaction utilities positively predict product evaluation [32,43]. Whether a featured product in CSP outperforms, one in ISP should depend on both types of utilities. However, according to the evaluation mode theory [21,22], as will be introduced in the next section, the ease of accessing these two types of utility may be diferent for vendors and consumers, resulting in a diference in their relative impact between these groups.

## 2.3. Evaluation mode theory

Evaluation mode theory suggests there are two basic evaluation modes—joint evaluation, in which multiple options are presented si multaneously and evaluated comparatively, and separate evaluation, in which only one option is presented and evaluated [22]. Hsee and Zhang [44] have suggested that evaluability (i.e., whether product quality or benefits are easy or dificult to evaluate) is high in joint evaluation because consumers can use one option as a reference to evaluate another. Because acquisition utility of a product is a function of its quality/benefits and its price [20,32], if the two options are at the same price or the prices are known, acquisition utility of a product in joint evaluation should be also easy to judge. In contrast, in separate evaluation, there are no external reference options, and consumers can evaluate the option only by its own characteristics. In this case, the evaluability is relatively low and it is dificult for people to judge the acquisition utility of a product. As a result, people have to draw on transaction utility. Previous research has also provided evidence that in separate evaluation people may be easily influenced by situational cues, such as the size of the percentage discount [45], which is positively associated with the psychological pleasure of purchase (i.e., transaction utility) [11].

As indicated above, acquisition utility can be assessed more easily in joint evaluation than in separate evaluation. Therefore, people are more likely to draw on acquisition utility, which is considered the actual economic value of a product [18], in the former case and transaction utility, which is more relevant to situational factors (e.g. [41,42]), in the latter case. Extensive research has documented that this diference leads to opposite preferences for the same option in joint versus separate evaluations (e.g., Refs [22,44,46]). For example, Hsee [47] found that consumers prefer an 8 oz. ice cream in a 10 oz. cup over a 7 oz. ice cream in a 5 oz. cup when the two cups of ice cream are presented side by side, but they are willing to pay more for the latter cup when evaluating each product separately. The explanation for the choices is that 8 oz. is obviously larger in size than the 7 oz. in joint evaluation, but the size of ice cream becomes dificult to judge without a com parison option. As a result, consumers rely on a situational cue $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ whether the cup of ice cream is over-full) in the separate evaluation [47].

Following the same logic, a diference in evaluation modes will also lead people in joint evaluation to mispredict the preference of people in separate evaluation. For example, vendors, who consider alternative prices simultaneously when deciding the price of a product, are able to compare the actual values of a product at diferent levels of price, leading to high price sensitivity, whereas consumers see only one final price and, thus, are less price sensitive. Because people usually project their own preference onto others [48,49], vendors are likely to overestimate consumers’ price sensitivity [50]. Similarly, presenters (i.e., people deciding what to ofer to others; typically, vendors) are in joint evaluation and see an offer as more attractive when an add-on is in cluded, despite the fact that evaluators (i.e., people deciding whether to take the ofer that presenters decide to provide; typically, consumers) find an ofer more attractive without the add-on [51].

When designing a promotion campaign, online vendors consider CSP and ISP synchronously and predict consumers’ responses to both types of sales displays. They are in joint evaluation [21,22,47]. In contrast, consumers see only one sales display according to vendors final promotion decision, and they are in separate evaluation [21,22,47]. The diference in evaluation modes may lead vendors to mispredict how consumers evaluate featured products in CSP versus

those in ISP.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Vendors’ prediction about consumers’ product evaluation

As discussed above, vendors are in joint evaluation when making a prediction about consumers’ preference. In this case, perceived acquisition utility (vs. transaction utility) should play a more important role in product evaluation. To evaluate vendors’ perceived acquisition utility of a featured product in CSP as compared to that in ISP, two aspects should be noted. First, people have an intuition that actual value (i.e., acquisition utility) of a featured product should be larger than that of nonfeatured products in a promotion [52]. As there are more products being promoted in CSP than in ISP, being a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) signals its outperforming more products, leading to its higher perceived acquisition utility. Moreover, a featured product in CSP seems to outperform products from multiple brands while one in ISP would seem to outperform products from only one brand. Thus, the product in CSP would obtain higher perceived acquisition utility. As vendors are likely to project their perception onto consumers [48,49], they may predict that consumers perceive higher acquisition utility of a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP).

Second, more products are on sale in CSP (vs. ISP) and consumers are more likely to find products that meet their purchase goals at a lower price [53,54]. Thus, when comparing these two types of promotion, vendors may think that CSP has a higher value for consumers than ISP. Because people’s perception of one object might spill over to another, related object [55–57], vendors’ prediction about consumers perception of sales displays (CSP vs. ISP) might transmit to their prediction about the corresponding featured products. Therefore, vendors are likely to believe that consumers perceive higher acquisition utility of a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP). The higher acquisition utility in CSP (vs. ISP) may consequently lead vendors to predict that consumers evaluate a featured product more positively. Formally:

H1. Vendors predict that consumers would evaluate a featured product in CSP more positively than that in ISP.

H2. The efect of sales display on vendors’ prediction is driven by their judgment about consumers’ perceived acquisition utility rather than perceived transaction utility.

## 3.2. Consumers’ actual product evaluation

When evaluating featured products in a promotion, consumers can see only one sales display, either CSP or ISP, according to the vendor’s promotion decision. Thus, they are in a separate evaluation [21,22,47], in which acquisition utility is dificult to judge and transaction utility plays a more important role. To evaluate perceived transaction utility, two aspects should be noted. First, when judging a featured product, consumers’ reference price may be lower in CSP than in ISP. To be specific, consumers’ reference price is likely to be influenced by consumers’ internal belief about the price range and distribution of similar products in the market [58,59]. In CSP (vs. ISP), as multiple brands are providing discounts, it is easier for consumers to imagine that, besides the featured product, there are many other discounted and low-price products, which also could meet their needs. Therefore, CSP (vs. ISP) should lower consumers’ reference price and their perceived transaction utility of the featured product would consequently diminish as well.

Relatedly, CSP, such as an online shopping festival, usually lead consumers to perceive high opportunity cost of their purchase because a lot of products from multiple vendors are on sale, and the purchase of a featured product usually means that consumers have to give up other similar products. In this regard, the hedonic benefits and emotional gains from obtaining the featured product at a lower price [60] in CSP would be relatively lower than those in ISP. Therefore, we predict that people tend to believe that perceived transaction utility of a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) is lower, leading consumers to evaluate the product in CSP (vs. ISP) less positively. Formally:

H3. Consumers evaluate a featured product in CSP less positively than that in ISP.

H4. The efect of sales display on consumers’ product evaluation is mediated by perceived transaction utility rather than perceived acquisition utility.

Note that the above theorization assumes that the perceived acquisition utility is the primary determinant of product evaluation, among both vendors and consumers. We propose that consumers rely on perceived transaction utility only when they are unable to judge acquisition utility, such as when they are in separate evaluation. But even in separate evaluation, if consumers have another source of access to acquisition utility, the proposed advantage of ISP (vs. CSP) should diminish. Product knowledge might enable consumers to evaluate acquisition utility.

Product knowledge refers to the extent to which a consumer has related knowledge about a product category. Knowledgeable consumers are familiar with the product category and have a deeper understanding of product information [61,62]. Previous research has indicated that people who are high (vs. low) in product knowledge have less dificulty judging acquisition utility when processing a product separately $[ 1 8 , 4 4 , 6 3 , 6 4 ]$ , and thus their product evaluation is more likely to depend on the product itself rather than external cues [24,65]. Therefore, knowledgeable consumers may draw on their internal knowledge to evaluate a featured product regardless of the type of sales display. Note that vendors in joint evaluation evaluate acquisition utility through comparison, whereas here knowledgeable consumers evaluate acquisition utility based on their internal knowledge. These diferent processes should lead to higher perceived acquisition utility in CSP (vs. ISP) in the former case, and no diference between CSP and ISP in the latter case. Formally:

H5. Product knowledge moderates the efect of sales display on consumers’ product evaluation. That is, while consumers evaluate the featured product of CSP (vs. ISP) less positively, this efect diminishes when consumers are high in product knowledge.

## 3.3. Study summaries

Fig. 1 presents our research model. Empirically, we conducted a series of four studies to test our hypotheses. Study 1 focused on vendors prediction about consumer judgment. With a survey-based design, Study 1A showed that vendors predicted that consumers would eval uate a featured product in CSP more favorably than that in ISP (H1). Study 1B replicated this finding using a more rigorous experimental design and further demonstrated that this efect was driven by their prediction about consumers’ perceived acquisition utility. Study 2 focused on consumers’ actual judgment. Specifically, Study 2A revealed that consumers actually evaluated a featured product in ISP more fa vorably than that in CSP (H3) and this efect was mediated by perceived transaction utility (H4). This study also demonstrated that the difer ence of display was not suficient, and thus, sales promotion was a necessary condition for consumers’ preference for ISP over CSP. Building on these findings, Study 2B further showed that the efect of sales display on consumers’ product evaluation occurred only among consumers low in product knowledge (H5), supporting the proposition that consumers preferred a featured product in ISP over that in CSP because of their dificulty in judging acquisition utility.

4. Study 1A: Vendors predicted that consumers preferred featured products in CSP on a survey

Study 1A was a survey-based study to test H1. We hypothesized that participants in joint evaluation, which fits the vendors’ situation, would predict that consumers’ evaluating the featured product in CSP more favorably than that in ISP.

## 4.1. Research method of Study 1A

## 4.1.1. Participants

Fifty-eight university students from China completed this study voluntarily. In general, students are active online shoppers and can generally represent the target population [66]. Many studies in the IS literature have used student samples (e.g., Refs [67–70]). Moreover, we believe that our hypotheses can be identified as universally applicable because they are rooted in fundamental principles of human cognition [69]. Taking this information together, we think that the use of student participants is appropriate for our research and do not discuss it further in the following studies. Demographic information of all the samples is summarized in Table 1.

## 4.1.2. Design and procedures

Study 1A had two parts. First, we introduced the definitions of CSP and ISP and gave examples (e.g., the Alibaba 11/11 shopping festival as an example of CSP) to all participants. Then, we asked all the participants to predict consumers’ relative evaluation of a featured product in CSP and one in ISP, on the premise that the discount levels were the same. The three items were adapted from a previous study [71]. Participants reported their attitudes on a nine-point Likert scale with two defined and extreme ends (1 = “absolutely ISP”, 9 = “absolutely CSP” for half of the participants, and 1 = “absolutely CSP”, 9 = “absolutely ISP” for the other half). Scores were recoded and then averaged so that higher scores indicated that, to a higher extent, participants considered consumers to prefer a featured product in CSP over one in ISP. Detailed measurements in each study are summarized in Table 2.

## 4.2. Results and discussion of Study 1A

To ensure that the data met the distributional assumption for parametric tests, we conducted Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests before data analysis in all four studies (see Web Appendix A for details) and do not discuss them here for the sake of brevity. To test H1, a one-sample t-test was conducted. The result showed that the participants scored higher than the midpoint of 5.0 (M = 5.74, SD = 1.90, t(57) = 2.97, p = 0.004; Cohen’s d = 0.39), which indicated that participants with information similar to that of vendors predicted that consumers would prefer featured products in CSP over those in ISP. H1 was confirmed.

## 5. Study 1B: A conceptual replication of Study 1A using an experiment

Study 1B was an experiment to replicate the results of Study 1A (i.e., H1). We asked participants to imagine that they were a vendor making a promotion decision and predicting consumers’ product evaluation. Moreover, to provide the evidence for the underlying mechanism, we asked participants to predict consumers’ perceived acquisition utility and perceived transaction utility. We hypothesized that participants would use their judgment of consumers’ perceived acquisition utility to make predictions about consumers’ product evaluation (i.e., H2).

This experiment employed a one-factor (sales display: ISP vs. CSP) within-subject design. First, participants were asked to imagine that they are the marketing oficer of a fictional brand “Landbot,” and de ciding which type of sales display should be adopted. ## 5.2. Results and discussion of Study 1B

## 5.2.1. Prediction of consumers’ product evaluation

We conducted a paired-sample t-test on vendors’ prediction about consumers’ product evaluation. Results showed that the participants predicted that consumers would evaluate the featured product in CSP more favorably than that in ISP (M = 7.06, SD = 1.54 vs. M = 6.37 SD = 1.84; t(82) = −2.59, p = 0.011; Cohen’s d = 0.28), supporting H1.

5.2.2. Prediction of consumers’ perceived acquisition and transaction utilities

A similar paired-sample t-test on perceived acquisition utility showed that participants predicted consumers’ perceived acquisition utility to be higher in CSP than in ISP (M = 7.12, SD = 1.24 vs. M = 6.70, SD = 1.49; t(82) = −2.17, p = 0.033; Cohen’s d = 0.24). ## 6. Study 2A: Consumers actually preferred featured products in ISP

Diferent from Studies 1A and 1B, in which participants made predictions from the perspective of vendors, Study 2A asked participants to make judgment of products from the perspective of consumers. While Studies 1A and 1B showed that vendors would predict consumers’ preferring a featured product in CSP to one in ISP, Study 2A aimed at showing that consumers’ actual evaluation was opposite to this pre diction. That is, consumers’ evaluation of featured products in ISP (vs. CSP) would be higher (i.e., H3). In addition, to explore the underlying mechanism, we also measured perceived transaction and acquisition utilities of the featured product in the sales promotions. It was predicted that perceived transaction utility, but not perceived acquisition utility, mediated the efect of sales display on product evaluation (i.e., H4).

Furthermore, Study 2A examined an alternative explanation. That is, we proposed that the key diference between ISP and CSP was that in ISP only one brand was on sale, while in CSP multiple brands were on sale. However, it was possible that consumers simply preferred a featured product in an advertisement with only one brand (i.e., the independent display) over one with multiple brands (i.e., the cooperative display) because processing the former required less cognitive efort [74]. To rule out this possibility, Study 2A included conditions in which no price discounts were mentioned; it showed that display type did not afect consumers’ product evaluation in this case.

## 7. Study 2B: the moderating role of product knowledge

As Study 2A confirmed that discount information was necessary for display type to make a diference on product evaluation, Study 2B fo cused on with discount conditions only to examine the moderating role of product knowledge (i.e., H5). ## 8. Discussion and conclusion

As an established promotional tactic, CSP has recently been widely applied in the online business context. However, empirical research examining consumer responses to the CSP is sparse. Based on the evaluation mode theory [21,22] and TUT [20], the current research shows that while vendors predict that consumers evaluate a featured product in CSP more positively than one in ISP, consumers’ actual evaluation is the opposite. This diference occurs because vendors compare CSP and ISP directly and, thus, are in joint evaluation, a situation in which it is easy for them to judge acquisition utility of the product, and they predict product evaluation according to it. Consumers see either CSP or ISP and, thus, are in separate evaluation, in which they have dificulty accessing acquisition utility, and evaluate the product according to perceived transaction utility. Moreover, the generalizability of the findings is increased by the use of diferent samples, diferent measurements for product evaluation, and diferent target products across diferent studies [76].

## 8.1. Theoretical implications

We believe that this research contributes to the existing literature in several ways. First, this study provides a more enlightened understanding of the determinants of consumer responses to e-commerce marketing campaigns. Previous IS research has shown multiple factors can afect consumers’ responses, such as ways to display information (e.g., whether product information is presented in a schematic way or in a paragraph, [80]; visual-based versus textual-based product presentation, [81,82]), and types of promotion strategies (e.g., displaying or not displaying the number of deals provided by a merchant, [83]; cash-back versus noncash-back promotion, [1]; location-based versu nonlocation-based mobile promotion, [25]). Our research identifies a new factor, namely sales display (i.e., CSP vs. ISP). We showed that consumers evaluated the featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) less positively, although the discount levels (i.e., 40% of) in the two sales displays were the same.

Second, it complements and extends past research on CSP and provides empirical insights for understanding how people react to CSP. Past studies have focused mainly on descriptive discussions of CSP (e.g., Refs. [5,15,16]) or empirical investigations from the perspective of channel management (e.g., Refs [4,17,84]). To the best of our knowledge, our research is the first to empirically examine people’s responses to CSP as compared to ISP. Our results reveal that while vendors believe that consumers evaluate a featured product in CSP (vs. ISP) more po sitively, consumers’ actual product evaluation is the reverse.

Third and relatedly, this research extends our understandings about the misprediction efects caused by diferent evaluation modes [21,22,47]. Previous research has showed that sellers, who are in joint evaluation, overpredict the price sensitivity of buyers, who are in separate evaluation [50]. Analogously, presenters in joint evaluation predict an ofer with an add-on as more attractive, while evaluators in separate evaluation actually prefer an ofer without an add-on [51]. In line with these previous studies, the current research points out that when deciding which promotion strategy to adopt, vendors are in joint evaluation; when encountering a promotion, consumers are in separate evaluation. Therefore, a similar misprediction efect may occur regarding product evaluation.

Last but not the least. this paper is the first to bridge TUT [20] and evaluation mode theory [21,22]. We use TUT as the in-depth foundation to explain why the diference in evaluation modes causes the misprediction efect. That is, our research suggests and verifies that people in joint evaluation (e.g., vendors in the current research) can easily judge acquisition utility of products through comparison and, thus, are more likely to rely on acquisition utility to make a judgment, whereas those in separate evaluation (e.g., consumers in the current research) have dificulty accessing acquisition utility and, thus, are more likely to rely on transaction utility to make a judgment. Our theorization and findings provide a new theoretical perspective and open opportunities for further investigation.

## 8.2. Practical implications

Our findings also provide implications for both online vendors and e-commerce platforms in determining their online promotion strategies. First, our results suggest that online vendors should not rely solely on CSP. ISP also should be considered, as it may help to enhance consumers’ evaluation of featured products, which, in turn, may increase product purchase and market performance. This strategy seems already to have been adopted by some vendors. For example, besides participating in CSP, such as Cyber Monday Sales, some brands also cooperate with deal sites (e.g., dealnews.com; slickdeals.com) to ofer exclusive deals for their brands (i.e., ISP) on regular days [85].

Second, CSP should be utilized strategically. It is true that at first glance CSP seems to have many advantages, such as more options, scale economy, and cost sharing [16]; and sales data for CSP, such as the Tmall 11/11 shopping festival in China, are encouraging. But CSP may not be a good option when a brand vendor aims at promoting a featured product and increasing “store trafic” through that attractive featured product [28,29]. Vendors should choose a promotion strategy based on their main motives.

Moreover, our results also suggest that sales display does not in fluence consumers who are knowledgeable about the product category. Thus, online vendors may find it best to keep in mind that their deci sions about sales display may have an efect only on novices who are more likely to draw on transaction utility to make decisions. Other strategies that increase consumers’ perceived acquisition utility might be more efective in attracting experts.

Finally, our theorization suggests that the misprediction efect oc curs because people in diferent roles draw on diferent types of utilities. This indicates that similar misprediction efects could exist under other contexts. For example, although designers confirm that clinical decision support systems can do better than experienced doctors [86], which suggests such systems can increase the acquisition utility of the patients, patients being served may feel that the nonhuman systems are not trustworthy and, thus, perceive low levels of psychological sa tisfaction (i.e., transaction utility). Thus, patient education that helps to draw their attention to the acquisition utility might increase the acceptance of these systems and increase patients’ benefits in the longterm [87]. For another example, for web designers, they might more focus on the “acquisition utility,” such as the information content and the objective quality. By contrast, users would pay more attention to the “transaction utility” including the information accessibility, web appearance, and ultimate experience. Therefore, balancing the two as pects is a crucial challenge for designers [80].

## 8.3. Limitations and future research

Despite the foregoing contributions, this work also has limitations. First, we only asked participants to play the role of vendors rather than recruiting actual vendors as subjects. It is possible that experienced vendors or vendors with certain characteristics (e.g., with high empathy skills) are more likely to put their feet in consumers’ shoes, and thus the observed misprediction efect may diminish. Future works could, therefore, examine these possibilities.

Second, in the current research, we investigated the efect of sales display only when discounts were involved. However, besides providing discounts, there are other types of promotion, especially in the context of e-commerce, including cash-back promotion [1], location-based mobile promotion [25], coupons ofering [26], etc. In this regard, future research might examine if our findings can be generalized to other promotion types and the possible moderating efect of promotion type.

Third, we investigated only one type of horizontal CSP in which competitive brands from one product category ofer sales promotions concurrently. More work needs to be done to gain a systematic understanding of CSP. One worthwhile extension would be to empirically examine consumers’ responses to other forms of CSP, such as another type of horizontal CSP in which complementary brands from diferent product categories are promoted concurrently, and vertical CSP run by diferent channel members, such as manufacturers and retailers [5,84,88].

Fourth, our only dependent variable was product evaluation. Consumer response is a heterogeneous and complex construct, invol ving product evaluation [89], perceived quality [80,90], unplanned purchasing [91], online impulsive consumption [92,93], online repurchase intention [94,95], and consumer satisfaction [96]. Future research could examine whether our observed efects on product evaluation extend to other types of consumer responses.

Finally, boundary conditions that we did not explore may exist. For instance, whether the product is an experiential product (e.g., tourism product) or a material product [97] and whether or not it is a highinvolvement product (e.g., digital cameras) [98] might also moderate the focal efects. Future research may fruitfully extend the initial model to include diferent moderators.
