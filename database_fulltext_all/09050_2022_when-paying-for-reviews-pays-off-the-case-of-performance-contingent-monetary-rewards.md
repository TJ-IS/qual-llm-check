# WHEN PAYING FOR REVIEWS PAYS OFF: THE CASE OF PERFORMANCE-CONTINGENT MONETARY REWARDS<sup>1</sup>

Yinan Yu Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, 3675 Central Avenue, Memphis, TN 38152 U.S.A. {yyu4@memphis.edu}

Warut Khern-am-nuai Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke St. W., Montreal, QC H3A 1G5 CANADA {warut.khern-am-nuai@mcgill.ca}

Alain Pinsonneault Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke St. W., Montreal, QC H3A 1G5 CANADA {alain.pinsonneault@mcgill.ca}

Several online review platforms offer monetary incentives to motivate individuals to write reviews and keep them engaged with the platforms. While existing studies have examined the effects of completion-contingent monetary incentives (which uniformly reward users as long as they write reviews on the platform), we know little about the effectiveness of performance-contingent monetary incentives (which reward platform users based on the quality of their reviews). In this paper, we examine the effects of receiving performancecontingent rewards on users’ continued contribution in terms of the quantity, quality, and valence of the reviews they generate. We leverage a quasi-experiment research design to analyze a large dataset that we obtain through a collaboration with a large restaurant review platform in Asia. Our evidence shows that after receiving performance-contingent rewards, individuals write more reviews and write reviews that are of better quality. Interestingly, receiving rewards does not significantly affect the valence of subsequent reviews. Our study extends past research by being one of the first to examine how receiving performance-contingent rewards affects subsequent behaviors of reviewers. Our results can also guide platform managers to design efficient and effective incentive policies.

Keywords: Performance-contingent monetary incentives, online reviews, review quality, review quantity, review valence, quasi experiment

## Introduction

In the last decade, online reviews have become essential to most online platforms (Huang et al. 2017). As such, one of the important challenges that platform managers face is how to keep review writers engaged and stimulate continued contributions (Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). This issue is especially important for users who contribute high-quality reviews because these reviews positively impact platforms’ value and sustainability (Van Mierlo 2014). To address this issue, many platforms have opted to use extrinsic incentives, particularly monetary incentives, to motivate their reviewers. Generally, there are two types of incentive mechanisms that online platforms commonly use. The first one, called completioncontingent incentives, uniformly rewards eligible individuals as long as they write reviews on the platform (e.g., Bestbuy.com, Kmart.com). The second mechanism, called performance-contingent incentives, rewards users based on the quality of their reviews (e.g., Epinions.com).

While several studies have investigated the impact of completion-contingent monetary incentives (e.g., Burtch et al. 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018), no prior research has explicitly examined the effects of performance-contingent monetary incentives. This gap in the literature is important because the extensive knowledge on the implications of completioncontingent incentives does not necessarily apply to performance-contingent incentives. First, the two reward mechanisms are prescribed in different manners. While the former is awarded to most eligible individuals who satisfy the task requirement (i.e., write a review), the latter is rewarded only to a few individuals who meet or exceed the standard (i.e., those who write reviews of high quality). As a result, current knowledge from studies on completion-contingent incentives, which mostly focus on understanding the implications of users’ eligibility to receive rewards, cannot be directly applied to performance-contingent incentives because the latter incentive mechanism is likely to affect only those who receive the rewards rather than those who are eligible for them. In addition, it is also likely that the underlying psychological effects of receiving performance-contingent rewards differ from those of completion-contingent rewards. Our paper examines the effects of receiving performancecontingent monetary rewards on the recipients’ subsequent review-generating behaviors and answers the following research questions:

RQ1: Would performance-contingent reward receivers continue to contribute high-quality reviews?

RQ2: Would there be an increase or decrease in the quantity of contributions and the valence of those reviews contributed by a reviewer after receiving the performance-contingent reward?

Answering these two research questions necessitates the development of new theoretical arguments. While it is intuitive that receiving performance-contingent rewards should lead to an increase in the quality of subsequent reviews (e.g., Gibbs et al. 2017; Pink 2011), there exist competing tensions of how receiving such a reward may affect review quantity and review valence. For instance, on the one hand, receiving performance-contingent rewards may encourage recipients to be more persistent, which could positively impact review quantity (Deci and Ryan 1991). On the other hand, receiving performance-contingent rewards is akin to being recognized as an expert by the platform, which may lead the recipients to contribute less due to the complacency effect (Goes et al. 2016). Similarly, rewarding review quality might create a tension as to whether such a reward would affect the recipients’ sentiment (and hence the valence of their reviews) positively through positive attitude spillover (Mabbe et al. 2018) or negatively because of the perceived self-expertise status (Ottati et al. 2015).

We empirically answer our two research questions by ana lyzing a unique dataset obtained through a partnership with a large restaurant review platform in Asia. In the next section, we survey related literature and develop our research hypoth eses by primarily drawing on cognitive evaluation theory (CET).

## Prior Research and Theoretical Underpinnings

In this section, we discuss relevant streams of literature that examine the impact of monetary incentives in the context of online reviews, establish the boundaries of our research, and review prior works that are closely related to our study.

## Related Literature

In general, prior studies on the impact of monetary incentives on online reviews can be broadly classified based on two dimensions. The first dimension relates to the focal point of studies. One group of studies examines the impact of the introduction of a monetary incentive policy on reviewers’ behavior. Authors compare review-related dependent variables before and after an incentive policy is implemented. Another group of studies focuses on the post-policy period and investigates the impact of receiving monetary rewards on reviewer behaviors. Specifically, these studies compare dependent variables of the same users before and after they receive rewards, while the incentive policy remains the same throughout the study. The current paper fits into the second group of studies.

In addition, studies can also be classified based on the type of incentive mechanisms they have examined. The completioncontingent monetaryincentive, which uniformly awards incentives to eligible users as long as they complete required tasks, is the most commonly studied mechanism in the extant literature. The performance-contingent incentive mechanism, which is less studied in the literature, provides rewards to recipients conditional on their task performance. The current paper examines the second type of incentive mechanism. We combine the two aforementioned dimensions to create a 2 × 2 matrix to summarize the extant literature on monetary incentives in the context of online reviews (see Table 1).

As can be seen in Table 1, most studies focused on the impact of introducing a completion-contingent monetary incentive policy (upper left quadrant). These studies examined how the implementation of the completion-contingent monetary incentive policy affected individual-level behavior and/or platformlevel variables. For example, in a randomized field experiment, Burtch et al. (2018) found that when reviewers became eligible for completion-contingent monetary incentives, they wrote reviews more often, but the length of their reviews did not change. Previous studies also examined the impact of introducing a completion-contingent monetary incentives policy at the platform-level. For example, Khern-am-nuai et al. (2018) used a natural experiment research design and a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis to compare platformlevel variables before/after the introduction of a completioncontingent monetary incentive policy. They found that the number of reviews and the number of new reviewers on the platform increased significantly after the platform offered the incentives. However, the average review quality (i.e., review length and the number of helpfulness votes) significantly decreased. In addition, they found that the completioncontingent incentive policy led to inflated positive star ratings on the review platform. Similarly, based on a quasiexperiment at the product level, Wang et al. (2016) found that a completion-contingent monetary incentive policy stimulated a larger volume of reviews, but it had no significant impact on review helpfulness.

Other studies examined the effects of receiving completioncontingent monetary rewards (upper right quadrant). For example, Qiao et al. (2017) examined how completioncontingent monetary rewards affected reward recipients’ behavior (i.e., comparing individual-level behaviors before/ after receiving a reward) while the incentive policy remained the same. They used a quasi-experiment design and relied on the propensity score matching technique to find control reviewers who never received a reward but behaved similarly to those who did. Based on a difference-in-differences analysis, they showed that after receiving a completioncontingent monetary reward, individuals wrote less meaningful reviews. Other studies also examined the effect of receiving completion-contingent rewards on participants behaviors in different contexts. For instance, in the context of online question and answer platforms, Hsieh et al. (2010) found that users answered more questions after they received a reward, but the quality of their answers did not appear to significantly change. Relatedly, Bénabou and Tirole (2006) suggested that receiving extrinsic rewards can induce reward recipients to behave in an undesirable manner (e.g., exerting less effort) because such rewards can negatively impact people’s intrinsic motivation. Overall, the evidence suggests that while completion-contingent rewards have some positive effects, they also create unexpected negative effects. The evidence is consistent in the literature on the impact of introducing an incentive policy as well as in the literature on the impact of receiving a reward.

Table 1 also shows that few studies examined the effects of performance-contingent monetary incentives (bottom row of Table 1). Specifically, Wang et al. (2012) studied the effect of introducing a performance-contingent incentive policy in the context of online reviews (bottom left quadrant). However, it is worth noting that the study examined the effect of introducing a monetary incentive policy that combined performance-contingent incentives with completioncontingent incentives through a laboratory experiment. They found that users who are eligible for the incentives and are also required to disclose this sponsorship wrote reviews of higher quality. Our study, which is situated in the bottomright cell of Table 1, extends the findings of Wang and her colleagues by offering an empirical evidence based uniquely on the impact of receiving performance-contingent rewards. Further, our study examines the effects of receiving performance-contingent rewards on two additional important variables (i.e., review quantity and review valence). The current paper is also based on a quasi-experiment, which complements the evidence provided by laboratory experiments and provides empirical results with a high degree of external validity.

Finally, our study also contributes to the broader literature on the effects of receiving performance-contingent rewards. Past studies have examined such effects in the offline context such as game playing (Ariely et al. 2009) and idea generation (Gibbs et al. 2017). Other studies have also investigated the influence of receiving performance-continent incentives in an organization setting where employees receive performancecontingent rewards as bonuses (Amabile 1996; Pink 2011). Most studies in this research stream only examine the effects of receiving performance contingent rewards on task quality. Our study expands the dependent variable of interest beyond contribution quality while the empirical research context is user-generated content, specifically online reviews.

## Hypotheses Development

In this section, we develop our research hypotheses. Recall that our focus is to examine the impact of receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards on recipients’ subsequent review-generating behaviors. Hence, our hypotheses are based on a comparison of the behavior of the same reviewer before and after receiving a reward, while the eligibility to receive the reward (i.e., the incentive policy of the platform) remains the same throughout the study period. We primarily draw upon the cognitive evaluation theory (CET) to develop our hypotheses.

First, we develop a hypothesis on the impact of receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards on review quality. According to CET, receiving a reward strengthens recipients perceived competence, which typically leads to enhanced performance ( Deci 1975; Deci et al. 1999). Consequently, receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards is likely to enhance intrinsic motivations since the rewards convey information about individuals’ capability to write reviews of good quality. The relationship between performancecontingent rewards and enhanced task quality is also observed in other contexts. For example, Pink (2011) describes how office workers are likely to perform better after receiving performance-contingent rewards. Therefore, we propose that:

Hypothesis 1. Performance-contingent monetary rewards enhance the review-generating behavior of reward recipients in terms of review quality.

Next, we develop our hypothesis on review quantity. As we mentioned above, CET posits that receiving a performancecontingent reward enhances perceived competence and increases recipients’ intrinsic motivation, both of which lead individuals to engage more with the activity and be more persistent in doing so (Deci and Ryan 1991; Ryan and Deci 2000). This suggests that reviewers who receive performancecontingent monetary rewards will generate a larger volume of reviews in the post-reward period. Similarly, Terwiesch and Xu (2008) use an analytical model to demonstrate that a performance-contingent reward structure helped increase engagement and reduce underinvestment in efforts to solve a task in innovation contests.

Other arguments, however, point to the opposite direction (i.e., that performance-contingent reward might lead to lower review quantity). Past literature indicates that the increased reviewer’s perceived competency after receiving performancecontingent rewards can result in overconfidence and lead to lower levels of persistence and engagement (Ferla et al. 2010). Furthermore, receiving a performance-contingent reward can be associated with attaining an “expert” status. Previous studies have found that users contribute less to the knowledge exchange community as they attain higher status (Goes et al. 2016). Moreover, it is possible that reviewers with such a perceived status are likely to try to preserve this status (Levina and Arriaga 2014). As such, because producing high-quality user-generated content requires substantial effort and time (Khern-am-nuai et al. 2017), reviewers who have limited time might try to maintain or enhance the quality of their reviews at the cost of quantity. This type of effect has been observed in the context of socioeconomic status and risk aversion preference (Griskevicius et al. 2011). Given that theoretical arguments and some empirical evidence support two opposing effects of receiving performancecontingent rewards on review quantity, we develop competing hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2A. Performance-contingent monetary rewards enhance review-generating behavior of reward recipients in terms of review quantity.

Hypothesis 2B. Performance-contingent monetary rewards diminish review-generating behavior of reward recipients in terms of review quantity.

Finally, we develop two competing hypotheses on the valence of reviews after an individual receives a reward since prior evidence and theoretical arguments suggest two opposite effects. On the one hand, the increased perceived competence caused by receiving performance-contingent rewards typically leads to an increase in self-efficacy (Bandura 1997). In general, an enhanced self-efficacy leads to a positive attitude toward a focal task or object. For instance, Gangadharbatla (2011) finds that college students’ Internet self-efficacy had a positive effect on their attitudes toward social networking sites. Relatedly, Hsu and Chiu (2004) find that Internet selfefficacy is positively related to users’ attitudes toward e-services. The evidence also indicates that such a positive attitude and evaluation can spillover to other related entities (Mabbe et al. 2018). Therefore, in our context, these findings suggest that receiving performance-contingent rewards might encourage recipients to have a positive attitude toward a review platform, and that this positive attitude might spillover to the perceptions of the restaurants they review. In addition, self-efficacy instilled by performance-contingent rewards may also enhance the perceived experience/expertise regarding the products they evaluate. In that regard, prior studies have demonstrated that individuals who possess more experience with a product tend to evaluate it more positively when rewarded (e.g., Tietje 2002). This literature suggests that reviewers are likely to generate reviews with higher valence after they receive performance-contingent rewards.

On the other hand, it can also be argued that receiving performance-based rewards may lead reviewers to contribute reviews with lower valence. First, in an effort to preserve their expert status associated with receiving performancecontingent rewards, recipients might lower the valence of their contributions because readers tend to perceive negative opinions as more informative (Bateman et al. 2011). Further, previous studies also indicate that an increase in perceived self-expertise status is linked to a more negative attitude in general (Ottati et al. 2015; Paddock and Swanson 1986). Additionally, prior studies suggest that the increased selfefficacy from receiving a performance-contingent reward may also induce a sense of perceived power (Ellyson et al. 1981). In that regard, studies in the performance evaluation literature demonstrate that a person with more power tends to devalue the performance of others (Georgesen and Harris 1998). Hence, we offer the following competing hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3A. Performance-contingent monetary rewards lead reward recipients to generate reviews with higher valence.

Hypothesis 3B. Performance-contingent monetary rewards lead reward recipients to generate reviews with lower valence.

## Data and Empirical Settings

## Research Context

In our study, we collaborate with a large restaurant review platform in Asia. This platform has a performance-contingent incentive program called “Review-of-the-Day” that offers monetary rewards to reviewers who contribute the review deemed most valuable each day. Consistent with the principle of performance-contingent monetary incentives emphasized by Pink (2011), the platform designed this incentive policy to encourage high-quality reviewers to continue their endeavors, rather than simply stimulate users to increase their efforts.

In this program, reviews are evaluated by the platform based on a two-step approach. In the first step, the platform develops an algorithm to filter “high quality” reviews based on several variables, including review length and the number of review helpfulness votes. Then, the platform manually evaluates these reviews, and a panel of judges selects the winner. The award is an exclusive gift such as two movie tickets or restaurant vouchers generally valued at \$5–\$10. Each program winner is notified by email only (i.e., the platform does not make any public announcement about the reward winners). Also, apart from the gift, the winner does not receive any other special recognitions (e.g., special badge, special title) by the platform. The platform provided us with the data, including customer reviews (e.g., review id, reviewer id, date, content length, rating, whether it won the reward, the number of helpfulness votes) and reviewer information (e.g., reviewer id, date of joining the platform, gender) over a period from July 2014 to December 2017. Further, the data of helpfulness votes in our study are also time-stamped, which allows us to measure review quality with more granularity and accuracy.

## Research Design and Data

Since our objective is to identify the effect of receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards on the recipients, a simplistic approach would have been to compare the behavior of reviewers before and after they receive the rewards. However, this approach would make it difficult to attribute the observed difference to the reward itself since previous studies found that review-generating behavior tends to vary across time (e.g., Li and Hitt 2008). For instance, reviewers might naturally write fewer reviews over time. Hence, even if we find that reviewers wrote fewer reviews after they received a reward, such a decrease could come from either receiving the reward or the natural behavior.

To address this identification issue, we employ a matching technique to construct a control group that mimics a randomized experiment. Specifically, for each reviewer who receives rewards (“treated”), we find a control reviewer who behaved similarly in every observable aspect before the treatment (i.e., the time that the treated reviewer receives rewards), but does not receive rewards. Meanwhile, all other circumstances (e.g., eligibility to receive rewards) remain the same for both control and treated reviewers, and both before and after the treatment. As a result, using the control group, we can control for any natural changes in behavior over time. Hence, we can causally identify the effect of receiving performance-contingent rewards.

We select a study period covering 24 weeks before and 24 weeks after the reward receiving date. Note that the treatment in our design is reviewers receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards. Because different reviewers receive rewards on different days, we calibrate each reviewer’s study period by a week index, so that the week a reviewer receives a reward is always week 0. Accordingly, we identify individuals who received rewards during the period from January 2015 to June 2017 as treated reviewers. Reviewers obtaining multiple rewards are excluded from our sample to ensure a clean identification of the treatment effect. Additionally, we only select reviewers who joined the platform at least 24 weeks before they received a reward, so that balanced panel data can be constructed throughout the study period.<sup>2</sup> Finally, we construct a panel dataset at the reviewer-week level such that each observation corresponds to an individual reviewer and each time period is one week.

This study examines four primary dependent variables. We first construct two variables to measure review quality following the literature (Deng et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2016). The first one is $L e n g t h _ { i t } ,$ which represents the average character count of reviews created by reviewer i in week t. The second variable, Helpfulness , represents the average number of helpfulness votes obtained during week t from reviews generated by reviewer i in week t. The variable ReviewCnt represents the number of reviews generated by reviewer i in week t (i.e., the quantity of reviews). The valence of reviews is measured by Rating , which represents the average star ratings of reviews generated by reviewer i in week t. For explanatory variables, Treatment is an indicator variable that takes the value of one if reviewer i belongs to the treatment group, and zero if the reviewer belongs to the control group. After is an indicator that takes the value of one if the observation occurs in the post-treatment period, and zero otherwise. The main independent variable of interest is

ReceivingReward , which is an interaction between Treatment and After . It takes the value of one for the observations from treated reviewers after the treatment starts, and zero otherwise. We control for reviewers’ tenure on the platform by imposing the variable Tenure , which is measured by the number of weeks since reviewer i joined the platform, calculated at week t.

## Analyses and Results

## Main Analysis

We conduct our primary analyses using the propensity score matching (PSM) combined with a panel difference-in differences (DID) estimation. The DID estimation is a commonly used econometric technique to analyze a quasiexperiment research design (Chan and Ghose 2014; Pamuru et al. 2021). Specifically, this technique measures the effect of the treatment by capturing the differences in pre- and posttreatment outcomes between the treatment and control groups. In our context, by comparing changes in the reviewgenerating behavior of reward recipients before vs. after they receive rewards relative to that of control reviewers, we can causally identify the effect of receiving performancecontingent monetary rewards.

## Propensity Score Matching

Recall that we use PSM to construct the control group such that reviewers in the control group behave similarly in terms of observable characteristics to those reviewers in the treatment group in the period before the treated reviewers receive rewards. In other words, we employ PSM to select a control group resembling the treatment group in terms of a set of observable pre-treatment characteristics, so that we may mimic a randomized experimental design. In our case, the propensity score is calculated based on three sets of observable characteristics: (1) review characteristics that reflect the quality, quantity, valence, and rating variance of review generation (i.e., Length , Helpfulness , ReviewCnt , Rating , and Variance ); (2) reviewer demographic information, including gender and tenure on the platform prior to the treatment (Gender and Tenure ); and (3) characteristics of the restaurants each user reviewed, consisting of restaurant popularity measured by the number of reviews and the number of check-ins each restaurant obtained (RestReviewCnt , RestCheckin ); restaurant quality measured by average star rating (RestRating ); the diversity of restaurant perception, measured by the variance of rating (RestRatingVariance ); and the affordability of the restaurant, measured by the price range (PriceRange ). We average characteristics captured by continuous variables across restaurants patronized by the focal reviewer i. In addition, to account for the type of restaurants that reviewers visit, we define RestCategory as the category of restaurants that are reviewed the most by reviewer i. We only include potential control reviewers who share the same with the treated reviewer in the matching process (i.e., after matching, each treated reviewer and that reviewer’s corresponding control reviewer always have the same RestCategory ).

One characteristic of our identification strategy is that our dataset does not have a united treatment start time $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ each treated reviewer receives a reward on different dates while the control reviewers never receive any rewards). Therefore, we adopt a two-stage PSM approach used in prior literature that faced a similar staggered treatment issue (e.g., Qiao et al. 2017). In the first stage, we apply a PSM technique to the entire study period specifically to define a treatment start time for each non-treated reviewer. Then, in the second stage, we perform another round of PSM, but only on the pre-treatment period, so we may create a matched pair between each treated and non-treated reviewer. We provide complete details regarding this two-stage PSM in the appendix. We obtain 132 reviewers in our sample, with 66 reviewers in each group. To evaluate whether our matching attempt is successful, we conduct a balance test on matching covariates between the treatment and control groups after matching. Our results (Table 2) indicate that none of the variables is significantly different between the two groups at the 95% level of confidence $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \mathrm { } \alpha = 0 . 0 5 )$ . Namely, the pre-treatment observable characteristics are balanced so that the control group is comparable to the treatment group before the treatment.

Table 3 presents the summary statistics of our primary variables separately for the two groups in both pre- and posttreatment periods, in which each period is 24 weeks long. The content length, the number of helpfulness votes, and the number of reviews written by the treated reviewers appear to increase after reviewers receive performance-contingent monetary rewards relative to the control reviewers. The difference in average star rating between the two groups does not change significantly.

In addition, we present model-free line plots of the difference of the average value of dependent variables between the treatment group and the control group, along with error bars that represent standard errors. Note that we construct our dataset such that each reviewer receives rewards at week 0. Figure 1 plots the trend of content length differences and the trend of helpfulness scores differences. We also plot the trend of the review volume differences in Figure 2, and that of average star ratings differences in Figure 3.

## Difference-in-Differences Analyses

We first utilize the relative time model (Angrist and Pischke 2008) to formally verify that our dataset satisfies the parallel trend assumption. Our results demonstrate that the parallel trend assumption holds for all of our dependent variables. We then test the treatment effect on our weekly panel data with the following model specification, in which we vary the time windows from 4 weeks to 24 weeks, both before and after the treatment.

The average treatment effect estimated in Panel B (based on the review helpfulness votes) demonstrates similar patterns. After reviewers receive monetary rewards, the average number of helpfulness votes attained by reviews of rewarded reviewers increase by 42.3% in the first four weeks, and this effect slightly attenuates afterward. These results support Hypothesis 1: that performance-contingent monetary rewards motivate reward recipients to generate content of higher quality. In other words, reward recipients appear to devote more effort to write informative reviews, which tends to be significantly longer, after they receive rewards. In addition, it appears that review readers recognize these reviewers valuable contributions and consistently vote for reviews written by rewarded reviewers more after these reviewers receive rewards. Meanwhile, reviewers’ tenure on the platform does not exhibit a significant impact on the quality of reviews.

Next, we consider the review quantity (i.e., the number of reviews) to test Hypothesis 2. We report our regression results in Table 5. The coefficient estimates on Receiving Reward are positive and statistically significant. Therefore, Hypothesis 2A is statistically supported: reviewers who receive performance-contingent monetary rewards generate more reviews. Moreover, taken together with our prior finding, we observe that performance-contingent monetary rewards not only enhance review quality, but also motivate reviewers to generate more reviews, which is highly desirable by the review platform. Additionally, our results demonstrate that while the impact of receiving monetary rewards on review quantity is decreasing gradually, the impact on review quality is relatively stable. It is plausible that this phenomenon occurs because performance-based rewards change recipients perceptions of their capability, which in turn increases review quality, as we explained in our hypothesis development section. Therefore, the impact on quality is relatively permanent, but the impact on quantity is transient.

Finally, we test Hypotheses 3A and 3B. Here, we follow the literature by considering the average review text sentiment as an additional measure of review valence, in addition to review rating (e.g., Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). Because the language of the reviews is not English and there is no opensource tool that allows us to calculate textual sentiment in that language, we use Google API to translate the reviews into English, and then use TextBlob in Python (Loria et al. 2018) to calculate the sentiment score for each review based on the review content. The score is in the range of -1 to 1. We observe that the effect of receiving performance rewards on valence is statistically insignificant for both measure (see Table 6). Therefore, neither Hypothesis 3A nor Hypothesis 3B is supported.

This result is particularly interesting. Even though there exist competing tensions that receiving performance-contingent monetary rewards may affect the valence of subsequent reviews in both positive and negative direction, it turns out that no significant change to the valence of subsequent reviews after reviewers receive the reward is observed. A potential explanation is that receiving performance-contingent rewards, which affirms recipients’ capability and efforts, might instill their sense of community. According to sense of community theory (McMillan and Chavis 1986), people who feel responsible tend to be fair, honest, and just toward other members in their community. Therefore, performancecontingent reward recipients are more likely to provide honest opinions to help others after they receive the rewards, rather than generate more positive ratings attributable to increased self-efficacy or more negative ratings to maintain their expert status.

## Alternative Specification

In addition to matching rewarded reviewers with nonrewarded reviewers by using the two-stage PSM method, we leverage the unique feature of our setting to develop an alternative approach to construct the control group to further validate our findings. In our context, the platform selects the “review of the day” based on the quality of all reviews generated that day, which only a few reviews are considered in the process. Since reviewers have a small probability of receiving the reward on any given day, those who generated reviews of good quality but did not win the reward could potentially serve as a control group. Therefore, we first perform PSM at the review-level based on a set of review characteristics (e.g., review length, helpfulness scores obtained within the day of the review generation, and star rating), such that for each rewarded review , we find a control review that resembles it but did not obtain the reward. In particular, we specify a probit regression and use the single nearest neighbor matching method without replacement and with common support to obtain a one-to-one matched control review for each of the rewarded reviews. As a result, we obtain 296 reviews in our sample, with 148 reviews in each group. As we show in Table 7, results of the balance tests conducted after the matching indicate that the covariates are balanced between the two groups.

We identify the corresponding reviewer of each review, construct the weekly panel data set at the reviewer level, and reestimate Equation (1) using this new matched sample. Our results (presented in Tables 8–10) are robust to this alternative specification.

## Discussion and Conclusions

In recent years, online platforms have increasingly offered monetary incentives to their users to attract and retain content contributors. While the literature on the impact of monetary incentives on online reviews has also grown substantially, it has mainly focused on completion-contingent incentives. Our knowledge on the implications of alternative incentive mechanisms, such as the performance-contingent incentives, especially how this incentive mechanism affects its recipients is severely limited.

This paper breaks new research ground by not only bridging such a gap in the literature but also opening up new avenues for future research on this important topic. The fundamental premise of the current paper is that performance-contingent incentives differ substantially from completion-contingent incentives. Therefore, the former approach is likely to impact review characteristics (quantity, quality, and valence of reviews) differently than the latter approach, which warrants a thorough investigation. Our findings provide support to this assertion. Particularly, our empirical results show that users who receive performance-contingent rewards tend to write more reviews after receiving the rewards. In addition, these reviews are of higher quality while the valence does not significantly change. We explain such results, which fundamentally differ from those in past studies that focus on completion-contingent monetary incentives, by drawing upon cognitive evaluation theory. Specifically, we theorize that performance-contingent incentives trigger psychological mechanisms that are unique to this type of incentives (e.g., perceived competence), and that these mechanisms are most likely different than those at play with complement-contingent incentives.

Our paper contributes to the literature on monetary incentives and online reviews by providing one of the first empirical evidence regarding the effects of receiving performancecontingent monetary rewards. Furthermore, our results also yield actionable insights for platform managers who are especially eager to learn about incentive mechanisms that can be used to retain and motivate valuable content contributors. Particularly, by providing performance-contingent monetary rewards, reward recipients are encouraged to continue con tributing valuable content after they receive rewards. This can have a ripple effect of allowing other consumers to gather more helpful information, leading to higher user satisfaction. This healthy content generation and consumption pattern can also nourish the sustainable development of the entire platforms. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the implementation of performance-contingent monetary incentives may involve operational difficulties, such as accurately and efficiently evaluating review quality. Managers should, therefore, optimize their incentive policy by taking these constraints into account.

Our study is subject to some limitations, which also provide opportunities for future research. First, although we empirically demonstrate the effect of receiving performancecontingent monetary rewards on subsequent review-generating behavior, it is particularly difficult for us to accurately identify the mechanism behind such a change in reviewer behavior. Thus, a qualitative research methodology that allows researchers to access reviewers’ state of mind, such as an interview or a survey, would be suitable for such a research agenda. Second, we contextualize our study in the restaurant review platform. Since the literature documents differences of review generating and consuming behavior between search goods and experience goods, it would be interesting to test whether the effect of receiving performance-contingent rewards remain similar for the reviews of search goods. Third, an additional research endeavor could be used to investigate different effects introduced by varied magnitudes of monetary incentives, which would assist platforms seeking to optimize their respective incentive policies. Finally, future research could empirically investigate the impact of the performance-contingent monetary incentive policy at the platform-level to identify whether any spillover effects exist that apply to the majority of reviewers who do not receive the rewards.

## Two-Stage Propensity Score Matching

One challenge to calculating pre-treatment values of the covariates in our study is that there is no well-defined treatment start time for reviewers in the control group. Since each treated reviewer receives rewards at different dates, there is no unified treatment time for the whole sample and for the control reviewers. To address this issue, we adopt a two-stage PSM approach that was used in prior literatures facing a similar issue (e.g., Qiao et al. 2017). Essentially, we define the treatment start time for control users in the first stage, while we perform the matching between control and treated users in the second stage.

In the first stage, we utilize all data across our study period to identify control reviewers who are similar to the treated reviewers in terms of overall review-generating behavior. If the overall review-generating behaviors of two reviewers are similar, it is reasonable to assign the same treatment start time for the control reviewer as the treated reviewer. Specifically, we conduct our propensity score analysis using the probit model since our treatment variable (reward receiving) is binary. In the probit model, the binary response model is expressed as

$$
P (y = 1 | \boldsymbol {x}) = \Phi (\beta_ {0} + \mathbf {x} \boldsymbol {\beta})
$$

Ö is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, which takes on values strictly between zero and one. x denotes the full set of explanatory variables. In our case, the propensity of receiving the treatment is calculated on variables that reflect the quality, quantity, and valence of review generation (i.e., Length , Helpfulness , ReviewCnt , Rating , Variance ), reviewers’ characteristics (Gender and Tenure ), and restaurants’ characteristics (RestReviewCnt , RestCheckin , RestRating , RestRatingVariance , and PriceRange ) during the entire study period. We use the single nearest neighbor matching method to obtain a one-to-one matched control reviewer for each of the 199 treated reviewers. We also specify a common support option and a caliper of 0.05 such that observations lying outside of the common support are discarded (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). Finally, we obtain 370 reviewers in the first stage matched sample, with 185 reviewers in each group.
