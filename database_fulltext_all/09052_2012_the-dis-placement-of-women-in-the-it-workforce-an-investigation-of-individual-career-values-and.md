# The (dis)placement of women in the IT workforce: an investigation of individual career values and organisational interventions

Jeria L Quesenberry\* & Eileen M Trauth<sup>†</sup>

\*Information Systems Program, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA, USA, email: jquesenberry@cmu.edu, and <sup>†</sup>College of Information Sciences and Technology, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, USA, email: etrauth@ist.psu.edu

Abstract. This paper reports on an investigation of career anchors of women in the information technology (IT) workforce that was directed at enhancing within gender theorising about career motivations of women in the IT profession. Our theoretical lens, the individual differences theory of gender and IT, enabled us to look more critically at how the effects of interventions are embedded in the range of women’s career anchors that takes within-gender variation into account. The analysis demonstrates that organisational interventions must be flexible enough to account for the diversity and variation among women. Further, the analysis shows that it is necessary to move away from ‘one size fits all’ organisational interventions that often reflect stereotypes about women in the IT workforce.

Keywords: gender, women, career anchors, interventions, individual differences theory of gender and IT

## INTRODUCTION

Despite the shortage of information technology (IT) professionals and the organisationa benefits of diversity, women are largely under-represented in IT educational programmes and the IT workforce (e.g. Chabrow, 2007). Hence, it is clear that interventions are needed in response to this IT gender gap as evidenced by the recent special issue of Information Systems Journal on the topic of intervention programmes aimed at increasing the representation of women in Information communication technologies (ICT) (Information Systems Journal, special issue on women and IT: ‘Increasing the Representation of Women in Information and Communications Technology: Research on Intervention Programs)’. Those who have written about the gender imbalance in the IT field have argued that in developing and deploying interventions, it is crucial to recognise that at the core of this issue are organisational and social structura explanations of female under-representation, not essentialist generalisations about men and women (e.g. Wajcman, 1991; Trauth, 2002; 2006; Harding, 2004; Butler, 2006; von Hellens et al., forthcoming). Consequently, developing interventions to address the gender gap requires an understanding of how these factors operate to create opportunities and constraints that influence career choice and retention of female IT professionals. The focus of this paper is on interventions in the IT workplace. Specifically, we report on an investigation of interventions related to career values and motivations or career anchors of women in the IT workforce.

This paper is structured as follows. First, we relate the literature on interventions and career anchors of IT professionals to gender and IT research. In the course of doing so, we highlight several long-standing, yet unsupported assumptions and inconsistencies in the literature about career anchors among women in the IT workforce. We then apply the individual differences theory of gender and IT (Trauth, 2002; 2006) to add theoretical nuance to conventional career anchor theory in order to address the theoretical gaps in the career anchor research. Next, we present findings from an interpretive mixed-method study. The data for this examination come from a qualitative and a quantitative investigation of women employed in the American IT workforce. We discuss the contribution to theory by showing how the individual differences theory of gender and IT has been used to extend career anchor theory. We discuss the contribution to practice by considering the implications for interventions aimed to increase the representation of women in the IT workforce.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Research on gender stratification in historically male-dominated professions provides a basis for understanding interventions used to address the IT gender gap. While interventions have been enacted for more than a decade, it is only recently that the conceptual coherence has been brought to this aspect of the gender research. For example, a recent special issue of Information Systems Journal (von Hellens et al., forthcoming) focused on theorising and evaluating interventions and considered them along a continuum from societal interventions (Ridley and Young, forthcoming) to interventions in the educational arena (Clayton et al., forthcoming) to workplace interventions (Panteli, forthcoming). It is this latter domain of interventions that is the topic of this paper.

Organisational interventions have been widely studied in the context of gender, science and technology in general (e.g. Schiebinger, 1999; Rees, 2000; Acker, 2004) and also in the IT profession in particular (e.g. Henwood, 2000; Kvasny et al., 2005; Lagesen, 2007). Arguably, three main categories of strategies for effective interventions are prominent: equality at a basic level; targeting interventions at the organisational level; and focusing on the long-term aspects of organisational interventions.

The first strategy – equality at a basic level – has been analysed by many researchers. For instance, in a study of gender equality in science in the European Union, Rees (2000) argued that equal treatment, such as equal pay, effective promotion procedures and work–life balance policies, would help to ensure women are treated equally in the workplace. Acker (2004) added that income and status inequalities between men and women are partly created in organisational processes. With regard to the IT workforce. the Dice (2009) Salary Survey estimated that in 2008–2009, women in the S. IT workforce earned an average of 12.43% less than men in annual salary figures as compared with 9.7% in 2007. Hence, establishing equal pay for equa work appears to be a basic organisational intervention that has yet to be fully realised in the IT workforce.

The second strategy – targeting interventions at an organisational level – has also been widely supported. Researchers have argued that interventions should be directed at address ing underlying organisational issues rather than requiring women to assimilate into the existing practices of the profession. Schiebinger (1999) disputed the claim that the solution to the gender gap in the sciences lies simply in adding more women to male-dominated professions and leaving the fields unchanged. She argued that it would be a mistake to assume that women should assimilate rather than modify the professions. Tapia & Kvasny (2004) sup ported her argument and suggested that it is not productive to increase the numbers of under-served minorities in the IT workforce only to have them drop or be pushed out by an unaccommodating industry.

Researchers have offered specific approaches in an attempt to build a greater understanding of how interventions can be used to reshape the underlying organisational policies and programmes. Gravely (2003) argued that the key to leveraging diversity and building inclusion is having more than corporate diversity programmes. Rather, organisations must foster sys temic change and build corporate support in order to create programmes that tie diversity to business value. For instance, Pless & Maak (2004) remarked that organisational environments should allow ‘people with multiple backgrounds, mindsets, and ways of thinking to work effectively together and to perform to their highest potential in order to achieve organisation objectives’ (p. 130). Rees (2000) suggested that positive actions, such as mentoring and networking interventions, should be encouraged in order to support women’s integration process. Lagesen (2007) found that interventions that reduce the masculine image of com puters, and foster a sense of community, and build inclusiveness lead to greater retention of women in the field. Furthermore, Henwood (2000) found that hostile learning environments can be extremely detrimental to retention interventions.

In the third strategy, researchers have acknowledged that organisational interventions must focus on long-term viability to include effective assessment and monitoring procedures. Along these lines, Rees (2000) explained that mainstreaming gender equality entails a wholesale programme of assessment of the gender impact and monitoring of existing and new policies. Further, Acker (2004) argued the gender segregation of work is created and maintained through organisational practices. Hence, organisations are specific types of institutions that invent, reproduce and widely disseminate cultural images of gender. Kvasny et al. (2005) argued that there is currently an insufficient understanding of gender and organisations that is vital for informing policies and strategies to retain women in the IT workforce.

This review of research on interventions raises important questions about the formulation of occupational policies designed to encourage, retain and support women in the IT profession. Applying a finer grained analysis of women’s occupational decisions may help in the development of appropriate interventions that are necessary to address the IT gender gap. One line of investigation that allows for such an analysis has come from the study of career anchors.

Career anchor theory was first developed by Schein (1971) and was used to describe what was considered to be an immutable self-perceived pattern of talents, values, needs, abilities, attitudes and the evolved sense of motives that attracted an individual to a particular occupation. Schein identified eight career anchors: managerial competence, technical/functional competence, entrepreneurship/creativity, autonomy/independence, service/dedication, challenge, lifestyle integration (i.e. desire to balance career with family and personal growth) and security/stability. DeLong (1982) extended the career anchors by adding identity (i.e. desire to have status derived from working at a prestigious organisation) and dividing security/stability into independent anchors: organisational stability and geographic stability.

Over the last two decades, researchers have established evidence of career anchors among IT professionals (e.g. Igbaria et al., 1991; Crepeau et al., 1992; Ramakrishna & Potosky, 2003). While researchers investigating career anchors in the IT workforce have found evidence of their existence, specific findings have been mixed. In an attempt to account for the wide range of findings, researchers have analysed career anchor differences by gender. One of the first such studies was conducted by Igbaria et al. (1991), who reported that women in their study were more lifestyle-oriented and less technically oriented than men.

Other researchers have found that gender is not a determinate factor of career anchors. Rommes (2005) investigated career anchor perceptions of teenagers interested in IT careers and found that gender was not relevant in career anchor identification. Challenging common assumptions about gender, her study found males and females within a given career anchor to be more related to each other than were males or females to their sex groups. Crook et al. (1991) found in their study of over 300 IT personnel that gender did not determine career anchor identification. Quesenberry (2007) found that women identify with a variety of career anchors, and this affiliation is more complex than male–female grouping comparisons.

This review of the career anchor research reveals an important insight about gender and IT careers. Career anchor research that focuses on gender differences is sufficiently contradictory so as to challenge the commonly held assumption that there is a consistent set of ‘woman’s’ career anchors. This suggests that gender is not an immutable factor governing women’s career anchors. This also suggests that the underlying theory of gender used in such research would need to take this variability into account.

Quesenberry (2007) explained that developing appropriate interventions that are necessary to address the IT gender gap also requires a better understanding of both the varied ways in which career anchors are enacted and the varied ways in which women experience interventions in the workplace. Therefore, in an effort to better theorise the variability in career anchors that might account for these inconsistencies in the literature and reveal insights that would inform recruitment and retention interventions, we revisited the assumptions about career anchors for women in the IT profession. Hence, the research question motivating this article was as follows: how can an understanding of career anchor variation among women in the IT workforce in the USA be used to inform interventions aimed at increasing female participation in the profession? (In this paper, we limit our investigation to a USA context. We acknowledge that doing so causes our analysis to be appropriate for a particular national context, and hence, all findings may not be extensible to additional contexts).

## METHODOLOGY

## Theory

While essentialist gender theories posit a common experience for all women, researchers such as Maccoby (1998) suggest that a combination of nature and nurture conceptualised as individual differences can better account for observed differences in human behaviour. Coltrane (1996) explained that three factors are typically used to explain individual differences: biological factors, cognitive factors and socialisation factors. She added that ‘biological predispositions never manifest themselves without the appropriate environmental conditions being present to activate them’ (p. 292). Similarly, feminist authors such as Barrett (1987), Butler (2006) and Hughes (2002) conceptualised within-gender difference as ‘experiential diversity’ arising from racial, class, age, sexual orientation or ethnic differences. Consequently, researchers have argued for investigations that account for greater variation among people through the use of alternative theoretical perspectives. Indeed, Wajcman, one of the most cited authors on gender and technology, has observed that ‘although studies do find evidence of differences between the sexes, the variation within the sexes is more important than the differences between them’ (Wajcman, 1991, p. 157). Although the authors focus on withingender differences, they acknowledge the depth of gender theories in the social construction and cultural orders discourse. It is outside the scope of this article to present a full review of this body of research but encourage readers to refer to the works of Butler, Harding Wajcman and others.

For these reasons, we chose the individual differences theory of gender and IT (Trauth, 2002; 2006; Trauth et al., 2009) as a lens for theorising within-gender variation in career anchors among women in the IT workforce. This theory has been used to explain women’s participation in the IT field by focusing on variation in the ways that women are exposed to, experience and respond to gender relations in the IT profession (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). It enables conceptualisation of gender relations in the IT field at two different levels of analysis One level is concerned with gender group biases that women encounter. The other level of analysis deals with the variation among women with respect to how they respond to gender group biases. This variation is posited as resulting from differences in demographic traits, personalities and individual and socio-cultural influences. Hence, the individual differences theory of gender and IT posits that, collectively, these constructs can explain the underrepresentation of women in the IT field by identifying differences among women in the ways they relate to the IT field, experience gendered discourses about IT and respond to them. The theory is composed of three constructs as presented in Table 1.

## Data collection and analysis

In this research, we used a sequential mixed-method approach comprised of two phases (see Quesenberry, 2007 and Trauth et al., 2009 for a more extensive description of the research approach). The purpose of the first phase was to identify and explore themes related to female career anchors and their role in career experiences and decisions. This phase is composed of analysing transcripts of 92 qualitative interviews conducted with women in the US IT workforce between 2002 and 2006 that were part of another study. The women were drawn from three regions: Boston, Massachusetts, Research Triangle Park/Charlotte, North Carolina and centra Pennsylvania. In this part of the study, open-ended interviews lasting approximate 90 minutes were conducted in order to explore personal characteristics, individual influences and societa influences that served as both barriers and facilitators of women’s careers in IT. Since the participants in the interviews were not explicitly asked about career anchors using Schein’s terminology, the data analysis for this phase of the research required that the authors apply both open coding and selective coding techniques.

## Lifestyle integration

In this study, 84 women (or 28%) identified with the lifestyle integration career anchor. These women particularly valued the ability to balance their careers, family and personal growth. The majority of the women who identified with the lifestyle integration career anchor also expressed satisfaction with their careers (69 women or 82%) with little intention to leave the IT workforce (71 women or 85%).

The most common intervention discussed by the women who identified with the lifestyle integration career anchor was work flexibility programmes such as job sharing, part-time work options and telecommuting. For instance, Kimberly, a 38-year-old project manager, valued her ability to telecommute during maternity leave, which helped her to become one of the first women at her IT consulting firm to be promoted to manager while on leave. Many of the women also spoke about the importance of interventions that allow them to make their own schedules. For example, Francie, a 26-year-old systems developer, enjoys the flexibility to choose her own work days and hours. She explained that the ability to work late and long hours is particularly essential during project deadlines and software builds. Likewise, Alma, 52-year-old business development lead, expressed how she values these programmes:

We have a flex policy [at my organisation] which is really nice. I don’t just work an eight to five job . . . You can work what is called a 980 week where you work nine hour days and then you have every other Friday off. Or you can work eight hours a day and work Monday through Friday. That brings you the flexibility to go do the things to balance the things that you have to do in your life. [Alma]

## Organisational security

Fifty-four women (or 18%) pursued a career in the IT workforce, in part, because of the security, salary and benefits. Several of these women discussed making career changes from other industries into the IT workforce because of the job security. This theme emerged from examining the cultural influences sub-construct (view of the IT profession) of the environmenta influences construct of the individual differences theory of gender and IT. Approximately half of the women who identified with the organisational career anchor also expressed satisfaction with their careers (30 women or 56%) yet expressed little intention to leave the IT workforce (only nine women or 17% intend to do so).

The main intervention discussed by women who identified with the organisational security career anchor was equal pay for equal work policies. For instance, Samantha, a 32-year-ol instructional designer, felt that salary was an important career-deciding factor and shared her view that ‘money was a driving force’. Conversely, the women shared negative experiences where equal pay policies did not exist. For example, Sandra, a 49-year-old Information systems (IS) director, explained:

[I had a male co-worker whose] salary increased astronomically, whereas mine [did not]. bet his salary increased a good 25 percent, maybe 30 percent while I [worked with him]. wasn’t getting increases like that. I got a promotion and they gave me a \$500 increase. He got a promotion and they gave him \$5000. [Sandra]

## Technical competence

Fifty-two women (or 17%) expressed sentiments consistent with the technical competence career anchor. These women spoke about valuing a career that afforded them opportunities to gain proficiency and build skills in technical areas. The majority of these women who identified with the organisational career anchor also expressed satisfaction with their careers (32 women or 62%) with only five women (or 10%) expressing an intent to leave the IT workforce.

These women frequently spoke about the importance of having a manager who is supportive of their varied needs. For instance, Francie felt very comfortable going to her manager if a technical question arose that she wanted to discuss. However, Sandra, a 49-year-old assistant information systems director, was frustrated that her male co-workers typically get praise from the male management, but she did not, despite performing better on the job:

I found that working [at my current employer] is a very male chauvinistic workplace . . . [I fee similar to] this little tiny dried pea in the corner while these guys [my male co-workers] are getting the glory and the praise. And I can do three times the amount of work that they do, in the same time period they take to do it. And they get praised for it. [Sandra]

Casey, a 23-year-old system support technician, spoke about presenting novel technica solutions to a complex problem but not being heard because their co-workers had a low perception of their aptitudes. In addition, Cynthia, a 21-year-old software developer, felt it was difficult to work with her co-workers because they underestimated her aptitude. She felt that as an African American woman in the IT workforce, she did not fit in because of her gender and race. As a result, she had to ‘prove’ her ability to work on technical projects. Hence, an intervention that the women spoke about as an important component of their retention decisions was having a supportive manager. They viewed this as a vehicle to gain specialised skills, make progress towards their goals and, subsequently, make a positive impact on their careers.

## Challenge/variety

Forty-two women (or 14%) spoke specifically about how doing IT work is a challenge. Some of these women spoke about working in a male-dominated industry as a challenge, in itself, that they enjoyed taking on. Overall, 32 of the women (or 76%) who identified with the challenge/variety career anchor felt very satisfied with a career in the IT workforce. Likewise, these women were less likely to turnover than women identified with any other career anchor. Several of the women mentioned that they would have little sense of organisational loyalty if the challenge or variety of their job was threatened.

Job progression and training programmes were the main interventions raised by the women who identified with the challenge/variety career anchor. The women felt these interventions were necessary to help them learn new skills, transition into more challenging positions and keep them from growing stagnant in their careers. For instance, Ivanna, a 40-year-old system administrator, explained why the variety of IT work attracted her:

Somehow I got into IT and I saw the type of activities going on. There was always action going on, and I liked that. It wasn’t the same boring thing. It wasn’t like being in accounting or banking. It is always something different. The work varies, and you always have to keep learning. That is what really attracted me to IT. [Ivanna]

## Managerial competence

Thirty women (or 10%) identified with the managerial competence career anchor. These women expressed an interest in working in IT and building an in-depth knowledge of the managerial aspects of their careers. This theme emerged from examining the personal influ ences sub-construct of the environmental influences construct of the individual differences theory of gender and IT. The women identified with managerial competence expressed moderate levels of career satisfaction (only 18 women or 60% felt satisfied with their career). Yet only four women (or 13%) intend to leave their career.

Mentoring and executive leadership programmes were the main interventions raised by the women who identified with the managerial competence career anchor. The women explained that these interventions are necessary for building managerial skills, identifying opportunities and establishing upward mobility within their organisations. Several of the women also expressed an interest in participating in job shadowing programmes where they could model their career upon success strategies of other female executives. For instance, Chloe, a 30-year-old technology marketing manager, wanted to move into a management position in the sales division of her company. Thus, she purposely built a mentoring relationship with a female co-worker already employed in that type of position. She felt this was crucial in helping her to identify and create career opportunities for advancement and retention in the organisation.

## DISCUSSION

The research question investigated in this article was as follows: how can an understanding of career anchor variation among women in the IT workforce in the USA be used to inform interventions aimed at increasing female recruitment into the profession? This examination was accomplished by focusing on particular career motivation and intervention themes related to gendered experiences that emerged from data. Throughout the analysis, we used two theories: career anchors and the individual differences theory of gender and IT to understand the variation in career values and motivations among women in the workforce. Our findings also provide evidence of three main categories of strategies for effective interventions identified in prior research: equality at a basic level; targeting interventions at the organisationa level; and focusing on the long-term aspects of organisational interventions. ## Gender equality in the organisation

This study demonstrates that equality in the organisation is vital in order to address the IT gender gap. For example, the women who identified with the organisational security career anchor spoke at length about how salary and benefits are important considerations in their occupational decisions. Hence, equal pay for equal work is a necessary intervention for women who identify with an organisational security career anchor. Further, the women who identified with the managerial competence career anchor spoke about how gender stereotypes and equal treatment is an important element for career progression. As Linda, a 51-year-old software architect, explained, ‘I believe that women don’t get promoted based on gender in a lot of places'. Thus. establishing egua/ treatment by reducing barriers for women interested ir pursuing management careers is a necessary intervention for females who identify with a managerial competence career anchor. If organisations want to recruit and retain more women, it is evident these interventions will make a difference.

For organisations developing and implementing interventions to improve the representation of women, it is important to re-examine the diversity discourse of the IT workforce to understand how it can be reached from multiple integrated perspectives. For instance, Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006) argued that we should take a comprehensive view of diversity that builds upon the notion of ‘diversity as difference’ and include in our consideration not only demo graphic differences but also socio-cultural and individual differences. Consistent with this premise, the results of this investigation suggest that the reason for the conflicting findings in the gender and career anchors literature is that gender alone is not a determining factor. That is, the fact that no consistent pattern of gender-based career anchors has emerged from the research suggests that a different type of gender theorising is needed.

The lack of clear gender-based differences calls into question essentialist gender theories as differences between men and women from this study suggest that more gender theorising is needed that is able to account for differences with respect to within-gender human behaviour. Thus, understanding why a woman is aligned with a particular career anchor is based on more than simply knowing her biological sex; it appears to be based on a variety of constructs including life experiences, talents and preferences. This finding is particularly important because it demonstrates the role of female agency in career choice and retention decisions. As the women in this study have shown, a ‘one size fits all’ interventions approach can be problematic.

## Targeting interventions at the organisational leve

Our findings also have implications for how organisations can use programmes and policies as interventions to increase participation of women in the IT workforce. Building an inclusive workplace is based on welcoming a range of perspectives rather than forcing conformity. Kalev et al. (2006) argued that a solid approach in this attempt lies in practices that assign corporate responsibility for change and encourage people from different parts of the organisation to take responsibility for pursuing the goals of integration. Consistent with this thought, the participants in our study spoke to the importance of supportive managers, mentoring and executive leadership programmes and work–life balance initiatives. Women identified with the technical competence career anchor spoke in great depth about having supportive managers and open environments. In contrast, women identified with the managerial competence career anchor spoke in depth about the importance of networking programmes to help build their managerial exposure and connections.

It is also important to consider the role of cultural context when considering interventions. Studies have shown that the relationships between gender and IT are different across countries. For example, in our analysis, we found ample evidence of women being interested in technology, which challenges a common view in much literature in the field. In order to account for these varied and sometimes contradictory findings, an increasing number of researchers are focusing on the role of individual differences to help explain phenomena about gender relations. For example, child-care policies may not be sufficient in some countries; elder care or telecommuting provisions may be needed as well. In addition, multinational corporations may need to consider whether their policies are consistent with cultural pressures on women in a particular country. Hence, while there may be common symptoms of the problem of the IT gender gap, we believe it is necessary to construct interventions that are consistent with the context in which they appear.

## Focusing on the long-term aspects of organisational interventions

Finally, our findings also demonstrate that organisations should think long-term about interventions. Namely, our results demonstrate the complex nature of the ways in which career anchors are manifested in the experiences of women. These findings show that women are typically aligned with more than one career anchor that cluster, dynamically, as a woman moves through her career.

It is also possible that some career anchors (e.g. lifestyle integration) change over time as women’s personal responsibilities shift (e.g. in and out of being the primary care provider for small children, aging parents, etc.). In the qualitative phase, the selective and open coding afforded the researchers the opportunity to examine career anchor identification across a woman’s entire life history vs. at a single point in time – as was the case with the quantitative part of the study. This revealed, e.g. the fact that the technical and managerial competence career anchors appeared at different times in the women’s lives. Technical competence typically appeared earlier in their careers during secondary or post-secondary education. However, values about managerial competence were recognised later in their careers once they had experiences as managers or the ability to move up in their careers. In contrast, the COI used in the quantitative phase restricted the women to expressing career anchors for the particular moment in time when the data were collected and limited them to expressing a single career anchor.

Given the range of career orientations and possible clustering effects, organisations interested in the recruitment and retention of women should consider career intervention programmes to specifically address the possibility of multiple career paths and career stages. Furthermore, career advancement opportunities and rewards should be diverse enough to satisfy the variety of values and motivations of all women IT professionals. Hence, our theorising of career anchors presents a ‘new story’ about career motivations that needs to be communicated to opinion leaders as an intervention

## CONCLUSION

In creating interventions to increase the representation of women in the IT profession, it is crucial to develop those that influence young girls’ perceptions of careers in the IT workforce. Part of that approach includes the influence of public perceptions in general – parents, teachers, media – so as to influence middle-school girls’ perceptions. Yet before we can get to the ultimate goal of interventions that reverse repellent perceptions about IT careers for young girls, we also have to address the actual ‘repellent factors’ currently in the workplace. To accomplish such a goal, we need nuanced theorising of the problem of career motivations that takes within-gender variation into account. This study demonstrates that organisational interventions must be flexible enough to account for the diversity and variation among women. The analysis shows that it is necessary to move away from ‘one size fits all’ organisationa interventions that often reflect stereotypes about women in the IT workforce.
