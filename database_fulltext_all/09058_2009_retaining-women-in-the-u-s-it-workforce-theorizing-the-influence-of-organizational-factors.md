# Retaining women in the U.S. IT workforce: theorizing the influence of organizational factors

Eileen M. Trauth<sup>1</sup>, Jeria L. Quesenberry<sup>2</sup> and Haiyan Huang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Information Sciences and Technology, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Information Systems Program, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>School of Management, Purdue University Calumet, Hammond, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Eileen M. Trauth, College of Information Sciences and Technology, The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, U.S.A.

## Abstract

The challenge of meeting the demand for information technology (IT) workers is addressed by examining three important organizational factors that affect women’s retention in the IT field. Much of the research on gender and IT assumes a unilateral effect: all organizational factors affect all women in the same ways. An alternative view that is explored in this research is that withingender differences offer rich insights into the gender imbalance in the IT profession. The individual differences theory of gender and IT enabled us to examine variation in organizational influences on women through analysis of transcripts from in-depth interviews conducted with 92 women in the IT workforce in the U.S.A. The results show that three organizational factors – work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring – affected the women’s career development in a range of ways. Our findings shed new light on what has been interpreted by other researchers as contradictory findings because our theoretical starting point is the assumption that women are not all the same, that within-gender variation is expected and that it provides an opportunity to develop a deeper understanding of gender relations in the IT field. Using this theory we were able to identify opportunities for the development of interventions by linking the themes embedded in the three workplace factors to the constructs of the theory. The individual identity construct revealed the ways in which a woman’s demographic and professional characteristics affect her career choices. The individual influences construct focused attention on the ways in which differences in personality, abilities, and influential people shape one’s career. Finally, the environmental influences construct characterized contextual influences on women’s participation in the IT profession. Our findings show that both research and interventions directed at increasing the retention of women must be flexible enough to respond to the variation that exists among women and within IT workplaces. ## Introduction

As both developed and developing nations move resolutely into the information age they are faced with a significant human capital dilemma. There is a continuing – in fact increasing – demand for an information technology (IT) workforce to fuel this expanding information economy (Trauth, 2000, p. 9). However, IT labor force predictions suggest that demand will not be met. This is a result of the impending retirement of the baby boomers combined with declining enrollment in IT degree programs. The latter is a result, in part, of fears about job losses due to outsourcing. This situation requires that serious attention be given to factors influencing the recruitment and retention of the 21stcentury IT labor force. Panko’s (2008) analysis of the IT labor force since the dotcom bubble outlines the dimensions of the dilemma in the United States (U.S.). The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics has projected that while jobs of all types will grow by 10.4% between 2006 and 2016, IT jobs will grow by 25.2% during that same time period (p. 188). Despite this optimistic projection, enrollment in IT degree programs has declined precipitously. Panko argues that an important need in addressing the IT workforce dilemma is to focus on gender since only 25% of computer-related positions are currently occupied by women (p. 194).

In recognition of this need, there exists a plethora of research on gender and IT as indicated by the number of research projects funded by such entities as the European Union and the National Science Foundation, the recent publication of an Encyclopedia of Gender and Information Technology (Trauth, 2006), the publication of special issues of Information Technology and People (Adam et al., 2002) and Information Systems Journal (von Hellens et al., forthcoming) on gender and IS, and the establishment of a track on gender issues in IT at the 2009 AMCIS conference. Coincident with this research and publication trend has been the establishment of organizations devoted to developing interventions to address the under representation of women in the IT field such as the National Center for Women and Information Technology (www.ncwit.org) in the U.S. This volume of research and intervention activity serves to illustrate the complexity of this need that requires equally complex solutions.

At the core of this complexity is the challenge of how to theorize gender under representation. Some have theorized the gender imbalance in terms of gender essentialism. That is, biological differences between males and females are used to explain women’s under representation. Others have theorized the gender imbalance as resulting from structural problems in society, which affect all women in the same ways. What both of these modes of theorizing have in common is that they assume a unilateral effect: all factors (whether biological or sociological) affect all women in the same ways. However, an alternative way of theorizing this issue is to view within-gender differences as a potential source of rich insight into the under representation of women in the IT field. This line of thinking would enable researchers to focus on both individual and environmental factors that account for those women who enter and remain in the IT field, and those who do not. The research presented in this article was conducted in response to this need for greater theorizing of within-gender variation in factors influencing women in the IT field. The particular focus is on organizational factors that help to explain the gender imbalance.

Organizational factors in the IT workplace constitute one set of determinants of women’s under representation in the IT field. Indeed, Ahuja’s (2002) review of the research literature posits that organizational factors wil negatively influence women’s choice of and persistence in IT careers if they perceive the workplace to be an unwelcoming, male-dominated, hostile environment. Her model of determinants of women’s careers in IT includes both social (e.g. cultural biases) and structural factors (e.g. lack of role models and mentors, and the proportion of women in top management). Similarly, a study by Wardell et al. (2006) of 2823 male and female IT professionals found that women are nearly two and a half times more likely than men to leave the IT labor force. The authors speculate that this lack of retention is due to a combination of organizational factors (e.g. organization size, climate, and environment) and societal factors (e.g. children and domestic responsibilities). This finding is consistent with Tapia et al.’s (2004) review of the research literature regarding the retention of women in the IT field. They identify the need for ‘gender/race/ethnic-appropriate mentors, sponsors, or role models in the work environment’ (p. 149). Workforce Aging in the New Economy that industry and policy initiatives to attract more women into the profession have not been met with success and the ratio of women in IT remains unchanged. Hence, these findings point to a link between organizational factors, and recruitment and retention within the IT workplace. Organizational factors can make a differ: ence both positively and negatively, and are not only comprised of formal policies (such as mentoring programs) but also informal behaviors (such as gendered exclusion from social networks) and attitudes (such as a negative stereotype that women are not capable of doing technical work or of being high level managers). The concern reflected in this literature is that when a woman reaches a threshold of exposure to such repellent factor she is more likely to leave that particular workplace or the IT field altogether.

While these studies point to the role that organizational factors play in explaining the gender imbalance in the IT field, there is a need for finer grained analyses of how these factors are enacted. Such analyses are needed in order to pinpoint places where interventions could be implemented to positively influence women’s workplace experiences. Those interventions could either be the insertion of proactive corporate policies or the removal of repellent practices. But to do this requires a better understanding of both the varied ways in which these factors are enacted and the varied ways in which women experience these factors in the workplace. Hence, there is a need for further research into the variation in women’s experience of organizational factors in the workplace. The objective of this article is to address this gap by reporting on an examination of within-gender variation in organizational factors that help to explain the under representation of women in the U.S. IT labor force. The particular focus is on factors that influence the retention of women. Much of the research to date is focused on recruitment of the individual – which seems to imply that once a woman has entered the IT profession, gender issues will not persist. In contrast, our interest is on factors in the workplace that help to explain women’s retention in the field.

A deeper scrutiny into the ways in which organizational factors affect women’s everyday workplace experiences (both positively and negatively) can, in turn, inform the promotion of existing positive behaviors and the mitigation of negative influences through formal and informal workplace practices. Through such interventions women’s retention in the IT field can, thereby, be increased. For this reason, we undertook the research project reported in this article. The research question motivating our study is: How do factors in the workplace affect the experience of women in the U.S. information sector? We wanted to understand not only what particular factors are affecting women in the workplace, but also how this is occurring. The ultimate objective is both to advance theory and to inform the development and implementation of interventions. We believe that the practical implication of this way of theorizing the gender imbalance is that it can inform new and more appropriately targeted interventions. These workplace interventions would include policies and procedures that organizations employ in order to improve the recruitment and retention figures of female IT professionals.

We begin by examining research to date on this topic. We then present an analysis of our data drawn from life history interviews with women in the U.S. IT workforce. We go on to suggest implications of our findings for workplace interventions. We conclude by considering implications for theory and research. We would like to note, at the outset, that we are considering a global issue – the worldwide under representation of women in the IT labor force – from a U.S. perspective. In doing so, we acknowledge that while there might be generic manifestations of the gender imbalance in the IT field, the resolution needs to fit the particular national or regional context. Thus, interventions that might work in the European Union or Asian countries, for example, might not be feasible in the U.S. (Adya, 2008). Hence, we believe it is necessary to construct contextually appropriate interventions.

## Gender retention in the IT workforce

Three factors consistently appear in both the practitioner and the scholarly literature about the retention of women in the IT field: work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring. These factors have recently received considerable attention in U.S. practitioner circles due to the publication in June 2008 of The Athena Factor: Reversing the Brain Drain in Science, Engineering and Technology by Harvard Business Review (Hewlett et al., 2008). This report analyzes the career trajectories of women in science, engineering and technology (SET) in the private sector, a category that includes IT careers. The findings document the seriousness of the retention issue for women. It reports that women hit a ‘breaking point’ in their mid- to late 30s which has resulted in 52% of highly qualified SET women quitting their jobs. The report’s finding shows that the sources of this retention crisis come from issues related to work–life balance, organizational climate (expressed as hostile macho cultures, reward systems and extreme work pressures) and mentoring (expressed as severe isolation and mysterious career paths).

Outside the U.S. similar results have been found. For example, Tattersall et al. (2006) investigated the working conditions and the cultural barriers affecting women in the IT industry in North West England. Their findings show that work–life balance, organizational culture and lack of equal opportunity are barriers experienced by women IT professionals in that region. Similarly, a report published by the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI Report, 2004) on Women in the U.K. IT industry reveals that women are drifting away from IT careers at two points: as a result of motherhood; and at a more mature phase in their careers. Work–life balance is cited as one of the main reasons why women with children leave the IT industry (DTI Report, 2004). The lack of supporting social networks, mentors, promotion prospects, adequate training and development suggest reasons why women often experience ‘glass ceilings’ during their mid-career progression (DTI Report, 2004).

## Work–life balance

The term ‘work–life balance’ refers to the negotiation of time and energy demands between one’s professional and personal lives. It is also referred to as work–family balance, management of work–family conflict, work–life conflict, work–life interference and/or work–life convergence. It reflects an individual’s positioning with respect to workplace (career) roles and personal life (non-career) roles (Greenhaus et al., 2002; Duxbury & Higgins, 2003). According to Greenhaus & Beutell (1985), the balance of work and life is ‘a form of inter-role conflict in which the role pressures from the work and family domains are mutually incompatible in some respect’ (p. 77). Greenhaus et al. (2002) explain that work–life balance contains three components: time balance, involvement balance and satisfaction balance. Time balance refers to devoting an appropriate amount of time to work and life roles. Involvement balance means allotting an equal level of psychological engagement to both work and life roles. Satisfaction balance means deriving an equal level of satisfaction from both work and life roles.

There is a large body of research on the consequences of work–life imbalance, pointing to negative impacts for individuals. From an individual perspective, work–life imbalance has been found to cause job dissatisfaction (Thomas & Ganster, 1995), job stress (Judge & Bretz, 1992; Armstrong et al., 2007) and overall poor well-being (Igbaria et al., 1994). A lack of workplace flexibility is also linked to depression (Googins, 1991), difficulty in falling asleep and/or staying asleep, changes in appetite, and tension related aches and pains (Guelzow et al., 1991). A study of work–life imbalance in the Canadian workforce showed that the stress level from work–life imbalance almost doubled over the past decade. The resulting reduction of sleep and rejuvenating time contributes to psychological and physical illness (McNaughton, 2001). Work–life imbalance also has an effect on home and personal lives. Reeves (2001) argues that work–life imbalance creates the need for more rigid and fixed timetables and schedules.

Some researchers and managers choose to view the negotiation of work–life balance issues as the sole responsibility of the individual. That is, negotiating the time demands of the workplace and the home are seen as the individual’s responsibility in a conceptualization of work and management that is unable to change. Others, however, conceptualize work–life balance as an organizational factor insofar as the construction of jobs, workplace culture and promotion norms all affect the balance between work and life responsibilities, and are, in fact, subject to change. This conceptualization recognizes that work–life imbalance can impair organizational effectiveness, primarily by affecting absenteeism, productivity at work and high turnover rates (Higgins et al., 1994). The majority of these problems arise as a result of child care and family obligations. Parents who are unable to provide adequate and affordable care for their children may experience time lost from work or a decrease in productivity. In addition, interruptions due to family obligations can infringe on work. Duxbury & Higgins (2003) report that employees with high role overload are three and a half times more likely to have high levels of absenteeism due to physical, mental or emotional fatigue than counterparts with low levels of role overload. The direct and indirect cost of absenteeism due to work overload is estimated to be between \$4.5 billion and \$6 billion per year in Canada alone. Another serious impact of work–life imbalance is high turnover rates (Duxbury et al., 1992; Glass & Estes, 1997), which are both expensive and disruptive to employers. Duxbury and Higgins report that employees with high role overload are 2.3 times more likely to report high turnover intent than counterparts with low levels of role overload.

Within the IT workforce literature, stresses related to work–life balance emerge consistently among the reasons for women’s under representation in the IT field. Webster (2002) argues that family structures and female roles may vary by cultural context, but typically women continue to be the primary provider for domestic and childcare responsibilities. Consequently it is particularly difficult for women ‘to reconcile with the working rhythms of IT work’ (p. 6). Armstrong et al. (2007) examine the linkages among managing family responsibilities, work stress, work schedule flexibility, and job qualities. Their findings suggest that women are facing both the challenges of family responsibilities and the challenges of work-related stress such as long working hours and investing effort and time to improve skills. Quesenberry et al. (2006) also found that societal messages are complex and difficult to digest and are processed in different ways by different women. Yet, these factors have a great impact on women’s decisions to pursue and remain in the IT workforce. A study by Riemenschneider et al. (2006) of barriers facing women in the IT workforce of a Fortune 500 firm used revealed causal mapping to explore factors influencing retention. The findings show that two of the four workplace barriers they had faced that had influenced voluntary turnover decisions or those of their female co-workers were linked to perceptions about family responsibilities and flexibility to determine work schedule.

It is also evident that work–life balance issues have the most negative effect on women’s performances in computer-related fields during early career choice and advancement to management stages (Ahuja, 2002). Ahuja adds that women may have to neglect certain family obligations to be eligible for promotional opportunities similar to those of men. In addition, Igbaria & Chidambaram (1997) found that women in IT tend to be younger, have shorter job tenure, have fewer years in the industry, and hold lower positions than men. Furthermore, Baroudi & Igbaria (1994) point to family-related constraints as a partial explanation for the under representation of women in managerial positions. Sumner & Werner (2001) found the burden on family-career balance from overtime and administrative tasks to be a barrier to women in management.

## Organizational climate

Organizational climate is related to and yet distinct from organizational culture (Ott, 1989; Reichers & Schneider, 1990; Sparrow, 2001). Organizational culture examines group understandings that represent ways of perceiving, thinking or feeling in relation to group problems. Hence, organizational culture is based on explicit system-sanctioned behaviors (Sparrow, 2001). Schein (2004) defines organizational culture as a pattern of shared basic assumptions that is considered valid and used as a mechanism to perceive, think, and feel about organizational problems. Payne (1991) suggests that culture is focused on beliefs, values, norms, and premises which underpin organizational behavior.

Organizational climate, on the other hand, is more implicit and reflects the distinctive patterns or regularities of behavior and the material artifacts they produce. It represents a synthesis of perceptions about a relatively stable set of value orientations of an organization, which, in turn, influence the behavior of employees (De Witte & De Cock, 1988). Organizational climate is rooted in an environment’s values, beliefs, and assumptions (Denison, 1996). In this sense, organizational climate is considered relatively static. According to Sparrow (2001), organizational climate taps ‘individual or personal constructs, which may in the interests of organizational analysis be aggregated across organizations in order to reveal conscious and shared perceptions of the organization’s standards and expectations for behavior’ (p. 89, italics original to author’s work). Researchers have defined a set of dimensions considered to be the most salient aspects of organizational climate (e.g., Likert, 1961; Campbell et al., 1970; Denison, 1996). Litwin & Stringer (1968) argue that climate encompasses both organizational conditions and individual reactions. They defined organizational environments in terms of nine climate dimensions: structure, responsibility, reward, risk, warmth, support, identity, conflict, and standards. Roldan et al. (2004) add that organizational climate studies, in both social sciences and management, typically focus on outcomes such as salary, promotions, and career aspirations.

Over the last several decades two key findings about organizational climate have appeared in the research literature. First, it has been shown to be an important indictor in career retention decisions. Second, organizational climate is typically not gender neutral. Guzman et al. (2004) suggest that the organizational climate of many firms in the IT workforce is characterized by the following: pride in possessing restricted IT skills; high value of technical knowledge; need for constant self reeducation in a continually changing environment; extreme and unusual time demands; feelings of superiority relative to the IT user community; high pervasiveness in non-work contexts (use of IT in leisure time); a typical lack of formal work rules in the IT occupational setting; and finally, cultural forms such as frequent use of technical jargon and social stigmatization as geeks or nerds. Little research has focused on applying these outcomes to the perceptions that women hold toward working in the IT workforce.

Roldan et al. (2004) believe that prior research on women in organizations supports a claim that organizational climate is not gender neutral. For instance, Wajcman (1998) states that ‘organizations are a crucial site for the ordering of gender and the distribution of power y gender power pervades perceptions of merit, performance, career choice, and authority’ (p. 54). Echoing this argument, Valian (1998) explains that perceptions or stereotypes of gender differences in society also shape norms existing in organizational contexts regarding quality and quantity of work. Furthermore, Tapia et al. (2004) add that the majority of recruitment and retention literature focuses on assimilating women and minorities into the dominant organizational environment as opposed to modifying existing values and perceptions within the organization. Ahuja (2002) also posits that occupational climate will negatively influence a woman’s choice of and persistence in IT as a career if she perceives the climate to be an unwelcoming, male-dominated, hostile environment.

Morgan (1998) adds that organizations have traditionally been dominated by what he considers ‘masculine value systems’. He defines masculine value systems as systems that tend to focus on logical, linear models of thought and action and the drive for results even at the cost of network or community building. In addition, Morgan argues that organizations are mainly a man’s world in which women were often physically and psychologically marginalized or excluded. As a result, he suggests that smaller female networks may evolve and can possibly conflict or create tension with the male power structures. Morgan argues that a shift is on the horizon: organizations with corporate climates that are shaped around a feminine value system tend to be more ‘likely to balance and integrate the rational-analytic mode with values that emphasize more empathic, intuitive, organic forms of behavior’ (p. 129). Furthermore, he says the evolution of flatter organizations, which better cope with uncertainty and turbulence of modern environments, typically reflect feminine qualities. Morgan believes that this development will contribute to a transformation of corporate climates away from the dominant influence of traditional masculine values.

## Mentoring

According to Higgins & Kram (2001), a conventional mentoring relationship usually involves a senior person working in the prote´ge´’s organization to provide him or her with both career and psychosocial assistance, and to assist with the prote´ge´’s professional development. Mentoring programs in organizations serve two major functions for prote´ge´s – career and psychological functions (Kram, 1985; Allen & Eby, 2004). The career functions of mentoring programs include enhancing the prote´ge´s’ technical and managerial skills, providing prote´ge´s with sponsorships and access to different networking opportunities, increasing their visibility, imparting tacit information such as organizational norms and cultures, and, as a result, preparing prote´ge´s for career advancement (Kram, 1985; Allen & Eby, 2004; Logan & Crump, 2007). The psychological functions of mentoring programs include enhancing the sense of competence and confidence of prote´ge´s (Kram, 1985; Allen & Eby, 2004). Mentoring programs are expected to provide benefits to mentors as well. They have the opportunity to develop new ideas, assess ideas, and acquire knowledge during the process of mentoring prote´ge´s (Mullen & Noe, 1999).

In general, mentoring relationships can be categorized into formal and informal mentoring (Ragins & Cotton, 1999; Singh et al., 2002). Formal mentoring relationships often are initiated through organizational assistance and develop through organizational interventions, while informal mentoring relationships usually develop on the basis of mutual identification between mentors and prote´ge´s, and the fulfillment of prote´ge´s’ career needs (Ragins & Cotton, 1999; Singh et al., 2002). An emerging form of mentoring is e-mentoring, referring to the means of establishing and maintaining mentoring relationships online using computer-mediated communication tools.

Headlam-Wells (2004) describes an e-mentoring project called EMPATHY (funded by the European Social Fund) that aims at providing mentoring opportunities to aspiring women managers and women on career breaks by pairing them with female managers within an ementoring community of practice. This form of mentoring is viewed as providing flexibility and easy access to numerous resources for those women who are constrained by disability, busy schedules or geographical locations, and providing extended networking opportunities to those women who would normally have difficulty in finding appropriate mentors because of their gender and ethnicity (Headlam-Wells, 2004).

There is considerable research to suggest that mentoring can serve as an important intervention to enhance the retention of women in the IT workforce. The role of mentoring in achieving the goal of overall workplace diversity (i.e. with respect to both gender and race/ ethnicity) consistently appears in the literature. For example, the analysis by Kalev et al. (2006) of federal data from 708 private sector establishments from 1971 to 2002 combined with survey data on employment practices examined the effect of different approaches to achieving greater workplace diversity. They found that mentoring was more effective than diversity training at moderating managerial bias. However, it was only moderately successful when compared to establishing explicit responsibility for diversity in an organization. With respect to women, in particular, the research literature shows that successful women point to having had higher level executives as mentors (Hennig & Jardim, 1977; Combs & Tolbert, 1980; Fitt & Newton, 1981; Morrison et al., 1987). For another example, Hewlett et al. (2008) examined factors influencing the career trajectories of women in SET in the private sector. Their findings suggest that the lack of a mentor or a clear road map to guide the mid-career progression of those women professionals in their mid- or late 30s is one of the major factors that account for the attrition of female talent.

Within the IT profession, mentoring has been shown to be correlated with professional advancement for both men and women (Cougar & Zawacki, 1980; Ferratt & Short, 1986; Turner & Baroudi, 1986; Cougar, 1988; Sumner & Werner, 2001; Allen et al., 2006). Studies of mentoring that focused on gender reinforced the benefit of mentoring to women’s careers. Logan & Crump (2007) interviewed 90 professional women living in New Zealand about the value of mentoring in their careers. Their findings revealed an uneven availability of mentoring. Women participating in this study who worked at large government or multinational organizations (13%) had access to formal mentoring programs. The other women in this study who participated in mentoring (44%) developed it serendipitously and usually with male senior colleagues. Other women in the study wanted but were unable to find mentors. There was general consensus that mentoring contributed to career advancement. Logan & Crump (2007) recommend that organizations make available to women mentors who are either internal or external to the organization. By the same token, Ahuja (2002) points to the absence of mentoring as key among the barriers to women’s retention in the IT field.

Higgins & Kram (2001) assert that the outcomes of mentoring programs are affected by both work environment and individual level factors. Darling et al. (2006) point out that individual differences such as gender, age, and racial, ethnic, and cultural background shape the needs of different prote´ge´s, and, hence, will influence the process and outcomes of mentoring programs. They argue that there is a need to closely examine and articulate these variables in order to provide insight into the key elements that are necessary for mentoring programs to be effective and successful.

Ragins & Cotton (1991) argue that women have fewer options than men to find same gender mentors because of the small number of female senior managers. And in cross-gender mentoring relationships Ragins & Cotton (1991) found that male mentors in their study granted women less time than they granted men. A study by Lyness & Thompson (2000) shows that female professionals reported greater mentoring barriers than did male professionals, and that female executives reported receiving less effective mentoring than did their male peers. Allen & Eby (2004) suggest that mentoring effectiveness may vary because of the gender of the mentorship participants and the characteristics of the relationship, in that male mentors tend to provide more career mentoring whereas female mentors tend to provide more psychosocial mentoring to their prote´ge´s. In addition, female prote´ge´s tend to receive more psychosocial mentoring than male prote´ge´s, but there is no differences in career mentoring that female and male prote´ge´s receive. Therefore, they propose that it may be helpful for individuals to cultivate multiple mentorships with mentors of different genders.

Soe & Yakura (2008) argue that while mentoring is important, in order to be effective at addressing the retention of women in the IT field, mentoring programs need to be part of a broader goal of changing organizational culture and assumptions about women and technology work. In the absence of this wider change, they warn, there is a danger that negative stereotypes could be reinforced. They cite evidence from other fields that bears this out. Peleeger & Mertz (1995) point out that it is important to have the organizational support of the mentoring process, reward the participants (both mentors and prote´ge´s), and make the mentoring process an integral part of organizational practices. De Vries et al. (2006) evaluated a 10-year mentoring program to support women’s advancement in Australian higher education. In this program, mentoring served not only to support women but also to expand the male mentors’ insights into women’s gendered experiences in the workplace.

## Methodology

## Theoretical framework

The preceding review of the literature shows that work– life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring are three organizational factors that have been shown to affect women in the IT workplace and, possibly, their retention. But where the literature is less vocal is in telling us exactly how these factors are affecting women’s workplace experiences. We believe the reason is due, in part, to the type of gender theorizing that underlies much of the research. Critics of gender and IT research have commented on the narrow theorization of gender and IT (e.g. Adam et al., 2004). That is, the current body of gender research focuses primarily on women as a group. Thus, much of the gender and IT research involves implicit – generally essentialist – gender theorizing. Essentialism is a theory of inherent group-level characteristics (in this case, possessed by women) that result from biological, psychological, or sociological factors (Marini, 1990; Wajcman, 1991; DeCecco & Elia, 1993).

In contrast, our approach to understanding how workplace factors affect women in the IT field is to problematize this notion of ‘women’s experience’. In this study we sought to theorize gender and the IT profession in a way that enabled us to more critically analyze the ways in which organizational management and individual experience of these three factors affect women’s experience of the IT workplace. Thus, in order to address our research objective of better understanding how these organizational factors affect the retention of women in the IT workplace, we sought theoretical tools that would help us to look more critically at the particular ways in which specific women experience these factors. This need drove our theoretical and methodological choices.

Rather than employing an essentialist theory that assumes a common IT workplace experience for all women, we sought to theorize gender and the workplace by invoking a theory that could account for the varied experiences of women in the IT workplace and the sometimes contradictory findings resulting from gender research. For example, although studies have reported that work–life balance is an important organizational issue, contradictory evidence has been found about how these issues may or may not vary by gender. Igbaria et al. (1991) reported that women in their study of IT professionals were primarily concerned with work–life balance issues when evaluating career values. The authors explained that female employees in their study tended to be relatively unconcerned with solving problems, service, and entrepreneurship. Yet, more recent studies of women in the IT workforce have conflicted with the findings of Igbaria’s study. For instance, Gomez-Mejia (1990) conducted a study of employee values in a high-tech corporation and found contradictory evidence that occupational differences were greater than gender differences. The author found that female managers were more similar to male managers than to female production workers at this particular corporation. Furthermore, Crook et al. (1991) found in their study of over 300 IT personnel, that gender was not a determinate factor in career values and motivations. Rather, men and women placed equal value on stable careers, helping others and on challenges in their careers.

Contradictory findings have also appeared in studies of gender and culture. Even though the under representation of women in IT is a worldwide phenomenon, studies have shown that the relationships between gender and IT are different across countries. For example, Weil & Rosen (1995) found contradictory evidence when they examined the level of technophobia among first year university students from 23 countries. Their findings showed that females in Israel and Hungary showed more computer anxiety than males in these two countries, but males in Thailand, Italy, and Kenya showed more computer anxiety than females in these three countries. Even within a country, the relationships between gender and IT have been shown to be different and dynamic. For example, Clarke & Teague (1994a, b) studied Asian and Caucasian computer science and engineering students in Australia, and found that while the Caucasian students viewed computing as a male domain, the Asian female students did not. Similarly, Trauth et al. (2008a) reported that due to the influence of their cultural backgrounds, some Asian women were not exposed to a societal message that women are not well suited for technical work. On the contrary, they were encouraged by families and teachers to pursue studies in SET fields.

In order to account for these varied and sometimes contradictory findings, an increasing number of researchers are focusing on the role of individual differences to help explain phenomena about gender relations. Maccoby (1998) suggests that a combination of nature and nurture conceptualized as individual differences can better account for observed differences in human behavior than essentialist gender theories that posit a common experience for all women. Coltrane (1996) acknowledges that a number of constructs can be used to investigate individual differences, but emphasizes three that are typically used: biological, cognitive, and socialization. She adds that ‘biological predispositions never manifest themselves without the appropriate environmental conditions being present to activate them’ (p. 292). Barrett (1987), Butler (2006), and Hughes (2002) believe that within-gender difference arises from racial, class, age, sexual orientation, or ethnic differences. Consequently, researchers have argued for investigations that account for greater variation among people through the use of alternative theoretical perspectives. Finally, Wajcman (1991) observed that ‘although studies do find evidence of differences between the sexes. the variation within the sexes is more important than the differences between them’ (p. 157).

For these reasons we chose the individual differences theory of gender and IT (Trauth, 2002, 2006; Trauth & Howcroft, 2006; Trauth et al., 2008a, b, 2004) as a lens for theorizing the experience of women in the IT workplace. This theory has been used to explain women’s participation in the IT field by focusing on variation in the ways that women are exposed to, experience and respond to gender relations in the IT profession (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). This theory conceptualizes gender relations in the IT field at two different levels of analysis. One level is concerned with gender group biases that women encounter. The other level of analysis deals with the variation among women with respect to how they respond to gender group biases. This variation is posited as resulting from differences in demographic traits, personalities, and individual and socio-cultural influences. We found this theory to be particularly suitable for this study because it enabled us to theorize the interaction of individual factors (such as parenthood) and organizational factors (such as cultural norms and policies in the workplace related to work–life balance) in an effort to better understand retention issues.

This theory is comprised of three constructs with associated sub-constructs and concepts. The individual identity construct consists of two sub-constructs: personal demographics, which includes concepts such as age, race, nationality, socio-economic class, and parenthood status; and career items, which includes concepts such as the industry in which one works and the type of IT work in which one engages. This construct was used to examine the work–life balance factor. The individual influences construct also consists of two sub-constructs: personal characteristics, which includes concepts such as educational background, personality traits and abilities; and personal influences, which includes concepts such as mentors, role models, and significant life experiences. This construct was used to examine the mentoring factor. Finally, the environmental influences construct consists of four sub-constructs related to the geographic region in which an individual lives. These are: cultural influences, which includes concepts such as national, regional or organizational attitudes about women or about women and IT; economic influences, which includes concepts such as cost of living; policy influences, which includes concepts such as laws about gender discrimination, and policies about maternity leave; and infrastructure influences, which includes concepts such as the existence of childcare facilities. This construct was used to examine the work– life balance and organizational climate factors. The individual differences theory of gender and IT argues that, collectively, these constructs can explain the under representation of women in the IT field by identifying differences among women in the ways they relate to the IT field, experience gendered discourses about IT, and respond to them.

This theorizing about within-gender variation as it relates to women in the IT profession draws from several feminist theories, thereby bridging feminist and IS gender literature. Employing Rosser’s (2006) classification of feminist theories related to gender and technology, this individual differences theory of gender and IT has theoretical linkages to: liberal feminism, socialist feminism, feminist standpoint theory (FST), postmodern feminism, and poststructuralist feminism. It is consistent with liberal feminism’s focus on removing barriers that prevent equal access for women to jobs historically seen as a male domain. But it also relates to strains in socialist feminism which considers gender to be among the societal factors such as class that explain the ‘social shaping of technology’ and technology professions. The recognition in the individual difference theory of gender and IT of individual characteristics and experiences aligns it with FST which emphasizes the situated knowledge of marginalized individuals (Harding, 2004) towards the goal of emancipation. Finally, the influence of postmodern and poststructuralist feminism are in evidence in this theory insofar as it recognizes that women do not all speak with a unified voice. Postmodern feminism believes that the variation in women’s national, class and cultural identities means that the category of ‘woman’ can no longer be regarded as smooth, uniform, and homogenous (Brooks, 1997). Because this theory provides a robust way in which to focus on the differences within rather than between the sexes, we found this to be the most appropriate theoretical perspective to guide our investigation of the ways in which organizational factors affect women’s experiences in the IT workplace.

## Data collection

An interpretive epistemology and a qualitative methodology were employed in this research because the goal was to uncover the subjective meanings held by the participants with respect to organizational factors and their influence on retention decisions. Data collection was accomplished through open-ended, face-to-face interviews with 92 female practitioners in the U.S. IT workforce as part of a larger, multi-year National Science Foundation-funded research project. These interviews lasted approximately 90 min and were carried out by the first author between 2002 and 2006. During the interviews each participant was asked to relate her own ‘life history’ as an IT professional by describing her earliest encounters with IT, her educational experiences, her career as an IT professional, and her own thoughts on the topic of gender and IT. Further detail about the interview items is provided in the Appendix.

The interviews explored three main areas. The first area explored the participant’s individual identity. This included demographic characteristics such as age, ethnicity and race, and educational and work histories. The second area explored the participant’s individual influences. This included personal characteristics such as interests, abilities, and personality traits. It also included significant people and experiences that influenced her career and personal development. The third area explored environmental influences on the participant’s path into the IT profession. These questions asked about the participant’s subjective perception of gender, and gender and IT in the environment in which she lived and worked. This environment included both the particular region in which the woman was currently living and working, and the organizational environment of her workplace.

Purposive sampling techniques were used in order to recruit women who represent a range of geographical locations, ages, demographic backgrounds, educational backgrounds, levels of management and job classifications, relationship statuses, and family compositions. The sample was comprised of women living in three geographical regions of the U.S.: the Northeast (greater Boston area of eastern Massachusetts), the Southeast (Research Triangle Park and Charlotte areas of North Carolina) and the Mid Atlantic (the area of central Pennsylvania consisting of small towns and bordered by Johnstown in the west and Reading in the east). These three regions were chosen for the economic and cultural variation they provided. Boston has a long-established high-tech industry due to its rich academic climate and the dense concentration of knowledge-intensive firms. The Charlotte and the Research Triangle Park areas of North Carolina have similar, albeit newer, information sectors that are fed by more recently established clusters in the high-tech industry. In contrast, central Pennsylvania has a small IT economy and does not have an established cluster of high-tech industries. In addition, the cultures of these three regions range from cosmopolitan and liberal eastern Massachusetts to an emerging cosmopolitan yet more conservative region of North Carolina to the rural and conservative region of central Pennsylvania. Hence, conducting the interviews in these different regions facilitated examination of the influences of the socio-cultural environment on women’s work and life.

Thirty-two of these interviews were conducted in Massachusetts, 30 in North Carolina, and 30 in Pennsylvania. The women range in age from 21 to 58 with the median age being 42 years old. Sixty-one of the women are married, 6 are unmarried but in committed relationships, 17 are single and 8 are divorced/not remarried. The racial/ethnic identity of the participants includes European Americans, African Americans, Asian Americans, Hispanics/Latinas, and Middle Eastern women. The degree concentrations range from traditional IT-related majors (e.g. MIS, CS and engineering) to liberal arts, psychology, and nursing. In addition, job titles include CIO and vice president of IT, programmer/project manager, systems integrator, software architect/engineer, quality assurance engineers, IT administrators, web developers, consultant and small business owner. A range of industries were also included such as the medical, financial and educational industries, and the primary and secondary IT sectors. The interviewees were assigned pseudonyms to guarantee confidentiality.

## Data analysis

All interviews were tape recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were then coded using a combination of selective and open coding techniques. To perform the coding process the following steps were followed. First, an initial coding scheme was developed based on the interview guide, an extensive analysis of prior gender and IT research and an a priori categorization derived from the constructs, sub-constructs and concepts in the individual differences theory of gender and IT. A considerable amount of effort was devoted to the development of this highly detailed coding scheme which was developed collaboratively by the first author and her two research assistants. Throughout the coding process, inter-coder reliability was sought in order to guarantee consistency of coding style across different cases by all the researchers (Leedy & Ormrod, 2005). In keeping with an interpretive epistemology, inter-coder reliability was achieved by applying the emergent coding scheme to a set of transcripts produced in prior gender research. When discrepancies among the three coders arose, they were discussed and analyzed until the differences were resolved and consistent conceptual understanding of the transcripts’ coding was achieved. At that point, detailed documentation about the meaning of the particular codes was produced for future reference. In this way, consistency across coders was achieved.

After the coding scheme was established and documented, the interview transcripts were subjected to selective coding. Selective (or theoretical) coding is the process of applying pre-defined codes to the appropriate areas of text of an interview (Glaser, 1978). During this process each interview transcript was read and segments of text were identified with the codes in the coding scheme. Once each interview transcript had been sub jected to selective coding, the interview was then subjected to open coding. Open coding is a part of the grounded approach to qualitative analysis developed by Glaser and Strauss based on inductive and iterative development of data categories (Strauss, 1987).

This was done for those sections of text that did not fit into the existing coding scheme. That is, open coding was used when a ‘breakdown’ occurred. According to Agar (1986), a breakdown occurs during interpretive analysis when a new theme is emerging. In this way, any conflicting issues were resolved using breakdown resolution in order to identify new categories or to modify existing categories. Following the approach taken by Trauth & Jessup (2000), open coding was conducted via an inductive process of identifying and organizing emerging themes. Themes from the interview text that did not fit into existing constructs, sub-constructs and concepts in the coding scheme were highlighted and a descriptive comment was made in the margin of the document. As multiple interviews were coded, patterns among these emergent themes became evident, were discussed until agreements were reached, and were subsequently added to the coding scheme. This process was iterative as categories were contentiously identified and organized in the coding scheme. Each interview transcript was read and coded until all themes in it could be categorized into a construct, sub-construct or concept included in the revised coding scheme. This typically required four to five reads of each interview.

After each interview transcript had been subjected to both selective and open coding, the interview and the codes were entered into a qualitative analysis software program (QSR N6). This software was used to facilitate computer-based analysis of the data. First, the original transcripts were imported to establish a database. Second, the entire coding schema was implemented into the software to set up tree nodes and serve as a basic structure for coding. Third, the corresponding text units of each transcript were coded following the coding strips and notes marked on each transcript document during the manual coding process. QSR N6 is capable of a variety of data analyses, such as searching relevant texts, generating different forms of reports, exploring nodes at both higher and lower levels, and exploring the relationships between or among different nodes.

## Evaluative criteria

Standard evaluative criteria employed for interpretive research in the information systems field was employed (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham & Sahay, 1999; Trauth & Jessup, 2000; Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2006). These include contextualization, triangulation, member checking, and authenticity. First, contextualization was achieved by analyzing the social and historical background of the regions in which the research was situated. Through the process of triangulation, interview data were supplemented by data from participant observation and from published materials about gender and the IT sector in the regions involved in the study, and about the economy of the regions. Member checking was achieved by inviting feedback from each participant about the emerging interpretations being developed. At the conclusion of each interview the participant was provided with a sample of the publications from the research to date and the website of the research project. Participants were invited to send follow-up feedback after reviewing these materials. Finally, authenticity was achieved by presenting the findings to audiences who were representative of the participants in the study. These included presentations to: industry meetings, individual companies, women in IT forums, academic conferences, and other gender and IT scholars. The purpose was to collect critical feedback about the interpretations and, in particular, to assess the extent to which the interpretations resonated with these representative audiences.

## Findings

The influence of three organizational factors – work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring – on women’s retention was theorized through analysis of interview data about their lived experience in the IT workplace. The importance of these three factors to the women who participated in this study is evident in the amount of the interview that was devoted to them. Following the coding of all the transcripts it was found that the 92 participants in this study, collectively, generated a total of 101,206 text units. Further analysi of these coded text units by each of these specific factor revealed the following emphases. Comments about work–life balance, including general, negative, and positive comments, were made by 78 participant (84.78%). The total number of text units devoted to thi factor is 13,299 (13.14%). On average, each of these 78 participants made 170.5 text units of comments on work– life balance. Comments about organizational climate, that spoke to attitudes towards women working and attitudes towards women working in IT, were made by 87 participants (94.57%). The total number of text units devoted to this factor is 13,948 (13.78%). On average, each of these 87 participants made 160.3 text units of comments about organizational climate. Finally, com ments about mentoring were made by 74 participant (80.43%). The total number of text units devoted to this factor is 6,807 (6.73%). On average, each of these 74 participants made 92.0 text units of comments about mentoring. When examining the importance ranking by either the number of participants or the number of text units, the order is: organizational climate, work–life balance, and then mentoring. When examining the ranking by the average number of text units of each of these participants, the order is: work–life balance, organizational climate, then mentoring. From this we can conclude that when participants did produce comments about work–life balance issues, they usually tended to be lengthy comments. The total number of comment made about these three factors is 34,054 or 33.64% of the total number of text units generated by the participants. The fact that one-third of the data resulting from these interviews is devoted to the topics of work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring tells us that the women considered these to be important topics in thei life history stories. Below we consider each of the factor in greater detail.

## Work–life balance

It should be noted that a question about work–life balance was not explicitly asked in the interviews. Rather, the women, themselves, introduced this organizational factor in the course of telling the story of their career progression in the IT field. For example, Alma, an IT business development lead, volunteered that her main priority is ‘balancing career and family life’. Early on she made the decision to have only one child so that she could devote as much time as possible to raising her daughter. Donna, a quality assurance professional, offered the viewpoint that work is extremely important in her life, but as a single mother she does not have the luxury of always putting work first. She is the primary provider for her daughter both financially and emotionally.

Several of these women also discussed ways in which culture influenced attitudes about women’s roles. Carol, a senior course developer, explained how her Chinese cultural heritage influenced her views of women and their domestic roles. She felt that women are responsible for two jobs in life: one outside the home and one inside of it. Likewise, Kayla, a product manager, explained how her Argentinean background influenced her views of women and their domestic roles:

In Argentina, the society is similar to here, in that women don’t work and women are responsible for the house and for the kids. The men are the ones that go outside and work. When I talk about what I want for my family, I want a family that is united and has strong ties. y It is impossible to have both worlds – to have the most organized house with the kids doing 1000 enrichment classes. And smile and not always be tired, and have a house that looks like a traditional house. And at the same time, work full-time and have a stressful job. y I have to give up one of them or get a trade-off where none of them is complete. y I want to return home. I want to pick up my girls. Return home and spend time with them. [Kayla]

The desire to balance personal and work lives appeared to be more of an issue when women had young children and became less of a focus as their children aged. It also expressed itself in very different ways across the women. Sylvia, a director of academic technology services, explained that for a number of years she valued a career that allowed her to balance work and personal life in order to care for her young children. When her children were older she did not place as much importance on work–life balance issues. For her, work–life balance was related to emotional and time resources. For Miranda, senior staff consultant, maintaining work–life balance referred to financial resources. Because her children attended a private secondary school, at that point in her career her children were a ‘strong motivator’ at work, and having a salary that could pay tuition bills was very important.

Flexibility in the IT profession was a theme that most often arose in discussions of work–life balance. However, it took on distinctly different forms in the experience of the women interviewed. Women such as Kayla believe that the IT workforce is inherently incompatible with balancing work and life:

I find in high tech that pressure is very high with time. The deadlines are usually very strict and there are continuous peaks of pressure. You can’t arrange pressure. It is much harder to arrange the peaks of pressure with your life outside of work. If the kids are sick or something happens at home it is harder to manage. [Kayla]

Eighteen of the 92 women described the IT workforce as a ‘workaholic’ culture with a ‘24/7’ (i.e. 24 h a day, seven days a week) work schedule made possible by the asynchronous and ubiquitous nature of computing. While this theme was expressed by women of all ages, it was most prevalent among women with children. Candace, a programmer analyst, has experienced a growing expectation that employees work extra hours and be ‘constantly available by computer or phone’. She feels this paradigm is incompatible with home life. She attributes this trend to technological advances that make it possible for people to be always accessible and, consequently, continuously on call. Others noted the demanding nature of technical work that frequently required long work hours. Fay, a department head for telecommunications and network engineering technology, explained how this affects her role as a mother:

[Follow up question] Do you think in looking for causes of women’s under representation, do you think the work–life balance for women in the IT field are substantially different and do you think that’s much of a reason, much of an explanation? [Interviewer]

Yes. The most and especially the IT department because at two o’clock in the morning the network’s down and you’ve got to do something. But your child may be sick or you’ve got to tell your husband and some of these women aren’t married and have kids, they’re single parents. Where do you take your child at two o’clock in the morning because you’ve got to go? Sometimes you can do it from your computer at home and you’re up all night trying to fix something. Then you got to take the kids to school, you’ve got to do this you’ve got to do that. I think IT is much more consuming in your life: keeping up the certifications, the time to learn new stuff, and trying to balance that with your family, it’s tough. [Fay]

Irene, an IT manager, added that she did not take maternity leave when her son was born because of the high value she placed on her professional life. But she also acknowledged the difficulties of doing so:

I didn’t take a maternity leave [when my son was born], which was a big thing in 1971. y I realized that I did not want to be a stay-at-home mom and I wanted to go back to work. y As it invariably happens I was on an application where I was on call. It was difficult. I think having a family or any responsibility, it could be an elderly parent or it could be anybody, but certainly a child [is difficult]. [Irene]

Yet other women documented the variety of ways in which their IT workplaces afford them the flexibility they need to balance work and family responsibilities. Debra, an IT consultant, chose a career as an independent IT consultant so that she could make her own work hours and schedules. Francie, a software engineer, and Alma both noted the importance they place upon being able to make their own work schedules so that they can better juggle the demands, timelines, and requirements of the job:

The environment that we have here is very laid back. Especially in the development department where if you want to come in at 8:30 AM and leave at 4:30 PM that is fine. Or if you want to come in at 9:00 AM and work until 5:00 PM [that is fine too]. As long as you get in your 40 h, [the managers] don’t care how you do it, which is very nice. y And I think that the environment that they try to convey here is a comfortable environment. If you want to wear sweat pants, fine wear them. We don’t care as long as we don’t have a special client [in the office]. Whatever makes you more comfortable. If you want to take an hour for lunch and go out and play golf go ahead. Just make sure you do vour time. [Francie

We have a flex policy here [at my organization] which is really nice. I don’t just work an eight-to-five job. Today is an off day where we have a choice of working. That is why there are not many people here today. You can work what is called a [company term] week where you work 9 h days and then you have every other Friday off. Or you can work 8 h a day and work Monday through Friday. That brings you the flexibility to go do the things to balance the things that you have to do in your life. [Alma]

Ease of commuting and telecommuting was another expression of workplace flexibility raised by several women. Ivanna, a systems administrator, took a different job in order to have a more flexible commute. Kimberly, a project manager, was able to telecommute during maternity leave and was one of the first women at her IT consulting firm to be promoted to manager while on leave. Nancy, a webmaster, enjoys telecommuting because she can make her own hours. Rose, a director of IT research, planning and architecture, feels that telecommuting gives her more control:

I wanted to have more control over my own schedule. y [and be] able to bring in some income, but at a schedule that was much more convenient to me, in terms of care for my daughter and the amount of time I was actually home. [Rose]

Other forms of IT workplace flexibility experienced by women in this study include a 4-day work week and working part-time while one’s children are young.

## Organizational climate

The role of organizational climate in the women’s careers represents a composite of answers given to questions in the interview about gender attitudes and to unsolicited statements about management support. One expression of the organizational climate was exclusion from the social networks that are beneficial in terms of gaining new skills, job leads, and mobility in their organizations. Sandra, an assistant information systems director, described the ‘boy’s club’ in her workplace where the men go to lunch together often and women are generally not invited. Fay felt the social network was closed to women:

You can’t get into the good old boy network. It is difficult. y You are excluded. Well that is how I feel. [Fay]

A few of the women pointed out that exclusion from these social networks limits their ability to build technical skills and knowledge. Debbie, a senior technical support engineer, explained how she is excluded from the male development group at her organization. Ivanna described her experience of isolation from her male coworkers:

Even in server management, the guys just tend to close up. They don’t want to share information. Let’s say, for example, something new comes out. The guys huddle together right away and they start messing with it and they don't include vou. So vou're on vour own. [Ivannal

Another part of the organizational climate relates to attitudes towards women working. The women spoke about gender stereotypes, workplace barriers, perceptions about aptitude, and gender biases related to physical appearance. They discussed the difficulties of reconciling their professional development with a socially constructed view of women as primarily mothers. Many women discussed the difficulty they faced in obtaining IT jobs because of attitudes towards women working outside the home. For instance, Janet, an IT marketing manager, spoke about a female co-worker of child bearing age who was held back by gender stereotypes. During an interview she was told, ‘Well you are young, you are going to get married and have children anyway, so there is really no point’ in hiring you. Pamela, a UNIX administrator, also experienced stereotypes about women working in the paid labor force:

[At my job I hear] people saying, ‘Women can’t work as hard. They gotta take care of their kids.’ That kind of thing. But if a man says, ‘Oh, I gotta pick up my son,’ well he is being a good father. He is putting his family first. [Pamela]

Sandra described a job interview in which she experienced such stereotypes and the impact they had on her career:

[The interviewer] said ‘well you are going to quit anyway.’ He said ‘you are going to get pregnant and quit. Then we will have to hire somebody else, or train somebody else to fill in while you are on maternity leave.’ I said ‘I already have two children and I am not [currently] married and I am not planning on having anymore children.’ And he said, ‘You will. You will get married and you will get pregnant and then we will be stuck here.’ I was not hired for that position. [Sandra]

To overcome these gender stereotypes and biases, the women shared accounts of having to work harder and longer hours than male co-workers. Joanne, a software developer, and her husband juggle child care responsibilities; it does not fall only on her shoulders. Carol adds that there is a misconception that female workers will leave a company to take care of their children or that their minds will only be on their children during the day. Ivanna says her life challenges this misconception: she has three children and an adopted niece and nephew, yet does not miss or leave work anymore than do her male counterparts.

According to Gina, an IT market researcher, the IT workforce is comprised of two stages. In the first stage an employee can progress in her career through hard work and motivation. But in the second stage it becomes difficult for women to progress. Gina explained that during the second stage, ‘there is this sort of ceiling that you hit’ that makes it difficult to progress into upper management. Casey, a system support technician, and Sandra both talked about their experience of presenting a novel technical solution to a complex problem. In both cases their voices were silenced by their co-workers’ low opinion of their technical aptitude. Cynthia, an information analyst, also felt it was difficult to work with her co-workers because they underestimated her aptitude. She felt that as an African American woman in the IT workforce she did not fit in because of her gender and race. As a result, she had to ‘prove’ her ability to work on technical projects.

Organizational attitudes about women working as it relates to one’s appearance was also raised by women in this study. They discussed this in several different ways. One was in talking about dress. The women explained that dressing professionally is important to establishing credibility and respect in their positions. Mia, a chief information officer, explained that she did not look the part of a stereotypical IT manager:

I was always very self conscious because everybody looked at how I dressed, what shoes I wore, what handbag I had. It was hard. I was young. I was a woman. I stood out. [Mia]

They also spoke about the role of attire in establishing credibility and respect in technical positions. Elsie, a website manager, explained that physical appearance of people at her organization is important and that she attempts to conform in order to fit in. In particular, she tries to dress in a more business casual look to resemble a ‘computer geek’ instead of a business person. Likewise, June, a business desktop consultant, explained:

You need to dress a certain way as a female to get even a little bit of respect.y [I had a vice president who once told me] ‘Maybe you should smile more and be more approachable.’ [I thought to myself] ‘What does that have to do with me doing my job?’ [June]

Those women who were older than the stereotypic age of the average IT worker felt additional pressure to also appear younger in order to fit in with their co-workers. Elsie is circumspect about her age because she is 47 years old and believes that most of her co-workers are approximately 20 years younger. Miranda explained:

I try real hard not to [mention how old I am]. I needn’t mention how many years I’ve been married. The job I’m in now I’ve been in two years and it took me a long time to get here. Most of the people I work with are in their 30s, maybe 40s. So I’m way over the hill. I don’t look it and hopefully I won’t be a target for job reduction, however, if I looked my age I wouldn’t belong. [Miranda]

In addition to dress and age, Sandra introduced another dimension of appearance that affects women: one’s voice. Sandra explained that it has been difficult for male co-workers to accept her technical solutions because she comes across as too assertive. So she started taking voice lessons so that she would sound less aggressive:

I've been told that I'm very assertive. But. I've also been told, because I’ve been to counselors about my job, why do I have these problems [with male co-workers]? One counselor said to me it’s your voice. You’ve got to work on your voice. You have to take voice lessons. [Sandra]

An important component of mitigating the negative influence of co-workers was management. However, the women’s experiences of management support were both positive and negative. Francie noted that her ability to speak with her supervisor whenever she needed to was an important aspect of management support:

[At my organization] the atmosphere is very relaxed. Everyone gets along really well. You can go directly to your supervisor or his supervisor if you need to talk about something. It’s a lot nicer to work here than the other place [I used to work]. [Francie]

Several women, however, discussed situations in which they had managers who were not supportive. Sandra was one of them:

[My old manager] was extremely temperamental, volatile, [and] threw things. He would call me into his office and say things like ‘I want you to go up [to the vice president’s office and find the employee attitude survey] read it, see what you can find out, see what it says about me, and come back and report to me.’ And I said to him ‘I won’t do that, I could get fired for that.’ And he would say he was angry at his boss for giving him a poor performance evaluation. He told me to follow his boss around, come back and report to him: who he spoke to, where he went. It was awful, it was unreal. [Sandra]

Sandra expressed her frustration about the work environment she experienced. She said that her male co-workers typically get praise from the male management, but she does not despite her perception that she performs better on the job:

I found that working [at my current employer] is a very male chauvinistic workplace. y [I feel like] this little tiny dried pea in the corner while these guys [my male coworkers] are getting the glory and the praise. And I can do three times the amount of work that they do, in the same time period they take to do it. And they get praised for it. [Sandra]

Jeria is a manager of applications development (and not one of the authors with the same name). She noted that you do not mess with your manager, the reality is that you are not going to progress because your manager is the one who will push you ahead. Unfortunately, Jeria found herself in a position in which her manager was not supportive:

I said ‘Okay, this isn’t going to work for me, so here’s my plan.’ I said, ‘Okay, take control over my own destiny, I’m going to do my job as best as I can do,’ which I did, ‘so that I don’t mess myself up going forward and you get put in this category that you’re never going to perform well.’ So I did my job to meet that manager’s need and then I set my own path that this isn’t going to be the place [I stay long term]. I was going to stay for my five year vesting. I said I made a five year commitment when I took the job in that city to stay and get my vesting. Then I’ll move on. [Jeria]

## Mentors

Comments about mentoring came from responses to an open-ended question asked in the interview about the role of ‘significant others’ such as role models, mentors or other influential people in the women’s career and life development. Mentors were identified as having positive effects, especially in the early stage of women’s careers. Mentoring was also linked to the broader notion of support and networking. Finally, some women discussed potential negative effects of mentoring programs on women’s career development and retention.

Brandy, who is in the early stage of her career as a software engineer, has two mentors. Her female mentor gives her career encouragement by referencing experiences in her own career. In contrast, she describes her male mentor as more of a ‘technical mentor’ in that their discussions are limited to technical topics. For Lu, a software engineer, the benefit of a mentor is that this individual brings an objective perspective on a situation. Some of the mentoring relationships were informal and related to managers with whom they worked. Other mentoring relationships were part of a formal program. The women also identified both males and females as good mentors in their careers. For example, Kirsten, an IT specialist, explained:

I’ve never had female manager until this position. My first manager, she was a female, she took on a very mentor-like roley[S]he would tell [me], ‘These are some books you should read. These are some things you might go on.’ And it wasn’t as a means of, it’s part of your job y but as a means of employee development. I felt that she specifically took an interest in me because I was a female. [Kirsten]

Chloe, a technology marketing manager, spoke about an informal mentoring relationship with a female executive with whom she worked. Since they worked together Chloe had the opportunity to observe this woman in a day-to-day setting and learn lessons about customer interaction, for example.

The women also spoke about the positive relationships they had with their male mentors. Tracey, who is currently a chief information officer, spoke about a man who was the most influential mentor of her career:

The man who hired me was excellent. [He was] always involving me in processes which were way above my level, because I was just a trainee. But [I had] a lot of opportunities. Always an excellent coach. After things would happen, he would bring me in and give the background, why he did what he did. How he handled something. It was an excellent environment. [Tracey]

Christine, a software engineer, described a male mentor – her first section manager – as one who did not conform to gruff masculine stereotypes. Rather, he was interested in bringing people along. He was truly interested in understanding her career goals. She credited him with helping her to translate what she had conceptualized as a ‘job’ into what he was conceptualizing for her as a ‘career’. The senior women in the study also shared perspectives on mentoring. Jenna, who is a vice president, believes that women in middle and top management levels have an important role to play in mentoring women early in their careers. After she retires she is considering devoting her energies not only to mentoring but also to outreach activities in high schools.

Some women viewed mentoring as but one aspect of an overall conceptualization of support that included other forms of networking. Kimberly feels strongly that social networking is essential in career advancement:

I totally 100% think you cannot work in society without a social network. There is only one job I’ve ever gotten through an ady Everything else has been who I knew and the right timing. Maintaining your networks is a job unto itself, whether you’re at work or not. Even now I’m routinely emailing people I’ve worked with in the past, touching base with them. I mentor old staff. I talk to old bosses. I belong to several online communities. y I think that you can quickly lose your contacts, but if you shoot people an email you know you haven’t talked to in a year, you can rekindle pretty quickly. It’s critical. [Kimberly]

Kristen, a director of information systems business applications, believes that the formal but optional mentoring program at her firm is necessary for women. In fact, 85% of the participants in it are females. According to Kristen, this is because they see it as their only avenue to develop relationships and networks.

In addition to mentoring programs in organizations, women’s associations also provide resources for mentoring. Miranda, who is a middle manager, strongly encourages women to participate in mentoring programs. She pointed out that she is involved in two different programs. One is through a women’s association in which she has been associated for six years. The other is with a vice president. The benefit to her has been these people’s willingness to share some of the things they have gone through and the do’s and don’ts and suggestions about how to get to where you want to be. To her, the changing structure of the IT workplace makes mentoring more important now than when she began her career:

When I first started working there were definite career paths. There were developmental plans. Now you need to take control of your career and figure it out. That’s why these mentoring groups are so important. [Miranda]

Perhaps serving as a cautionary tale, two middle aged women spoke about negative effects of mentoring. Helen, a systems analyst, described a female vice president who she experienced as a micromanager. On the one hand, Helen felt this manager gave her little opportunity to engage in independent action, something she needed for career development. At the same time, Helen characterized this individual as a 'militant feminist' who tended to interpret all organizational experiences as a gendered ‘us against them’ situation. And Rose had the difficult job of being mentored by a man she definitely did not want to emulate!

Probably the one that was the most memorable was when I was with [a particular firm]. The head of that company was the person who hired me and he kind of fancied himself as my mentor. But I think he had terrible management skills.

In her view, he wanted a clone, something she refused to become. However, she did acknowledge that this mentoring relationship did influence her career development, albeit in a negative way:

There are a lot of things that I would never do to other people. [Rose]

## Discussion

This examination of women’s lived experience in the IT workplace was conducted in order to theorize the influence of three organizational factors – work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring – on the retention of women in the IT field. It was done by focusing on particular themes about women’s gendered experiences that emerged from women’s discussions of these factors. The themes of child care, workplace flexibility, and gendered domestic roles were expressions of the work–life balance factor. The themes of exclusion, attitudes toward women, and management support emerged in discussions of the organizational climate factor. Finally, three themes arose in relation to the mentoring factor: early career benefits; response to exclusion and women’s organizations; and negative mentoring.

Our overall results are consistent with findings elsewhere in the literature that these factors do, in fact, suggest opportunities for interventions in order to address the retention of women in the IT sector. However, our manner of theorizing about the data enables us to look more critically into these observations. The theory that we chose to inform our data collection and analysis is the individual differences theory of gender and IT. This theory provided us with conceptual tools that enabled us to characterize both the influences on women and the variation among them with respect to their experience of the three factors explored in this article. It also provided us with the tools to consider the source of some of the variation in the women’s experiences. It posits that three groups of constructs, collectively, account for women’s participation in (or attrition from) the IT profession: individual identity, individual influences, and environmental influences. In this section we employ the individual differences theory of gender and IT to examine, in greater detail, our findings about the ways in which these three factors affect the retention of women in the IT profession. We do this by considering how each of the theory’s constructs facilitates a deeper and more critical examination of the linkage between our findings about organizational factors and the retention of women in the IT profession. We, then, relate this analysis to implications for interventions.

## Individual identity

The individual identity construct focused attention on the ways in which aspects of a woman’s personal and professional identity affect her career choices and trajectory. We use it here to examine two themes about work–life balance challenges in the workplace: child care and workplace flexibility. Within the individual identity construct, the sub-construct, personal demographics, includes the concept, parenthood status. One theme that emerged from the interviews was about balancing the demands of family with the demands of the job. The work–life balance factor affected women in different ways, depending upon their parenthood status. For example, the women in this study who had children indicated that the tensions diminish as their children get older. For women with older or no children, work–life balance issues would be different. They would more likely relate to personal health or the care of one’s spouse. Further, the intensity of the work–life balance factor for women’s retention would vary across women based upon the concept of parenthood status. Thus, a 24/7 culture that might degrade work–life balance for a single mother with young children might not be an issue for a woman with no or grown children. Hence, a one-size-fits-all approach that assumes a level of intensity of this factor across all women or that interprets work–life balance only in terms of child care issues will not address the varied needs of all women in the IT workplace.

A second theme under the work–life balance factor that emerged from the interviews is workplace flexibility. This theme relates to the sub-construct, career items, and the concept within it of work characteristics. The women relayed both negative and positive experiences of this theme. The negative connotation of workplace flexibility derived from the ubiquitous nature of information and communications technology (ICT) that promoted a ‘24/ 7’ work ethic in some companies. Flexibility for these women means they are expected to be always on call. The nature of IT work in these companies requires on-call availability in order to ensure continuous operations of the IT infrastructure of their own or a client’s company. However, the theme of workplace flexibility was also discussed by some women as a positive feature of IT work. In these IT workplaces it provided time and location flexibility. Women gave examples of workplace flexibility that greatly enhanced the quality of work–life. From this, we see that while workplace flexibility does, indeed, affect women’s experience of work–life balance, it is not necessarily in a favorable way. Consequently, organizational policies need to probe beneath the surface of simplistic gendered assumptions about workplace flexibility to exploit the positive dimensions and mitigate the negative ones. Positive dimensions of workplace flexibility would include offerings such as: flextime, flexible weeks, and telecommuting. But different kinds of policies would be needed to diminish the negative consequences of ubiquitous IT, for example, by specifying times when an individual is able to be ‘off call’.

## Individual influences

In our examination of the mentoring workplace factor we drew upon the individual influences construct that focused attention on the ways in which individual differences in personality, abilities, interests, and experiences serve to shape one’s career. One of the sub-constructs is personal influences, which refers to people who have played an influential role (both positive and negative) in a woman’s career. The concept within this sub-construct that is explored in this article is mentors. This concept was revealed through three themes that enabled us to consider the variety of ways that significant others can positively and negatively affect a woman’s career development. The particular themes that emerged from the interviews included: positive experiences with mentoring (the theme of early career benefits), the absence of mentoring (the themes of response to exclusion and women’s organizations), and negative experiences with mentoring (the theme of negative mentoring).

The theme of early career benefits emerged in discussion of the overall value of mentoring. While mentoring was seen as beneficial throughout one’s career, it was seen as particularly beneficial in the early stages. Further, male as well as female mentors were identified as being helpful to the women. This point is noteworthy insofar as some people believe that mentoring can only be provided by those with similar identity characteristics such as gender or race. This leads us to conclude that the concept, mentors, is sometimes equated with a similar concept, role models, where identity characteristics do matter. The theme, exclusion and women’s organizations, arose from comments by the women in this study about mentoring as being particularly important when it serves as a way to counteract exclusion from male networks in the workplace. What was also noted in discussions of exclusion was the establishment of women’s organizations. These groups can provide support to women who are otherwise excluded from support networks. In addition, they can also serve as a source of mentoring for women. The final theme about the concept of mentoring that some women discussed was ‘negative mentoring’. This was generally the result of a manager who was too controlling, wanted to develop ‘a clone’ or who was manipulating the mentoring relationship in order to have the prote´ge´ further the mentor’s political goals in the workplace.

## Environmental influences

Finally, the environmental influences construct facilitated critical analysis of the work–life balance and organizational climate factors through the characterization of contextual influences on women’s participation in the IT profession. These contextual influences exist at both the societal and the organizational levels. The particular subconstruct examined in this article is cultural influences. This sub-construct refers to the amount of ‘gendered air that women breathe – in both the home and the workplace.

The concept, national cultural attitudes, was in evidence in discussions about work–life balance. In particular, it applied to the theme of gender roles in relation to cultural norms about a woman’s responsibility at home and at work. This theme was manifested in discussions about the influence of assumed gendered domestic responsibilities on career decisions. Carol said that her Chinese cultural heritage influenced her view that professional women have two full-time jobs: one inside the home and one outside it. Kayla acknowledged that her Argentinean cultural background resulted in a similar view about women’s domestic roles. This finding is consistent with the literature on culture and gender. Trauth et al. (2008a) found that perceptions of a woman’s role which are embedded in a society constituted a significant socio-cultural moderator influencing IT career choice. Likewise, Adya & Kaiser’s (2005) model of females’ career choices in IT include culture as a core factor. Finally, Minguez (2005) argued that the stereotype of a man as the primary breadwinner in a family has influenced IT use and career decisions of women in Spain.

The concept, organizational culture attitudes, was revealed in three themes that arose in the interviews: exclusion, attitudes toward women and management support. Exclusion from the ‘boy’s club’ has been discussed in both the practitioner and the research literature (see, for example, Morgan et al., 2004; Hewlett et al., 2008). However, the typical theorizing about these themes has complicated the effort to develop appropriate responses. The reason is that to the extent that gender is considered to be monolithic, it is difficult to make a case for the existence of gendered exclusion. This is because a single counter example – that is, a single instance in which a woman is not excluded – can then be used to invalidate the claims by those who are. But the individual differences theory of gender and IT allows for the considerable variation in women’s experiences. Thus, while the organizational climate of some IT workplaces is exemplary from a gender relations perspective, in others women are suffering. Beyond exclusion, women identified other characteristics of a ‘toxic’ gendered workplace. They discussed gender stereotypes about a woman’s technical proficiency and her appearance. Finally, they commented on management support. Once again, the women evidenced both positive and negative aspects of these themes. As their comments illustrate, if this air becomes sufficiently toxic, they may decide not to breathe it any longer. While some women could rely on management support in the face of exclusion or gender bias, others had no other recourse than to develop an exit strategy.

## Interventions

Consistent with the theoretical focus of this research on within-gender variation is our recommendation for organizational interventions that are flexible enough to account for employee diversity. For example, interventions to help employees balance work and life could include a variety of offerings such as: child-care programs, elder-care programs, job sharing, flexible work schedules, and flexible leave policies (Nixon & Simmering, 2006). The main point is that interventions should provide support throughout different phases of employees’ careers. An example of implementing policies that allow flexible working hours is the ‘Flexible Leave of Absence’ program of IBM. Employees can work out an arrangement with managers to take leaves for personal reasons. They can remain connected to IBM even if they need to take an extended leave of as much as three years. According to Hewlett et al. (2008), the program is considered to be a cornerstone of IBM’s corporate policy, and has significantly contributed to retention.

Organizational interventions related to organizational climate could be used to motivate change in the workplace, create a vision for change, and manage the transition to a more inclusive environment. Our analysis frequently identified times when the women interviewed considered leaving the IT workforce because of issues related to organizational climate. For example, Sandra worked in an environment where her manager supported her in private, but not in public. Sandra was forced to quit her job when her manager became ‘derogatory’ and, in her opinion, ruined her reputation at the firm. Further, Pamela discussed the hostile work environment at her company. She added that she intends to quit her job, but wants to carefully plan her exit strategy. She does not want to sever connections and wants to leave on good terms. In order to create a critical mass of women in senior positions and to foster an inclusive environment for retaining female talent, Cisco utilizes the Executive Talent Insertion Program and Executive Talent Assimilation Program to hire a large number of women at the executive levels (Hewlett et al., 2008).

Political support and resources would be vital in the process of reinforcing new behaviors. For example, Klawe et al. (2009, p. 74) warn that top level executives need to become aware of their own unconscious biases that often cause them to interpret the competence and qualifications of men and women differently. When this happens, they say, women are at a significant disadvantage when it comes to advancement (Eagly & Carau, 2002). It is important to bear in mind, however, that not all women in organizations encounter unconscious bias. The challenge, therefore, is to recognize that the absence of unconscious bias in one part of a company does not preclude its existence in another. Thus senior leaders are urged to learn about this phenomenon through workshops and other means. This will enable them to more critically examine current evaluation practices in order to remove unconscious bias where it occurs. George (2003) points out that critical corporate culture issues must be addressed in order to dismantle traditional patriarchies, and such cultural changes must have the genuine endorsement of senior executives and managers. This is echoed by Klawe et al. (2009), who also suggest that having senior managers’ commitment and placing more women in senior leadership positions can facilitate the organizational climate change, in order to recruit and retain female talent.

While mentoring was seen by all as an important component of career progression, the women’s varied experience of it suggests that organizations should critically examine the mentoring landscape before implementing interventions. Further, it should be understood that without careful implementation mentoring could turn out to do more harm than good. This critical analysis should identify existing barriers to equal mentoring of men and women, networks from which women are excluded, and instances of negative mentoring. And the interventions should include mechanisms for selecting qualified mentors, providing mentoring training, and matching mentors and prote´ge´s.

Hewlett et al. (2008) emphasize the importance of developing mentoring programs and cite the ‘Mentoring Ring’ at Microsoft as a good example. The ‘Mentoring Ring’ initiative matches pairs of senior female executives with small groups of junior women to help them develop a progression strategy, obtain visibility, and accelerate their careers. Similarly, Klawe et al. (2009) note the importance of making mentoring a basic part of the organizational culture. They cite Sun Microsystems’ SEED program as an exemplar for others to emulate. In the SEED program, new hires are accepted into the program through a formal process and are paired with senior-level engineers and executives who are volunteer mentors. One of the major goals of the SEED program is to foster diversity and improve retention. It is reported that the attrition rate of SEED participants is consistently lower than that of Sun employees overall (Dickinson, 2006). According to Dickinson (2006), consistent executive sponsorship and a company culture that values mentoring are two key success factors of the SEED program.

It is also important to note that interventions related to work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring are interconnected. Pfleeger & Mertz (2006, p. 867) point out that the success of mentoring programs depends on the degree of commitment of the participants and of their organizations, and that organizations must formally sanction the values of the mentoring programs. Armstrong et al. (2007) argue that some work–life interventions such as flexible work schedules could turn into drawbacks in terms of promotion opportunities for female IT professionals and lead to voluntary turnover. George (2003) suggests that work–life balance initiatives, for example, should not only be reflected in corporate policies, but should also be reflected in the organizational climate. Those changes may include discouraging early morning and late afternoon meetings, allowing employees to take leave in small increments, senior managers and executives leaving the office at reasonable hours to set an example, discouraging employees from taking company mobile phones and laptops on holidays, etc. These examples illustrate the systemic nature of change that is needed when developing and implementing organizational interventions.

Finally, it is important to recognize that different countries are addressing gender under representation in quite different ways, and those interventions that might work in one setting might not be possible in another. In Europe, for example, European Union equality policies regarding gender mainstreaming and positive discrimination might be possible. But such policies are not part of the political culture in the U.S. where a more grass roots, private sector approach to societal change is required. Thus, while there may be common symptoms of the problem of gender under representation in the IT workforce, we believe it is necessary to construct interventions that are consistent with the context in which they appear.

## Future research

There are several opportunities for future research that would build upon this work. First, the purpose of this article was to examine – at an individual level of analysis – the ways in which women respond to organizational factors that influence retention in the IT workforce. Hence, there is an opportunity for future research that would examine these same factors at the organizational level of analysis. Second, since the women who participated in this study were recruited from the population of women who are currently employed in the IT workforce the voices of those who did not select IT careers or who have left the IT workplace are silent. Consequently, there is a need for research into the factors that cause women to initially select or migrate toward careers outside of the IT profession. These findings show that women at different career stages, with different family statuses, or in different geographical locations may experience these organizational factors differently. Therefore, a third avenue of future research is to examine the correlations among age, relationship status, family status, IT occupation, career progression, environmental influences (by region or country), work–life balance demands, attitude and perception of organizational climate, and mentoring needs. Doing so could facilitate the development of dynamic and robust organizational initiatives to effectively retain women’s talents in the IT industry. Finally, the findings discussed in this article were generated in the U.S. While the research literature has demonstrated that women in every country are affected by such things as gender stereotypes and cultural norms about a woman’s role, it is also the case that environmental influences (such as culture, economy, public policy, and infrastructure) operate in different ways in different countries. Therefore, a promising line of future research would be more comparative studies of environmental influences on women in the IT profession in different countries.

## Conclusion

There is a theoretical challenge at the core of the practical challenge of meeting the renewed demand for IT workers by increasing female representation in the IT profession. It is to employ a theory whose constructs can help to explain the gender imbalance in such a way that appropriate interventions based upon them can be developed. The individual differences theory of gender and IT – our choice of a sensitizing device for understanding women’s experiences in the IT workplace – does just that. It enabled us to conduct a detailed examination of the variation among women with respect to three workplace factors that are known to influence retention: work–life balance, organizational climate, and mentoring. By using the constructs of the theory – individual identity, individual influences and environmental influences – we had the conceptual tools to identify reasons for the variation in the effect (both positive and negative) of these workplace factors on women.

Our method of theorizing enabled us to account for what has been interpreted by other researchers as contradictory findings. Prior literature has labeled as ‘contradictory’ the inconsistent findings regarding the importance of work–life balance, career motivations, IT self efficacy, masculine stereotypes of the IT field, and other attitudes held by women. This is because the underlying assumption was that all women are the same with respect to their values and attitudes about the IT profession, and that the search for between-gender differences was the most fruitful avenue to investigate. In contrast, our theoretical starting point is the assumption that women are not all the same, that within-gender variation is expected and that it provides an opportunity to develop a deeper understanding of the gender imbalance in the IT field. Consonant with our theorizing, the finding of differences among women about workplace factors is not a methodological barrier to be overcome but rather a source of insight about the influence of culture, nationality, age, life circumstances or other aspects of women’s life history on their retention in the IT profession.

The use of this theory enabled us to identify opportunities for the development of interventions by linking the themes embedded in the three workplace factors to the constructs of the theory, which articulate the range of influences on women’s professional development. The individual identity construct revealed the ways in which a woman’s demographic and professional characteristics affect her career choices. The individual influences construct focused attention on the ways in which differences in personality, abilities, and influential people shape one’s career. Finally, the environmental influences construct characterized contextual influences on women’s participation in the IT profession.

The implications of this research for practice lay in the concrete and tailored ways in which the theory links research findings to the interventions that are suggested. For each of the three workplace factors that have been consistently shown in the literature to affect the participation of women in the IT workforce, we identified specific themes that can be directly translated into interventions. For example, the exclusion of women from male networks can be addressed in a variety of ways, depending upon the context. One would be establishing or strengthening mentoring programs. Another would be greater management accountability for exclusionary workplace behaviors. The use of ubiquitous ICT to provide beneficial rather than oppressive workplace flexibility is a third example.

In theory, a woman’s personal profile – her demographic characteristics such as parenthood status, her personality, and her support system – has been shown to interact with the particular characteristics of her IT job and the culture of her IT workplace to influence her satisfaction and, ultimately, her retention.
