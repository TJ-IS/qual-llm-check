# Lateral Coordination Mechanisms and the Moderating Role of Arrangement Characteristics in Information Systems Development Outsourcing

S. Balaji

College of Business and Economics, University of Wisconsin–Whitewater, Whitewater, Wisconsin 53190, sankarab@uww.edu

Carol V. Brown

Howe School of Technology Management, Stevens Institute of Technology, Hoboken, New Jersey 07030, carol.brown@stevens.edu

lthough increased information systems (IS) development outsourcing is the trend, many of these arrangements fail to meet client expectations. We take a coordination perspective and adopt an information processing lens used by prior organization theorists to conceptualize sets of formal structural and informal nonstructural mechanisms, and predict their positive impacts on the strategic IT benefits achieved by the client. Utilizing a strategic alliance lens, we also predict that two characteristics of the client-vendor arrangement will moderate the impacts of both sets of coordination mechanisms. We test our hypotheses using hierarchical regression techniques on field survey data collected from 141 IS managers in client firms, responsible for IS development outsourcing arrangements. We found that the implementation of both structural and informal mechanisms positively impact the client’s strategic IT benefits. In arrangements with greater resource provisioning by the vendor, the positive impacts of informal governance mechanisms are strengthened. In arrangements with higher value similarity, the positive impacts of structural governance mechanisms are strengthened, but the positive impacts of informal mechanisms are weakened. A post-hoc analysis of a mediation model reveals that values similarity also has a positive relationship to both structural and informal governance mechanisms. This study therefore provides empirical support for the validity of an information processing lens to theorize lateral mechanism solutions to the coordination challenges of IS development outsourcing. Implications for research and practice are discussed, including the need for future research to better understand how client managers evolve sets of formal and informal mechanisms over the life of an outsourcing arrangement to achieve strategic objectives for their IT organization.

Keywords: IS outsourcing; lateral coordination mechanisms; structural governance; informal governance; outsourcing arrangements; outsourcing performance benefits

## Introduction

Recent years have seen a significant increase in the number of organizations in developed countries engaging in information systems (IS) outsourcing arrangements, with the annual value of global application development and maintenance contracts exceeding \$5 billion during 2013.<sup>1</sup> IS application outsourcing arrangements can involve the delegation of a range of software application activities (e.g., new software development, maintenance, enhancements, packaged implementation) to an onshore or offshore vendor. Although increased IS development outsourcing is the trend in recent years, practitioner reports also suggest that many arrangements fail to meet client expectations (McKendrick 2012).

A principal challenge in steering an outsourcing arrangement to success relates to governing the outsourcing relationship (Feeny and Willcocks 1998, Rai et al. 2009). These governance objectives can be broadly classified as relating to two aspects: control and coordination (Sabherwal 2003). The majority of extant research on governance has addressed IS outsourcing challenges from a control perspective. However, as noted by Sabherwal (2003, p. 155): “Coordination focuses on managing interdependencies 0 0 0 Control focuses on improving performance relative to a certain goal 0 0 0 0” Governance mechanisms that address coordination challenges (i.e., lateral coordination mechanisms) are therefore theoretically distinct from those that address control challenges for managing an outsourcing arrangement.

In this study, we use organization design theory as a theoretical lens for investigating the impacts of formal and informal coordination mechanisms in clientvendor relationships. Coordinating the interdependencies between IS and business units requires formal mechanisms such as steering committees, IS standing teams, and cross-unit integrator roles, as well as informal mechanisms that include nonstructural devices such as physical colocation of IS staff with business staff and interdepartmental events (Brown 1999). In recent years, IS researchers have studied some specific formal coordination mechanisms and their antecedents in IS outsourcing contracts (Poppo and Lacity 2002, Levina and Vaast 2008). Prior case study research has also captured the evolution of coordination mechanisms usage in offshore projects (Sabherwal 2003). However, we know of no prior study that has examined how sets of both formal and informal mechanisms for coordinating IS outsourcing activities, as conceptualized by organization theorists, impact outsourcing performance outcomes. We also lack knowledge about differential impacts of lateral coordination mechanisms due to characteristics of the client-vendor arrangement.

Our study addresses these research gaps in the context of IS development outsourcing arrangements. Building on prior relevant IS governance research and IS outsourcing studies, we conceptualize coordination mechanisms in IS outsourcing as consisting of formal structural governance (i.e., structural overlays) and nonstructural informal governance mechanisms. For studying the impacts on outsourcing performance, we focus on the strategic IT benefits for the client as conceptualized by Grover et al. (1996) from a client-vendor arrangement, rather than on contractual or project management outcomes for a single project.

Further, in recognition that client-vendor arrangements are exchange relationships (Willcocks and Kern 1998, Poston et al. 2009), we also apply a strategic alliance lens to study the potential moderating effects of selected characteristics of an outsourcing arrangement on the impacts of both types of coordination mechanisms. The two overall research questions that this study therefore seeks to address are the following:

• Is the implementation of formal and informal lateral coordination mechanisms positively related to strategic IT benefits for the client from the IS development outsourcing arrangement?

• How do client-vendor arrangement characteristics influence the impacts of these mechanisms on the strategic IT benefits for the client?

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review selected literature on lateral coordination mechanisms, potentially influential client-vendor arrangement characteristics, and IS outsourcing performance benefits. Then, we build on this literature to separately theorize the impacts of structural and informal lateral mechanisms designed for clientvendor coordination of IS development activities from an information processing lens. Next, we apply a strategic alliance lens to theorize the moderating effects of two arrangement characteristics: the level of technical resources provided by the vendor, and the extent of values similarity between the client and the vendor. After describing the methodology we use to test our six hypotheses, we present our quantitative results and discuss our findings. We then acknowledge the limitations of our study, and describe our conceptual and empirical contributions to the IS outsourcing literature, as well as the implications for future research and practice.

## Literature Review

A review of the literature reveals a growing body of work that explicates the importance of IS governance mechanisms. In the IS outsourcing literature, the majority of these studies draw on transaction cost and agency theory to study the usage of control modes and mechanisms and their impacts on project performance. More specifically, applying control-theoretic perspectives, these researchers have focused on outcome and behavior (formal) modes of control, and clan and self (informal) modes of control. Empirical evidence suggests that not only the systems development capability of the client but also the measurability of project-level outcomes and behaviors affect the choice of control modes for insourced (Kirsch 1996), outsourced (Kirsch 1997), and offshored (Kirsch et al. 2002, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003) projects. Srivastava and Teo (2012) also offer a finer distinction between control mechanisms and control modes: control modes refer to broader categories such as “outcome” and “clan” control, whereas control mechanisms are defined as the specific ways in which control modes are enacted–the “what” and “how” control modes operate (p. 117). There is also empirical evidence for extracontractual relational aspects (i.e., flexibility in clientvendor relationships) positively influencing project performance, with exchange hazards (i.e., requirements uncertainty, employee turnover, human asset specificity) as antecedents to the utilization of these relational mechanisms (Gopal and Koka 2012). These and other researchers have also investigated the positive and negative effects of certain combinations of formal and informal control modes and mechanisms (Poppo and Zenger 2002, Tiwana 2010, Tiwana and Keil 2010, Srivastava and Teo 2012). This body of research has therefore contributed extensively to our understanding of governance mechanisms to address control challenges and aspects such as exchange hazards from a transaction-cost theoretical lens. However, these mechanisms are distinct from the lateral coordination mechanisms for managing client-vendor interdependencies that are the focus of this study.

## Lateral Coordination Mechanisms

In addressing intra-firm coordination issues, organization theorists have long espoused the utilization of a range of lateral mechanisms to supplement an organization’s internal (vertical) organizational hierarchy to coordinate activities across different organizational units (Galbraith 1973, Mintzberg 1979, Nadler and Tushman 1988). Lateral mechanisms such as groups (e.g., committees, teams, task forces) and integrator roles (e.g., managerial linking roles, liaison roles) can be conceptualized on a continuum based on their information processing capabilities. Although the specific placement of different examples of groups and integrator roles on a continuum by these theorists has varied somewhat, these types of formal mechanisms can all be classified under the umbrella term “structural overlays.” In addition, Galbraith (1994) extended his continuum of formal structural mechanisms to include “informal organization” mechanisms. Specifically, he argued that nonstructural informal mechanisms (e.g., physical colocation, interdepartmental social events) also facilitate information processing and are less costly and easier to implement than formal mechanisms. Thus, organizations implement both formal and informal lateral coordination mechanisms for more effective decision making and problem solving.

Prior IS governance research provides support for this dual perspective of formal and informal lateral governance mechanisms for managing the IS function. Adapting Galbraith’s (1994) extended continuum of formal and informal mechanisms, Brown (1999) found that both structural overlays and nonstructural informal mechanisms were implemented for coordinating IS work across business and IS units, as well as across centralized and decentralized IS units. There is also evidence for superior organizational outcomes when the choice of lateral governance mechanisms is aligned with organizational goals (Weill and Ross 2004) and different types of CIO roles (Weill and Woerner 2013).

The usage of formal and informal lateral mechanisms has also previously been recognized as important for in-house as well as outsourced software development activities. For example, Anders and Zmud (2001) found that software development activities managed through informal, rather than formal, coordination approaches were more successful.

Sabherwal (2003) categorized four types of mechanisms being used for offshore software development, including both formal and informal mutual adjustment mechanisms as conceptualized by Malone and Crowston (1994). Other researchers have also reported the importance of individual lateral coordination mechanisms, such as boundary-spanning integrator roles in offshore development (Levina and Vaast 2008).

## Arrangement Characteristics

Based on prior research on strategic alliances and IS outsourcing, we identified two characteristics as potential moderators: (a) resource provisioning—i.e., the extent to which technical resources are provisioned by the vendor versus the client, and (b) values similarity—i.e., the extent to which the organizational values and norms of the client and vendor are similar.

Resource Provisioning. Prior research on strategic alliances highlights the importance of coordinating human resources in an interorganizational arrangement. The extent to which resources are provisioned by each of the partner firms (Dyer and Singh 1998, Das and Teng 2000) can impact interfirm exchange performance (Harrison et al. 2001, Ireland et al. 2002). Furthermore, the resources provided by the partner firm can result in an influx of new knowledge and learning, which in turn enhances alliance performance (Ireland et al. 2002).

In this study, we focus on the extent to which the vendor firm provisions technical IS resources relative to the client. The importance of coordinating technical IS resources in an IS development outsourcing arrangement can be understood from two perspectives. First, the nature of the application(s) outsourced and the client’s software development capability can dictate the extent to which technical skills are provisioned by the vendor (Kirsch et al. 2002, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). Second, software development activities are information-intensive and knowledge-intensive activities (Rai et al. 2009). IS development outsourcing vendors may have developed a portfolio of technical skills such as requirements determination, systems analysis and design, as well as software implementation expertise (Ethiraj et al. 2005, Swinarski et al. 2006) through their experiences with a variety of customers and situations (McFarlan and Nolan 1995, Saunders et al. 1997). As the extent of technical IS resources provided by a vendor for a given arrangement can vary (Gopal et al. 2011), the effectiveness of lateral coordination mechanisms to leverage the technical skills and the knowledge inflows from the vendor can also vary.

Values Similarity. Prior strategic alliance researchers have found that compatibility or congruence in organizational values and norms between the two exchange partners can be an important prerequisite in alliance formation and leads to superior performance outcomes (Parkhe 1991, Madhok and Tallman 1998, Sarkar et al. 2001). The values and norms of partner firms can be manifested in their organizational work cultures, such as work ethics and worker outlooks on various issues, including goal setting and meeting deadlines (Sarkar et al. 2001). A greater similarity in values and norms between exchange partners can facilitate social interactions and communications across the partners (Parkhe 1991), and is associated with realizing the synergistic potential of an exchange relationship (Madhok and Tallman 1998).

In this study, we focus on the extent to which the client perceives similarity in the values and norms between the two organizations. Prior IS outsourcing studies suggest that greater similarity in organizational values and norms between the client and the vendor results in more frequent interactions, which in turn help to guarantee a synergistic working relationship (Willcocks and Choi 1995, Willcocks and Kern 1998). Shared values and norms provide a basis for better social interactions between client and vendor personnel, and thereby help build trust-based relationships that are more likely to avoid negative outcomes such as cost overruns (Marcolin 2002). In contrast, incompatibilities in values and norms between the client and vendor can be a “major stumbling block” in facilitating effective outsourcing project performance (Goles and Chin 2002, p. 232). Even if the client and vendor firms in an outsourcing arrangement operate under similar country- or industryspecific regulations, individual firms internally foster distinctive organizational values and norms, and their approaches to getting work done may significantly differ (Kirsch 1997, Goles and Chin 2002, Sabherwal 2003). The need to coordinate the IS activities of workers employed by organizations that have different organizational values and norms therefore becomes a major management challenge (Oshri et al. 2007). As the extent of values similarity between the client and vendor can vary, the effectiveness of lateral mechanisms for addressing these coordination challenges in IS development outsourcing can also vary.

## IS Outsourcing Performance

Most prior studies of IS development outsourcing that have included a performance variable have focused on outcomes at the project level, such as systems development ambidexterity (Tiwana 2010), profitability (Gopal and Koka 2012, Srivastava and Teo 2012), and software quality measures (Gopal et al. 2011). In contrast, our study is interested in performance impacts from an IS development outsourcing arrangement in a client organization. More specifically, as initially conceptualized by Grover et al. (1996) for outsourcing in general, we examine the strategic, technological, and economic benefits accrued by the client’s IT organization from a client-vendor arrangement.

In the next section, we develop our hypotheses for the impacts of formal and informal lateral coordination mechanisms on the strategic IT benefits achieved by the client firm from a given outsourcing arrangement, as well as the moderating roles of these two client-vendor arrangement characteristics that are associated with managing client-vendor coordination challenges.

## Hypothesis Development

## Formal and Informal Coordination Mechanisms and Their Impacts

Consistent with the concept of structural overlays espoused by organization theorists (e.g., Galbraith 1994), the utilization of liaison roles and governance committees for managing an outsourcing arrangement are documented practices to enable formal communication channels between the client and vendor (Lacity and Willcocks 2000, Poppo and Lacity 2002, Gopal and Gosain 2009). For example, the effective use of boundary spanning integrator roles, such as client IS managers, has been found to reduce the status differences perceived among team members in an outsourcing relationship—in this case, status differences arising from different national contexts (Levina and Vaast 2008). From an information processing perspective, other mechanisms such as ongoing design review meetings are also formal mechanisms for client-vendor decision making, and provide twoway communication channels for conflict resolution and collaborative problem solving (Sabherwal 2003).

In this study, we therefore define structural governance mechanisms as a set of formal mechanisms that include committees, regular review meetings with vendor counterparts, and mechanisms for escalating issue resolution to coordinate client and vendor activities. Based on information processing theory, structural governance mechanisms facilitate formal information exchanges between the client and vendor. Such formal exchanges help the client firm communicate key requirements, and understand and track the current status of the outsourced work, while interacting with liaisons and key members in the vendor organization (Sabherwal 2003). Structural mechanisms also provide ongoing opportunities for the vendor to share know-how about technical issues (Vlaar et al. 2008), thus enhancing the client’s organizational competence in designing technical solutions and responding to new business demands. Formal mechanisms can also enable the client firm to realize economies of scale in managing its own IS resources. Therefore, we postulate the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> The implementation of structural governance mechanisms in IS development outsourcing arrangements enhances the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

Consistent with the concept of an “informal organization” as espoused by Galbraith (1994), informal mechanisms facilitate the free flow of information across multiple workgroups involved in software application development (Crowston 1997). These mechanisms are essential to ensure an informal means of communication between users and developers (Kraut and Streeter 1995), and they facilitate crossunit activity alignment via “peer-oriented information exchange” (Anders and Zmud 2001). Similarly, in software development outsourcing, informal mechanisms such as colocation and informal social events enable impromptu communications and provide an informal means for problem solving (Sabherwal 2003) that could avoid the need for more costly formal problemsolving approaches.

In this study we therefore define informal governance mechanisms as a set of interpersonal mechanisms that enable frequent casual interactions and information sharing (Galbraith 1994) including the physical location of onsite vendor personnel next to client personnel (colocation), informal review meetings with vendor personnel, social networking events, and employment-related privileges similar to those given to client employees. Exogenous to structural overlays, informal mechanisms are relatively easier to implement and are a less costly alternative to structural mechanisms (Galbraith 1994, Brown 1999).

Whereas structural governance mechanisms provide formal modes of communication, informal governance mechanisms provide an informal, peopleoriented means of communicating with the vendor (Sabherwal 2003). They enable the client to informally tap into the know-how of vendor personnel that would not be shared through formal channels. Through informal review meetings, client and vendor personnel can share spontaneous updates about relevant activities (Galbraith 1994) to quickly and informally resolve any arrangement-specific issues, thereby providing a timely alternative to formal mechanisms. In addition, informal governance mechanisms can facilitate the sharing of external information such as industry “best practices” and standards, from which the client firm also acquires new professional and industry-specific knowledge. These mechanisms therefore yield strategic IT benefits such as greater overall IS competence, access to key information technologies, and an enhanced ability to respond to new business demands. Therefore, we postulate the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> The implementation of informal governance mechanisms in IS development outsourcing arrangements enhances the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

Moderating Role of Resource Provisioning. Prior research on strategic alliances and IS outsourcing documents the importance of coordinating the human resources provided by the partner firms. Greater resource provisioning by the vendor in relation to the client suggests an imbalance in the division of labor between the client and vendor. Compared to coordinating internal client work, this imbalance can contribute to increased information processing requirements for the client to communicate with the vendor. Structural mechanisms such as review meetings and oversight committees are well suited to coordinate such requirements (Galbraith 1994), because these mechanisms provide formal ways for the client and vendor to communicate and share information about the various software development and implementation activities.

Further, when more vendor skills and expertise are provisioned in relation to the client, there is an increased potential for an influx of new knowledge, and therefore the potential for greater learning from the vendor partner (Ireland et al. 2002). In particular, prior research shows that lateral mechanisms such as steering committees, project groups, and integrator roles (“cooperation managers”) render a more effective alliance performance when utilized to manage knowledge-based assets (Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009). Therefore, the accrual of strategic IT benefits to the client IT organization will be enhanced in arrangements that involve greater vendor versus client resource provisioning. We, therefore, posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> In IS development outsourcing arrangements, higher resource provisioning by the vendor strengthens the extent to which structural governance mechanisms enhance the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

The effects of informal mechanisms on strategic IT benefits are also likely to be strengthened in the presence of greater resource provisioning by the vendor. As stated above, an imbalance in the division of labor between the client and vendor increases the information processing requirements for the client to communicate with the vendor. Nonstructural informal mechanisms provide an alternative means to structural mechanisms, as they foster interpersonal channels of communication (Galbraith 1994). For example, the utility of informal coordination mechanisms can become especially salient in situations common to outsourcing, such as when a vendor transitions specific personnel to other engagements, and reassigns or hires new personnel to provide requisite skills for the client (Carmel and Tjia 2006). Further, informal mechanisms such as colocation and social events have the potential to tap into the tacit knowledge of the vendor personnel (Willcocks et al. 2004) through peer-oriented information exchanges. Therefore, it follows that the effects of informal governance mechanisms on the client’s strategic IT benefits will be enhanced in arrangements that involve greater vendor versus client resource provisioning. We, therefore, posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> In IS development outsourcing arrangements, higher resource provisioning by the vendor strengthens the extent to which informal governance mechanisms enhance the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

Moderating Role of Values Similarity. A high similarity in values and norms between exchange partners can facilitate social interactions and communications across the partners (Parkhe 1991). In turn, an increase in social interactions can facilitate trust, and thus enhance the working relationship between business partners (Ferguson et al. 2005, Malhotra and Lumineau 2011). Prior research on strategic alliances has also provided evidence that trust can act as a complement to governance mechanisms in an exchange relationship (Mellewigt et al. 2007, Gulati and Nickerson 2008). Similarly, prior IS outsourcing research has shown that shared relational norms (e.g., accommodating each other’s requests, flexibility in responses, information sharing) enhance the impacts of the governance dimensions within service-level agreements (Goo et al. 2009). That is, akin to trust, shared values and norms can act as a “lubricant” of the social relationship between the client and vendor (Goo et al. 2009), to complement the effects of formal structural mechanisms. For instance, if both the client and vendor possess similar outlooks regarding work prioritization, following “hard” deadlines (i.e., nonpostponement of work), and completing assigned work on time or well ahead of the deadline, formal structural governance mechanisms implemented by the client, such as oversight committees and regular meetings to coordinate the arrangement, will be more effective. We, therefore, posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> In IS development outsourcing arrangements, higher values similarity between the client and vendor strengthens the extent to which structural governance mechanisms enhance the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

However, values similarity and informal governance mechanisms both facilitate informal information sharing and problem solving—although through distinct pathways. Values similarity facilitates effective sharing of information through social interactions and trust-based relationships (Gulati and Nickerson 2008, Lumineau and Henderson 2012). Informal governance mechanisms facilitate information sharing and problem solving by providing informal opportunities for communication–such as resource colocation and social events–that increase the likelihood of interpersonal interactions outside of formal channels (Galbraith 1994). Thus, the simultaneous presence of both values similarity and informal mechanisms can result in informal mechanisms having less effective impacts because of their redundancy. Stated differently, high values similarity and informal mechanisms can act as substitutes, so the effects of informal communication channels provided by informal mechanisms implemented by the client will be dampened. We, therefore, posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> In IS development outsourcing arrangements, higher values similarity between the client and vendor weakens the extent to which informal governance mechanisms enhance the achievement of strategic IT benefits.

## Methodology

A positivist research methodology was deemed appropriate for this study since our focus is on the testing of hypotheses based on prior theory, rather than on theory building (Straub et al. 2004). Given the scope of our study and the level of the constructs of interest, we chose a cross-sectional field survey design to test our hypotheses with client IS managers knowledgeable about a given outsourcing arrangement as our target respondents.

The following steps were performed for the instrument development phase of our study. Initially, we generated a pool of items to measure each construct based on prior theory and relevant literature. To establish face and content validity, we then conducted an expert review of all of the initial survey measures with six industry experts chosen from client and vendor organizations based on their extensive knowledge about IS outsourcing practices. This review process led to a number of changes to specific survey items. We next conducted a pilot study with a convenience sample of IS managers from 22 client organizations. Based on the descriptive statistics analysis, a few additional changes were made to some of the mechanism items. The pilot study also provided an opportunity to test the online survey administration method.

The specific population sampled for the final survey administration consisted of client IS managers who were members of the Project Management Institute-Information Systems Specific Interest Group (PMI-ISSIG). The online survey administration methods were designed to satisfy the anonymity requirement of the PMI-ISSIG. An email soliciting participation with a brief description of the purpose of the study and a link to the online survey instrument was sent to all members (facilitated by the ISSIG coordinator). Respondents were instructed to choose an outsourcing arrangement completed within the last year, or inprogress with at least one major milestone completed, that they were most familiar with, irrespective of the size or outcome of the arrangement. To encourage initial nonrespondents to participate, two additional email solicitations were sent two weeks apart. A total of 141 useable responses were obtained from the three rounds of email solicitations. A nonresponse bias test among the multiple rounds of responses indicated no significant differences. Based on the demographic details (provided by 121 respondents), 67% were male and the median age was between 40 and 50; 76% were senior IS managers (Directors, CIO, VP, etc.) and the remaining 24% were IS managers at lower levels.

The items that were used to measure each of the constructs to test the six hypotheses are presented in Table 1. The indicators for the dependent variable— strategic IT benefits for the client—were adapted from the aforementioned construct developed by Grover et al. (1996). The measures for both sets of lateral coordination mechanisms were based on Galbraith (1994) and prior IS literature, and then refined during the expert review process. The resource provisioning items were measured on a seven-point scale that captured the extent to which technical skills in software design and implementation were provided by the vendor relative to the client. The survey measures for values similarity were primarily adapted from Sarkar et al. (2001) for the IS outsourcing context.

To establish construct and discriminant validity, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis of the items with Varimax rotation. Factor loadings of items reflect the correlation of the item measure to the corresponding construct (Fornell and Larcker 1981). As seen in Table 2, the factor loadings satisfy the criterion for construct validity (Hair et al. 1998).

Discriminant validity was established by comparing the item loadings of the corresponding constructs, and the cross-loadings of items with other constructs. The loadings of items on the constructs are above 0.6, and the cross-loadings are lower than the item loadings by at least 0.2 (Hair et al. 1998), thereby establishing discriminant validity. We also conducted an exploratory factor analysis separately on each construct and its corresponding items. The item loadings satisfied the criterion for construct validity, and the variance explained by the items for each factor ranged from 56% to 81%. We also verified the factor structure through CFA (confirmatory factor analysis) using PLS, and utilized the internal consistency reliability indicators from CFA to establish the reliability of items. As seen in Table 1, the reliability scores are above 0.7 for each construct, indicating adequate composite reliability (Hair et al. 1998).

## Results

To test our hypotheses, we first created scores for the construct variables by using the averages of the items for each corresponding construct. The examinations of skewness and kurtosis were within established standards (within ± twice the standard error) and consequently showed no significant deviations from normality (Hair et al. 1998). The descriptive statistics and correlations for all control variables and constructs are provided in Table 3. We utilized the hierarchical linear regression model to test our hypotheses. In the first regression model, we included only the control variables. In the second model, we introduced the independent variables to test their main effects in the regression equation. In the third model, we introduced the interaction terms, which were computed by multiplying the corresponding variables, to test for the moderation effects. To control for potential correlations between the constructs and the interaction terms, we included the mean-centered terms for the independent variables (Aiken and West 1991). In addition, we introduced an interaction term of structural governance and informal governance in the third model. We also checked the VIF (variance inflation factor) for all variables: the values ranged from 1.01 to 1.99, indicating that multicollinearity did not pose a significant problem in the interpretation of results.

## Discussion

Our overall objective for this study is to contribute to our knowledge about the impacts of lateral coordination mechanisms on IS outsourcing development performance. Specifically, this study was designed to address two research questions:

• Is the implementation of formal and informal lateral coordination mechanisms positively related to strategic IT benefits for the client from the IS development outsourcing arrangement?

• How do client-vendor arrangement characteristics influence the impacts of these mechanisms on the strategic IT benefits for the client?

Based on prior organization theory, we first conceptualized two sets of lateral coordination mechanisms for the context of IS development outsourcing: formal structural mechanisms and nonstructural informal mechanisms. To address our first research question, we hypothesized that the implementation of both structural governance mechanisms (formal) and informal governance mechanisms is positively associated with the achievement of strategic IT benefits

These findings therefore provide support for the dual nature of lateral coordination mechanisms, as espoused by organization theorists and also found in prior IS governance literature (Brown 1999, Weill and Ross 2004). Neither set of mechanisms enhanced or suppressed the other’s performance impacts when performance was measured at the arrangement level and included strategic IT impacts, not project-level outcomes.

Support was also found for three of the four moderating effects that we hypothesized. First, when technical resource provisioning is greater by the vendor than the client, the positive performance benefits from the implementation of informal mechanisms are strengthened (H4). That is, informal mechanisms provide interpersonal channels of communication for information and knowledge sharing, and therefore greater accrual of strategic IT benefits for the client.

We also found strong evidence for the moderating effects of values similarity. When the client and vendor are perceived to have greater similarity in values and norms, the effects of structural coordination mechanisms are strengthened (H5). That is, shared values and norms can act as a “lubricant” of the social relationship between the client and vendor (Goo et al. 2009), and complement the effects of formal structural mechanisms in exchange relationships (Mellewigt et al. 2007, Gulati and Nickerson 2008). However, in the presence of high values similarity, the effects of informal mechanisms are dampened (H6). That is, in arrangements with high values similarity between the client and vendor, informal mechanisms such as colocation and social events designed to establish frequent peer-oriented interactions are redundant. Contrary to our H3 hypothesis, the positive impacts from implementing structural governance mechanisms were not significantly strengthened in the presence of higher technical resource provisioning by the vendor. One plausible explanation is that formal structural mechanisms are effective at coordinating codified technical information and knowledge, but not at eliciting new tacit knowledge from a vendor partner that could result in even greater strategic IT benefits for the client.

In the development of our hypotheses and the collection of our cross-sectional survey data we did not distinguish between lateral coordination mechanisms implemented by the client at the outset of an arrangement versus those implemented over time. Thus, we conducted a post-hoc analysis of the coordination mechanisms as mediators between the two arrangement characteristics and the strategic IT benefits accrued to the client.<sup>2</sup> These mediation tests reveal that both formal and informal lateral coordination mechanisms partially mediate the relationship between values similarity and strategic benefits, but are not mediators between resource provisioning and strategic IT benefits. ## Limitations

Although we believe that this study makes important theoretical contributions to our understanding of the performance impacts of different types of governance mechanisms in the presence of two specific clientvendor characteristics of an IS outsourcing arrangement, there are some inherent limitations. First, we collected survey responses from IS managers within client organizations only. Although we sought to control for social desirability bias (by asking respondents to respond about a recent arrangement they were most familiar with, whether or not it was successful), our measures only capture the client’s responses about a software application outsourcing arrangement of their own choice. Second, our study captured the constructs as perceptual measures. Although our tests for common method bias (Online Appendix B) were not significant, obtaining the responses through a cross-sectional survey from a single respondent is a recognized limitation of our study. Third, our sample respondents were members of a specific interest group of the PMI (ISSIG), and a systematic bias in this population could be reflected in our survey responses. Fourth, since the email solicitations to participate in the survey were distributed through the PMI-ISSIG coordinator, the actual response rate could not be calculated; although the sample collected appears to be representative of the targeted respondent population, additional tests of nonresponse bias (except late response) were not possible. Finally, our study only focused on IS development outsourcing arrangements. Because our hypotheses and construct measures were developed for this type of outsourcing arrangement, we also acknowledge that the findings of our study may not be generalizable to other outsourced IS functions or contexts.

## Contributions and Implications for Research

Our study makes three significant contributions to the literature on governance mechanisms and IS outsourcing. The first contribution relates to the applicability of prior theory of lateral coordination mechanisms (e.g., Galbraith 1994) for governing IS development outsourcing. Our study provides not only additional empirical evidence for the importance of coordination mechanisms for the interfirm context of IS development outsourcing (Sabherwal 2003), but also offers a theoretical explanation for how coordination mechanisms are distinct from the control mechanisms that have been the focus of much of the prior IS outsourcing literature.

Our second contribution relates to the significant role played by both formal structural and informal nonstructural mechanisms for addressing the clientvendor coordination challenges in software development activities. In particular, “informal organization” mechanisms (Galbraith 1994) have received little attention in the prior literature. Our overall finding is that greater utilization of each set of mechanisms enhances the strategic-level IT benefits for the client of an IS development outsourcing arrangement.

Third, we believe that our application of theoretical precepts about exchange relationships from the strategic alliance literature is also a unique contribution to the literature on IS outsourcing: the two characteristics of the client-vendor arrangement that we focused on as moderators were identified from strategic alliance studies of exchange relationships. Based on a synthesis of these studies and other literature, we hypothesized how the extent of vendor resource provisioning and values similarity strengthen or weaken the impacts of structural and informal governance mechanisms, and found evidence for differential effects. Our findings suggest that high values similarity between the client and vendor can serve as a complement to the formal communication channels established by structural governance mechanisms, but high values similarity becomes a substitute for informal mechanisms.

Future research can expand on our findings in several important ways. First, longitudinal studies designed to examine how the coevolution of the sets of structural and informal governance mechanisms impact outsourcing performance at various stages of the arrangement are needed. For instance, within the context of offshore outsourcing of application development, Sabherwal (2003) found that reduced availability of information and efficiency considerations can influence the evolution of formal and informal “mutual adjustment” mechanisms. Specifically, the client firm “pulled” the outsourcing relationship more toward informal mutual adjustment, whereas the offshore vendor “pulled” more toward formal mutual adjustment. These case study findings, as well as our own findings, therefore point to the need to increase our knowledge about not only the antecedent conditions that predict choices of coordination mechanisms but also events that trigger augmentations or reductions to the usage of structural and informal mechanisms in both onshore and offshore engagements.

Future studies are also needed to examine other arrangement characteristics that could potentially play a role in explaining variations in the impacts of lateral coordination mechanisms. For example, prior research suggests that software development outsourcing clients depend on business expertise from internal resources, while relying on technical expertise from the vendor (Kirsch et al. 2002, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). Our own study focused only on technical resource provisioning for software design and implementation. Future research is therefore needed to examine the potential moderating influences of different types of vendor expertise under different types of arrangement characteristics.

Additional research is also needed to examine other aspects of the values similarity construct not considered here. For example, trust between the client and vendor has previously been found to be associated with improved software development outsourcing project performance (Rai et al. 2009), and this variable could be explicitly captured and modeled as a potential moderator. Other aspects of relational governance examined by Goo et al. (2009) in the context of service level agreements (e.g., harmonious conflict resolution and mutual dependence) and differences due to regional and national values (Gallivan and Srite 2005) also need to be examined from the perspective of lateral coordination mechanisms to address client-vendor coordination challenges.

Further, more research is needed to examine the potential complementary and substitution effects of the structural and informal coordination mechanisms operationalized for this study from an information processing lens. Other studies of outsourcing governance mechanisms from control-theoretic (Tiwana 2010, Srivastava and Teo 2012) and relational exchange perspectives (Poppo and Zenger 2002, Goo et al. 2009) have found evidence for these types of effects, but at the individual mechanism level. Future research is therefore needed to tease out the effects from a coordination perspective. We also theorized and measured the extent to which formal (and informal) mechanisms were implemented by the client, rather than whether any of the mechanisms were specified as part of a contract. Therefore, future studies could be designed to take into account both a priori specifications found in IS development outsourcing contracts as well as other mechanisms implemented by client managers.

Although we focused on coordination mechanisms from an information processing perspective to address a major gap in the prior IS outsourcing literature, and our findings provide strong support for our arguments for distinguishing between coordination and control mechanisms, we also acknowledge that IS managers of an IS development outsourcing arrangement must address both control and coordination challenges. As previously theorized, control mechanisms are better suited to address exchange hazards and transaction cost issues in a relationship (Gopal and Koka 2012, Srivastava and Teo 2012), whereas coordination mechanisms are needed to manage interdependencies between the client and vendor (Sabherwal 2003). The examination of the performance impacts associated with control and coordination mechanisms in the same study, including theorizing differential impacts under different arrangement characteristics, could also yield additional insights.

Finally, more research is needed in general to examine the impacts of formal and informal mechanisms on performance outcomes for different types of IS outsourcing relationships. For example, an arrangement involving infrastructure outsourcing is clearly different from IS development outsourcing. Also, client firms have become more sophisticated in their vendor selection and outsourcing arrangements as they have transitioned from single-vendor to multivendor arrangements (Poston et al. 2009), including migrating from engagements that focus on costs to those that involve strategic partnerships and collaborative innovation (Whitley and Willcocks 2011). If the client perceives a current vendor to be a potential long-term partner in the future, this could also have an impact on the choice of coordination mechanisms and how they evolve over a specific client-vendor arrangement.

## Implications for Practice

This study also has important implications for practitioners. Our findings show that by not only implementing formal structural overlays, such as committees and liaison roles, but also facilitating client-vendor communications via colocation, informal meetings, and social events, the likelihood of achieving strategic IT benefits from an outsourcing arrangement that go beyond specific project benefits is significantly increased. Our results for the moderating roles of vendor resource provisioning and values similarity also have immediate practical implications. Many client firms focus on the technical capabilities of a potential vendor and may even utilize the capability maturity model (CMM) as a key criterion for selecting vendors (Gopal et al. 2003). However, in addition to focusing on coordination mechanisms to leverage the vendor’s technical capabilities, our findings illustrate that the degree to which organizational values are shared across the client and vendor can significantly strengthen or weaken the effects of mechanisms designed to increase information processing across individuals and work teams. Hence, practices for software package selection such as consultations with and on-site visits to existing or prior clients of a vendor under consideration may be critical for assessing values similarity prior to a contract negotiation process.

We also found that greater usage of informal governance mechanisms is associated with greater strategic IT benefits when the client relies more heavily on a vendor’s technical resources than their own. That is, implementing “informal organization” mechanisms increases the likelihood that the client’s employees informally engage with the vendor’s personnel, and thus are more likely to elicit the tacit knowledge of vendor personnel, especially in the presence of this type of resource asymmetry. Although these interpersonal mechanisms can be implemented easily for vendor personnel located at a client site, more innovative approaches are needed for developing mechanisms to informally engage with offsite personnel; in such situations, technology solutions designed to promote informal virtual interactions are needed. In sum, by implementing both formal and informal mechanisms designed to coordinate IS development activities for onshore and offshore arrangements, client firms can reap greater benefits from either single outsourcing arrangements, or a well-managed set of vendors (Poston et al. 2009).

## Conclusion

The objective of this study was to theorize and test the impacts of structural and informal governance mechanisms designed to coordinate the activities of clients and vendors under different arrangement characteristics. By drawing on both organization design theory and the strategic alliance literature, we believe that this study addresses significant theoretical and empirical gaps in the prior IS outsourcing literature. Focusing on coordination rather than control mechanisms, we applied an information processing lens to theorize the impacts of sets of formal and informal mechanisms, and found empirical evidence for client-side strategic IT benefits from the outsourcing arrangement. We also provide empirical support for differential impacts due to two key characteristics of this type of exchange relationship—the extent of resource provisioning by the vendor versus the client, and the extent to which the client and vendor have high values similarity. As described in the implications section, some of the ways that future researchers can build on these findings include exploring the potential complementary and substitution effects of individual formal or informal coordination mechanisms, increasing our understanding about how and why sets of governance mechanisms evolve over the life of an arrangement, and examining the potential moderating roles of other arrangement characteristics.
