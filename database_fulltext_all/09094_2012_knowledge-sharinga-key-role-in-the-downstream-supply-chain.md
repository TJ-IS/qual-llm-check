## A B S T R A C T

We explore the role of knowledge sharing within a downstream two-echelon supply chain. Drawing on chaos theory and the literature on knowledge management, we contrasted the information and knowledge sharing contexts. More specifically, we have provided a real-world case study of knowledge management practice at a U.S. Fortune 40 firm. We reviewed the major issues in this firm’s downstream supply chain operations and have described its knowledge management initiative. Finally, we discuss the implications of knowledge management on managerial practice.

## 1. Introduction

Since Dell Computer introduced the concept of built-to-order computer manufacturing in 1985, the general approach to production has shifted from mass production to mass customization. After more than two decades of development, the process has been extended to industries such as the automobile, shoes, clothing, and furniture. Furthermore, manufacturers have attempted to combine the built-to-order approach with others, such as just-in-time, lean manufacturing, and time-to-market, to respond more effectively to specific and changing customer needs.

For any manufacturing strategy to work well, it is necessary to stabilize demand and reduce supply chain uncertainty [13]. One way to decrease such uncertainty is to integrate the IT system with the supply chain network resulting in lower production cost, shorter lead times, and faster product delivery.

Chaos theory suggests that uncertainty in the supply chain can be managed by imposing rules or principles guided by existing knowledge and experience. Supply chain management (SCM) helps organizations manage the flow of information, money, and products beyond the physical boundaries of the organization. Members of forecast-driven supply chains generally focus on estimating demand at the next link in the chain and consequently often face problems created by poor forecasting and the so-called bullwhip effect, which results from a lack of transparency of the actual demand. An effective solution to such problems is to promote the efficient flow of information in the supply chain.

IT may be used to streamline the supply chain information flow [18]. Clearly, the goal is to avoid information manipulation and prevent inaccurate forecasts. While the bulk of the supply chain literature has focused on information management in the upstream, we addressed the importance of information to downstream operations. More specifically, we explored the role of information sharing in the business processes of downstream supply chain operations.

Information sharing is suitable for efficient day-to-day operations but when the decision making processes become more complex and chaotic, knowledge management is better because it makes it easier to address the uncertainty and changes in the environment. Previous experience reflects tacit rather than explicit knowledge. However, decision makers often need to rely on both explicit and tacit knowledge.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Chaos theory

Supply chains may face chaotic situations due to uncertainty or complexity. For example, forecasting customer demand is difficult. While the needs of a customer is difficult to predict, a comprehensive demand planning model requires the aggregation of future demand to be made by all consumers – a significantly more complex undertaking [20].

Chaos theory deals with complex, nonlinear dynamic systems – all parts being complex, not random. In other words, it seeks to explain apparent disorder in a very orderly way.Often, managers in different parts of a supply chain face different and conflicting objectives which hinder cooperation. Prater [17] asserted that chaos may result from decision processes or control systems such as ERP, warehousing, or inventory management, caused by variability, complexity, uncertainty, and spikes. IS researchers have tried to render chaotic situations manageable. Recent advances view supply chains as complex adaptive systems (CAS) that are best managed by understanding how and why they occur.

## 2.2. Tacit and explicit knowledge

Knowledge creation, organization, and use are facilitated by the flow of information. It is important to distinguish between data, information, and knowledge. The primary distinction lies in its degree of organization and usefulness. Data are raw stimuli with little organization or ready utility. Knowledge is a high value form of information that may be useful in making decisions and prompting actions. Tacit knowledge is difficult to codify, transmit, or convey: it contains data that are processed, organized, and useful, but the logic of its organization is frequently complex, implicit, and ambiguous [4]. It is rooted in actions within a specific context. It is important for solving problems that are intractable, complex, or variable.

As phenomena become better understood, the knowledge necessary for decision-making becomes more explicit. As solutions to a problem become more routine, they can be codified more easily. This simplifies the extraction and processing of information. Explicit knowledge is discrete and digital, and may be easily transmitted via formal and systematic means. Examples include records from the past, such as from archives or databases. In contrast, tacit knowledge represents a higher level of abstraction than explicit knowledge.

The degree to which knowledge is tacit influences the choice of communication channels most appropriate for its dissemination. For example, when knowledge is only tacit, it may not be transferable through face-to-face interaction.

Alavi and Tiwana [1] stated that knowledge management (KM) involves both technological and social-cultural factors and should focus on the flow of information and remain a management issue, with technology performing the role of supporting it.

## 3. Knowledge sharing

## 3.1. Information sharing

Within a supply chain, all the information needed for its efficient operation should be available at the right place and time. Leading firms facilitate information flows to encourage the sharing of point of sale (POS) information with others. The contribution of information sharing to performance improvement has been empirically validated.

According to Kaipia [12], supply chain characteristics can be balanced by a coordination mechanism that uses information to support both material flow and resources allocation. Thus flexible material flow needs frequent updates based on accurate information. If the material flow is inflexible, there is no need to update information frequently. However, the likelihood of excess inventory or capacity rises.

In supply chain operations, delays, amplifications, or oscillations in the information flow may adversely impact inventory and production efficiency. However, Steckel et al. [21] found that information sharing is not enough to improve performance unless customer demand is stable. As Kulp et al. [14] stated, information sharing is necessary to remain competitive but not sufficient to earn superior profits. As a result, some firms share knowledge both internally and externally to their customers, suppliers, and partners.

## 3.2. Knowledge sharing

Knowledge sharing focuses on the supply side of KM. In contrast, knowledge creation concentrates more on the demand side. Johnson and Whang [11] proposed the term e-collaboration for systems that facilitate Internet-based coordination of decisions across all members of the supply chain. Coordinating information flow requires a system to identify, analyze, and transform useful information into reusable knowledge which can be used to make decisions with extensive impact along the supply chain.

Uncertainty contributes to variability in a supply chain: unpredictable customer demand is its primary source. An ecollaboration approach may significantly speed up knowledge sharing. Park et al. [16] identified the behavioral process of knowledge transfer from vendor and client matched-pairs from 87 IT outsourcing projects: they found that the client’s IT human capability was a crucial factor in effective knowledge transfer during IT outsourcing. IT personnel who considered the vendors’ competencies in IT practice and tackled related technological challenges contributed effectively to knowledge transfer.

Wadhwa and Saxena [25] noted that the competitive potential of a flexible supply chain (FSC) was driven by KM. They also provided directions for applying KM to FSCs that were more complex, involved multiple autonomous players, and/or included different technical cultures, managerial backgrounds, and degree of SCM exposure. In KM practice, technical culture influences the knowledge mindset. Different managerial backgrounds may affect the decision making and prior SCM exposure may affect the attitude toward knowledge sharing.

Compared to information sharing, knowledge sharing contributes more to efficient and effective decision making. The biggest challenge is in establishing a trusting relationship between the sharing parties [5]. Partnership quality, as pointed out by Vijayasarathy [24], significantly influences performance.

## 3.3. Decision knowledge sharing in the downstream supply chain

Decision knowledge sharing involves proactive knowledge management. As the level of cooperation and coordination in the downstream supply chain increases, the need for both transactional and decision-making data increases because the singlefaceted transaction information alone is not helpful to a customer facing a product-related problem. This requires that customers have access to a knowledge database that helps them understand the problem and navigate the appropriate steps to a solution.

Dell Computers nicely illustrates this. The firm adapted a flexible infrastructure to support its mass customization agenda, thereby coordinating its suppliers and customers beyond Dell’s geographical boundaries. In addition, Dell allowed its customers to access internal support information online using the same routines as its own technical support teams. Dell’s customers can access purchasing and technical information about their own specific hardware and software configurations as well as benefit from an in-depth knowledge base for self-service problem solving. Thus, decision-making processes accommodate the needs of Dell’s service representatives and their customers.

Mason and Leek’s [15] longitudinal study emphasized (a) knowwhat is needed to develop a shared-framework between firms, and (b) know-who should investigate the improvement of the decision-making processes. They found that organizations identify and link together the key actors. Further, they provide actors with appropriate knowledge types and knowledge transfer mechanisms to fit their specific needs. Such changes improve performance, provide learning through practice, and emulate experienced mentors [2]. The knowledge transfer mechanisms can be established through developing communities of practice [6]. In an analogous fashion, decision knowledge sharing can be considered as a set of decision-making processes in a downstream supply chain. The initiation of a new order in the downstream triggers appropriate knowledge from the knowledge base. As a result, the best context-relevant knowledge (linked to the upstream chain) is identified and sent to the decision makers in the downstream chain. This process is repeated until a ‘‘solution’’ is found that suits all components of the supply chain. ## 4. The use of IT in knowledge sharing

Knowledge sharing for decision-making requires faster and precise operations at each level of the supply chain, which resembles a serial multi-echelon inventory system with an first class control policy; the inventory database should be unique to allow effective inventory management to be computed at all points in the supply chain. Even though decentralization is an inevitable act in managing a large organization, it can disrupt the alignment between suppliers and customers. While IT can help reduce the number of echelons and streamline business processes of each supply chain partner, our case study focused on aspects of IT that manage knowledge sharing in the decision-making processes.

A predefined ontology is needed for effective KM: it ensures exact meanings when communicating with supply chain partners [23]. When an enterprise participates in a supply chain network, it may acquire the characteristics of a complex, multi-agent system that spans several KM domains. Ontology is also needed for builtto-order (BTO) production networks that operate with multi-facet knowledge from different sources to build customized products for customers without using traditional steps [10]. BTO manufacturing may be chaotic and resemble a downstream supply chain that needs significant scheduling, real-time coordination, and uncertainty.

Wycisk et al. [26] utilized complex theory and comparative analysis to illustrate Complex Adaptive Logistic System (CALS) which provided solutions under chaotic operations. They found that the business model evolved from an agent-based model of interactions using communication devices such as RFID in the supply chain [22]. The agent-based model represents the intelligent learning capability and evolves from internal, external and/or environmental changes. This process of learning to change may create negative effects in CALS. In sum, the overall objective of CALS is to react to the complex and changing environment in order to sustain the firm’s competitive advantage [3].

To manage the complex and changing customer demands as well as to cooperate with all partners in the supply chain, a firm may consider other approaches such as Enterprise Application Integration or Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) to integrate across applications and business processes. ## 5. Case study—knowledge management (KM) in a HVAC firm

Our case study involved a US firm in the HVAC (heating, ventilating, and air conditioning) industry. We focused on the effect of coordinated decision making in its downstream supply chain to improve pre-sale tasks (such as demand forecasting and order fulfillment) and after-sale services and maintenance processes. The time frame of this case study was from 2000 to 2001. In particular, we sought to clarify the role of KM in the downstream part of the firm’s two-echelon supply chain.

## 5.1. Firm’s background

The firm had a worldwide network of hundreds of distributors and thousands of retailers, dealers, and repair centers that sell, install, and repair HVAC products in over 150 countries. With over \$ 9 billion in annual revenue, the firm offers a wide range of products in three major areas: home heating and cooling systems (e.g., air conditioners, heat pumps, and furnaces), commercial heating and cooling systems for business office and school buildings, and commercial refrigeration and transport refrigeration systems (i.e., truck/trailer refrigeration equipment and transport air conditioning systems for the bus, rail, and marine industries). It designs and manufactures its products in 20 engineering centers and over 100 plants in five continents. Within the US and Canada, the firm has over 90 service offices with an average of 12 service technicians per office.

## 5.2. Case study objectives

Our study addressed knowledge sharing and KM issues in the downstream part of a two-echelon supply chain; we focused on knowledge sharing practices via effective knowledge assimilation and distribution over the Internet.

## 5.3. Case study methodology and process

## 5.3.1. Case study research design

Our study was designed to investigate approaches that may reduce the impact of bullwhip and similar effects. We sought to examine the ‘‘why,’’ ‘‘how,’’ and ‘‘what’’ questions about a set of SCM related events over time. The ‘‘how’’ question was of paramount importance that demands a holistic, in-depth investigation [9].

## 5.3.2. Research team

The members of the research team initially included senior managers of the firm (Director of Product Delivery Systems and Senior Manager of Supply Chain Operations), systems engineers (including one of the authors) from the Research Center, and consultants from a third-party consulting firm (KPMG), software firms (SAP), and computer equipment firms (Sun Microsystems). The researcher/author had the following responsibilities: develop the baseline plan and its critical path, conduct research, assimilate the results, and manage the simulation environment to develop and execute enterprise-level models of the aftermarket supply chain processes. Consultants from KPMG helped manage the software implementation and demonstration activities. Members from Sun served as the technology suppliers, technical system architects, and system demonstration developers.

## 5.3.3. Facilities and equipment

The Research Center of the firm provided both program management and technical support in various areas of applied research, such as: operations optimization, SCM, and IT. Its Distributed Systems Laboratory was made available for providing a visual workspace for system model development and simulation of super-hybrid supply chain systems. This facility was primarily used for distributed systems that dealt with complex communication issues in downstream supply chains, knowledge sharing environments, and diverse computing platforms. For system modeling, design, and testing activities, the laboratory was equipped with sophisticated tools. A distributed-object framework (CORBA) was used as the lab’s primary computing framework. Distributed computing was performed through PC clusters and Sun Microsystems workstations. C++ and Java were the main computer languages used for development, testing, and demonstration, while running on hybrid computing systems, including Microsoft Windows and Unix.

## 5.3.4. Baseline planning

As Chen et al. [7] have noted, IT has changed the focus of business processes from an intra- to cross-enterprise. Thus collaboration is considered crucial for successful performance. We made sure that data collection captured the essential characteristics of both intra- and inter-enterprise systems. A functional requirements document, and baseline report, was developed during the initial phase of the study. This captured essential KM and information processing functions, assessed dependencies among various components of the downstream supply chain, and evaluated alternative knowledge sharing strategies and solutions. We also estimated the corresponding values and associated risks of these strategies and solutions. These steps required a number of brainstorming sessions, including the development of an explicit baseline of the intended enterpriselevel knowledge management system and learning how its subsystems interacted; generating alternatives to the baseline, evaluating the value of each, and selecting the highest-valued for development; and ensuring that key decision points, technical risks, modeling and prototyping goals were thoroughly understood by the research team.

## 5.3.5. Data collection

Data were collected from multiple sources. A case study repository was developed to store key data elements along with detailed descriptions of the elements and the data source. The qualitative evidence collected included semi-structured individual and group interviews (50 informants) as well as a study of relevant internal business documents, reports, and notes. In addition, extensive field notes provided a rich context for interpreting qualitative data. Four weeks were spent on these tasks, which included an initial study, interviews, on-site observations, and data collection and assimilation.

(a) Study of current supply chain operations. After reviewing the firm’s current systems and operations, the research team concluded that its supply chain reflected two types of complexity (time-scale dynamic range and problem size) that characterize super-hybrid systems. Although supply chains can evolve with characteristic time-constants of weeks and months, the so-called time-scale dynamic range results in ‘‘stiff’’ systems that are very difficult to simulate accurately and efficiently. The problem size characteristic indicates that the whole supply chain may track thousands of units where each unit represents hundreds of actions, and where each action represents a unique supply chain operation. Overall, therefore, this presents a very complex supply chain problem.

(b) Interviews. A series of in-depth group and individual interviews were conducted with fifty people. Each session lasted from 30 min to an hour. The discussions were taped and transcribed. Over 20 interview sessions were with people representing key areas such as demand management, production planning, customer services, transportation, logistics, warehousing management, supply planning, purchasing, and enterprise distribution. To capture the firm’s long-term perspectives and strategic goals, a number of senior managers were also interviewed (the President of the Residential and Commercial Products Group, General Manager of the Residential Cooling Products Group, Vice President of the Heating Products Group, Director of IS, and business managers and representatives from various functional areas. Representatives from selected distributors were also interviewed. Interview questions focused on identifying KM strategies, knowledge sharing scenarios, supply chain objectives and processes, IP capabilities, key challenges and issues of SCM, opinions on opportunities for improvement, key performance metrics, etc.

(c) On-site observations. These were recorded at various locations, including manufacturing floors and factory offices; they allowed us to perform detailed contextual analysis of a limited number of events or conditions in knowledge sharing. Field notes from the on-site visits provided a detailed overview of our observations of current systems.

(d) Study of relevant business documents, reports, and notes. These efforts sought information about key aspects of the firm’s downstream supply, its supply chain processes, inventory profiles, and customer service performance.

## 5.3.6. Data analysis and proof-of-concept

All the data and facts gathered in the previous phase were documented, classified, and cross-referenced. Analytic strategies were developed to ensure that raw data and the essential facts were linked to provide a context to the research questions.

The next phase involved implementing the principle of proof of-concept to demonstrate the viability of the case study. It included two major tasks, scenario-building and scenario-based simulation:

\- Scenario-building. A series of scenarios were developed to test the supply chain interactions and knowledge sharing activities of different segments (computers, local networks, etc.). A multiplescenario design was developed to reflect the system’s behavior and capture the underlying problems of knowledge sharing in the downstream supply chain. This helped us find attainable solutions that satisfied the requirements of each entity in various interaction and knowledge sharing scenarios.

\- Scenario-based simulation. To assure proper assessment of the way that knowledge was managed and shared, a scenario-based simulation was developed. An integrated visual process simulation environment [8] was created to capture key coordination processes in the supply chain. The supply chain’s performance was assessed by simulating the impact of the proposed redesign alternatives prior to their selection and implementation. This approach helped us discover the best KM practices and strategies for predefined business objectives. A test-bed simulation environment was created to help control for potentially confounding variables. We observed and cross-referenced similar phenomena in multiple scenarios of knowledge sharing operations and thus developed generalized results. Multiplescenario data were analyzed and structural analysis was performed to identify patterns inherent in knowledge sharing.

## 5.3.7. Validation of case study results

The next step involved systematic validation and analysis of simulation results. We specified validation criteria to facilitate knowledge sharing practice in the downstream supply chain. To do this, a validating process was performed to examine whether the model correctly represented the essential aspects of the downstream supply chain network in a real-life context. Two important perspectives of validity were addressed: first, the simulation model was checked to assure that it accommodated all the requirements and performance criteria previously established; then the model was validated to determine that it tracked system user requirements and fully addressed the system users’ intent.

## 5.4. Problem description

We were then able to identify several emergent issues posed by the unique characteristics of the firm’s downstream supply chain. A manger in the Operations Optimization Group described the chaotic nature of the supply chain as:

‘‘First, our downstream supply chain tracks up to hundreds of thousands of business units, such as warehouses, distribution centers, dealers, and so on. Each business unit further performs hundreds of actions every day. Second, in a supply chain like ours, the object types range from material properties, inventories, technical skills, to transportation dynamics, which form a complex supply chain taxonomy. Furthermore, our downstream supply chain can be described as a ‘‘stiff’’ system that can evolve vigorously with characteristic time-constants of minutes, hours, weeks or months.’’.. ‘‘As you may have been aware, a special phenomenon, the so called bullwhip effect, has occurred regularly in our downstream supply chain. This effect has long been under investigation to study how the decision making process can be a major predicament due to scarce knowledge on demand. The bullwhip effect usually takes place as the demand order variability in the supply chain is overly amplified when they moved to the upstream supply chain. Information distortion on demand from our end customers, dealers, and retailers to the factories can result in excessive inventories and enormous operational inefficiencies.’’

As shown in the simulation window of Fig. 1, even when the error in forecasting customer demand for a sample HVAC product was relatively small (about 12%) at the product group level, this fact acquired substantial significance and volatility (almost 75%) at the stock keeping units (SKU) level because of demand order variability at retailers and dealers. Fig. 2 further illustrates that a high variability of forecast error can cause large stocks of finished goods at factories and in the downstream supply chain.

To effectively address the bullwhip effect, it was vital to eliminate information distortion in the supply chain through innovative KM strategies. The firm has recently experienced rapid changes and wild swings in customer demand that have produced chaotic conditions within its supply chain. This phenomenon usually has a direct impact on supply chain optimization [19].

One senior operations research (OR) engineer explained, ‘‘In our supply chain environment, dramatic change can occur unexpectedly. Chaotic spikes in demand can simply happen without any predictable patterns. In a supply chain, simply an infinitesimal change in the initial conditions can considerably alter the long-term behaviour of a system. Eventually, these situations can increase our operational costs and inventories drastically, and in turn, slash our delivery performance and customer service quality. In a supply chain, it can present both short term and long term performance deterioration due to the chaotic situations.’’ . . . ‘‘Even with the apparent random data generated from chaos, interestingly, we could find patterns in the data. Nevertheless, these patterns never repeat themselves twice. This can be characterized as aperiodic behavior. In a sense, irregular oscillations in the supply chain never repeat the same state or pattern more than once. For instance, the inventory level in each distribution center, factory, or warehouse never replicates itself.’’

Chaotic phenomenon was also described by another OR engineer:

‘‘The lack of aggregated demand forecast information often leads to oscillations in demand and inventories as orders pass through the supply chain from our local dealers and retailers to the distribution centers and to our manufacturing factories. The uncertainty and chaos can cause excessive inventories to cushion the effect. Very likely, chaos can result in late deliveries, or even order cancellation.’’

According to a field research report compiled for our study, the firm’s supply chain had a number of chaotic systems. Through the analysis of the time series data of supply chain and logistics, it was found that changing the business conditions (such as the service levels) in one regional retailer could significantly affect the fill rate and order-to-ship lead time of many upstream factories. Obviously, such business situations are unpredictable and therefore, the decision making tasks become extremely intricate.

Partially due to its rapid expansion, the firm continues to run some of its businesses under different ISs than those in the rest of its businesses. A key reason for the firm’s recent productivity impasse is that it failed to coordinate and integrate the ISs of all its global units. Without coordination, some of the parts do not fit properly into the entire system, and thus do not form a synergistic whole. One project manager said:

‘‘Coordination problems in a supply chain are likely to surface as most of our downstream business units have very restricted knowledge of the entire supply chain environment. Lack of knowledge about the global environment will result in making the decisions more difficult and creating enormous demand information distortion. In addition, it’s a challenge for us to properly evaluate the current and future situations and the possible outcomes of any decision made.’’

## A system integrator in the IS Group said:

‘‘Simply put, most of our enterprise applications are only interfaced rather than integrated. In addition, our legacy ISs cannot generate real-time information and only do batch processing of production reporting. As a result, higher inventory levels must be maintained to compensate for lack of timely information, to allow the firm to maintain competitive market position and profitability.’

In response to this challenge, the top executives of the firm have searched for an innovative business model to turn its geographically dispersed business operations into a seamless global supply chain.

## 5.5. Web-centric knowledge management initiatives

In 2001, some of the firm’s downstream business partners (dealers and retailers) were signing up for the virtual trading marketplace. The firm’s major e-business initiative was the deployment of the web-centric extended enterprise knowledge sharing (WEEKS) system. The purpose of this system was to enhance supply chain performance through electronic knowledge sharing.

The lack of a viable mechanism for knowledge sharing among supply chain partners results in destructive effects on overall operational efficiency and profitability. Knowledge sharing was recognized by the firm’s supply chain optimization steering committee as one of the key success factors for improving the degree of success of current SCM endeavors. As a result, coupling the KM initiative with current SCM processes was expected to help raise the supply chain performance.

## 5.5.1. The Web-centric Extended Enterprise Knowledge Sharing

The WEEKS system was developed as an important step in the KM initiative. Its Framework is the underlying technological structure for advancing KM solutions for effective knowledge sharing and supply chain collaboration. A conceptual diagram of the WEEKS framework is shown in Fig. 3.

Key features of the WEEKS system include data warehousing, multiple enterprise portals, inter- and intra-organizational information and knowledge exchange, content aggregation and hosting, product catalog aggregation, syndication of metadata, and user registration and authentication. By using WEEKS knowledge can be shared among internal business units and external partners. Examples of explicit knowledge include costs of inventory and expedited transportation as well as customer responsiveness in terms of reliability, flexibility, and lead time. Collaborative Planning, Forecasting and Replenishment (CPFR) provides a mechanism to represent explicit knowledge, especially coordination tasks within a supply chain. Tacit knowledge, such as understanding the policies or best practices in managing late deliveries or material shortages, is an essential part of the system’s knowledge base.

WEEKS adopted a hybrid approach to knowledge sharing (see Fig. 4). In a centralized knowledge sharing mode, the knowledge sharing engine runs on a site that resides at the firm’s factory. The DKS engine runs on each registered business partner’s website and handles only information exchanges and knowledge sharing between a particular plant and its selected portal partners.

Focusing on the logic of knowledge acquisition, we note that explicit and tacit knowledge is created by mining the ‘‘pulled’’ information from downstream business partners. In turn, the pertinent applicable knowledge will be ‘‘pushed’’ throughout the supply chain. Fig. 5 illustrates the pulled and pushed knowledge moving through the centralized and distributed knowledge sharing portals at various levels of the supply chain.

The decision-making process in a supply chain usually engages both KM and SCM. Such information is often scattered across the supply chain; therefore, aggregating them from multiple sources is an important task in the KM initiative. The key to concurrent planning requires seamless coordination across supply chain functions. The WEEKS system uses synchronized and concurrent planning to help managers make rapid and correct decisions, which resolves conflicts between production priorities and available capacity.

The WEEKS system provides a solution after receiving the pertinent information and knowledge. This helps the decision maker evaluate alternatives. Furthermore, applicable business situations (such as seasonal trends, product life cycle trends, price fluctuation, or rationing and shortage gaming) are also taken into consideration by the system.

The greater visibility provided by more relevant knowledge helps decision makers to perceive the problem from a broader perspective, which helps achieve uninterrupted decision making by eliminating direct feedback and removing chaos in the supply chain. For example, a manager may reduce the recommended order quantities for a particular part because it is due to be replaced in the near future. Similarly, based on the sales trends and quantity discount programs, the decision maker may increase the quantity ordered to a level qualifying for discounts.

A value-focused innovation technique was employed to supplement the validation effort. The value-focused innovation technique included best practice functional modeling, value analysis, and supported brainstorming methods to model potential problems caused by each segment of the supply chain.

## 7. Discussion and implications

The purpose of this case study was to put our knowledge sharing theory into practice. Our case study should encourage supply chain practitioners and managers to use the experience and learning from this real-world application to develop and refine the application of KM principles and strategies in the downstream supply chain.

We examined how downstream SCM can increase efficiency and effectiveness by incorporating KM techniques. Our WEEKS system, along with the knowledge sharing models, provides a viable framework for building a seamless, collaborative supply chain network. Nevertheless, our goal was not to provide a recipe book but to present a pragmatic, structured framework that is likely to help supply chain mangers develop more pragmatic KM and SCM solutions.

Our work delineates how WEEKS works to share knowledge among different parties. Basically, the push and pull of information/knowledge on WEEKS are codified and made more explicit. However, when the decision maker bases decisions on knowledge, it tends to have a more tacit character. Thus alternative decisions are comprised by the combination of explicit and tacit knowledge that may be intuitive, personal, and experience-based.

Decision-makers may still follow certain principles based on chaos theory to make decisions with risks and uncertainty. They may also choose to develop contingency plans after making such decisions.

## 7.1. Discussion of case study results

Our case study accurately showed how information was shared among different parties in the supply chain. It also highlighted decision-making processes that rely on a Web-centric knowledge sharing system that steered decision makers toward improved KM practice. From this study, the following suggestions may be given for future SCM and KM endeavors.

\- To improve supply chain performance, a firm should focus on knowledge sharing rather than cost savings alone.

\- The key to mitigating chaos in a supply chain is to create a knowledge sharing environment that provides global knowledge. Firms should avoid direct feedback loops residing at many disjoint ISs.

\- Long-term planning is usually infeasible because planning cannot accommodate fluctuations in daily operations or chaotic environments. WEEKS provides real-time knowledge that facilitates just-in-time decisions.

\- In global competition scenarios, rapidly changing customer demand may warrant special consideration by partners in the downstream supply chain. This characteristic is exacerbated for global supply chains where the partners have varying backgrounds and exposures. The development of a generic knowledge sharing and management model for supporting an enterpriselevel super-hybrid environment requires significantly higher levels of innovation and advanced technology.

\- Developing a generic system to advance enterprise-level knowledge sharing system is an undertaking that demands integration of numerous business processes, data, and ISs. A great deal of risk and uncertainty are inherent in this endeavor.

\- The development of knowledge-based supply chain depends on the nature of knowledge flow in the entire chain. Supply chain partners will find it very useful to share decision knowledge on a timely basis. However this requires change in managerial mindsets and corporate culture. Furthermore, support and commitment from top management are essential to ensure successful implementation of knowledge sharing.

\- KM guided SCM systems involve proactive KM strategies.

## 7.2. Limitations

The goal of knowledge sharing practice is not restricted to the transmission of knowledge to target receivers. Successful knowledge sharing must assure that knowledge is absorbed and used by recipients. Knowledge sharing is more effective if it is better aligned with the organization’s culture. This could be a shortcoming of our case study because the research attention centers on just one firm.

The WEEK system is better adapted for short-term supply chain planning though it can also be used as a mechanism for long-term planning. Potential problems in developing and/or deploying such a system may arise from barriers to knowledge creation or knowledge sharing. Thus, willingness of all partners to view the system as unit of the supply chain network is essential. The knowledge sharing environment still needs to be developed after the firm has implemented the WEEKS system. Indeed, the assimilation of the WEEKS system needs continuous improvement.

## 7.3. Managerial implications

To improve supply chain performance, the firm should focus on knowledge sharing rather than cost savings. Mitigating chaos in a supply chain is essential to creating a knowledge sharing environment that provides the one-and-only KM system.

In practice, the WEEKS system should be used to make shortrather than long-term planning. It is important to treat a supply chain as a complete system instead of adhering to the orthodox and autonomous principles of local management. A decision-making policy could be established to empower or incentivize local management to consider the benefit of viewing the supply chain as a whole. To enhance the performance of the entire supply chain, the firm needs to establish mutually trusting relationships with its business partners. Thus, trust-building instruments such as fair profit allocation rules should be in place to avoid potential disputes.

## 8. Conclusion

Our case study demonstrated how the downstream operations of a supply chain can benefit from incorporating knowledge sharing practice. The WEEKS system developed by the case firm, along with the knowledge sharing models, provided a viable framework for building a collaborative supply chain network which renders a pragmatic, structured way to help supply chain mangers develop more pragmatic KM and SCM solutions.
