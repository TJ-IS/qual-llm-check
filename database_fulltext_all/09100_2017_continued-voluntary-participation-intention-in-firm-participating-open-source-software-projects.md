# Continued Voluntary Participation Intention in Firm-Participating Open Source Software Projects

Shuk Ying Ho,<sup>a</sup> Arun Rai<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Research School of Accounting, Australian National University, Canberra, ACT 2601, Australia; <sup>b</sup> Center for Process Innovation, Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303 Contact: susanna.ho@anu.edu.au, http://orcid.org/0000-0002-9450-5776 (SYH); arunrai@gsu.edu, http://orcid.org/0000-0002-3655-7543 (AR)

Received: July 22, 2013 Revised: July 10, 2014; June 4, 2015; April 27, 2016 Accepted: July 2, 2016 Published Online in Articles in Advance: June 21, 2017

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2016.0687

Abstract. Firm participation in open source software (OSS) development is a noteworthy phenomenon and includes two types of firm-participating OSS projects: community founded (developed from an open project) and spinout (spun out from an information technology firm’s internal project). OSS project leaders implement quality controls to improve the quality of developed products. They may not be aware that their implementation of quality controls produces a side efect—quality controls signal unobservable project quality to volunteers and promote volunteers’ continued participation intentions (VCPI). We focus on two quality controls—accreditation and code acceptance, which, respectively, map to the input and output quality of an OSS project—and compare their respective efects on VCPI in community-founded and spinout projects. We propose that accreditation and code acceptance influence VCPI by signaling unobservable input and output quality to volunteers. As we focus on continued participation, we theorize as to how volunteers’ tenure in OSS projects moderates the relationships between the signaling efects of input and output quality controls and VCPI. Furthermore, we theorize as to how the OSS project type moderates the efects of quality controls on VCPI. We surveyed 304 volunteers from 40 OSS projects and constructed a two-level model of project and developer factors to explain VCPI. Our findings indicate that both accreditation and code acceptance enhance VCPI. The signaling efects on VCPI associated with accreditation decline with volunteer tenure, but those associated with code acceptance do not. Accreditation and code acceptance influence VCPI, with community-founded projects exhibiting weaker direct positive efects and spinout projects exhibiting stronger direct positive efects. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings.

## 1. Introduction

Firm participation in open source software (OSS) development is a noteworthy phenomenon. According to the ninth annual Future of Open Source Survey, 64% of 1,300 firm respondents are actively involved in OSS projects worldwide, and over the next three years, 88% are expected to increase contributions to OSS development (Black Duck 2015). Well-known examples include IBM’s participation in the Linux kernel and Google’s participation in Android. Microsoft, the most prominent global critic of OSS, originally challenged the OSS premise by questioning whether anyone could aford to do free professional work; however, it too is now engaging in OSS development. At the end of 2006, Microsoft joined the Linux kernel development project to enhance the integration between Windows and Linux. In 2012, Microsoft was the 17th-largest corporate contributor to the Linux kernel and contributed 1% of the kernel change. Our study focuses on firm-participating OSS projects. Hereafter, we use the term “OSS projects” to refer to these projects.

Voluntary participation is crucial to OSS projects, with inadequate voluntary participation resulting in 80% of OSS projects canceled during development (Fang and Neufeld 2009). This is a concern for firm sponsors, as some want to leverage volunteers’ development resources to contribute to their code base (Spaeth et al. 2015). Therefore, the question of how to promote volunteers’ continued participation intentions (VCPI) is an important one. Prior research reveals that project quality (such as code quality and developer quality) is a motivator to retain volunteers in an OSS project (Baldwin and Clark 2006a, Santos et al. 2013). To increase project quality, OSS project leaders implement quality controls. To our knowledge, prior research has not examined how quality controls influence VCPI. We argue that project leaders may not be aware that the presence of quality controls produces a side efect— quality controls signal unobservable project quality to volunteers and increase VCPI. This leads to our first research question:

RQ1: How do quality controls of OSS projects influence VCPI?

In continued participation, volunteers’ direct experience with code and their interactions with developers in the same project provide information on project quality, and so, the efects of quality controls on VCPI may vary with volunteer experience. We suggest that this information is relatively limited, and therefore volunteers rely on signals associated with quality controls for a more comprehensive assessment of project quality to inform their continued participation intentions. We theorize and empirically test the signaling efects associated with quality controls on VCPI. We suggest that volunteers gain information about project quality through their participation and examine how volunteer tenure in the projects alters the signaling efects associated with the quality controls on VCPI. Accordingly, we formulate our second research question as follows:

RQ2: How does volunteer tenure change the relationship between quality controls and VCPI?

Firms have many ways to engage in OSS development. There are two common methods of firm participation. In the first method, firms associate themselves with open communities of hobbyists through sponsorship arrangements. The most common form of sponsorship is to assign projects to paid developers (Black Duck 2015). For example, Linus Torvalds initiated the development of the Linux kernel for fun (Hall 2010), and by 1999, Linux became a prominent operating system. Google and Samsung became frequent contributors to Linux and assigned their developers to work full time on the Linux kernel for integrating operating systems from their mobile devices with the Linux desktops and servers (Brodkin 2013). In the second method, firms release the proprietary source code of an internal project to start a new OSS project (Alexy 2009). For example, Sun Microsystems (now owned by Oracle) released its OpenOfice.org source code in 2000, with the aim of reducing Microsoft Ofice’s dominant market share by providing a free and open alternative. In accordance with the terminology used by West and O’Mahony (2005), we refer to the former type of OSS project in which a firm participates (e.g., the Linux kernel) as a community-founded project and the latter type (e.g., OpenOfice.org) as a spinout project. As community-founded and spinout projects difer in start-up processes and objectives, they attract volunteers with diferent characteristics. We anticipate that the efects of the quality controls on VCPI may vary between these types of OSS projects. As prior research does not diferentiate between VCPI in communityfounded and spinout projects, it is unclear to firm sponsors how quality controls might be utilized diferently to generate VCPI in either type of OSS project, motivating us to examine how the OSS project type moderates the efects of quality controls on VCPI. This leads us to formulate our third research question as follows:

RQ3: How does the type of OSS project—community founded or spinout—moderate the relationship between quality controls and VCPI?

We examine two quality controls: (1) accreditation (as an input control to increase the quality of developers) and (2) code acceptance (as an output control to increase the quality of new code, as well as the entire system). Accreditation examines the managerial aspects of developer selection, whereas code acceptance pertains to quality appraisal of the developed software. Project leaders implement accreditation and code acceptance as quality controls to improve the functioning of projects. They may not be aware that these two quality controls also act as signals to communicate unobservable project quality to volunteers. The concept of signaling has its origins in the economics literature and is used to describe a mechanism to reduce information asymmetry in the market, thus increasing the extent to which partners in an exchange are able to make eficient decisions (Spence 1974). We conceptualize accreditation and code acceptance as input and output quality signals, respectively, to volunteers in OSS projects, and suggest that they influence VCPI by signaling unobservable input and output quality to volunteers.

Our study has both theoretical and practical significance. From a theoretical perspective, we show that quality controls afect VCPI by signaling the input and output quality of the project. Moreover, we test the boundaries of signaling theory. Prior research has typically used signaling theory to investigate how a signal reduces information asymmetry between two parties in a new relationship. By contrast, we focus on the applicability of signaling theory to continued participation and empirically test how the moderating role of volunteer tenure in the project influences the signaling efects associated with quality controls on VCPI. We also demonstrate how the diferent types of OSS projects moderate the efects of quality controls on VCPI, thereby extending the focus of OSS research on this issue from volunteer-only projects to firmparticipating projects. From a practical perspective, our findings provide insights into how project leaders can leverage input/output quality controls to signal unobservable project quality characteristics to volunteers and increase the likelihood of retaining them. Moreover, as we take volunteer tenure and OSS project type into consideration, our findings inform project leaders on how the efectiveness of quality controls on retaining VCPI difers in new or established projects, and in community-founded or spinout projects.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Continued Participation in OSS Projects

As voluntary participation afects the sustainability of an OSS project (Fang and Neufeld 2009, Spaeth et al. 2015, Xu et al. 2009), researchers have examined how volunteers’ motivations influence their participation. For instance, von Hippel and von Krogh (2003) found that VCPI emerged from two extrinsic motives—peer cognition and status. Hann et al. (2013) examined how the economic returns—an extrinsic motivator—influence voluntary participation. They found that the credentials volunteers earned through a merit-based ranking system in an OSS project to be associated with an 18% increase in wages in other jobs, and that this additional accrual in earning motivated them to continue participating in the project. Recognizing the importance of volunteers’ motivations on VCPI, von Krogh et al. (2012) reviewed the relevant OSS literature to identify 10 motivational categories to contribute to OSS development. They extended the line of studies of volunteers’ motivations to contribute to OSS development with a social practice perspective that focused on action to explain how the social practice in a project mediates the efects between volunteers’ motivations and sustained contributions. In addition to volunteers’ motivations, prior research has examined the efects of OSS license choice (Stewart et al. 2006), intellectual property rights enforcement against developers (Wen et al. 2013), and project team configurations (Colazo and Fang 2010, Daniel et al. 2013) on OSS project activities—a manifestation of volunteers’ participation at the project level.

From an output quality perspective, there is evidence suggesting that high code quality is crucial for retaining volunteer participation in OSS projects. For instance, Baldwin and Clark (2006a) found that welldesigned code increases volunteers’ incentives to join and remain involved in an OSS project. Some information systems researchers consider code quality to be an extrinsic motive for voluntary participation (for example, Hertel et al. 2003). From an input quality perspective, there is evidence suggesting developer quality afects VCPI. Based on data collected from 4,000 OSS projects, Santos et al. (2013) found that developer quality directly influenced the amount of contributions to development made by volunteers. Xu et al. (2009) examined how developer-to-developer interactions influence the level of voluntary contributions. They found that a good interpersonal relationship with other developers and shared ideology among developers increased volunteers’ contributions. Qureshi and Fang (2011) studied how peripheral developers’ social interactions with core developers motivated volunteers to progress their status from peripheral to core developers in projects and found that a critical mass of core developers enhanced the sustainability of an OSS project.

Through continued participation, volunteers gain direct experience with code and with developers in the project. This direct experience enables them to make judgments about project quality. For instance, in code reuse, volunteers study existing code modules. In doing so, they obtain information about the quality of the studied code modules. Volunteers may seek assistance from other developers on technical problems. Through developer-to-developer interactions, volunteers obtain information about the technical knowledge of those developers who have ofered assistance. We now discuss how well the direct experience of volunteers helps them assess project quality.

## 2.2. Volunteers’ Direct Experience and Assessing OSS Project Quality

There are three reasons why the direct experience of volunteers is unlikely to provide them with complete information on project quality. First, although OSS projects are open in terms of code and who contributes code, it is labor intensive for a volunteer to inspect this open information to assess the quality of the entire OSS system. In OSS projects with firm participation, firms take part in projects by developing code, assisting in the testing phase, reporting bugs, contributing to packaging, suggesting functional requirements, or writing documentation (Goldman and Gabriel 2005). In addition, firms may ofer financial, marketing, and logistic support, or even coordinate the project (Lee 2012). Thus, these projects are often huge, involving thousands of geographically dispersed developers and a code base of millions of lines of code. For instance, the Linux 3.10 release had 16 million lines of code. Since the beginning of the git era (the Linux 2.6.11 release in 2005), a total of 9,784 committers have contributed to the Linux kernel and the number of active committers for the Linux 3.10 release is about 1,500. Apache Hadoop 2.3—a relatively new project, started in 2011— had 86 committees, a project management committee of 53 people, hundreds of bug fixers, and contained 320,000 lines of code. In general, even if volunteers sampled part of the code to “estimate” code quality, they may need to inspect a large chunk of code to detect a code defect. Defect density (number of defects per 1,000 lines of code) is a commonly used measurement for software quality. An analysis conducted by Coverity Inc. (2014) found that OSS projects with less than 100,000 lines of code had a defect density of 0.40, and those with between 100,000 and 500,000 lines of code had a defect density of 0.60. In other words, a volunteer has to engage in a labor-intensive inspection process and will need to inspect, on average, 1,700 to 2,500 lines of code to pick up a defect.

One may suggest that a modular OSS architecture enables volunteers to focus on a subset of code modules and their interfaces aligned with their software needs in code development. With code modularization, volunteers are only required to study a few code modules in code development. However, code modularization does not provide volunteers with complete information about the quality of the entire code. In other words, a modular OSS architecture enables eficient code development, but does not provide a complete assessment of project quality.

Second, volunteers need sophisticated knowledge and skills to assess project quality and some may not have such knowledge and skills. Software development is a complex knowledge-intensive process and involves requirement design, algorithm design, code design, and data structure design (Weiss 1998). The quality of each of these aspects of software development is crucial to the overall quality of the system. An average volunteer may not be capable of assessing all these aspects. Thus, even though code is available, volunteers are unlikely to be able to assess the quality of the entire system thoroughly. Although automated software inspection tools can help identify software faults (e.g., coding standard noncompliance, security vulnerabilities, redundant code, division by zero, and memory leaks) that are not detected in the compiling process, these tools can fail to detect algorithm defects and are limited in their ability to inspect requirements and design (Nagappan et al. 2004, Zheng et al. 2006).

Third, volunteers in certain roles in OSS development gain limited direct experience with code and have little interpersonal experience with other developers of the same projects. Thus, volunteers cannot only rely on direct experience to assess project quality. To elaborate, volunteers in OSS projects take on diferent roles according to their personal interests. Depending on their roles, some volunteers will read only a small portion of the source code of the OSS project, and some volunteers will not need to interact with other developers. For instance, bug fixers fix the bugs that they discover or those reported by bug reporters. Bug fixers only have to read and understand the portion of the source code of the OSS system where the bug occurs, and thus they gain limited information on project quality through direct experience in aspects other than bug fixing. As another example, consider peripheral developers. They occasionally contribute new functionality or features to the existing OSS system, and their contribution is irregular and the period of involvement is short and sporadic. Consequently, their direct experience with code quality and developer quality is constrained.

In light of the above three reasons, volunteers are likely to observe the quality of a minor portion of the project through their direct experience. Thus, volunteers may seek additional information through other means for a more comprehensive assessment of project quality to make a better decision about continuing their participation in the OSS project. Our study suggests that signaling associated with quality controls is helpful to volunteers in assessing project quality and thereby influences VCPI.

## 3. Conceptual Framework

Our conceptual framework shown in Figure 1 proposes that two quality controls—accreditation for input and code acceptance for output—influence VCPI through signaling. There are two moderators: the volunteers’ tenure at the developer level and the OSS project type at the project level. We anticipate that the moderators will alter the signaling efects associated with the two quality controls (accreditation and code acceptance) on VCPI. We elaborate on the foundations of our conceptual framework and the signaling perspective of accreditation and code acceptance in OSS projects.

## 3.1. Signaling Perspective of Quality Controls in OSS Projects

Signaling theory describes behavior when one party has more information than the other, and the former party (the signaler) sends signals to the latter (the receiver) to reduce this information asymmetry (Boulding and Kirmani 1993, Connelly et al. 2011). The signaler has to decide how to communicate (or signal) the information. Most prior research has assumed that signalers deliberately send signals to receivers, but it is also possible that signalers signal receivers without being aware they are signaling (Spence 2002). One typical example of “unaware” signaling is Spence’s (1974) job-market model, where people, by acquiring an education, may not think of themselves as signaling their ability to recruiters.

In OSS development, project leaders implement quality controls with an aim to produce a functioning system for high-quality code development. It may not be their intent to use these quality controls as a signal to inform volunteers about the unobservable input and output quality of the project. Since our study focuses on VCPI, regardless of whether OSS project leaders (signalers) intentionally or unintentionally use quality controls to signal project quality to volunteers, it does not afect how volunteers (receivers) respond to signals. In other words, it is possible that project leaders use quality controls to signal to volunteers “without even being aware they are signaling” (Connelly et al. 2011, p. 59).

Our research focuses on two quality controls: accreditation and code acceptance. These two quality controls are applied to difering extents in OSS projects (e.g., de Laat 2007, Markus 2007, Shah 2006, Sharma et al. 2002). By implementing accreditation and/or code acceptance, project leaders signal their intent and the quality of their work to volunteers. According to the theory of costly signaling (Bird and Smith 2005), these signals are reliable because high-quality projects are in a better position than low-quality projects to absorb the costs of implementing the controls that are the basis for the signals. That is, as the primary task in OSS projects is to write code, high-quality projects are likely to allocate resources to implement quality controls (e.g., assessments for developer quality and code quality) over the long term, whereas low-quality projects are unlikely to sustain these activities. The high costs of these control activities make cheating or false signaling dificult. Table 1 describes project leader signals to volunteers through accreditation and code acceptance. In Section 4.1, we will develop hypotheses to examine the direct efects of quality controls on VCPI.

## 3.2. Rationale for Volunteer Tenure Weakening the Signaling Efects of Quality Controls in Continuing Relationships

In signaling theory, signals can be sent to receivers who start a new relationship with the signaler, as well as to those who have already established a relationship with the signaler. The focus of our study is the latter case, as we examine volunteers’ intentions to continue their participation in OSS projects. Although we are unaware of research in OSS development that had used signaling theory to examine the continuing relationships of developers (or other stakeholders) with OSS projects, research in organizational behavior has employed signaling theory to examine how firms’ actions influence their continuing relationships with stakeholders. Table 2 presents some examples of this research and sheds light on the applicability of signaling theory to the examination of VCPI in OSS development.

It is less straightforward to apply signaling theory to examine signal efects on receivers who are in a continuing relationship with a signaler than it is to apply the theory to a new relationship. In a continuing relationship, where the receiver has had the opportunity to observe or experience the quality that was originally unobservable, signal efectiveness can be expected to change over the tenure of the relationship.

Signal fit, a concept from signaling theory, captures the correlation of the information about an unobservable trait that is provided by a signal to a receiver with the lack of information about the unobservable trait available to the receiver through other means such as their observation or experience (Connelly et al. 2011). In a new relationship, receivers have limited information about the unobservable traits that are signaled, creating the conditions for receivers to place a greater reliance on signals. By contrast, in a continuing relationship, receivers are likely to observe and experience cues related to these traits over time, thereby decreasing signal fit and consequently their reliance on signals. As this reduced reliance on a signal adversely afects signal efectiveness (Connelly et al. 2011), signal efectiveness should decrease as the tenure of a receiver– signaler relationship increases.

## 3.3. Rationale for OSS Project Type (De)amplifying the Signaling Efects of Quality Controls

In signaling theory, signals are qualitative and require interpretation; receivers have to decide whether to attend to the signals and how to interpret them. Connelly et al. (2011) defined receiver attention as “the extent to which receivers vigilantly scan the environment for signals” and receiver interpretation as “the process of translating signals into perceived meaning” (p. 54). The signal has no efect if receivers are not attending to it or do not know what to look for in the environment. After receivers attend to the signal, they “calibrate” the signal; in other words, receivers compare the signal with, for example, their prior experience to interpret it.

Both receiver attention and receiver interpretation are determined by the receivers’ interests (i.e., the factors they consider before taking any subsequent action). We first discuss receiver attention: when a signal carries information matched to their interests, receivers are likely to attend to the signal (Gulati and Higgins 2003). In an example provided by Dunham (2011) in a business context, marketers (signalers) transmit advertisements (signals) to entice consumers (receivers) to buy products. The most efective advertisement is one that provides information that matches the consumers’ interests. For example, the tag “the lowest possible price” can easily draw the attention of price-sensitive consumers. Receivers’ interests also influence how they interpret the signal, as their interests determine which perspectives they use to process the signal and therefore result in diferent interpretations (Perkins and Hendry 2005). Following the example by Dunham (2011), since the lowest possible price tag does not match the interests of environmentally aware consumers, these consumers consider it irrelevant and interpret it less favorably than price-sensitive consumers.

As the efectiveness of signals varies according to the receivers’ interests, we now proceed to theorize as to why the concentration of need-driven developers should amplify the signaling efects of quality controls on VCPI (Section 3.3.1) and why the two types of OSS projects (community founded and spinout) difer in the concentration of need-driven developers (Section 3.3.2). We then build on this theoretical rationale to develop hypotheses pertaining to the moderation of OSS project type on the signaling efects associated with quality controls on VCPI (Section 4.3).

3.3.1. Concentration of Need-Driven Developers as an Amplifier of Quality Control Efects. OSS volunteers’ motives play a vital role in their decision making about voluntary participation (Hertel et al. 2003, von Krogh et al. 2003). Volunteers with difering motives are likely to difer in their concerns regarding quality controls and therefore may attend to, and focus on, diferent features of quality control or apply varying perspectives to interpret the pros and cons of the quality control. Therefore, we anticipate that volunteers’ motives should reinforce or dampen the efects of the quality controls on VCPI.

Among the various motive types, our study focuses on need-driven motives. Need-driven developers contribute code to an OSS project for enhancing its software to fulfill their personal or company software needs (Shah 2006), and therefore, they have instrumental motives for caring that an OSS project is concerned with high-quality code development. As quality controls enable eficient and high-quality code development (Markus 2007), quality controls should exert a particularly strong efect on need-driven developers, compared with their efect on volunteers who have other motives.

An OSS project consists of a group of developers, and the signaling efects associated with quality controls depend on the concentration of need-driven developers in the project. To elaborate, need-driven developers consider quality controls beneficial and are likely to remain involved with projects with quality controls. We argue that in an OSS project, signal efectiveness would be further amplified in a project with a high concentration of need-driven developers. When a project contains a high concentration of need-driven developers, the volunteers are more likely to interpret the benefits of quality controls from a similar perspective and thus form a consistent interpretation. As per the psychology literature, people have a tendency to form judgments that increase confidence in their correctness (Frey 1995, Tetlock et al. 1992). Therefore, volunteers who are not need driven or newcomers to the project are likely to process information selectively to form an interpretation that is consistent with that of others involved in the project. The efect of quality controls on VCPI in a project with a high concentration of needdriven developers is anticipated to be stronger than in a project with a low concentration of need-driven developers.

3.3.2. Relative Concentration of Need-Driven Developers for Community-Founded vs. Spinout Projects. We compare the concentration of need-driven developers between community-founded and spinout projects by examining project creation and project development. With regard to project creation, a community-founded project emerges from an open project run by a loose coalition of volunteers (McConnell et al. 2002). This coalition of volunteers holds diverse OSS philosophies; for example, some are altruists and some are need driven. They initiate an open project for various reasons. For example, Linus Torvalds had a strong interest in programming and was exposed to UNIX when he was studying at the University of Helsinki, after which he initiated the development of the Linux kernel for fun (Hall 2010). Brian Behlendorf became interested in the early development of the Internet while he was a student at the University of California, Berkeley. In 1994, Behlendorf and his friends started the development of Apache, with the aim to produce web server software with user registration features for the public. Miguel de Icaza also had a strong interest in programming: before he initiated the GNOME project, he was a hobbyist who wrote code for early versions of the Linux kernel and Wine. He was also an altruist—in his job interview with Microsoft, he persuaded his interviewers to free the Internet Explorer code even before Netscape did so with their own browser. Overall, it can be concluded that volunteers initiate projects for diferent reasons. Even when the project becomes community founded, these initiating volunteers remain involved in the project. For example, Torvalds is still the chief architect of the Linux kernel, Behlendorf is still on the board of directors for Apache, and a number of former University of California developers still work on FreeBSD. Their diferent OSS philosophies continue in the project groups.

Conversely, a spinout project is created by software teams at an information technology (IT) firm. Their motives for creating the project are very focused; that is, to develop a software package that benefits the firm. For instance, in 2001, IBM released many of its Java development tools in open source form (to help establish these tools as de facto standards) when it founded the Eclipse project. Even though the Eclipse project has been spun out, IBM continues to provide hundreds of programmer years to extend its core modules (Chesbrough and Vanhaverbeke 2008). Moreover, IT firms such as Borland, Red Hat, SAP, and SuSE assigned their employees to extend IBM’s Java development tools to meet their development needs. Another example is IBM’s Jikes: in 1996, in response to IBM’s growing interest in Java, two IBM researchers built a prototype of a Java compiler. IBM received many customer requests to improve the Java compiler, and eventually, in 1998, IBM spun out this Java compiler in open source form—namely, Jikes—to attract voluntary eforts to extend the compiler to address these customer requests. These examples illustrate how spinout projects often start with a group of need-driven developers who only work for the IT firm’s benefit, and in some cases (e.g., Eclipse), this group of need-driven developers remain involved in the project. Additionally, when spinning out their internal projects, some IT firms are unwilling or unable to relinquish control of the source code they have written and therefore use a source license to create a gated community. In a gated community, developers have to agree to the source license to enter the community and see and use the source code. The Java community is a good example of a gated community: Java technology was originally created by Sun Microsystems and released under the Sun Community Source license. The Java community is an open organization of international Java developers and licensees whose charter is to develop and revise Java technology specifications, reference implementations, and technology compatibility kits. In a case study of a gated community, Shah (2006) found that it was “populated almost exclusively by need-driven developers” (p. 1004).

With regard to project development, communityfounded projects are often structured to prevent a takeover by the firms who sponsor the projects (West and O’Mahony 2005). Thus, while the firms contribute many resources for ongoing development in community-founded projects, they can impose few, if any, restrictions on volunteers (de Laat 2007, O’Mahony 2005, West and O’Mahony 2005). For example, Torvalds initiated the Linux kernel project and remains the leader (at least for the kernel), even though numerous IT firms sponsor the project. Projects initiated by the Apache Software Foundation have established policies for granting “commit rights” that allow volunteers to modify the code base (Capra and Wasserman 2008). By contrast, an IT firm in a spinout project usually retains some control, which it can accomplish in two ways. The first method of retaining control is to release part of the system as OSS, but to retain ownership of key components. Sun Microsystems used this approach by intentionally withholding access to the core code of OpenSolaris prior to the release of its commercial counterpart. Additionally, IT firms can set up a gated community so that they remain the primary driver for their spinout projects. The second method of retaining control is to use license terms that restrict the ability of volunteers to modify or use the software. For example, Doom<sup>1</sup> source code is dual licensed under the Doom Source License (DSL) and General Public License (GPL). DSL does not include the copyleft<sup>2</sup> provisions that are included in the GPL, which require the release of the source code of any derivative version. This allows the authors of ZDaemon and Skulltag<sup>3</sup> to keep their source code secret. These restrictions deter the participation of altruists and hobbyists because altruists dislike the fact that the proceeds of the OSS project go to the firm’s financial interests (Hars and Ou 2002) and hobbyists dislike engaging in tasks where such restrictions are imposed (Shah 2006). Therefore, community-founded projects attract volunteers with varied motives, whereas spinout projects are less likely to attract volunteers who dislike the constraints imposed by the firms who sponsor the projects and are more likely to involve need-driven developers who only want a software package to fulfill their software needs.

## 4. Hypotheses Development

Our research model and hypotheses are shown in Figure 2. We develop a total of six hypotheses that include the direct efects of accreditation and code acceptance, respectively, on VCPI (H1 and H2), the moderation efects of volunteer tenure on the relationship between quality controls and VCPI (H3 and H4), and the moderation efects of type of OSS project on the relationship between quality controls and VCPI (H5 and H6).

4.1. The Direct Efects of Quality Controls on VCPI 4.1.1. Accreditation as an Input Quality Control Signal. Accreditation increases the quality of labor input resources for OSS development by checking the qualifications of candidate developers before allowing them to work as committers (Sharma et al. 2002). For example, Eclipse and OpenSolaris place candidate developers who want to be committers on probation. These candidate developers work on a project for a certain period, and the quality of their work is assessed by a core group of developers. At the end of the probation period, a consensus vote by the core group confers formal membership to a candidate developer (Sharma et al. 2002). FreeNet uses joining scripts to ask candidate developers to work on a certain level and type of activity to demonstrate their abilities (Holck and Jorgensen 2005, von Krogh et al. 2003). At Debian, contributors who want to be developers with committer privileges must complete a three-step application process with a Debian developer to demonstrate their knowledge of, and allegiance to, the OSS philosophy. At the final stage of accreditation, contributors must prove their identity by having their cryptographic key signed while they are face-to-face with current committers (Holck and Jorgensen 2005).

The signals associated with accreditation provide additional information about input project quality to volunteers. As OSS leaders intend to gather competent and motivated volunteers to work on the core development and as the function of accreditation is to assess the knowledge and skills of new developers, project leaders can use accreditation as a way to adapt the knowledge and skill diversity of developers. Accreditation signals that OSS project leaders have the intention to employ high-quality developers with the knowledge and skill sets to meet the needs of the core code development. These signals are reliable because the implementation of accreditation is costly for OSS projects (Kogut and Metiu 2001). Specifically, in an accredited project, a series of managerial activities unrelated to the primary activity (i.e., coding) in OSS development needs to be conducted. There are situations when project leaders might prefer not to implement any accreditation procedures, such as when the project has a few good developers from which to select, or when it lacks the necessary resources to undertake the administrative work directed at input quality control. When the perceived input quality is high, volunteers consider the chance of producing a good software package to be high and are attracted to continue participating in the project. Overall, the signal associated with accreditation provides additional information about the input quality of an OSS project, and hence, enhances VCPI.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Accreditation exerts a positive efect on VCPI.

4.1.2. Code Acceptance as an Output Quality Control Signal. Code acceptance consists of a set of procedures that examine the quality of code submitted by developers and ensure that developers’ coding activities are conducted according to established expectations in the project (Markus 2007). Although all volunteers are able to create and submit new code, an OSS project with code acceptance has the authority to accept or reject code for incorporation into the experimental version and, if it exists, into the stable product version. In the Linux kernel project, Torvalds personally evaluates all code changes to the experimental version. In projects such as Apache, Debian, GNOME, Mozilla, and Net-Beans, it is up to the committers in a given module to make the decision, which can include taking a vote.

The signals associated with code acceptance provide additional information about output project quality. Volunteers’ direct experience with code only provides partial information on code quality, and thus they may rely on the signals associated with code acceptance to seek additional information about code quality and form their VCPI. The signals tell volunteers about OSS project leaders’ intent to produce a properly functioning software system. Although code acceptance involves many checks that slow down the software development process, it ensures that all new code is properly developed and adheres to outcome standards. This safeguards the output quality for the new code and the entire software system. Moreover, volunteers’ eforts to develop new code are not in vain, as they are built on a version of well-written code (de Laat 2007). There is a low risk for volunteers to obtain the code outputs they need. Code acceptance is interpreted as a reliable signal because of its high implementation cost, which results from project leaders allocating developers with high expertise to check the code. When the perceived code quality is high, volunteers consider the chance of producing a good software package to be high and are likely to continue participating in the project. Taken together, the signals associated with code acceptance provide additional information about the output project quality, and therefore enhance VCPI.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Code acceptance exerts a positive efect on VCPI.

## 4.2. Volunteers’ Tenure Moderates the Direct Efects of Quality Controls (Accreditation and Code Acceptance) on VCPI

In OSS development, project leaders announce relatively stable accreditation and code acceptance policies that can serve as signals to inform volunteers about input and output project quality. As argued in Section 2.2, volunteers’ direct experience with code and with other developers in a project provides (partial)

information about originally unobservable project quality. As such, volunteer tenure, which increases volunteers’ access to information about quality through their direct experience, reduces the fit between the information provided by project quality signals and the extent to which volunteers are unable to observe what is signaled. Since signal fit positively afects signal efectiveness (Connelly et al. 2011), the efectiveness of signals associated with quality controls decreases with volunteer tenure. We anticipate observing this declining pattern for both accreditation and code acceptance across the two types of OSS projects.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The positive efect of accreditation on VCPI decreases with volunteer tenure in (a) communityfounded projects and (b) spinout projects.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The positive efect of code acceptance on VCPI decreases with volunteer tenure in (a) communityfounded projects and (b) spinout projects.

## 4.3. OSS Project Type as a Moderator of the Direct Efects of Quality Controls on VCPI

4.3.1. OSS Project Type Moderates the Direct Efect of Accreditation on VCPI. Accredited projects signal to volunteers the project leaders’ intent and their act of selecting proficient developers with strong knowledge and skill sets. In spinout projects, volunteers are mostly need-driven developers. Their primary interests are to develop high-quality code. Since accreditation aims to increase developer quality, which has a direct efect on code quality, the quality signals associated with accreditation match need-driven developers’ primary interests. Hence, receiver attention is strong. In addition, proficient developers selected by an accredited project add high-quality labor to software development, and need-driven developers interpret the signals of accreditation as reinforcing. Thus, receiver interpretation is favorable. Additionally, the high concentration of need-driven developers leads to consistent interpretation across developers in the project. This biases other developers to seek consistent information to support this interpretation of the signal. Consequently, this amplifies the favorable efect of signaling through accreditation in spinout projects.

Conversely, in community-founded projects, the concentration of need-driven developers is low. Since the volunteers’ motives for participation vary greatly, accreditation cannot address every volunteer’s primary interests. For example, altruists are concerned with developers’ allegiance to the OSS philosophy, and hobbyists derive their enjoyment from the challenge of seeking solutions and learning programming on their own (Hars and Ou 2002). The involvement of proficient developers in the project does not necessarily help altruists and hobbyists fulfill their primary interests. Hence, they may not attend to accreditation quality signals or may interpret them less positively. Receiver attention is low, and receiver interpretation is less favorable. In addition, the low concentration of need-driven developers does not create a force to unify their interpretations. Consequently, the efect of signaling through accreditation on VCPI is stronger in spinout projects than in community-founded projects. We hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 5 (H5). The positive efect of accreditation on VCPI is stronger in spinout projects than in communityfounded projects.

4.3.2. OSS Project Type Moderates the Direct Efect of Code Acceptance on VCPI. Project leaders use code acceptance to signal to volunteers their intent to enhance and maintain the quality of the developed software (Markus 2007). Code acceptance ensures that the developed software does not have defects and is ready for use. The positive efect of signaling through code acceptance is reinforcing to need-driven volunteers, whose primary concern is the quality of the developed software. They are concerned with the quality of the collective efort, rather than the means–ends relationships among code contributed by individual developers. Output controls are particularly important to people who are concerned about the quality of the collective efort (Ouchi 1977); that is, need-driven developers in our case. Hence, need-driven developers are likely to attend to, and interpret favorably, signals of code acceptance. The concentration of need-driven developers in spinout projects is high. Furthermore, this high concentration forms a consistent (positive) interpretation of the signals of code acceptance across the group. As a result, the positive signaling efects of code acceptance on VCPI are amplified in spinout projects.

Conversely, in community-founded projects, the concentration of need-driven developers is low. Hobbyists derive enjoyment from mastering dificult challenges in coding on their own, and those who are learning to code want to expand their skill base through their participation (Hars and Ou 2002). As these volunteers’ primary concern is their own experience, they show more concern for the process of participation than the output of participation. Thus, output controls (in this case, code acceptance) are less meaningful to people whose concern is their own experience (in this case, hobbyists) (Ouchi 1977). Moreover, those who are intrinsically motivated (such as hobbyists) may not appreciate others checking what they do (Enzle and Anderson 1993). Hence, hobbyists interpret these signals less favorably. Even though there are need-driven developers in community-founded projects, their concentration is low and volunteers are not influenced to adopt a single interpretation. Overall, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 6 (H6). The positive efect of code acceptance on VCPI is stronger in spinout projects than in communityfounded projects.

## 5. Data and Methods

We used a survey as the primary data collection approach to test our research model. This section describes the survey design, data collection, and measures.

## 5.1. Levels of Analysis and Measurement

As volunteers are nested in projects, we adopt a multilevel conceptualization to examine VCPI in OSS projects. Accordingly, we (i) define the level (developer or project) at which each construct operates, (ii) define the level at which each construct is measured, and (iii) determine whether it is appropriate to aggregate individual responses for constructs measured at the individual level to derive a consensus measure at the project level (Chan 1998, Harrison and Klein 2007).

We consider the levels of analysis and measurement of VCPI, the outcome variable, and the two quality controls (accreditation and code acceptance). As VCPI is a volunteer’s self-assessment of their continued participation, we measured it at the individual level (Gully et al. 2002). By contrast, the two quality controls are implemented at the project level—the same accreditation procedures are applied to all developers and the same code acceptance policies are used to structure all program modules in a project; thus, they operate at the project level. One way to measure these constructs is to refer to a single reliable source (e.g., assess information on project websites) that reports on a project’s accreditation requirements and codechecking policies. If collecting data from a reliable source is not feasible, Chan (1998) provides five composition models that use the data collected and aggregated at the lower level to form scores at the higher level.<sup>5</sup> We measured accreditation at the project level. When we checked the code development policies, we observed that some OSS project websites did not provide their accreditation policies. However, this does not mean that there were no accreditation practices. Accordingly, we adopted Chan’s (1998) direct consensus composition approach to assess accreditation for an OSS project: we first conceptualized and operationalized accreditation at the developer level and then used volunteers’ within-group consensus to obtain a functionally isomorphic form of this construct at the project level.

## 5.2. Data Collection

The survey was posted for eight months on Source-Forge.net, Freshmeat.net, and websites of major OSS projects (such as Linux kernel, Apache, and MySQL). Every month, one or two reminders were posted. The survey was started by 663 developers, and eventually completed by 451. Of the 451 respondents, 100 came from developers associated with volunteer-only projects and 47 from paid developers. As our study focuses on volunteer participation in firm-participating projects, we discarded these 147 responses, which resulted in a usable dataset of 304 responses from 40 OSS projects. Table 4 shows the sample profile of developers. Among the 304 respondents, 144 were from community-founded projects, and 160 came from spinout projects. These 40 projects represented the development of many diferent software applications, including operating systems, encryption tools, code generators, file managers, and groupware. Results from a two-sample t-test indicated that there was no significant diference in project size between the two project types $( p < 0 . 1 )$ . In addition, all of the projects in our sample were active, consistent with our objective to assess VCPI. Nearly 70% of the projects had their last major release within 48 months before our data collection. The “least active” project produced the last major release 73 months prior to our data collection.

Common method bias can threaten the validity of paths that link two variables measured in a single survey instrument. We employed the marker variable technique (Lindell and Whitney 2001, Malhotra et al. 2006). Specifically, we chose the second-smallest positive correlation among the constructs (i.e., <sup>−</sup>0.02, the correlation between code acceptance and code modularization, a control variable) as a conservative estimate of common method variance (CMV) to produce the CMV-adjusted correlation matrix (Lindell and Whitney 2001). Following Malhotra et al. (2006), we produced a CMV-adjusted correlation matrix and then used it to estimate CMV-adjusted path coeficients and explained variance. The results show that after controlling for the common marker, explained variance in the dependent variables decreases, but the drop is not substantial (i.e., 2.2%). In addition, the adjustment did not afect the sign and significance of any of the paths. Additionally, 14 months after the first survey, we sent a follow-up survey to invite the respondents to report their current participation in OSS projects. Seventy-one respondents replied to the follow-up survey. The correlation (0.43, p < 0.01) between VCPI (as reported in the first survey) and their actual behavior (as reported in the follow-up survey) was significant. Thus, the threat of common method biases was low.

To assess nonresponse biases, we compared the means for the demographics and all of the major variables for early and late respondents.<sup>7</sup> Other statistical tests, including demographic profiles, the extent of communication, and accreditation, were not significant. The only significantly diferent variable was the number of lines of code contributed by a developer— not surprisingly, more active developers tended to respond earlier.

## 5.3. Measures

5.3.1. Dependent and Independent Variables. Appendix B shows the survey items. All constructs were measured using multiple-item scales informed by previously validated measures and reworded to suit the context of OSS development. The dependent variable, VCPI, was measured using two items associated with the behavioral intention construct in the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1991). Volunteer tenure was measured using a single-item indicator capturing the number of years a volunteer had worked on the current project. This measure is meaningful across volunteer roles (e.g., programmer, designer, packager, and bug fixer) and is also consistent with how past work has measured tenure in a job (e.g., Campbell et al. 2012, Conway and Coyle-Shapiro 2012, Morris and Venkatesh 2010, Rai and Hornyak 2013).

There were two independent variables: accreditation andcode acceptance.Accreditationwasmeasuredusing three reflective items based on Pavlou (2002). Code acceptance in each OSS project was assessed independently by two researchers with expertise in software development and software engineering. Specifically, we used two formative indicators to measure code acceptance: whether the OSS project had a quality control group to evaluate code quality (Rai et al. 1998), and whether there was a versioning practice that differentiated experimental and production versions of code (de Laat 2007). Two raters independently coded each of the following as dummy variables: the presence of a developer or a group of developers to review the developed code, and the presence of multiple versions of code to keep track of an experimental copy and a stable copy. The interrater agreement was acceptable, with the κ agreement coeficient being 0.80 for the first dummy variable and 0.87 for the second. Finally, we compared the two sets of independent scores, discussed inconsistencies, and produced two sets of scores to describe code acceptance for each OSS project.

5.3.2. Control Variables. We controlled for the following developer-level variables: programming experience in years, experience in OSS development in years, and number of functional domains on which each volunteer worked. We also controlled for project-level variables. We controlled for project size, as a large project was likely to be a successful project that could attract volunteers to stay. Project size was measured by the number of developers associated with the project. We controlled for cooperative norms<sup>8</sup> and the extent of communication,<sup>9</sup> because Cohen and Bailey (1997) indicated that these two variables might influence effectiveness. Cooperative norms was assessed as a group psychological trait that emphasizes cooperation, using the three-item measure from Pavlou (2002). Prior research (e.g., Stewart and Gosain 2006) reveals that the extent of communication among developers enables volunteers to exchange quality information and to provide a supportive working atmosphere, resulting in a favorable efect on VCPI. Hence, we included the extent of communication as a control variable. This was measured as a composite index; that is, the mean of the following three indicators of the extent of communication among OSS developers identified by Stewart and Gosain (2006): timeliness, correctness, and usefulness. We also controlled for code modularization. Prior research (e.g., Baldwin and Clark 2006b, Darcy et al. 2005, MacCormack et al. 2006) reveals that code modularization helps developers to coordinate the coding process eficiently so they experience reduced costs and risks in code development. We anticipated it to exert a positive efect on VCPI. Code modularization was measured using program code publicly available on project websites. Specifically, we computed a composite index; that is, a mean of four indicators.<sup>10</sup>

The first indicator was the number of source files that represents the extent of separation of program code into small manageable modular units. To measure the first indicator, we downloaded the most recent version of source code for each of the 40 OSS projects, identified the main folder (such as kernel/in Linux and modules/in Apache), and counted the number of source files in the main folder. The second indicator was the number of functional areas that represents the extent of separation of code from the related subprojects within an OSS project.<sup>11</sup> To measure the second indicator, we assessed project websites to identify the number of project subgroups. The third and fourth indicators were the ratio of global–local variables and internal–external function calls. For each of the 40 OSS projects, we randomly selected 20 source files that had been edited in the past year and contained at least 100 lines of code. We independently coded<sup>12</sup> the source files with respect to (1) the number of global variables, (2) the number of local variables, (3) the number of function calls for which the called function was located within the source file that originated the request, and (4) the number of function calls for which the called function was outside the source file that originated the request. For each source file, we computed the ratio between (1) and (2) to determine the ratio of global– local variables, and the ratio between (3) and (4) to determine the ratio of internal–external function calls. Subsequently, we averaged the respective scores across the 20 source files to determine scores for global–local variables and internal–external function calls for an OSS project.

## 5.4. Assessment of Within-Project Agreement of Responses

To determine whether aggregating volunteers’ responses on accreditation (as well as the two control variables, the extent of communication and cooperative norms, see Section 5.3.2) was appropriate, we applied three recommended criteria to evaluate within-group agreement and between-group variability (Bliese 2000). First, we computed rwg to assess the interrater agreement among volunteers in a project for accreditation. An rwg value greater than 0.70 reflects a strong withingroup agreement. The average rwg of accreditation across the 40 projects was 0.69 and did not meet the recommended 0.70 threshold. Second, we computed the intraclass correlations (ICC). It had an ICC value greater than the desired cutof of 0.05, suggesting that group efects are likely to be salient and must be considered. Third, we computed the design efect. It had design efects greater than the desired cutof of 2.0, suggesting that the data should be modeled using multilevel methods. The accreditation construct passed two of the three tests. The path coeficients in the structural models based on all OSS projects and the path coefficients in the structural models based on those with rwg below 0.70 were very similar.<sup>13</sup> Consequently, it was reasonable for us to aggregate individual perceptions of accreditation to form a group-level construct. ## 6. Analysis and Results

## 6.1. Testing for Factor Invariance

We tested the hypotheses with a multigroup (spinouts and community-founded), multilevel (project and developer) structural model using Mplus version 5.1 (Byrne 2011, Kline 2010). As two of our hypotheses (H5 and H6) pertained to whether there are diferences in the efects of input and output project quality controls on VCPI in community-founded and spinout projects, the data analysis involved a multigroup analysis. A preliminary stage of this analysis involved testing for an invariant factor structure (i.e., the indicators in the two subgroups relate to a set of underlying constructs to the same extent) (Byrne 2011, Kline 2010). If the fit of a confirmatory factor analysis model with equally constrained loadings is not appreciably worse than that of the unconstrained model, then the indicators should measure the factors in comparable ways in each group. We found the diference in the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic between the constrained and unconstrained models to be nonsignificant $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } ( 9 ) = 1 7 . 3 2 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . Thus, the factor structure between the two types of projects was assumed to be invariant; that is, the constructs had the same meanings across the two project types.

## 6.2. Measurement Model Results

The next step was to test (separately) the validity of latent constructs in the two types of OSS projects. We first examined the reflective constructs (accreditation and cooperative norms—a control variable) and assessed their unidimensionality, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Regarding unidimensionality, the eigenvalues from the exploratory factor analysis results suggested that cooperative norms and accreditation were two separate constructs. All composite reliabilities were greater than 0.70, indicating acceptable reliability for the measures (Gefen et al. 2000) (see Table 5).

Our results also suggested that the constructs exhibited convergent validity, as the average variance extracted (AVE) for all reflective constructs exceeded 0.50 (see Table 5), and all items loaded highly on their factors (>0.80) (see Table C.1 in Appendix C) (Gefen et al. 2000). Finally, we tested for discriminant validity. As the square root of all AVEs was larger than all other cross-correlations (see Table 5) and all items loadings on their factors were greater than their cross-loadings on other factors (see Table C.1 in Appendix C), discriminant validity was supported.

## 6.3. Structural Model Results

## 7.2. Theoretical Contributions

We extend the discussion on VCPI in OSS projects (e.g., Roberts et al. 2006, von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). Prior literature has investigated the efects of developer factors (such as status motives) on VCPI. Our study extends the prior literature by investigating OSS project factors—specifically, quality controls—and their efects on VCPI. The signaling mechanism provides a plausible explanation, as volunteers are likely to observe the quality of a minor portion of the project through their direct experience and they seek additional information about project quality through other means for a more comprehensive assessment of project quality to make decisions on continued participation.

As OSS project leaders implement quality controls to improve the quality of developed products, they may be unaware that quality controls do play an additional role of signaling unobservable input and output project quality to the volunteers whose participation they are seeking to retain. Our results reveal that the implementation of quality controls, accreditation and code acceptance, plays an important signaling role and promotes VCPI.

Drawing on signaling theory, we elaborate the nature of the relationship between project quality and VCPI by incorporating the moderating role of two contextual factors—OSS project type and volunteer experience. We draw on the signaling concepts (receiver attention/interpretation and signal fit) for the inclusion of the two moderators (OSS project types and volunteer tenure) in our theoretical model. We theorize that OSS project type (through concentration of needdriven developers) afects the extent to which volunteers attend to and interpret quality signals, thereby leading OSS project type to moderate the efects of quality controls on VCPI. We also theorize the role of volunteer tenure in determining the efectiveness of quality signals as volunteers learn about project quality through participation in the project. By revealing the moderating roles of project and volunteer contextual factors, we elaborate our understanding of the efects of quality controls on VCPI in a more nuanced way.

Our finding of the moderating role of the OSS project suggests that diferent types of projects attract volunteers with diferent motives and therefore lead to differential impacts of quality controls on VCPI. Most prior research has treated all firm-participating OSS projects as one category, comparing it with volunteeronly projects (see von Krogh and von Hippel 2006 for a review). Although Shah (2006) did not directly address volunteer participation in firm-participating projects, she did diferentiate between volunteer participation in open and gated projects. However, not all volunteeronly projects are open and not all firm-participating projects are gated. To extend this discussion on diferences in the types of OSS projects, we have drawn on work that has examined the startup and mature phases of firm-participating OSS projects (West and O’Mahony 2005), contrasted community-founded and spinout projects specifically with respect to concentration of need-driven developers and mapped this diference to signaling concepts (receiver attention and interpretation). Our findings suggest that a greater concentration of need-driven developers in spinout projects reinforces the signals associated with accreditation for the quality of the inputs and code acceptance for the quality of the outputs. A broader implication is that the diferences in the composition of developer motives across types of OSS projects can afect the efectiveness of signals from various project-level controls on individual volunteer behavior.

We contribute to the OSS and signaling literatures by demonstrating that experienced volunteers rely less than new volunteers on the signals associated with input quality controls in making a decision to continue their participation. A large body of research on signaling has examined how a signaler communicates information about unobservable traits, such as quality, to a receiver in a setup where a signaler and a receiver do not have much direct interaction. Our study elaborates signaling theory by investigating the role of signals in a diferent context: where there is a continuing relationship between a receiver and a signaler. In this context, the receiver is able to gain information about an unobservable trait through their direct experience. However, when accessing such information is labor intensive, or when interpreting such information is dificult, the receiver is likely to gain partial, but not complete, information through their direct experience. As signal fit decreases with the receiver gaining information through other means, the receiver reduces their reliance on the signal, thereby weakening signal efectiveness. We applied the concept of signal fit to theorize how the impacts of quality controls of an OSS project on VCPI are conditional on volunteers’ tenure in the project. Our study responds to the suggestion by Connelly et al. (2011, p. 56) that “incorporating longer periods of time into signaling theory represents a viable area for future research.” Our study opens up opportunities for researchers to use signaling theory to investigate the role of signals vis-à-vis other sources of information in continuing relationships between signalers and receivers. By considering a longer period of a relationship between a signaler and a receiver rather than a snapshot, future research can uncover changes in signal fit and signal efectiveness with the progression of a relationship.

## 7.3. Practical Implications

Firm participation is a noteworthy trend in OSS development. However, it may demotivate volunteers, as volunteers do not know whether and how firm participation afects project quality. Since voluntary participation is crucial to the sustainability of OSS projects, it is practically important to understand how to retain voluntary participation in firm-participating projects. Based on our findings, OSS project leaders can implement quality controls for retaining volunteers by signaling unobservable project quality to them. In particular, project leaders can establish accreditation procedures to assess the quality of newcomers and, by sharing information about accreditation, signal to volunteers the high-quality input to OSS projects. Project leaders can also establish a team of developers that checks and accepts program code, thereby signaling to volunteers that the quality of output is high. While these quality controls are costly, project leaders can accrue a key benefit: VCPI, a fundamental challenge confronting OSS projects.

Additionally, the efectiveness of quality controls in retaining VCPI may decline with volunteer tenure in the current projects—input controls demonstrate this pattern, whereas output controls do not. Our findings reveal that after volunteers accumulate experience with the projects, accreditation (as an input control) becomes less efective in sustaining voluntary participation across the two types of OSS projects. Hence, project leaders who plan to use accreditation to retain volunteers should first assess volunteer composition in their projects. For recently launched projects or projects with a constant influx of volunteers, volunteers have relatively little experience with the projects, and so accreditation can promote their intentions to stay in the projects. Conversely, for projects with a high proportion of long-term developers, accreditation can mainly act as a functioning system to improve the project, but is not a means to promote VCPI. As we did not find evidence that the efectiveness of code acceptance (as an output control) to promote VCPI declines with volunteer tenure in the projects, project leaders can utilize code acceptance policies as an efective means to promote VCPI.

Finally, using quality controls to promote VCPI is more efective in spinout projects than in communityfounded projects. Firm sponsors have more controls on code base in spinout projects; as a result, needdriven developers are dominant in spinout projects. In a study comparing one open project and one gated project by Shah (2006), a similar pattern was found: an OSS project with increased controls by firm sponsors attracts and retains need-driven developers. An investigation of what proportion of spinout or community-founded projects are gated, under which conditions, and in what sense, is practically interesting but is outside the scope of this paper. Since spinout projects contain volunteers with concentrated needdriven motives, OSS project leaders for spinout projects can simply implement procedures to assess developer quality and to check developed code, and these actions tell their volunteers about project leaders’ intentions to run a high-quality OSS project and enhance voluntary participation intentions. Conversely, communityfounded projects contain volunteers with diverse motives. Although we found a positive efect of quality controls on VCPI in community-founded projects, the positive efect is weaker than in spinout projects. Hence, leaders for community-founded projects are encouraged to seek other means, such as strengthening cooperative norms, to retain their volunteers.

## 7.4. Limitations and Future Work

Our study is not without limitations. First, the crosssectional design of our study limits the outcome variable to be a volunteer’s intention to remain with the project. Future work can use a series of survey waves to examine the realization of this intention. Other outcome indicators, such as developers’ code contribution, their number of concurrent versions system commits, their task completion eficiency, and OSS product market share, capture additional aspects of project success. Researchers could examine how community-founded and spinout projects difer in these outcome indicators. Second, our study considers various factors in project creation and project development to argue that community-founded and spinout projects difer in concentration of need-driven developers. However, it is possible that the two types of OSS projects difer in other key characteristics. Future research could investigate other diferences (such as the project’s structure and governance) between the two types of OSS projects. Third, we conceptualized and measured volunteer tenure as their experience with coding. Future research can consider other conceptualizations adapted from research on engagement with online communities (Ray et al. 2014). For instance, researchers might use a volunteer’s self-identity verification and how an OSS project identifies their volunteers to capture volunteer tenure. Last, our data on spinout projects came mostly from those projects with goals of increasing market share or establishing their technology as de facto standards, but not from those spun out from IT firms’ abandoned internal projects. This might influence the profile of volunteers who join these projects and limit the generalizability of our findings to those specific spinout projects. Future work could focus on spinout projects only and examine how the initiation of spinning out a project influences VCPI.

## 8. Conclusion

An OSS project’s quality controls—accreditation and code acceptance—exert positive direct efects on VCPI, as they signal to volunteers the unobservable quality management abilities of the project. These positive efects are stronger in spinout projects than in community-founded projects. Since quality controls are less efective in retaining volunteers’ intentions to continue their participation in a community-founded project than in a spinout project, we have explained why it is more challenging to structure a communityfounded project than a spinout project for sustaining voluntary participation. Furthermore, the positive efects of quality signals associated with accreditation decline with volunteer tenure in the projects, but we did not detect the same pattern for quality signals associated with code acceptance. Overall, these findings enhance our understanding of how quality controls in firm-participating projects influence voluntary participation intentions.

## Appendix A. Assessment of the Developers’ Need-Driven Motives Concentration in OSS Project Types

This appendix describes an auxiliary study that provides further empirical evidence corroborating the empirical evidence in the main study that there is a higher concentration of needdriven developers in spinout projects than in communityfounded projects—a claim underlying the development of the model and hypotheses, as specified in Sections 3 and 4, respectively.

To collect the data for the auxiliary study, we followed a similar approach to the one that we used in the main study and recruited survey respondents from popular OSS discussion forums. We posted a survey on six OSS discussion forums (Linux, Apache, GNOME, Open Ofice, MySQL, and Firefox) for two months. Altogether, these six forums had nearly 30,000 registered members. Developers were asked to report whether they participated in community-founded and/or spinout projects and, if they did, they were asked to complete the survey. The survey was completed by 381 developers. On average, they had participated in OSS development for seven years. Among the 381 respondents, 151 respondents had participated in both types of OSS projects, 129 had only worked on community-founded projects, and 70 had only worked on spinout projects. The remaining 31 respondents indicated that they had not contributed to the development of any firm-participating projects and were disqualified from the survey. The demographic profile of the 350 respondents who had contributed to development in OSS projects was similar to the main study— 98% of respondents were male, the average age was 30, and the average number of contributed lines of code was above 20,000. This demographic profile was also similar to prior OSS studies (e.g., Hars and Ou 2001, Hertel et al. 2003). We asked these 350 respondents to report their major motives for participating in the current projects. Consistent with our theorizing and the empirical evidence in the main study, the concentration of need-driven developers is higher in spinout projects than in community-founded projects (82% need-driven and 18% nonneed-driven in spinouts; 58% need-driven and 42% nonneed-driven in community founded).

The auxiliary study provides corroborative evidence to the main study in support of the claim that spinout projects are likely to have a greater concentration of need-driven developers than are found in community-founded projects.
