Abstr act : A large body of research has examined the performance effects of diversification. However, these results have been mixed, and scholars have called for examining contingencies under which the effects of different types (related, unrelated, geographic) and levels of diversification on performance vary. This study attempts to fill this gap in the literature by arguing that examining the performance effects of diversification is incomplete without taking into consideration the firm’s information technology (IT) spending. We posit that IT, by enabling coordination and control in firms, is likely to moderate the relationship between diversification and performance. Combining arguments from both resource-based view and the organizational controls literature, we theorize about the moderating effects of IT spending under different types and levels of diversification. Using data from large U.S. manufacturing firms, we test our research hypotheses. Our results indicate that while IT spending interacts with related diversification to have a positive effect on firm performance, similar interactions with unrelated diversification do not have any effects on firm performance. Furthermore, the interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification is positively associated with performance only when the level of geographic diversification is low. We interpret and discuss these results and highlight the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

Key wor ds and phr as es : business synergy, coordination and control, firm diversification, information technology, IT business value.

Or ganizat ions bot h s mal and l ar ge have witnessed a sustained period of change in the past two decades in their form, structure, and scope. Many new organizational forms such as networked organizations and virtual corporations have emerged while firms’ scope or boundaries also seem to be changing. Interest in firm scope can be traced back to Coase’s [30] work on property rights. Referred to as the boundary that confines a firm’s businesses, firm scope and its interplay with other organizational factors have been an intriguing research subject. Among other factors, information technology (IT) has been credited with enabling changes in firms’ scale and scope [50]. The causal relationship between IT investments and firm scope (e.g., diversification and vertical integration) has been the focus of scholars in the information systems (IS) field who have used transaction costs economics to study the impact of IT on firm boundaries (see, e.g., [17, 36, 50, 60, 82]).

The performance effects of changes in firm diversification have been extensively researched in the strategy and finance literature. The literature on diversification has been focused on the economic rationale behind the diversification–performance relationship. Depending on whether the new business is similar or related to existing businesses, firms may diversify into related markets or unrelated markets. Related diversification is believed to lead to better performance than unrelated diversification because the former leverages significant business synergies while the latter suffers from agency costs and inefficient resource allocation [3, 85]. Although the extant literature in this area is quite rich both theoretically and empirically, the empirical findings have not been consistent. Accordingly, diversification researchers have suggested and used a contingency perspective to scrutinize the diversification–performance link [32]. More recent studies have successfully explored contingencies such as home country environments [128], institutional environments [21], human capital [62], and managerial policies to maintain organization slack [43], thus supporting the contingency view of the diversification–performance linkage.

Despite the inconclusive findings, diversification has been an effective firm strategy for growth. Managing diverse businesses, however, presents a great challenge to top managers. To ensure the competitiveness of their firms, managers have realized that changes in organizational factors such as structures and control arrangements must take place to fit diversification strategies. Such coadjustments not only are necessary to realize the benefits of diversification, but also lead to unique strategic combinations that are difficult for competitors to imitate. Besides, firms may also need to adjust certain corporate spending to the diversification strategy. For example, multibusiness firms outperform single-business firms when the former have a large research and development (R&D) expenditure [87], and related diversifiers spend more on R&D than do unrelated diversifiers [8, 67]. Like R&D, IT spending represents an important expenditure to be decided by corporate managers. Since previous research has shown that non-IT factors account for 50 percent of IT-enabled productivity enhancements [15], it is imperative to examine the context in which the IT expenditure occurs. Validated research findings, therefore, are needed to offer guidelines to corporate managers as to how IT spending should be adjusted to support a specific diversification strategy.

If synergy is the principal motive and reason for firms to diversify [125, 126], then we may expect IT spending to have a significant role enabling such synergies because they build the firm’s coordinative capability and lower transaction costs. Recent studies in the IS literature have posited and found that IT reduces internal coordination costs [36, 60, 61], enables effective knowledge transfer across business units, and generally helps create synergies across business units [123]. These factors underpin the rationale for the diversification–performance relationship. Moreover, studies have also found that IT could impact the control systems in firms [4], which also have implications for the effectiveness with which diversified firms could be managed. Thus, it could be argued that examining performance effects of diversification in contemporary organizations is somewhat incomplete without considering the effects of IT; that is, the effect of diversification on performance could be different with and without appropriate IT spending. This study adds value to the IS and strategy literature by theorizing and providing empirical evidence that IT spending enhances the performance effect of certain diversification strategies but not others.

## Critical Review of Firm Diversification Research

In t his s ect ion, we pr ovide a det ail ed r eview of past research on the diversification– performance relationship, identify gaps in this literature, and thereby set the stage for the work done in this paper. Firm diversification has received attention from strategy and finance scholars who have been interested in understanding whether diversification leads to improved firm performance. In recent years, the diversification–performance research has shifted from focusing on the relative performance of diversified and undiversified firms to a more fine-grained focus on the performance differences between unrelated and related diversifiers [97]. While the unrelated–related diversification dichotomy focuses largely on product diversification, for decades international management scholars have been exploring the competitive effects of international diversification as well [65]. The remaining part of this section will discuss product diversification and geographic diversification, respectively, with respect to their implications for firm performance. Table 1 summarizes the relevant literature on diversification–performance research.

## Performance Effects of Product Diversification

Product diversification, the extent to which a firm operates in multiple and disparate product markets [65], has been extensively studied by strategic management researchers who often emphasize the benefits from diversification into related businesses. A recent review by Palich et al. [97] summarizes these studies by suggesting a curvilinear relationship between firm performance and the nature and level of diversification. Compared with single-business firms, firms engaging in related diversification are able to exploit synergies across product units by consolidating business activities in manufacturing, marketing, raw material purchases, and R&D, and thus achieve both scale and scope economies [3, 107]. For example, at Texas Instruments, defense electronics, semiconductors, and computer businesses share R&D activities and manufacturing facilities in an attempt to leverage efforts across units and gain necessary efficiencies [97]. In addition to improved learning curve efficiencies, intrafirm product/process technology diffusion [6] and enhanced market power [3] are also argued as the benefits associated with related diversification. In the long term, it is expected that related or moderate diversifiers will outperform their single-business counterparts and seize a better competitive position in the market. Nonetheless, as the diversification degree increases, the associated costs also escalate. Coordination costs with higher levels of diversification can increase significantly when the diversified firm has tapped into businesses that have little in common. The top management in unrelated diversifiers often has little firsthand knowledge of the operating affairs of a particular division’s industry, technology, or geographic region [37], further impeding coordination efforts. As a result, the marginal cost of diversification increases rapidly as it hits high levels, and one could conclude that firms experience some optimal level of diversification [97]. These arguments lead to the inverted U‑ shaped relationship between firm diversification and performance.

The mixed empirical evidence on the effect of diversification on performance casts doubt on the direct relationship between the two. Scholars have frequently reported conflicting results regarding whether and how diversification strategies are associated with firm performance. There is evidence that focused firms actually outperform diversified firms when performance is measured by Tobin’s Q [130]. Also, related diversifiers do not necessarily outperform unrelated diversifiers [96]. Alternative theoretical explanations [93], measurement issues [104], and incomplete model specifications [32] all account for the inconclusiveness of empirical findings. Perhaps most importantly, the process to realize diversification benefits is not costless. Relatedness exploitation, for example, is subject to inefficiencies arising from governance costs, incentive degradation, and bureaucratic distortions [97]. Until managers can effectively attend to these problems, the benefits from diversification may not readily materialize.

Unlike strategy researchers, finance scholars in general view diversification as a value-decreasing strategy.<sup>1</sup> As Martin and Sayrak [85] summarized, a consensus among finance researchers is that corporate diversification destroys shareholder value [31, 86, 110, 116, 118]. Evidence includes that diversified firms tend to have lower Tobin’s Q [76, 130], that they trade at discounts of up to 15 percent when compared to the value of a portfolio of comparable stand-alone firms [9], and that the stock market reacts favorably to increases in corporate focus [31]. Diversification allows firms to pool cash flows from divisions and reallocate cash to divisions in accordance with financial criteria, thereby setting up an internal capital market [106]. The access to an internal capital market, however, may provide managers a greater opportunity to overinvest because of excess or free cash flow, while being diversified makes it even more difficult to resolve the agency problem using equity participation [85]. Recent work [101, 111] has suggested that conglomerates may sell at a discount as a result of lower efficiency in resource allocation and not necessarily agency problems, as resources can flow toward the most inefficient division leading to less firm value. To sum up, corporate diversification is believed to be market inefficient because it runs against one of the oldest ideas in economics that specialization is productive [86].

## Performance Effects of Geographic Diversification

Geographic diversification is defined as expansion across the borders of regions and countries into different geographic locations or markets [65]. A firm can gain abovenormal returns by exploiting its firm-specific assets, especially intangible ones, in global markets [18], and the exploitation is further reinforced by imperfections found in markets for trading of these assets [34]. Empirically, Morck and Yeung [94] found that the degree of impact of geographic diversification is proportional to R&D and advertising expenditures, therefore supporting this internalization theory that the value of multinationality comes from intangible assets. Their finding is consistent with Mishra and Gobeli [88], who found that the combined effects of R&D expenditure and multinationality on market valuation are positive. Errunza and Senbet [40, 41] theoretically and empirically showed that multinational corporations (MNCs) experienced a positive valuation effect relative to purely domestic firms because of their role as financial intermediaries. MNCs provide investors who face differential cost barriers to direct holdings of assets across national boundaries with international portfolio diversification services. Benefits of geographic diversification could also be ascribed to scope and scale economies [75], improved market power, the spread of risks across international markets, and the ability to source lower-cost factor inputs [74].

However, geographic diversification does bear some significant costs as well. Finance scholars suggest that global diversification may have value-reducing effects because it can also lead to cross-subsidization of less profitable business units [35]. Some empirical studies have shown that capital markets penalize corporate multinationality by putting a lower value on the equity of multinational corporations [29], and this negative valuation effect has been consistent over the last two decades [24, 25, 26]. More recently, Lepetit et al. [77], in an event study, found that in general the market responds positively to geographic specialization type of mergers and acquisitions. Higher geographic dispersion substantially increases transaction costs and managerial information-processing demands [64], and as these costs outweigh the benefits, the effect on firm performance becomes negative. As an attempt to reconcile the conflicting empirical findings, Hitt et al. [65] proposed an inverted U‑ shaped relationship between international diversification and firm profitability. It is interesting to observe the similarity in the effects of both geographic and product diversification on firm performance. Arguably, many of the costs associated with product diversification— coordination difficulties, information asymmetry, and incentive misalignment between headquarters and divisional managers—also apply to geographic diversification [80]. As Palich et al. observed, “This indicates increasing advantages as both product and global diversification rise, but it also demonstrates the negative utility of these activities beyond some optimal level of diversity” [97, p. 169].

In summary, while a large body of research has examined the performance effects of firm diversification, a key gap in this literature stream is the limited attention paid to how IT could affect the relationship between diversification and performance. Drawing from Datta and colleagues’ [32] observation that research on the diversification– performance relationship would benefit by incorporating contingent factors, we seek to explore how and why the performance effects of diversification would differ with different levels of IT spending.

## Theoretical Foundations

Teece [125, 126] t heor ized t hat t he mot ive for diversification revolved around the reuse of physical resources, technological resources, and other intangible resources such as brand and reputation, which are indivisible and subject to market failure. The implied scope economies or synergies in key production factors or value-chain processes entail the well-acknowledged notion that related diversifiers outperform single-business firms or unrelated diversifiers [97, 107, 120]. In related diversifiers, IT may potentially enhance cross-unit business synergies [10, 13], such as centralization of components procurement [28] and intrafirm knowledge sharing [2, 123]. As a result, IT spending may moderate the diversification–performance relationship through increasing the effect of synergies, or through reducing the costs of enabling such energies to occur, which will be discussed below.

The mixed empirical findings have questioned a direct relationship between diversification and performance. A useful lens to explore the subtleties of the linkage is the organizational structure and controls used to support diversification strategies. Chandler [22] found that a strategy choice of product diversification caused companies with functional or holding-company structures to change to multidivisional (M-form)

structures. Williamson [131] argued that M-forms simplify the informational and decision situations by clearly differentiating between the long-range policy decisions to be handled by the general office and the short-term operation decisions to be determined at the divisional level. Hill [54] extended Williamson’s theory by recognizing heterogeneity in M-form structures. In particular, Hill [55] discussed the potential economic benefits of what Williamson termed “corrupted M-form structure,” in which the head office intervenes in the operating affairs of divisional units, and suggested that the centralized M-form (Hill’s terminology for the “corrupted M-form”) was compatible with related diversification strategy to exploit interrelationships between subunits. Empirical studies have supported the performance implications of strategy–structure fit [55, 58, 66].

Heterogeneity in M-form structures entails different internal control configurations installed to support specific diversification strategies [58]. Interdependence among related divisions creates performance ambiguities [57, 58]. Involvement of the head office in divisional affairs reduces divisional autonomy, which makes the use of objective financial criteria inappropriate to evaluate divisional performance as poor performance might result from inefficiencies within other interrelated divisions or from poor decision inputs from the head office [54]. To address the problem, firms can rely on strategic controls, which increase information-processing requirements by the head office but allow corporate executives to pinpoint the actual divisional efficiency. Such controls place emphasis on openness in corporate–SBU (strategic business unit) relations and the use of data in evaluating the performance of SBU or division managers [49]. An example of strategic control systems could be the balanced scorecard [99], which is a control system that integrates strategic goals, operating outcomes, customer satisfaction, and continuous improvement into an ongoing strategic management system.

As firms become increasingly diversified, controls typically shift from strategic to financial because of the escalating information-processing burden [8, 63]. Financial control represents a type of control system typified by the use of objective financial criteria to evaluate division managers’ performance [8]. Increases in span of control (number of operating divisions) and scope (difference between divisions) limit the ability of corporate staff to understand business unit strategies and force them to be more dependent on standardized performance criteria [67], such as accounting-based measures such as return on investment (ROI), and market-based measures, such as economic value added (EVA). Such a switch actually reflects a fit between the control arrangement and firm strategy within the M-form framework [56]. Emphasis on objective financial criteria is central to internal capital market efficiency, one of the major advantages of unrelated diversification, whereas to exploit interdependence among related businesses, a certain level of involvement in divisional affairs by the head office is necessary.

Studies on international strategy have also found evidence of fit between strategy and structure in MNCs. Egelhoff [38] looked at a set of geographic diversification variables—size of foreign operations, size of foreign manufacturing, and number of foreign subsidiaries—with respect to their information-processing requirements and found that they were inversely related to centralization of the structure adopted by MNCs. Egelhoff [39] furthermore pointed out that decentralized structures such as geographic regions or area divisions facilitated responsiveness to regional and national interests. Like related diversification, a strategy of pursuing low levels of geographic diversification, as reflected by the limited number of countries that the firm operates in, aims at exploiting commonalities in consumer tastes and preferences and standardization of products on a global basis [59]. Hence, MNCs pursuing such a strategy may realize enormous economies of scale and scope through a high level of centralization [53, 59]. Meanwhile, decentralization and subsidiary autonomy often characterize the structure used by firms operating in many countries. Due to increasing national differences, centralized structures are inferior for those firms because they, instead of stressing scale and scope economies, emphasize strategic adaptation to individual regional or national markets to enhance local responsiveness [119].

Combining the above arguments, it seems that when MNCs expand into more foreign markets, the chances to exploit scale or scope economies diminish due to increasing national differences. As a result, they tend to delegate operating decisions and strategic decisions to their subsidiaries and assign substantial autonomy to the subsidiaries. Using the organizational controls argument, we may infer that when geographic diversification is high, firms tend to use more financial controls, whereas firms with low geographic diversification may prefer the use of strategic controls. Although product diversification and geographic diversification represent two different types of diversification, they indeed share some commonalities in structures and control arrangements instituted to support the strategy. In related diversification or low geographic diversification, structures are characterized by centralization and strategic controls so as to exploit scale and scope economies. In unrelated diversification or high geographic diversification, decentralization and financial controls feature the structure to ensure divisional autonomy and local responsiveness. Through the dimension of coordination and control we are able to line up the two different types of diversification strategies.

In the same manner that fit between diversification strategies and control systems affects performance, we could argue that fit between control systems and IT spending in diversified firms would have performance implications.<sup>2</sup> Given that strategic controls require rich information flows between the corporate managers and divisional units, it can be argued that higher IT spending would fit strategic controls. Limitations in human capacities of information processing can be offset to a degree through the use of IT [69, 123]. This could enable firms to use strategic controls at higher levels of diversification that were not feasible without the information-processing capacity enabled by IT. In addition to altering when strategic controls are likely to be used, IT can also impact the effectiveness of such control systems in diversified firms. Strategic controls are likely to be effective only when key decision makers at both the corporate and divisional levels interact frequently and the top management exerts close monitoring to ensure concordance between firm-level strategies and division-level strategies. IT can generally enhance the ability of organizational members to communicate with each other, share information, and thereby coordinate actions and decisions. In this respect, a certain level of investments in information and communication technologies becomes necessary for effective strategic controls in diversified firms.

However, the cognitive limitations of managers that compel highly diversified firms to rely on financial controls may not be completely offset through the use of IT. As the number of divisions and the difference among them increase, escalating informationprocessing requirements will eventually drive corporate managers to adopt financial controls to lessen the burden. As a result, top managers in highly diversified firms tend to focus almost exclusively on financial results, particularly the rate of return on invested capital [68]. Although IT can be used to automate the financial report generation or to present results electronically, this is not likely to lead to performance effects. Moreover, past research suggests that coordination and control in highly diversified firms is achieved through incentives and contracts rather than through frequent monitoring [56]. For example, a critical aspect of financial controls is the type of incentive used to reward divisional managerial performance [68]. Of the two control mechanisms designed for agency problems, monitoring and incentives, the former clearly involves more information processing than the latter [36]. Thus, with more emphasis on incentive-based organizational controls, the potential for IT-controls fit diminishes. This is partly reflected in the findings that related diversifiers demand more IT than unrelated diversifiers [36, 79], and geographic diversification has an inverted U‑ shaped relationship with IT spending [79]. Thus, the scope for IT to enhance the effectiveness of control systems shrinks in highly diversified firms.

Table 2 summarizes the above arguments, which suggest that the level of IT spending represents an important contingency factor upon which the performance effects of diversification depend. The fit between IT spending and control mechanisms dictates that diversification strategies associated with strategic controls result in better performance in the presence of high IT spending. In other words, IT spending moderates the relationship between diversification and performance, but the moderating effects vary according to different types or levels of diversification.

## Moderating Effects of IT Spending in Product Diversification

In related diversification, the synergistic use of firm resources and capabilities across lines of businesses may not translate into improved performance unless its benefits outweigh the costs [71], the latter being manifested by administrative burdens in coordination and control across business units. Theoretical and empirical research has demonstrated that IT significantly cuts down coordination costs within organizations [33, 36, 50, 60, 82, 112]. Furthermore, IT can be used to enhance the effectiveness of strategic controls, which is compatible with the strategy of related diversification. As technology can be used to mitigate the costs of related diversification and enhance the benefits of the diversification strategy at the same time [28, 123], we may infer that IT spending positively moderates the relationship between related diversification and firm performance.

Unlike related diversification, synergies leading to scale or scope economies are absent in the case of unrelated diversification, which substantially reduces the scope of IT to enhance the economic benefits of diversification. Financial motives such as internal capital market efficiency drive nonsynergistic diversification moves [3, 85]. Excess cash is reallocated to divisions through the internal capital market mostly based on financial criteria, a process referred to as financial controls. As argued earlier, high IT spending is unlikely to fit financial controls, consistent with the previous finding that IT spending is weakly related to unrelated diversification [36, 79]. The weaker role of IT in financial controls and unrelated diversification leads us to suspect that their interaction effect on performance is much weaker or more likely to be nonexistent.

Rajan et al. [101] illustrated the cost of diversification by examining the inefficient allocation of internal funds within diversified firms. The capital misallocation problem leads to cross-subsidization, where resources flow toward the most inefficient division, resulting in less valuable firms. The presence of the internal capital market also exacerbates agency problems that drive further diversification discount [85]. Agency behaviors are controlled usually by the use of incentives such as equity-based compensation, rather than IT-enabled monitoring [36]. Also enforcing financial controls may prevent the misallocation problem. Employing incentives and financial controls are less IT-intensive processes. Therefore, diversification discount is unlikely to be mitigated by the amount of IT spending. The following hypotheses summarize the above arguments:

Hypothesis 2b: The interaction between IT spending and unrelated diversification will have no effect on firm valuation.

## Moderating Effects of IT Spending in Geographic Diversification

Global IT infrastructure (i.e., computer hardware, software, and data communications shared across country borders) can support the multinational firm as it seeks to coordinate global operations, diffuse innovation worldwide, or provide integrated services to a global corporate customer [70]. We propose two major advantages from geographic diversification that can be facilitated by IT—achieving scale/scope economies and exploiting intangible assets globally. First, geographically diversified firms are characterized by their dispersed and heterogeneous markets. Multinational firms can realize cost advantages in traditional input factors such as labor and raw materials [117]. IT enables firms to realize economies of scale and scope by electronically sourcing raw materials worldwide at lower costs. Second, internalization theory premises that intangible assets, such as technological know-how and patents, are information intensive and unevenly distributed across countries. A firm can gain abnormal returns by exploiting its firm-specific assets, especially intangible ones, in international markets [19]. IT can enhance the coordination capacity of informationintensive activities such as R&D efforts across countries [52]. Siemens’s ShareNet [127], for instance, is a successful case in which IT is heavily employed to facilitate the global transfer of knowledge. As a result, IT spending may have a positive moderating effect on the relationship between geographic diversification and performance. Also, the stockholders and analysts would positively view the joint decision of higher IT spending and broader geographic scope of the firm as a managerial intention to maximize firm value. Therefore, we could expect the interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification to have a positive impact on firm profitability as well as firm valuation.

However, this moderating effect may indeed depend on the degree of diversification. First, low geographic diversification favors centralized structures and strategic controls to exploit scale and scope economies, whereas high geographic diversification necessitates decentralization and autonomy assigned to foreign subsidiaries to ensure local responsiveness. Our earlier arguments suggest that the IT-controls fit is absent in firms with high geographic diversification where financial controls are emphasized. Findings that IT spending has a curvilinear relationship with geographic diversification [79] seem to support this argument. Second, to create synergy between IT and geographic diversification, firms need an integrated IT infrastructure that spans different countries. While a common IT infrastructure is relatively feasible when the firm operates in only one country or across only a few countries, it can be much more difficult to establish among a large number of countries given the enormous difference in the levels of IT investments across countries, not to mention differences in cultural norms and managerial practices. Thus, the costs and the difficulties involved in creating an appropriate IT infrastructure may outweigh the likely benefits of using IT for coordination beyond some levels of geographic diversification. To sum up, the scope of IT to fit geographic diversification will diminish as the diversification level increases, making the synergistic effect hardly achievable when the firm operates in a large number of foreign countries. Hence, we argue that

Hypothesis 3a: The interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification will have a positive effect on firm profitability only when the level of geographic diversification is low.

Hypothesis 3b: The interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification will have a positive effect on firm valuation only when the level of geographic diversification is low.

Hypothesis 4a: The interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification will have no effect on firm profitability when the level of geographic diversification is moderate to high.

Hypothesis 4b: The interaction between IT spending and geographic diversification will have no effect on firm valuation when the level of geographic diversification is moderate to high.

## Research Methodology

Dat a t o t est t he r es ear ch model were compiled from several secondary sources. IT spending data were obtained from surveys published by InformationWeek from 1991 to 1996. Performance measures, diversification levels, and other control variables were obtained from Compustat and the Directory of Corporate Affiliations. We first matched the firms listed in InformationWeek with firms in the Compustat database; 963 observations remained after matching. After dropping observations for which dependent variables or other explanatory variables data were not available, 514 observations remained in the sample, in which manufacturing firms predominated (403 observations, 78.4 percent). Restricting the sample to only manufacturing firms allows us to minimize confounding effects of the fundamental differences between manufacturing and service firms. Moreover, there are significant differences between manufacturing and service firms in their disaggregation of financial data by business activities [48]. For these reasons we, chose to look at only manufacturing firms (primary SIC [Standard Industrial Classification] code from 2000 to 3999). Our approach is similar to those adopted in past research in the diversification literature [34, 45, 47, 48, 65, 110] as well as the IT payoff literature [7, 129, 133].

## Measures

## Dependent Variables

The dependent variables in this study are firm profitability and firm valuation. As in prior diversification studies [34, 65, 120], we define firm profitability using the accounting-based measure, return on assets (ROA). Accounting-based measures of a firm’s profitability have been critiqued [1] but there is enough justification for their use [68]. Managers and external analysts frequently use ROA or return on sales (ROS) as a measure of management effectiveness, and the various measures of profitability are typically related [104]. In addition, changes in stock prices tend to follow the announcement of figures such as ROA or ROS, indicating their importance in signaling firm performance [42]. We compiled the ROA data for the firms in our sample using data from Compustat.

We used Tobin’s Q to measure firm valuation. Tobin’s Q has been widely used in the diversification literature to measure organization performance, particularly firm value. In the IS field, an interest in employing Tobin’s Q as a performance measure has emerged [11, 61, 122]. Tobin’s Q, as a market-based measure for performance, is forward-looking and risk-adjusted. More importantly, it taps into a firm’s long-term performance as well as the value of the intangible assets a firm possesses, both of which are likely to be affected by IT [11]. Of many proposed methods to calculate Tobin’s Q, we used the one suggested by Chung and Pruitt [27] as depicted below:

$$
\text { Tobin's } Q = (M V E + P S + D E B T) / T A,
$$

where MVE is the market value of the enterprise (the product of a firm’s year-end share price and the number of common shares outstanding); PS is the liquidating value of the firm’s outstanding preferred stock; DEBT is the value of the firm’s short-term liabilities net of its short-term assets, plus the book value of the firm’s long-term debt; and TA is the book value of total assets. All of the required inputs to compute Tobin’s Q were obtained from Compustat.

## Independent Variables

Several methods have been proposed for measuring product diversification, including the entropy measure [36, 96], the Herfindahl index [60, 76], the concentric index [60, 130], and counts of SIC codes [108]. In this study, we used the entropy measure not only because it is widely accepted but also because as one of its key properties, it allows for us to distinguish between related and unrelated diversification [96]. Given our focus on related and unrelated diversification, this measure is more suited than other available measures of product diversification. Following procedures used in past studies [36, 96], we calculated the entropy measures for related and unrelated diversification using business segment data from Compustat.

Similar to product diversification, several measures for geographic diversification have been used in past research, including the measure of international sales as a percentage of total sales [44, 45, 48, 120], the number of overseas subsidiaries [94], the Herfindahl index [19], the entropy measure [65, 73, 74], and the number of countries in which a firm has overseas subsidiaries [80]. Each measure has its own merits and limitations. Given the research focus of the present study, we prefer a measure that reflects the dispersion of the firm’s operations across countries. Therefore we chose to use the number of countries in which the firm has foreign subsidiaries. We handcollected the data from the Directory of Corporate Affiliations, a LexisNexis publication providing current, accurate corporate linkage information and company profiles on nearly 200,000 of the most prominent global public and private parent companies and their affiliates, subsidiaries, and divisions.

We measured IT spending by using the annual IT spending data from the surveys conducted by InformationWeek during the 1991–96 period. Every year since 1989, a list of 500 companies and their IT spending has been published in a special issue of the magazine in September. The companies are drawn from the population of U.S. firms with over \$1 billion in sales. The IT spending data include hardware and software investments in multiple categories (PCs, workstations, servers, mainframes, peripheral devices, software, local and wide area networks, and telecommunications). The IT spending figures do not include spending on IT by departments other than the IS department. Using IT spending or IT budget figures has been conventional in past IS studies (see, e.g., [5, 11, 78, 81, 89, 90]). As in these studies, we used the ratio of IT budgets to total sales of the firm—namely, IT intensity—as the measure for IT spending to control for the effect of firm size on IT spending.

## Control Variables

Christensen and Montgomery [23] and Montgomery [92] found evidence that performance differences among diversified firms were attributable in part to differences in market structures among the industries in which the firms competed. Hence, as in Grant et al. [48] and Tallman and Li [120], we introduced industry and firm-level controls in our models. Following Geringer et al. [45] and Grant et al. [48], we included industry dummies at the two-digit SIC level in the model to control for industry effects. Year dummies are also included since the observations range from 1991 to 1996. Since within the same industry environment firm performance varies due to the different strategies they pursue, firm characteristics such as size, R&D expenditure, capital intensity, and financial leverage were included as controls in our analysis. We realize that a firm’s marketing capability, as reflected by its advertisement intensity, is an important explanatory variable of firm profitability. However, since only one-fourth of the entries in Compustat had data on advertising expenditures, we were left with many missing values for this variable. Hence, we decided not to include advertisement intensity in the model.

Firm size, a commonly used control variable was measured as the natural logarithm of total employees. R&D expenditure, which is related to firm innovation and technological assets, has been suggested as a performance predictor [102]. Following past studies [34, 83, 84], we used R&D intensity, the ratio of R&D expenditures to the firm’s total sales, as a control variable. Firm leverage was measured by the widely used ratio of long-term debt to total capital (debt plus equity) [45, 120]. Capital intensity is measured as the percentage of capital expenditures to sales [11, 83]. Table 3 reports the summary statistics and correlations between the variables.

We also split the sample into groups using the median of relatedness and geographic diversification. Relatedness is defined as the ratio of related entropy index to the sum of related and unrelated indices. It measures the proportion of product diversity attributed to related businesses. A value of 0 indicates that the firm’s businesses are all unrelated whereas a value of 1 suggests that they are all related. For single-business firms, the variable was assigned the value of 1. The resulting four groups were firms with low product relatedness, firms with high product relatedness, firms with low geographic diversification, and firms with high geographic diversification. We present in Table 4 the characteristics of the key variables (IT intensity, firm size, ROA, and Tobin’s Q) in each group to help the reader understand the changes in these variables across different types and levels of diversification. ## Statistical Analyses and Results

Our dat a s et is an unbal anced panel dat a with 403 observations over six years and 134 firms. Since on average three observations are available for each firm, instead of employing an advanced time-series technique, we are using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression for the pooled data. Using an unbalanced panel data may violate the homoskedasticity assumption of OLS, resulting in biased estimates of standard errors. To correct for this, we ran the analysis with Huber–White standard errors clustering on firms, which will produce a robust estimation of standard errors given the presence of arbitrary correlations in error terms within the cluster. This method, also called Rogers standard errors [105], is robust to different specifications of the dependence in error terms within a cluster. Petersen [100] compared multiple methods of calculating standard errors for panel data, including OLS, Huber–White robust standard errors, Fama–MacBeth estimate, and fixed effects. He concluded that “the standard errors clustered by firm are unbiased and produce correctly sized confidence intervals whether the firm effect is permanent or temporary” [100, p. 40]. Since the precise form of the dependence in the residuals is unknown, an estimate that is robust to different specifications is advantageous [100].

As suggested by Carte and Russell [20], we interpret the main effect and the interactive effect simultaneously by using partial derivatives with respect to IT intensity [46, 49].<sup>7</sup> We analyzed only the regression model of product diversification because the model of geographic diversification had three-way interactions that made the interpretation difficult and impractical. The total effects of IT intensity on performance can be shown by the following partial derivatives:

$$
\partial R O A / \partial I T = - 0. 1 8 9 + 2. 1 8 5 \times \text { related   diversification }
$$

$$
\partial Q / \partial I T = - 1 1. 5 9 7 + 1 5. 5 6 4 \times \text { related   diversification }. ^ {8}
$$

Therefore, as long as the firm has some degree of related diversification, IT intensity becomes positively related to ROA due to the positive interactive effect. The coefficient of IT intensity is significantly negative in the Tobin’s Q equation, which is probably driven by firms with unrelated diversification. In fact, when the firm has an index of related diversification above 0.745 (11.597/15.564), IT intensity becomes positively related to Tobin’s Q. All the above strongly indicate that the effects of IT spending on performance are contingent on the relatedness among the firm’s businesses.

## Robustness Check

We conducted several tests to check whether the results discussed above are robust. First, past research has found that both IT investments [14] and strategic moves such as diversification [45, 48] have lagged effects on firm performance. Hence, we used one year lagged ROA (the next year ROA) and two years lagged ROA (the following year ROA) as the new dependent variable. In both cases, the results were similar and consistent with the earlier results. Second, we used alternative measures for firm profitability and valuation to ascertain whether our results are sensitive to the different measures of the dependent variables. We reran the analyses using ROS as an alternative measure of firm profitability and growth options (measured by the ratio of the market value of the firm to book value of assets where the numerator is equal to the market value of equity plus book value of liabilities) as a measure of firm valuation. The results were very similar to the earlier results and all the regression coefficients were consistent with those in the original models in terms of their signs, magnitudes, and significance levels. Thus, our results are robust despite the different performance measures used. Third, we ran the regression models using a subsample extracted from the full sample. We removed the observations of the first year (1991) and the last year (1996) from our sample and constructed a new sample for the 1992–95 period. Table 6 presents the regression results using the reduced sample. These results were quite consistent with those of the original analysis. We found a positive relationship between the interaction of IT intensity and related diversification and two performance measures. No significant impact of the interaction of IT intensity and unrelated diversification was found on either ROA or Tobin’s Q. The interaction between IT intensity and geographic diversification, again, had a significant and positive net effect on ROA only when the level of geographic diversification is low (dummy = 1).

## Discussion

We st art ed t his inquiry wit h t he int ent of bridging the gaps in both the diversification– performance literature and the IS literature. Our review of the diversification literature revealed that the empirical results linking diversification to firm performance have not been consistent. We posited that examining the performance implications of diversification in contemporary firms without considering appropriate IT spending would be incomplete. Specifically, we argued that the performance effects of diversification will be moderated by the extent to which firms spend on IT. We drew from both the resource-based theory and the literature on organizational controls to examine the interactions between IT spending and product and geographic diversification and the performance implications. Our results indicate that while IT spending interacts with related diversification to have a positive effect on both firm profitability and firm valuation, a similar interaction with unrelated diversification does not have any effects on firm performance. The moderating effect of IT spending on the relationship between geographic diversification and performance is positive only when the diversification level is low.

The study extends past research on the role of IT in diversified firms [36, 50, 60] by examining the performance implications of the interaction between IT spending and firm diversification. While earlier research has alluded to the potential moderating effect of IT on the diversification–performance relationship [28], empirical testing has not been undertaken. Later researchers have empirically investigated the interaction effect of IT and firm characteristics such as strategic choice and strategic direction [113, 114] but these studies dealt with only product diversification and lacked a rich theorizing about the role of IT in diversified firms, perhaps due to a somewhat loose connection to the large body of diversification literature. By taking advantage of the abundant research on diversification in the strategy and finance fields, our work makes two major contributions: (1) elaborating the role of IT using both synergy and control arguments and thereby developing rich theoretical arguments for the moderating effects of IT in both product diversification and geographic diversification contexts, and (2) providing empirical evidence of these moderating effects.

This study also attempts to add to the growing body of knowledge on the business value of IT by examining the contingencies upon which IT could contribute to improvements in firm performance. It is well accepted now among practitioners and academics that performance effects from IT arise through the complementarities between technology and other firm assets and capabilities [103]. Production inputs, managerial practices, and firm capabilities in particular have been proposed to interact with IT to affect firm performance. For instance, the effects of IT on firm performance have been found to depend on its complementary relationship with certain types of organization structures [12] and investments in intangible assets [16], as well as front-end e-commerce capability [132]. Particularly in this study, as suggested by the supplemental partial derivatives analysis, IT spending may be positively associated with firm performance when the diversification level is low—that is, related diversification or low geographic diversification.

Although product diversification and geographic diversification represent two different types of diversification, our study suggests some commonality in the two diversification strategies. Particularly when examined from the structural perspective regarding coordination and control, both diversification strategies exhibit similar congruence patterns with compatible structures—related diversification and low geographic diversification require centralized structures and strategic controls to enhance business synergies whereas unrelated diversification and high geographic diversification rely on decentralized structures and financial controls to ensure divisional autonomy and local responsiveness. The similarity, however, should not be overstated. Conceptually different, the two diversification strategies can be viewed as two dimensions of firm diversification. Therefore, although beyond the scope of this study, examining the role of IT in different combinations of both diversification strategies may be a promising research direction.

To provide some preliminary results and stimulate future research, we split the sample into groups using the level of product and geographic diversification as two dimensions. We used the medians of relatedness and geographic diversification to partition the sample into four cells. Then in each cell, we further split the observations into two groups using the median of IT intensity and reported mean ROAs and the difference in ROAs. These results, summarized in Table 7, provide some interesting insights.<sup>9</sup> In general, high IT is associated with high ROA except in the lower-right cell, which is the combination of high relatedness and high geographic diversification. The largest difference in ROA appears in the lower-left cell, which seems consistent with our argument that high IT spending fits both related diversification and low geographic diversification. It is also interesting to see how IT moderates the relationship between product relatedness and ROA at different levels of geographic diversification. For example, when geographic diversification is low, an increase in product relatedness leads to a significant increase in ROA when IT spending is high. By contrast, when geographic diversification is high, the opposite is observed, suggesting that the presence of high geographic diversification might suppress the role of IT in product diversification.

Recent studies have argued that accounting-based measures of firm performance are retrospective and that IT’s contributions to long-term firm performance and the value of the intangibles that arise from IT spending are better captured by measures such as Tobin’s Q [11]. These arguments are even more salient when one examines both IT spending and firm diversification in the same study as both have long-term effects that are best reflected by the responses of the capital markets. We found that the capital market reads IT spending differently based on differences in firm characteristics, particularly firm scope in our study. While the market perceives IT as a contributor to firm value in related diversifiers, it appears to react indifferently to IT spending in unrelated diversifiers. The subtlety in the market response to IT spending is largely due to the different roles of IT in different types/levels of diversification, as argued earlier in this paper. While such information may be initially available only to internal managers, given the efficient market assumption, the outside market will eventually acquire such information, which might be reflected in the reactions of the market. These findings lend further support to the views expressed by some IS scholars that stock market–based measures should be integral to IT value assessments.

Past research has documented the presence of a diversification discount based on the low valuation placed on diversified firms or the negative stock market responses to diversification moves [76, 85, 130]. Our findings raise an intriguing question: Can IT offset some of the diversification discount? And if so, under what contexts? Reasons underlying the observed diversification discount include inefficient capital allocation and coordination problems that pose difficulties in realizing synergies among related businesses in diversified firms. In this study, we posited and found that the coordination challenges can in fact be overcome through the use of IT and that such IT deployment would be reflected in higher firm performance. While more systematic research is required to tease out the sources of diversification discount and the extent to which IT mitigates some of these sources, our findings do indicate that this line of inquiry is warranted and might be a potentially useful research direction to pursue in our quest to understand the business value of IT. It should be pointed out that the results of a recent study linking IT business value to multifocused business strategies [121] is relevant here and is consistent with the direction for future research suggested here.

We found that the interaction between IT spending and unrelated diversification did not have any effect on firm profitability or valuation. These results must be interpreted with caution and should not be taken as evidence that unrelated diversifiers do not have a need for IT resources. Our theoretical explanation lies in the argument that expanding into unrelated businesses does not necessarily increase the demand for IT and hence any adjustment in IT spending might reflect other reasons than dealing with the increased coordination needs. Firms invest in IT for a number of reasons such as responding to competitive pressures and regulatory needs. Likewise, the roles played by IT in firms are highly variable. For example, if one looks at the benefits derived from IT such as lower production costs [90], enhanced agility [109], and improved customer orientation and knowledge codification [10], it is worthwhile to invest in technology to facilitate these benefits even in unrelated diversifiers. Thus, managers in unrelated diversifiers may consider these benefits to justify their IT spending.

## Limitations of the Study

The first limitation concerns the data set that we used. Because the companies used in the sample were quite large in terms of total sales and thought to be “effective IT users,” the results might not be generalizable to a larger population. Second, as the data set was from the 1990s the results obtained have to be cautiously applied to today’s context. But recent research has shown that in contemporary multibusiness companies, IT is serving as a platform for coordination and control and hence enhances the cross-unit business synergies [123, 124]. In fact, in recent years, the use of IT by firms to coordinate across business units has increased. While the technologies that firms invest in today could be different from those they invested in the 1990s, the role of IT as a coordination technology remains unchanged. Moreover, there is some preliminary evidence that the economic outcomes of IT spending are not significantly different today as compared with that in the 1990s [95]. Thus, we believe that our results are relevant for firms today and do have important implications for IT investing today.

In addition to the fact that our findings are likely to hold true today, our use of a data set pertaining to the 1990s time frame has some advantages. Much of the diversification–performance research pertains to this time period and the empirical findings pertain to this time period. As we pointed out earlier in this paper, the empirical findings of this research stream have not been conclusive or complete as the effects of IT have not been taken into account. By using a data set that pertains to the same time frame as those of other empirical studies, it would be possible to compare the results across studies and thereby add to the cumulative body of empirical findings about the performance effects of diversification.

Third, the measure for IT spending might underestimate the actual IT spending made by the firm. This is because the IT budget figure reported in the InformationWeek surveys was the annual budget of the central IS department and did not include IT spending by other departments [11]. However, it is likely that the total IT spending is proportional to the central IS budget for firms and therefore the analysis will not be seriously affected [89].

## Directions for Future Research

First, in the present paper we used IT spending as an aggregate indicator of IT assets and capabilities of a firm. As different categories of technology (e.g., hardware, software, and telecommunications) impact the value of the firm differently [51], future research could use more fine-grained IT spending measures or direct measures of IT-enabled coordinative capabilities in examining the interactive effects of IT and firm diversification. Using these measures will allow richer theorizing and enhance the internal validity of the model. Second, the negative moderating effect of IT spending in high geographic diversification deserves further investigation. While we have provided some possible reasons to explain this finding, there is a need to theorize what the likely effects of IT in highly geographically diversified firms could be. The negative effects are of much interest and a promising research topic for future researchers to explore. Third, the present study only addresses the breadth of diversification, while diversification itself is by all means multidimensional. For example, Simmonds [115] suggested that two dimensions of diversification, breadth and mode, if combined, explain more variance in firm performance. Therefore, a viable direction for IS researchers is to investigate whether IT exerts different performance effects in various breadth–mode combinations. Fourth, although using only manufacturing firms enhances the internal validity of the analysis, it may confine generalizability of our findings. We strongly suggest future researchers use data from other industries to further test the moderating effect of IT spending in the diversification–performance linkage. Service firms, for instance, differ greatly from manufacturing firms in the use of IT. Given the information-intensive content in services, empirical studies could reveal different patterns in the moderating effects of IT.

## Concluding Remarks

Pr evious IS wor k s uggest ed that strategic factors such as firm diversification, when leveraged with IT, will become a sustainable source for competitive advantages [28, 72]. Few studies, however, have been undertaken to theorize how IT may change the diversification–performance relationship or provide empirical evidence of such effects. The present study attempts to fill this gap in the literature. By employing a moderation model, we find empirical evidence that the level of IT spending represents an important contingency in the diversification–performance linkage. In practice, managers in highly diversified firms should view IT as a valuable asset given the performancecritical role played by IT when implemented with diversification. However, they must also be aware that the moderating effects on performance are contingent on types of diversification. In firms with unrelated product diversification or with high geographic diversification, IT may not contribute to performance as much as in related diversifiers and in low geographic diversification contexts. Our study suggests that managers in these firms focus not only on the coordination benefits of IT but also other enabling aspects to justify their IT spending.
