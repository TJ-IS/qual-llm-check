# The Moderating Role of Information Load on Online Product Presentation

Abstract: Online product presentations, which help consumers in obtaining product information, have an important role in shopping websites. Two major information types, visual and textual information, govern the modalities of consumers in acquiring productrelated information from most online product presentations. Previous studies have debated on which of these two information types can enhance the shopping performance of consumers. However, these debates have resulted in dichotomous outcomes. In this regard, the factor(s) that contribute to such divided results must be examined. In this study, we use level of processing theory and information load to examine the effect of online product presentations on the online shopping performance of consumers. A laboratory experiment has also been conducted. The data analysis results show that information load has a significant moderating role on the effects of online product presentations. The experimental results show that visual-based online product presentation has a stronger effect on the shopping performance of consumers in high information load condition; by contrast, textual-based online product presentation provides performance advantages than visual-based product presentation in low information load condition. The implications of these findings to both academics and practitioners are discussed at the end of this paper.

Keywords: online product presentations, information type, information load, level of processing

#

## 1 Introduction

Online product presentation is an indispensible component in most shopping websites (Yoo and Kim 2014). To facilitate the product learning processes of consumers, online merchants deliver their product information to consumers through online product presentations (Kelton et al. 2010; Zhao et al. 2009). For example, these merchants can place textual descriptions or still images of a laptop computer when delivering online product presentations to consumers (Lin et al. 2013).

Limited by the network bandwidth and server load of shopping websites, textual and visual (i.e., still image) product information have become the major information types in typical online product presentations (Li et al. 2012). Academics and practitioners have begun to evaluate the effects of th two information types on the online shopping performance of consumers (Kelto . 2010). Studies on this research topic have produced dichotomized findings over the years, wherein some studies suggest that visual product information is superior than its textual counterpart in terms of influencing shopping performance of consumers (Blanco et al. 2010). On the contrary, other scholars have reported that visual product information does not outperform textual product information in facilitating the shopping performance of consumers (Kim and Lennon 2008). These divided findings create a salient research gap on understanding the information processing mechanisms of humans in the ecommerce context. These findings also confuse shopping website practitioners on deciding which type of information is suitable for embedding in online product presentations (Jiang and Benbasat 2007a; Zhao et al. 2009).

#

Such research gap can be addressed by demonstrating how the effects of online product presentation on the online shopping performance of consumers are contingent on factors that represent information load (Sicilia and Ruiz 2010; Speier 2006). “Information load” refers to the amount of information conveyed through online product presentations (Goodwin and Etgar 1980; Malhotra 1982). This idea originates from the fact that consumers not only receive different types of information, but also varying amounts of product information via online product presentations (Wang and Benbasat 2009). In this regard, examining information load in online product presentations may help reveal the underlying mechanisms of humans when processing different types of product information in the online shopping context.

Despite extreme conditions, such as information overload, information load varies from low to high considering the available cognitive processing capacity of humans (Chernev 2003). Online product presentations with high information load may create a significant cognitive processing consumption, which leads to poor behavioral outcomes (Nelson et al. 1977b). However, such an argument has yet to be theoretically and empirically verified with different types of information. Previous studies on information load also suggest conflicting perspectives. For example, some scholars argue that a high information load facilitates information processing, which leads to a more knowledgeable decision (Chewning and Harrell 1990), while other scholars suggest that a high information load significantly consumes the bounded cognitive capacity of consumers, which impedes their processing of product attributes, and subsequently results in poor purchasing decisions (Jacoby et al. 1974). Therefore, the influence of

information load on the product information processing of consumers and on their online shopping performance must be investigated.

To address these issues, this paper seeks to answer the following question: In an online shopping context, how does information load influence the effects of online product presentation on the online shopping performance of consumers? We use level of processing (LOP) theory as theoretical foundation (Craik and Lockhart 1972; Olson 1980) to answer the above question. A laboratory experiment is also performed to examine the relationships between information load and online product presentation. The experimental result shows that visual information presentation outperforms textual information presentation in a high information load condition. Interestingly, visual information presentation does not provide significant influence on online shopping performance of consumers when the information load decreases.

This paper improves our understanding of the effect of online product presentation on the online shopping performance of consumers. Unlike prior studies that only consider the direct effects of online product presentation (Lee and Tedder 2003), the current paper highlights the moderating role of information load on the causal relationship between online product presentation and online shopping performance. We find that the effect of a particular product information type in an online product presentation may vary across different information load conditions. In this regard, this study provides valuable insights on the underlying information processing mechanism of humans in an e-commerce environment.

This paper is organized into four sections. Section 2 reviews the previous literature on LOP, online product presentation, and information load. Section 3 presents the

research model and hypotheses development. Section 4 and Section 5 discuss the research methodology and data analysis results. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

## 2 Literature Review

Online product presentations provide different types of product information to consumers (Chau et al. 2000). Consumers can mentally process information through these presentations to learn more about a product and then apply the obtained knowledge to facilitate their shopping performance (Tam and Ho 2006; Thompson and Hamilton 2006). This section reviews the extant studies on product presentation and information load by using LOP theory.

## 2.1 LOP Theory

LOP theory, which is anchored on the information processing paradigm of the human brain, discusses the different information-encoding processes and their effects on human recall and/or recognition (Craik and Lockhart 1972). “Recall” refers to the retrieval of information from memory, whereas recognition refers to the identification of correct information from retrieved memory (Craik and Lockhart 1972; Hockley 2008).

LOP theory has a profound background from psychology and cognitive science (Nelson et al. 1977a). LOP theory argues that the access of an individual to his/her memory depends on the amount of his/her expended cognitive work. An individual processes incoming information (in response to external stimuli) at different mental encoding levels, which ranges from “shallow” sensory encoding to “deep” semantic encoding. A deep encoding produces a higher effect on recall compared with sensory encoding (Olson 1980). “Sensory encoding” refers to the information-encoding process of the original physical stimulus for the appearance of information. For example, when seeing the word “dog,” an individual only interprets its spelling and pronunciation. Semantic encoding refers to the understanding of the meaning and associative characteristics of the original information (Craik and Lockhart 1972). For example, an individual may interpret the word “dog” as “a highly variable domestic mammal.”

According to LOP theory, the level and intensity of information processing is influenced by either information type or information load. Specifically, scholars have investigated the effects of information type on information-encoding performance at different processing stages (Sundar 2000). Researchers have discovered that visual information often triggers sensory encoding, whereas textual information typically invokes semantic encoding. For instance, Alvarez and Cavanagh (2004) found that visual information (e.g., picture, image, and graph) can activate visual storage in working memory for information processing, such as sensory encoding. Meanwhile, Kim and Lennon (2008) found that textual information (e.g., word, number, and symbol) can activate the cortical activity of the left inferior prefrontal cortex in the human brain for executive interpretation, such as semantic encoding.

Information load influences the level of information processing by constraining human cognitive capacity (Craik and Lockhart 1972). An individual has a higher available cognitive information processing capacity when facing a low information load. As a result, an individual can process information at a “deeper” semantic encoding stage (Craik and Lockhart 1972). When facing a high information load, the cognitive information processing capacity of an individual is used for consuming a large volume of information. In this regard, the level of information processing will remain at sensory encoding stage (Nelson et al. 1977a).

## 2.2 Online Product Presentations

Online product presentations act as a fundamental IT artifact for facilitating the online shopping of consumers. Two product information types, textual and visual information, comprise the bulk of product presentations in typical online shopping websites (Wang and Dai 2013; Yoo and Kim 2014). Textual online product presentations mainly offer product information using words, numbers and/or symbols, whereas visual online product presentations provide product information using images, pictures, and/or graphs (Kim and Lennon 2008). Based on their research focus, extant studies can be classified into two streams

The first stream highlights the advanced role of visual product information in online product presentations. Scholars in this stream believe that visual product information is superior to textual information in invoking the recall or recognition tasks of consumers (Speier 2006). Prior studies have mainly supported the multiple modalities proposition in explaining the superiority of visual information presentation. For instance, Maclnnis and Price (1987) argued that visual information (e.g., pictures and images) could invoke image information processing in the human brain. Image processing provides multimodality sensory information and concrete information processing triangulation in one’s working memory (Petrova and Cialdini 2005). As a result, mutual support sensory information processing enables consumers to make confident and knowledgeable decisions (Maclnnis and Price 1987). Other scholars found evidence supporting the superiority of visual information from the dual information processing perspective (Paivio

#

and Clark 1991). Information processing is conducted through two separate yet interconnected subsystems, namely, the verbal processing and imagine processing subsystems (Jiang and Benbasat 2007b). The verbal processing subsystem is activated when consumers receive textual information from the external world, which then helps consumers perform sequential information encoding. In contrast, the imagine processing subsystem is activated when consumers receive visual information, which helps them in parallel information encoding (Paivio and Clark 1991). Parallel information encoding can associate coded information from visual and textual information and then generate coding redundancy, which results from the superiority of visual over textual information in terms of recall and recognition (Jiang and Benbasat 2007b).

The second stream supports the proposition that textual product information outperforms visual product information. Kim and Lennon (2008) found that textual product information is superior to visual information in terms of product attributes recall and perception. To extend this finding, scholars in this stream have focused on determining the decision-making condition in which textual information acts as the superior information presentation type. Childers and Houston (1984), for example, observed that when consumers process textual and visual information at the semantic encoding stage, textual product information would generate similar effects on consumer recall and memory as that of visual product information. Other scholars have discovered a high correlation between textual information and the semantic encoding process (Savolainen 2009).

However, these two research streams have not yielded a congruent finding, which can be attributed to their lack of consideration of several factors, such as information

load. As indicated by LOP theory, information load can influence the encoding process of an individual when processing different types of information (Craik and Lockhart 1972). In this regard, information load must also be considered in the study of online product presentations.

## 2.3 Information Load

Previous studies on online product presentations have implicitly assumed that consumers have a sufficient available cognitive processing capacity to encode product information before making their decisions (Chernev 2003). However, the available cognitive capacity of humans is bounded and influenced by the information load imposed by external stimuli (Nelson et al. 1977b). Researchers have concluded that information load directly reflects the available cognitive capacity of individuals (Alvarez and Cavanagh 2004; Malhotra 1982). For instance, Chernev (2003) discovered that the available cognitive capacity of humans would be lessened when facing a high information load. Other scholars have also concluded that different levels of information load can lead to significant differences in the cognitive processing performance of consumers (Lee and Lee 2004). Although individuals have various available cognitive capacities, scholars reported that the available cognitive capacity of humans can occupy seven information blocks in working memory during information processing tasks (Miller 1956). If an individual receives more than seven information blocks, the effectiveness of the cognitive information-processing task will be impaired. Moreover, Bettman and Zins (1979) demonstrated that consumers could optimally process a maximum of six alternatives when making decisions. The findings of the abovementioned studies jointly support the effect of information load on the available

In this regard, the information load may not have been congruently manipulated across the two research streams of online product presentation. Therefore, distinctive findings on the effect of textual- and visual-based online product presentations can be drawn from these streams.

## 3 Research Framework and Hypothesis Development

In this study, we propose that the effects of different online product presentations (textual- and visual-based) on online shopping performance, manifested by product attribute recall and decision confidence, are contingent on information load. Figure 1 depicts the research framework.

##

LOP theory states that information encoding influences the recall and recognition of humans. In accordance with this theory, we examine the online shopping performance of consumers by assessing 1) their decision confidence (recognition) and 2) recall of product attributes (recall). The decision confidence of consumers refers to the subjective and qualitative judgment behind their decisions (Kamis and Davern 2004), whereas recall of product attributes refers to the number of product attributes that consumers can recall from their memory (Dee-Lucas and Larkin 1995). Both constructs are essential for evaluating the online shopping performance of consumers, because decision confidence can quickly drive consumers to accept a product (Washburn and Plank 2002). In other words, a higher level of confidence leads to greater product acceptance. At the same time, recall of product attribute can enhance the choice of consumers for evaluating products. Therefore, the online shopping performance of consumers is enhanced when they can recall a larger number of product attributes (Biswas and Sherrell 1993). Zmud (1979) and Hong et al. (2005) argued that decision confidence and product attribute recall (quantity of relevant information) could holistically represent the performance of consumers while shopping online.

## 3.1 Low Information Load Condition

We predict that textual-based online product presentation yields better online shopping performance under a low information load condition. When receiving informational stimuli from online product presentations, individuals will conduct their information-encoding processes at different encoding levels (Craik and Lockhart 1972). According to LOP theory, the level of encoding process is influenced by the available

#

cognitive information processing capacity of an individual (Nelson et al. 1977a). Compared with high information load, a low information load will not bring additional burden on information processing. Thus, low information load can lead to a relatively larger amount of available cognitive information-processing capacity for an individual (Lee and Lee 2004; Tam and Ho 2006). As a result, information processing has a high tendency to reach the semantic information encoding level.

Different types of information can also influence the information-encoding process. According to LOP theory, when the information-encoding process is at the sensory encoding level, visual information is more readily learned than textual information. Inversely, when the encoding process is at the semantic encoding level, textual information can be learned more easily than visual information (Childers and Houston 1984; Kim and Lennon 2008). In this regard, textual information can generate a viable performance on recall and relative recognition tasks in a low information load condition. Moreover, textual information can make explicit and specific claims about product attributes as well as provide readily understandable information to help individuals learn more about a product. Therefore, textual information can positively influence the product attribute recall and decision confidence of consumers. Contrary to the intuitive perception towards the superiority effect of visual information in online product presentations, we propose two hypotheses.

H1: Textual-based online product presentation will generate higher decision confidence than visual-based online product presentation in a low information load condition.

H2: Textual-based online product presentation will generate better recall of product attributes than visual-based online product presentation in a low information load condition.

## 3.2 High Information Load Condition

As the information load increases, we predict that visual-based online product presentation will produce a greater effect on product attribute recall and decision confidence of consumers. According to LOP theory, the increasing amount of information will constrain the available cognitive information processing capacity of individuals. Hence, consumers will process incoming information at a “shallow” sensory encoding level (Craik and Lockhart 1972). The visual-based online product presentation contains pictorial/graphical information, which enables sensory encoding process to facilitate the retrieval of information from the working memory of consumers and then influence their recall and recognition (Sundar 2000). Unlike visual product presentation, the textual product presentation is processed based on the phonemic and orthographic components of the textual product information at the sensory encoding stage (Craik and Lockhart 1972). In this regard, the sensory encoding of textual information cannot lead to a meaningful interpretation of a product. Therefore, we posit the next two hypotheses below.

H3: As the information load increases, visual-based online product presentation will result in higher decision confidence than textual-based online product presentation.

H4: As the information load increases, visual-based online product presentation will result in better recall of product attributes than textual-based online product presentation.

## 4 Research Method

## 4.1 Experiment Design

We performed a laboratory experiment to test the aforementioned hypotheses. We combined two types of product information (textual and visual) with two levels of information load (high and low) to generate four treatments. The design details are shown in Table 1.

We manipulated product information type in the experiment by providing the descriptive text and still images of the product as textual and visual product information, respectively. We adopted the method of Mackay and Villarreal (1987) to establish equal semantic content between these two types of product information. This method can help us to minimize excessive information. Specifically, Mackay’s method used either textual (e.g., the market share of product A is 30%) or visual information format (e.g., a pie chart that represents 30% market share) to present the market share of the rival product. In this regard, each information type has the same semantic information. In the same vein, we presented the product features either in textual format (e.g., “the color of the digital camera is silver grey”) or in visual format (e.g., a picture that shows the silver grey color). This was done to minimize the differences in information content and to focus on the presentation of information type.

#

To quantify information load, we used a simple sentence that contained one independent clause (Montgomery and Evans 2009). For instance, the product feature of a digital camera can be presented with the simple sentence, “The weight of the digital camera is 500 g.” We provided 4 and 12 simple sentences to the participants in the low and high information load conditions, respectively (Malhotra 1984). These information load settings are suitable for differentiating the available cognitive processing capacity of an individual. Such manipulation of information load is also in accordance with previous studies on information presentation (Chernev 2003). Examples of high and low information load settings are provided in Appendix B.

For this experiment, we built a simulated online shopping website comprising two modules, namely, (1) an online product presentation module, which showed either textual or visual product information to the participants, and (2) an evaluation and decision-making module, which provided shopping decision options to the participants. The setting of the simulated website was set such that it was similar to a typical online shopping website (Kumar and Benbasat 2006). In our experiment, an experiment scenario was displayed to the participants upon logging in to the website. After reading the scenario, participants proceeded to the shopping process, in which the twoabovementioned modules represented product attribute information and shopping decision options, respectively. Upon reading all the product attribute information, participants then decided whether to buy the product. After making a shopping decision, participants were then asked to answer the follow-up questionnaire.

We used a fictional brand in the simulated website to rule out brand effect (Tan et al. 2010). The participants were not given a time limit during the experiment, and they were free to decide when to proceed on to the product procurement step. A time limit was not set to consider the differences in the learning speeds and capacities among the participants.

We measured the dependent variables using two methods. For recall of product attribute, we asked the participants to recall as many attributes as possible of each product that they procured. Two coders were employed to code the answers independently according to the number of attributes that the participants articulated. Any discrepancy in the coding of the number of attributes was resolved in the presence of the two coders. Only the correct answers (i.e., the mentioned attributes associated to a product) were counted. An inter-rater reliability rate of 0.95 was achieved in the first round of coding (i.e., before resolving the discrepancy).

Meanwhile, for decision confidence, we adopted seven-point Likert scales from prior studies (Kamis and Davern 2004). In addition, we measured prior product knowledge and involvement of the participants by adopting items from prior literature (Baumgartner and Steenkamp 2001; Smith and Park 1992). These two variables will serve as control variables in this study. The questionnaire can be found in Appendix C.

## 4.2 Laboratory Experiment

Participants: We recruited 66 participants from a public university in the Asia-Pacific region and then randomly assigned them into two groups, each having 33 participants. Among the participants, 18 (27.3%) were females and 48 (72.7%) were males. These participants were recruited through email and advertisements. Their mean age was 22 (δ=2.320) years. After the experiment, each participant was given a monetary incentive of USD 10.00.

Procedure: The experiment was conducted in the following sequence. Upon their arrival to the laboratory, participants were randomly assigned to one desktop computer. They were then asked to provide their demographic information, listen to pre-recorded introduction illustrated the features of the simulated shopping website and provided instructions on how to use the online product presentation module as well as the evaluation and decision-making module.

Four product categories (e.g., baby car seat, camcorder, projector, and GPS device) with fictional brands were used in the experiment. The participants were asked to “purchase products for their closest friend.” This experiment scenario is consistent with that used in most studies on information-seeking and decision-making behavior (Wood and Lynch 2002). We used this scenario because requiring the participants to internalize four products for their self-consumption would not be realistic. Such purpose of purchasing a product for a close friend is commonly used in consumer behavior literature (Simonson and Nowlis 2000). Specifically, participants were asked to purchase one product from each product category. The purchasing order of these four types of product was randomized.

We provided visual-based online product presentation to one group and textualbased online product presentation to the other group. The participants within each group needed to buy a product in both high and low information load conditions. The sequence of the different information load presentations was also randomized to screen out the effect of cognitive capacity heterogeneity between the participants (Bednar et al.

2011). This setup was consistent with that of most experimental studies on decisionmaking behavior (Haubl and Trifts 2000).

## 5.1 Manipulation Check

Next, we conducted a manipulation check to validate the correct provision of different types of online product presentations and different levels of information load. We asked participants to report the type of product information that they saw (i.e., textual or visual). All participants reported the product information type correctly. For information load provision, we referred to the system log file to check the accuracy of the different levels of information load (low and high). Based on the results, the information load was successfully manipulated.

We also conducted the following analysis to ensure that the information load was appropriately manipulated. As mentioned earlier, we did not control the time spent by each participant on learning product information from the online product presentations. In this regard, the participants could reduce their information load by spending more time digesting the information presented in the high information load condition (Huang 2000), which would then confound the manipulation of information load. To address this issue, we performed ANOVA to check whether significant differences existed among the times spent by the participants for learning product information. No significant difference was observed between the high and low information load conditions (see Table 2), thus indicating the successful manipulation of online product presentation and information load.

## 6 Discussion

This paper aims to improve our understanding of the effects of online product presentations on the online shopping performance of consumers. We demonstrated that information load could act as a viable moderator to alter the impact of visualbased/textual-based online product information presentation on consumers’ online shopping performance.

#

In the low information load condition, we find that textual-based online product presentations enhance the decision confidence of consumers. This finding is in line with prior studies on the product judgment of consumers (Bettman et al. 1998). These consumers obtain benefits from a higher degree of available cognitive processing capacity if the information load is low. Afterwards, they process such information at the semantic encoding stage (Craik and Lockhart 1972), which then generates a detailed meaning of the product attributes to help the consumers gain a better understanding of these products. As a result, consumers gain more confidence in their selection of products (Coupey et al. 1998).

In a high information load condition, LOP theory suggests that the encoding of a large amount of visual product information can generate additional information cues to delineate reasons for judgment (Sanfey and Hastie 1998). These cues, in turn, play a role in enhancing decision confidence. However, the enhanced decision confidence in the visual-based product information presentation is not significantly different from the one in textual-based product information presentation. One plausible explanation could be due to the consumers’ heuristic decision method. Considering the high information load provision in the online shopping process, consumers usually adopt EBA (elimination by aspects) heuristic method to reduce the decision effort for comparing different product features (Tversky and Kahneman 1973). In this regard, consumers may shift their focus from understanding the meaning of product features to counting whether the product provides sufficient features. For example, consumers may first examine whether a camcorder has CCD sensor, then, they will examine whether the camcorder has exchangeable lens. This process will continue until they make the final

#

decision. In our study, the sensory encoding of both the visual-based and textual-based product information cannot generate significant differences on describing the quantity of product features. Hence, no significant effect is found in high information load condition.

With the regards of recall of product attribute, consumers do not remember additional product information through textual-based online product presentations in the low information load condition. This outcome can be attributed to the low information load condition itself. Product attributes from either textual- or visual-based online product presentations can be readily remembered in a low information load. In addition, the semantic information-encoding process cannot generate new information regarding product attributes from the physical description of the product (Craik and Lockhart 1972). In this regard, textual-based online product presentation cannot enhance product attribute recall in a low information load condition. Another plausible explanation is that consumers may encode visual-based product information and textual-based product information with the same amount of accuracy when they have plenty of cognitive resources in the low information load condition. In this regard, the recall of product attribute could be similar with the use of both these product information presentations.

With the increase of information load, our finding suggests that visual-based online product presentation significantly affects the recall of product attributes (Craik and Lockhart 1972), which is similar to the experiential processing of the cognitive information processing system of humans (Li et al. 2012). The sensory encoding process can generate a broad, elaborate memory of product attributes (Epstein et al. 1996), thereby inherently strengthening the recall of product attributes (Thompson and Hamilton 2006).

#

The varying impacts of online product presentation on consumers’ online shopping performance demonstrate the moderating role of information load. However, scholars have argued that the poor performance of textual-based online product presentation in a high information load condition is mainly caused by information overload. For example, prior studies have suggested that information overload can result in poor performance (Eppler and Mengis 2004). Information overload occurs when the amount of information for decision making exceeds the available cognitive information processing capacity, hence impairing an individual’s decision performance (Malhotra 1984).

In our experiments, we provided a manageable amount of information to control for the contaminant effect of information overload in our experiment. Specifically, in a high information load condition, we provided 12 simple sentences to the participants, which were significantly fewer than the number of other information overload manipulations from prior literature (Malhotra 1982). We also performed a post-hoc analysis on textualbased online product presentation by comparing the online shopping performances of consumers in high and low information load conditions. We find that a high information load does not significantly attenuate online shopping performance (t<sub>decision</sub> <sub>confidence</sub>= - 0.352, p>0.10; t<sub>recall</sub> <sub>of</sub> <sub>attribute</sub>=-0.689, p>0.10). In this regard, we demonstrate that the changes in the online shopping performance of consumers are not due to information overload.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

Anchored on LOP theory, this paper investigates the moderating role of information load. Several theoretical implications can be drawn from this paper.

#

First, this paper provides new insights that can harmonize the conflicting views regarding the effect of textual/visual product information on the online shopping performance of consumers. These conflicting views are generally attributed to the following reasons: (1) prior studies have focused on discovering possible informationprocessing approaches (e.g., dual-processing) and on the matching between information type and possible processing approaches (Blanco et al. 2010; Jarvenpaa 1989); (2) previous scholars have attempted to strike a balance between the amount of different information types in terms of their negating confounding effect (Biehal and Chakravarti 1982). Despite producing fruitful outcomes, these studies have failed to investigate whether information load has been manipulated similarly across different papers.

To fill the research gap, we find that the information load of online product presentations has a key moderating role on the effects of information types in the online shopping context. Specifically, we provide salient implications on understanding how consumers process product information. Unlike prior studies, we find that visual information does not always produce a greater effect on the online shopping performance of consumers than textual information or vice versa, thereby implying that the effects of different information types depend on the level of information load in online product presentations. In this regard, this paper provides a possible explanation with which to address the conflicting views in prior literature; the findings can also help scholars develop a deeper understanding of the influence of information presentations in the online shopping context.

##

Second, this study supports the theoretical foundations of online product presentation literature. Previous studies are mostly anchored on fit theories (e.g., cognitive fit theory) (Hong et al. 2005), information encoding theories (e.g., dual-coding theory, cognitive information processing paradigm), and media richness theory to investigate the relationship between online product presentation and the behavioral performance of consumers in the e-commerce context (Blanco et al. 2010; Lu et al. 2014). These theoretical foundations successfully explain how a particular information type can be adopted/used for accomplishing a task (see summary of prior literature in Appendix A). However, these studies do not consider the amount of information provided for finishing a task, which is also an important determinant of behavioral performance (Sasaki et al. 2011). For example, cognitive fit theory argues that the correspondence between task and information presentation type leads to superior task performance for individual users. This theory also explains the performance differences among users across different information presentation types, such as tables and graphs (Vessey 1991). However, cognitive fit theory does not account for the variances in the performance of different information types under different information load conditions despite their relevance in the real information processing environment. Additional insights can be gained by considering the different levels of information load when examining the performance of different information types. We find that the effects of information type found in online product presentations differ across various levels of information load. In this regard, a particular information type will have an advanced (or inferior) performance under a specific information load condition. Prior theoretical foundations can greatly benefit by reexamining the relationship between information

type and task while controlling information load. Therefore, this study provides valuable insights that can improve the present understanding of the information processing patterns of consumers in the online shopping context.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

This study also provides several key implications for practitioners. Our findings primarily indicate that the effects of the different types of product information can vary across different levels of information load. In this regard, website designers and managers can enhance the online shopping performance of consumers by implementing textual-based online product presentations in a low information load condition. Meanwhile, in a high information load condition, website managers are advised to provide visual-based online product presentations when presenting their product information in online shopping websites.

An adaptive product information presentation system for e-commerce must also be implemented across multiple platforms. This system can detect the screen size of a device and the amount of pixels in the screen. With such data, the system can automatically detect the amount of product attribute information and the information load. If the information load is low, the system can automatically adjust the information presentation to textual information. Otherwise, visual information will be used for the information presentation. In this regard, only one product information input generate different types of product information presentations, thus leading to enhancement of consumers’ behavioral performance. This system can also concurrently strengthen the performance of product presentations across many user-end platforms, such as laptops, desktop computers, tablets, mobile phones, and televisions. The display format and the product information content can both be adjusted and customized before launching marketing activities. This way, the product marketing strategy would be consistent across different platforms.

## 6.3 Limitations and Concluding Remarks

Despite the efforts outlined above, this study has several limitations that may provide directions for future research. First, we did not directly measure the informationencoding stages owing to the nature of the information-encoding process (Craik and Lockhart 1972; Kirkpatrick and Epstein 1992). Epstein suggested that information encoding (processing) is an automatic process that no longer needs to be activated by information stimuli (Epstein 1991). Moreover, Thompson and Hamilton (2006) found that information encoding (processing) is automatic and intuition-activated when consumers compare advertisements. We hope that this limitation will be addressed in future works. The aforementioned studies may (1) implement a think-aloud session and/or (2) use brain-scanning aids to capture the information processing stages. Minas et al. (2014) discussed the benefits and challenges of using several neurophysiological tools for investigating information-encoding processes, such as fMRI and EEG. Future studies must concentrate on the use of these tools for examining the cortical activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of humans, in which the working memory is located (Li et al. 2014). Despite this limitation, this study seeks to present preliminary yet non-trivial findings on the effects of different types of online product presentations on the shopping performance of consumers.

Second, we only considered the dichotomy in product information type (textual and visual). These information types are fundamental in online product presentations (Chau

#

et al. 2000). Given the recent technological developments, online shopping websites can now provide multi-media product information presentations (e.g., image-and-text (hybrid) information presentation, video information presentation, and virtual product experience) to their consumers. These product information types have a promising effect on the decision-making performance of consumers, thus requiring further investigation in the future. However, this paper focuses on the moderating role of information load and the conflicting views on the performance of textual/visual online product presentation. Hence, comparing hybrid product information (or video product information) with textual/visual information in different information load conditions is considered inappropriate. Moreover, the hybrid and video product information cannot strengthen our understanding of the divided view of previous studies regarding the effects of textual/visual product information. Therefore, hybrid and video product information are beyond the scope of this paper. We acknowledge this as a research limitation and recommend it as a topic that warrants further research.

Third, we only considered search products in this study because most online shopping websites are selling such products (e.g., Amazon.com). A rich data source exists for acquiring product information. However, other product categories (e.g., experience and credence products) must be investigated in future research to generalize our findings (Lim et al. 2013).

## 7 Conclusion

The current research discusses how online product presentations can be utilized to enhance the online shopping performance of consumers. Essentially, we find that information load, as an important moderator, influences the effect of online product

presentation on the shopping performance of consumers. Hence, this study is a modest step toward the development of a theoretically sound measurement of the performance and effectiveness of product information presentations in the online shopping context.
