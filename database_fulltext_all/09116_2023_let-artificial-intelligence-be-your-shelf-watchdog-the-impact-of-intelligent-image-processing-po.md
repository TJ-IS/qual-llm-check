# LET ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE BE YOUR SHELF WATCHDOG: THE IMPACT OF INTELLIGENT IMAGE PROCESSING-POWERED SHELF MONITORING ON PRODUCT SALES<sup>1</sup>

Yipu Deng HKU Business School, University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, CHINA {yipudeng@hku.hk}

Jinyang Zheng Krannert School of Management, Purdue University, Lafayette, IN, U.S.A. {zhengjy@purdue.edu}

Liqiang Huang School of Management, Zhejiang University and Center for Research on Zhejiang Digital Development and Governance, Hangzhou, CHINA {huanglq@zju.edu.cn}

Karthik Kannan Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {kkarthik@arizona.edu}

We collaborated with a leading fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) manufacturer to investigate how intelligent image processing (IIP)-based shelf monitoring aids manufacturers’ shelf management by using data from a quasi-experiment and a field experiment. We discovered that such artificial intelligence (AI) assistance significantly and consistently improves product sales. Several underlying mechanisms were revealed by our quantitative and qualitative analysis. First, retailers are more likely to comply due to the greater monitoring effectiveness enabled by AI assistance. Second, the positive effect of IIP-based shelf monitoring partially persists after it is terminated, implying that human learning takes place. Third, the value of IIP-based shelf monitoring can be attributed to independent retailers rather than chain retailers. Since the degree of contract heterogeneity is the major difference between these retailers in terms of monitoring, this finding further suggests that AI is relatively more scalable when coping with more heterogeneous instances. Apart from these great benefits, we demonstrate the low marginal costs of implementing IIP-powered shelf monitoring, which indicates its long-term applicability and potential to generate incremental value. Our research contributes to several literature streams and provides managerial insights for practitioners who consider AI-assisted operational models.

Keywords: Economic value of artificial intelligence, quasi-experiment, field experiment, monitoring, shelf management

## Introduction

Shelf management is critical for fast-moving consumer goods (FMCGs) manufacturers. Because the way in which products are displayed on shelves directly affects customer choice and serves as a key determinant of product sales (Frank & Massy, 1970), inappropriate shelf layout has the ability to kill a product/manufacturer (e.g., Dreze et al., 1994; Nierop et al., 2008). As a result, retailers contractually agree with manufacturers on how shelf space will be used to showcase products. A standard contract between physical retailers and manufacturers specifies the shelf location and space, as well as the number of products to be displayed. The contract can even set constraints such as displaying products at eye level or making product labels visible. To verify contract terms, manufacturers send their delegates to monitor shelf display (i.e., retailers’ compliance) in each store. In the past, delegates representing manufacturers have effectively executed contract monitoring (e.g., Copple, 2002; Gómez & Rubio, 2008); however, this dynamic is changing, as manufacturers attempt to reach more global audiences. In emerging retail markets, manufacturers are increasingly distributing their products across a large number of independent retailers that have high levels of shelf heterogeneity. Enforcing nonunified shelf display standards across independent retailers requires considerable scalability. Thus, conventional shelf management practices that rely solely on human judgment (i.e., human-based monitoring) are becoming obsolete. A natural solution to this challenge would be to consider artificial intelligence (AI) / machine learning (ML) methods such as intelligent image processing (IIP) to overcome these limitations. Three key questions arise with respect to this solution: (1) Does the use of the IIPpowered shelf monitoring system improve shelf management and thus product sales? (2) If yes, what are the possible mechanisms that account for the improved sales? and (3) How successful will the IIP-powered system be in the long run? To answer these questions, we collaborated with a leading global FMCGs manufacturer with headquarters in the E.U. (hereafter referred to as “the manufacturer”) and conducted systematic, analytical experiments.

Our collaborative research included three stages. In Stage 1, we examined the incremental impact and economic value of launching IIP-powered shelf monitoring by using a quasiexperiment scheme and analyzed observational data. In Stage 2, we conducted a randomized field experiment to more precisely quantify the causal effect of implementing IIPpowered shelf monitoring. We also included a treatment group that terminated AI assistance (i.e., the IIP monitoring system) after a while. This design is consistent with the literature on monitoring (e.g., Staats et al., 2016) and allowed us to investigate the persistence of AI assistance, which improved our understanding of the long-term adoption effect and the existence of learning reflected in this program. We attempted to disentangle several underlying mechanisms in Stage 3, which was divided into four parts. First, we postulated that the incremental value derived from IIP-powered monitoring stems from the superior scalability of AI in dealing with heterogeneous instances. Therefore, we anticipated a more salient impact when the correspondingly monitored shelves were more heterogeneous. We tested this assumption by looking at the heterogeneous treatment effects of IIP-powered monitoring across different store types based on contract heterogeneity. Second, we assumed that the observed increase in product sales resulted from retailers’ improved compliance behaviors and verified this expectation by analyzing the change in retailers’ compliance rates using shelf photos. Third, we isolated the effect of solely taking shelf photos. Finally, we interviewed the manufacturer’s delegates and retailers to conduct a qualitative examination of our quantitative findings and the underlying mechanisms.

Our analysis revealed several notable findings. First, all our analyses consistently indicated the existence of “AI appreciation.” Specifically, retail stores that implemented the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program boosted their product sales. More interestingly, we found that improved sales derived from IIP-powered shelf monitoring can largely be attributed to independent retail stores, which have more heterogeneous contracts and are more dominant in emerging markets. In contrast, these improved sales were insignificant for chain stores, which typically have more homogeneous contracts and are more common in mature markets. This finding supports our expectation as well as our decision to encourage our manufacturer to adopt this program in its emerging market. This finding further bolsters the scalability of AI and shows how it can be expanded to include repetitive tasks with heterogeneous instances, which are normally more challenging for humans to complete.

In terms of the behavioral persistence induced by IIPpowered shelf monitoring, we found that gains in incremental sales dropped after the program ended. We also show a diminished worth: treatment stores that launched the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program sustained higher sales even when the monitoring program was terminated, as compared to control stores that continued to use humanbased shelf monitoring. These findings collectively suggest that the launch of an IIP-powered shelf monitoring program greatly impacts human behavior and leads to learning opportunities for retailers and delegates.

Finally, we provide evidence for possible causal chains linking AI assistance to improved sales. For example, AI assistance could lead to more effective monitoring by delegates, and this enhanced monitoring could result in better compliance behaviors among retailers, which would yield improved sales. In addition, rather than taking shelf photos, we notice that the benefit of deploying this IIP-powered shelf monitoring system comes from AI. In other words, a placebo monitoring system that only takes shelf photos will not boost sales.

Our study offers several contributions to the literature. We note that the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system is an example of an AI-assisted model in which AI augments human tasks. While the benefits of AI-driven models (in which AI replaces human tasks) have been generally well studied (e.g., Bennett & Hauser, 2013; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Esteva et al., 2017; Ferraro et al., 2011; Huang & Rust, 2018; Luo et al., 2019), the impact of AI-assisted models has received less attention. Our study narrows this gap by providing empirical evidence that AI can be built to complement human processes. We also add to the extant literature on the business value of AI by extending the scope to shelf management. Second, we contribute normatively and substantively to the existing literature on human attitudes toward algorithms by offering the first examination of the effectiveness of AI-based regulatory assistance in the retailing industry. Third, we contribute by investigating behavioral persistence with respect to IIP-powered shelf monitoring and demonstrating human learning from AI assistance. Fourth, we contribute by exploring the business circumstance under which AI assistance is more applicable due to its scalability. Finally, our results add to the literature stream on shelf management by focusing on the perspective of manufacturers (i.e., how manufacturers could effectively manage their products on retailers’ shelves).

Our study generates several managerial insights for practitioners, especially FMCG manufacturers, regarding the use of AI assistance. The incremental value of IIPpowered shelf monitoring indicates that practitioners could leverage AI-based assistance to manage retailers’ shelf displays, particularly when there exists a high degree of contract heterogeneity among retailers. Such heterogeneous retail stores not only account for the majority in emerging markets but also exist in developed scenarios. The insights of our cost-benefit analysis and the behavioral persistence of IIP-powered shelf monitoring programs should help practitioners understand how to apply AI assistance and enact policies to ensure long-term success. More broadly, the rise of the sharing economy necessitates the handling of heterogeneous instances, which is becoming an increasingly salient challenge for managers. For example, Airbnb and Uber must manage millions of individual hosts and vehicles, respectively, that can be quite different from each other. Our findings imply that such managers should seek AI assistance when their business scope involves managing heterogeneous instances, so that they may benefit from the incremental value of AI’s scalability.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the Literature Review section, we survey the literature related to our study. In the Research Context section, we briefly describe the collaboration with our manufacturer. In the next three sections, we elaborate on the three stages of our investigation—the quasiexperiment, field experiment, and mechanism analysis, respectively. The last section of our paper provides a costbenefit analysis, followed by the conclusion.

## Literature Review

Our work is closely related to three research streams. First, we extend the research on the business value of AI. Second, we add to the body of literature on monitoring, particularly the growing stream that explores using AI as a monitoring tool. Lastly, we contribute to the literature on shelf management.

## AI

## Retailer Management and AI

Collaboration between manufacturers and retailers (i.e., retailer management) has been well documented (e.g., Sheu et al., 2006). A McKinsey report (Brinkhoff & Großpietsch, 2014) categorizes collaboration efforts into four types: (1) supply-chain flows and processes, (2) demand planning and fulfillment, (3) promotion strategies, and (4) in-store layout/visual merchandising (including shelf management). The literature has demonstrated the value of IT in aiding the first three of these four types of collaborations. For instance, Bowersox and Daugherty (1995) showed that IT has significantly improved logistics strategy and practice. Similarly, Sabath et al. (2001) demonstrated improved efficiency in the supply chain of an automatic replenishment program enabled by IT. Oh and Lucas Jr. (2006) revealed that IT enables strategic pricing decisions for both manufacturers and retailers. However, to the best of our knowledge, the literature on improving visual merchandising is scarce, perhaps because visual merchandising/shelf management has been largely performed by people (e.g., delegates), thereby limiting traditional IT’s role in this context.

The introduction of AI assistance and its enhanced scalability (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2020) could change this domain of visual merchandising. Prior and emerging research has recognized the benefits of AI across various fields. For example, in finance, Ferraro et al. (2011) showed that AI applications can improve fraud detection and asset management. In healthcare, studies have explored how AI can help doctors diagnose cancers (e.g., Esteva et al., 2017; Leachman & Merlino, 2017) and help hospitals improve operational efficiency (Bennett & Hauser, 2013). In marketing, research has shown that AI can be used to understand consumers’ emotional status (Huang & Rust, 2018) and provide better customer service (Luo et al., 2019). In addition, AI has been recognized as most suitable for tasks that require heavy processing of text, speech, image, and video data (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). Therefore, leading FMCG manufacturers are seeking effective AIbased applications to manage their visual merchandising.

Following Luo et al. (2021), we help our collaborator develop and experiment with an AI-human monitoring assemblage (i.e., an IIP-based monitoring system to assist delegates) to check the compliance of its retailers with respect to shelf displays.

Thus, our investigation into the effectiveness of AI-based applications for managing retailers’ shelf displays responds to a gap in our understanding of how IT helps retailer management, especially visual merchandising. In addition, we extend the scope of the emerging literature stream on the business value/impact of AI to shelf management and draw generalizable insights into the business scope to indicate how AI-based monitoring might generate incremental value.

## Human Attitudes Toward AI

The reluctance of human decision makers to use algorithms has been well documented (e.g., Dawes, 1979; Tambe et al., 2019). This tendency is referred to as “algorithm aversion” by Dietvorst et al. (2015). Other studies, such as Dijkstra (1999) and Dietvorst et al. (2018), have shown that humans are generally willing to use algorithms, even allowing machines to override their own potentially inferior decisions. This tendency is called “algorithm appreciation.”

Humans’ attitudes toward algorithms have recently been revisited, as a surge in the use of AI techniques has piqued people’s curiosity. Several studies have demonstrated the existence of “AI appreciation.” For example, Fuegener et al. (2022) undertook several lab experiments via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and demonstrated that humans have no reluctance to use AI to classify images. According to Logg et al. (2019), humans appreciate AI judgments in numeric forecasts and in dating and music recommendations more than those made by humans. Similarly, Kim et al. (2021) found that humans (e.g., tutors) with a large number of tasks and a high level of task complexity often seek AI-based assistance. In contrast to these findings, there also exists widespread evidence of “AI aversion.” For example, Logg et al. (2019) found that individuals prefer their own judgments to those of AI. Despite the potential usefulness of AI assistance, Kim et al. (2021) discovered that some people may choose not to utilize it, resulting in no substantial change in their service outcomes. According to Liang and Xue (2022), physicians’ perceived face loss is positively associated with their resistance to AI systems.

The impact of AI assistance on delegates’ monitoring tasks is not straightforward. If delegates choose not to rely on AI assistance even when it is made available, we would expect no major change in sales following the launch of our IIPpowered shelf monitoring system. In contrast, if delegates embrace AI because they believe it will assist them with their monitoring tasks, we may observe a huge boost in sales. Therefore, using a large-scale field experiment, our study attempts to examine delegates’ attitudes toward AI-based regulatory assistance in the retailing industry.

## Monitoring

Monitoring, defined as the implementation of observation systems that gather information about individuals’ activity or tasks (Ranganathan & Benson, 2017), has been empirically studied in several contexts, including healthcare and production lines, among others (e.g., Staats et al., 2016; Ranganathan & Benson, 2017; Hubbard, 2000). The effect of monitoring on process compliance captured in previous studies is mostly positive. For example, Staats et al. (2016) found that individual electronic monitoring can dramatically improve caregivers’ dispenser usage. Hubbard (2000) provided evidence showing that monitoring in onboard computers can improve truckers’ driving performance. Similarly, Nagin et al. (2002) argued that monitoring can decrease the rate at which employees shirk because the perceived cost of opportunistic behavior is increased. Duflo et al. (2012) found that monitoring can reduce teacher absenteeism. Further, Pierce et al. (2015) showed that monitoring can not only improve the quality of customer service but also reduce employee theft.

In the labor-intensive retail industry, shelf management requires retailers’ compliance with contracts, for which monitoring is needed. Such monitoring used to be executed by humans and may be biased (Ibanez & Toffel, 2019); however, thanks to improved technology, the process of monitoring can be automated. For example, a radio frequency identification (RFID)-based system could be deployed to automatically monitor caregivers’ hand hygiene compliance (Staats et al., 2016). Similar RFID systems have been adopted to monitor inventory record inaccuracy (Heese, 2007). AI could also be utilized to facilitate the automation of monitoring.

However, the role of AI in automated monitoring is not clear. We extend this literature by providing real-world empirical evidence that captures the positive impact of IIP-powered monitoring in retailing, thereby showing that the incremental value of IIP-powered monitoring comes from its improved scalability when the objects being monitored are more heterogeneous.

Additionally, in behavior analysis, the persistence of behavioral interventions is worth exploring. In particular, some interventions lead to long-term changes in behavior that can persist even after interventions are terminated, while other interventions result in only short-term behavioral changes that disappear once the interventions cease (Frey & Rogers, 2014). Practically, it is critical to analyze behavioral persistence and thus the long-term sustainability of the IIPpowered shelf monitoring system because the introduction of this AI-assisted system incurs a huge fixed cost. Theoretically, the persistence effect of the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system is not straightforward. On the one hand, once IIP-powered shelf monitoring is terminated, retailers’ sales may drop below the control level because the implementation of the AI system could crowd out retailers’ internal motivation for complying (e.g., Charness & Gneezy, 2009; Staats et al., 2016). On the other hand, retailers’ sales may persist yet degrade over time after the discontinuation of IIP-powered monitoring (e.g., Allcott & Rogers, 2014; Charness & Gneezy, 2009; Ferraro et al., 2011).

Through a large-scale field experiment, we contribute to this stream of literature by inspecting the persistence of IIPpowered monitoring on retailers’ compliance behaviors. Since the additional monitoring effort typically incurs costs, the persistence of the effect could determine how manufacturers should apply IIP-powered monitoring in the long term (e.g., whether there is any need to continuously enforce such monitoring).

## Shelf Management

Shelf management is the practice of managing existing shelf space, which includes deciding on the amount of space to allocate to an item and determining the location of the item on a shelf. Existing operations research has focused on optimal product selection and shelf space allocation on the retailers’ side (e.g., Anderson & Amato, 1974; Hansen & Heinsbroek, 1979; Corstjens & Doyle, 1981). Specifically, numerous optimization models have been developed for retailers, subject to constraints like store capacity (e.g., Corstjens & Doyle, 1981), product availability (e.g., Bultez & Naert, 1988; Lim et al., 2004), and strategic interactions between retailers and manufacturers (e.g., Amrouche & Zaccour, 2007; Kuo & Yang, 2013). Furthermore, the literature has proposed solutions to help retailers deal with shelves that are out of stock (OOS) and inventory record inaccuracy (IIPI), such as using third-party audits (Chuang et al., 2016), utilizing RFID (Heese, 2007) and implementing information-sensitive replenishment systems (Mersereau, 2013).

In fact, shelf management (i.e., managing products on retailers’ shelves) is also crucial for manufacturers since shelf display can greatly affect product sales (e.g., Cox, 1970;

Curhan, 1972) and thus manufacturers’ revenue. For example, Frank and Massy (1970) showed that for manufacturers that produce frequently purchase and brand grocery products, the number of product facings on retailers’ shelves can significantly impact sales. Likewise, Nierop et al. (2008) argued that shelf layout (i.e., location and number of facings) have both a direct and indirect impact on sales. Further, Cox (1970) found that additional shelf space allocated to a manufacturer could lead to higher product sales. However, this rule is not applied to manufacturers that produce staple products and impulse brands with low consumer acceptance.

Prior studies have advanced our understanding of shelf management from the retailers’ perspective and also illustrated the importance of shelf management for manufacturers. However, much remains to be understood regarding how manufacturers should effectively manage their products on retailers’ shelves. Our research adds additional insights with respect to shelf management from the manufacturers’ point of view by recommending that IIPpowered shelf monitoring systems be used to increase retailers’ compliance rates regarding shelf display.

## Research Context

## Background

We collaborated with a leading multinational food product manufacturer with headquarters in the E.U. Its product line includes specialized nutrition, waters, and beverages, as well as dairy and plant-based products, all of which are FMCGs. The manufacturer follows the standard practices of retailing in the FMCG industry. In particular, because FMCGs are typically pushed spatially close enough to potential consumers to achieve market penetration, manufacturers build a largescale retail network consisting of an enormous set of brickand-mortar retail stores, including delis, convenience stores, grocery stores, supermarkets, and so forth, all of which are evenly and densely distributed in every market.

To sell products in retail stores, our manufacturer negotiates and enacts shelf space rental contracts with each retailer (e.g., chainlevel merchandise managers or store owners). The contract clearly specifies the quantity of products to be displayed, the portion of the shelf rented to display those particular products, and penalties for noncompliance.<sup>2</sup> To ensure that the shelf space rental contract is executed properly, a large number of delegates are utilized to inspect the shelf display. The delegates are fulltime employees of our focal manufacturer who are only responsible for our collaborative manufacturer and the products it manufactures. Also, the delegates serve as important connections between the manufacturer and retailers. For example, delegates need to check whether the shelf space rental contract is being properly enforced. If any noncompliance is found, delegates can provide retailers with verbal feedback and impose penalties. In addition to shelf management, their responsibilities also include visiting stores, delivering ordered products to stores, taking orders, and collecting feedback from retailers on (at least) a weekly basis. Each delegate is responsible for approximately 150 retail stores (e.g., independent stores, chain grocery stores, chain supermarkets) in a specific geographic area.

Over the past century, human-based monitoring has been a standard practice that has been shown to be effective in mature markets in Europe and North America. However, due to differences in market structure, manufacturers are now finding it challenging to replicate this conventional practice in emerging markets such as Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Retail structures in emerging markets are more fragmented and decentralized than those in mature ones. In emerging markets, manufacturers largely rely on independent retail stores owned by different owners to distribute their products, rather than chain stores, which are primary players in mature markets. Unlike chain stores, which have a unified shelf condition and can thus sign homogeneous contracts for product displays with manufacturers, these independent retail stores have a higher degree of heterogeneity in shelf conditions (e.g., shelf layout, shelf availability). Thus, a contract is uniquely specified for each independent store.

The heterogeneity of the contracts makes the traditional practice of shelf monitoring potentially challenging. When contracts are more homogeneous, a delegate’s examination might gain some scalability because the next display might be comparable to the current one. The more shelves a delegate checks, the faster the next monitoring will be, indicating a marginally diminished time cost. Indeed, an experienced delegate could quickly check a shelf in a short amount of time; however, when contracts exhibit heterogeneity across retailers, delegates must handle each store independently, indicating a linearly increasing time cost with a growing number of stores. As a result, shelf monitoring undertaken solely by delegates in emerging markets may be less scalable. Because the number of stores for delegates to monitor is similar to that in mature markets, delegates in emerging markets facing the same time constraint might be hard-pressed to fulfill the same monitoring task as their counterparts do in established markets.

Because of insufficient monitoring, retailers who behave like rational cheaters (Nagin et al., 2002) might not comply with the standards specified in contracts for the following reasons. First, it takes effort for retailers to comply with shelf space rental contracts. Second, retailers have different utility functions from those of our manufacturer. For example, in our study, our manufacturer only cares about the sales of its focal products. However, retailers want to maximize sales for all products in the same category by optimizing product placement. Third, when shelf monitoring is not sufficiently effective, noncompliance behaviors can be easily ignored, which will not result in any penalties to retailers. If retailers fail to execute product display plans as specified in contracts, the sales of products by the manufacturer could be negatively affected.

To facilitate shelf monitoring in emerging markets, the manufacturer decided to explore the opportunities afforded by an IIP-powered shelf monitoring program that is based on a mobile app. <sup>3</sup> This app is installed on delegates’ company/working smartphones and no CCTV infrastructure is required in any store by this system. Only delegates (rather than store owners) can use this mobile app (i.e., IIP-powered shelf monitoring system) to take photos of shelves while visiting the (treatment) stores for which they are responsible. In addition to taking real-time photos of shelves, this app can also be used to upload photos to cloud storage such that a manufacturer’s products can be automatically detected. The detection, fulfilled by an image recognition model using AI-based computer vision technology, also identifies the number and location of each product. With this IIP-powered shelf monitoring system, AI helps delegates supervise retailers’ shelf displays, which provides the manufacturer with a direct channel to monitor its rented shelves.

The introduction of IIP-powered shelf monitoring changes the process of shelf management. In particular, delegates must no longer manually check to see whether a shelf display is complying with a contract. Instead, the delegate can use the app to take shelf photos and determine retailers’ compliance based on AI-generated reports. The sketch of AI reports is provided in Table 1. At the same time, the store owners/retailers can realize changes in the monitoring process by observing delegates’ visits and practices in their stores or by responding to changed feedback from delegates about their compliance. The manufacturer expects that through the assistance of AI, delegates can increase their capacity for shelf monitoring under time constraints, thereby making retailers more likely to display products as specified in their contracts with the manufacturer, all of which would be expected to lead to better sales.

## Research Stages

Since IIP-powered monitoring is relatively new, there is no existing anecdotal or academic evidence regarding its effectiveness. The manufacturer is thus seeking the help of researchers to understand its incremental value. The investigation of the effectiveness of IIP-powered shelf monitoring programs consists of three stages. We briefly explain the context of each of these stages as follows.

Stage 1: We note that prior to our participation in a field experiment, the manufacturer applied IIP-powered shelf monitoring in a group of selected retail stores. Therefore, we first collected the corresponding data from this period and empirically investigated the effect of IIP-powered shelf monitoring based on a quasi-experiment. In the pilot study, the manufacturer selected around 2,500 retail stores in six cities in China, one of its emerging markets, to implement this IIP-powered shelf monitoring program. The selection of treated stores was based on certain criteria (e.g., store location, gross floor area), and the majority of treatment stores were independent retail stores (e.g., delis, grocery stores), rather than chain stores (e.g., supermarkets). From the manufacturer’s perspective, the monitoring of independent retail stores was more worthwhile because the disorganized shelf displays that are often characteristic of independent retail stores made it more difficult to monitor without the help of AI. In April 2019, the delegates who were responsible for the selected treatment stores were required to use the app, which was built by the manufacturer, to take time-stamped shelf photos once a month. Given that one delegate usually oversees around 150 stores, the delegate could be simultaneously in charge of both treatment and control stores. With respect to the shelf monitoring of control stores, delegates continued their conventional practice (i.e., human-based shelf monitoring).

Stage 2: Although quasi-experiments are powerful inferential tools, they are nonetheless subject to several limitations. For example, such experiments do not feature actual randomization of treatment and control group assignment, which can thus undermine the validity of causal inference in observational settings (Dunning, 2008). To precisely gauge the causal impact of IIP-powered shelf monitoring, we conducted a field experiment with the manufacturer. The field experiment further enabled us to investigate the impact of IIP-powered shelf monitoring at a deeper level. In particular, to better understand the persistence of AI assistance, we created an experimental group with stores that were no longer monitored by AI after some time. We note that control stores and stores that discontinued IIP monitoring still received the conventional human-based monitoring. The field experiment included around 3,800 retailers randomly selected from our manufacturer’s retailing network in six major cities in China and lasted from May 2019 to August 2019. The details of the experiment setup, randomization, and data collection were all explained in the Stage 2: Field Experiment section.

Stage 3: We conducted our mechanism analysis in four phases. First, we extended our models in prior stages by considering the heterogeneity of store types. Second, we studied the change of retailers’ compliance to complete our casual chain between AI assistance and improved sales. Third, we teased out the effect of only taking photos. Finally, following Venkatesh et al. (2013), we conducted a qualitative study by interviewing delegates and retailers.

## Role of AI

Since the amount of allocated space and the position of products on shelves are well established as important determinants of sales (e.g., Cox, 1970; Curhan, 1972; Frank & Massy, 1970; Nierop et al., 2008) and comprise the two main aspects of shelf space rental contracts, our manufacturer used the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system and applied the following IIP techniques to extract these two qualitative metrics from shelf photos. <sup>4</sup> We note that this system implements machine perception and automates delegates’ decision-making processes and can thus be regarded as an AI-assisted system.

Number of facings: The concept of “facing” in retail refers to products that are placed at the front edge of a shelf with the brand label turned forward. For each photo, the number of facings is identified through the following steps. First, the app gathers substantial 360-degree product photos of our focal brand and others. Second, all product photos are transformed into constructs that can be logically analyzed by the computer using a feature detection algorithm. Third, the app trains a neural network with thousands of relevant photos and then obtains a classification model. Finally, delegates’ shelf photos are fed into this classification model to recognize our focal products and then calculate the number of facings. A larger number of facings indicates that more space is allocated to the brand.

Position on the shelf: Given that products on lower shelves usually receive less consumer attention than those placed at eye level, our manufacturer wanted to examine the position of products on shelves in each shelf photo. The position of a product is measured by the vertical distance between the product and the ground floor. In general, if the product is placed at a height of 120-180 cm (i.e., 48-70 inches), it is considered to be in compliance with the manufacturer’s shelf display standards. We collected information about the product’s position on the shelf through the following steps. First, we trained a neural network to recognize tiers of shelves. Second, after identifying our focal products, the app was then employed to calculate the shelf number upon which the product was placed. Third, we inferred the location of the product based on both the shelf height and product height.

## Stage 1: Quasi-Experiment

We leveraged the manufacturer-conducted quasi-experiment to estimate the impact of IIP-powered shelf monitoring on product sales.

## Data

Our manufacturer provided store-level data from January 2017 to August 2019, which covered the period of our quasiexperiment. The data set contained information pertaining to each retail store, including the monthly sales of each specific product in the product line of the manufacturer, store location (i.e., latitude and longitude), gross floor area, the number of cashiers, whether the store employed a point-of-sale (POS) system, and whether the store provided shopping baskets or carts. Further, for the stores that were monitored by the IIPpowered tool, we had access to shelf photo-level data, which contained a photo’s time stamp, the number of facings, and the shelf location of the focal product.

In total, the dataset included records from 42,097 retail stores in the observation window. The quasi-experiment was conducted during the entirety of April 2019 and 2,217 stores were treated with the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program; the remaining stores continued to follow conventional human-based shelf monitoring as before. The dataset was constructed into a panel structure at the store level such that each observation was a retail store, and each time period was a month. Our main dependent variable Log(Sales)<sub>it</sub> denotes the log-transformed focal product sales in Chinese Yuan (¥) for store i in month t. Meanwhile, our independent variable of interest is the treatment indicator, Monitoring<sub>it</sub>, which indicates the status of the IIP monitoring program for store i in month t. Later in this paper, we discuss the propensity score matching (PSM) scheme we used to refine our data and then provide descriptive statistics of our data after we completed the PSM process.

## Identification Strategy

We note that the retail stores that the manufacturer selected as treatment stores may be significantly different from other control stores. Therefore, we used the propensity score matching (PSM) method to create a group of treatment stores that began IIPpowered shelf monitoring in April 2019 (“treatment group”) and a group of control stores that were similar to the ones in the treatment group but that did not adopt IIP monitoring (“control group”). By doing so, we structured our data to mimic a controlled experiment, which allowed us to control for multiple confounders and tease out the causal effects of IIP-powered shelf monitoring more precisely. Following that, we used the difference-in-differences (DID) technique as the regression specification for our quasi-experimental setting. This approach is typically used to estimate the effect of an exogenous treatment by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between treatment and control groups (e.g., Chan & Ghose, 2013). DID is suitable in our research context because we fundamentally observed a quasi-experimental design in which the treatment (i.e., IIP-powered shelf monitoring) was applied to a subset of retail stores, whereas many other control stores did not receive this treatment. For treatment stores, product sales were expected to be affected by AI assistance after the launch of this program; in contrast, there was no such effect for control stores. In addition, the sales data also covered a long period prior to the treatment for all stores in both the treatment and control groups.

## Propensity Score Matching

As previously mentioned, the selection of treatment stores that adopt IIP monitoring was not purely exogenous. Since the manufacturer selected treatment stores based on several store characteristics, treatment stores may have been different from their control counterparts. Therefore, we used PSM to eliminate systematic differences between the treatment group and the control group (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). Essentially, each store that launched the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program was paired with a control store that was similar to the treated one in terms of its probability of being treated, which allowed for a fair comparison between these two groups of retail stores. Because store characteristics rarely change over time, we utilized static matching (Xu et al., 2016) in our study. We first approximated store $\dot { \textbf { \textit { i } } } \mathbf { s }$ overall propensity score as a logistic function of a vector of store characteristics. The covariates in the vector consist of static characteristics, including store location (i.e., latitude and longitude), gross floor area, the number of cashiers, whether the store employs a point-of-sales (POS) system, and whether the store provides shopping baskets or carts. These variables cover most of the observable and recorded characteristics of a store to the manufacturer. Our baseline matching utilized oneto-one matching with replacement to derive the closest matched control store.<sup>5</sup> Finally, we obtained 2,217 pairs of comparable stores with 2,217 treatment stores and 584 unique control stores in total. Summary statistics are provided in Table 2. In total, our dataset comprises $^ { 7 8 , 4 2 8 }$ observations for 2,217 treatment stores and 584 control stores from January 2017 to April 2019. To evaluate the success of PSM, we show summary statistics for the treatment group and control group respectively in Table 3.

## Difference-in-Differences

Next, we utilize the difference-in-differences approach to uncover the causal effects of IIP-powered shelf monitoring on store sales. In particular, our model specification for the quasi-experiment setting is:

$$
L o g (S a l e s) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \beta M o n i t o r i n g _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

in which $L o g ( S a l e s ) _ { i t }$ refers to the log-transformed focal product sales of store i in month t. The independent variable of interest is the treatment indicator, Monitoring<sub>it</sub>, which is one if store i is in the treatment group and month t is after the launch of IIP-powered shelf monitoring (i.e., April 2019). To account for the time-invariant, unobserved factors (e.g., store-level competition) that are specific to the retail store and that may affect product ${ \mathrm { s a l e s } } ^ { 6 } ,$ we incorporated a store fixed effects term ${ \bf { q } } _ { i } .$ To account for the time-variant unobservable factors (e.g., seasonality) that affect all retail stores, we included a time fixed effects term γ<sub>t</sub>. $\epsilon _ { i t }$ represents the error term. This specification identifies the impact of IIPpowered shelf monitoring on focal product sales by the key parameter β. To reduce heteroscedasticity concerns, we leveraged robust standard errors clustered at the store level (Bertrand et al., 2004).

## Relative Time Model

The validity of our previous identification strategy that leveraged the matching and DID model relies critically on the pre-treatment parallel trend assumption (i.e., that there is no significant difference between treatment and control stores before the treatment). To test this assumption, we utilized the relative time model with lead and lag periods (Autor, 2003). With this model, we added a series of time dummies that indicated the relative chronological distance between observation time and treatment time. Intuitively, this model enabled us to not only measure the effect of treatment over time (i.e., both before and after the treatment is applied) but also to determine whether a pre-treatment trend exists. The specification of our relative time model is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {L o g (S a l e s) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \sum_ {j} \tau_ {j} P r e _ {i t} (j) +} \\ & {\sum_ {l} w _ {l} P o s t _ {i t} (l) + \epsilon_ {i t},} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

in which $L o g ( S a l e s ) _ { i t }$ represents our focal product sales for store i in month $t , a _ { i }$ and $\gamma _ { t }$ denote store fixed effects and time fixed effects, respectively. The newly added term $P r e _ { i t } ( j )$ is an indicator function that equals one if month t is j month(s) prior to the treatment. Similarly, the term $P o s t _ { i t } ( l )$ is an indicator function that equals one if month t is l month(s) after the launch of the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program. To estimate all these effects, we gathered all pretreatment periods that were greater than or equal to six months prior to treatment into one dummy. Hence, the coefficient τ<sub>j</sub> for $j \ge - 6 , - 5 , \ldots , - 1$ captures the pre-treatment trend of our focal product sales while the coefficient ω<sub>l</sub> for l = 0 captures the effect of shelf monitoring in a post-treatment period. Consistent with prior work, we set a period prior to the time of treatment as the baseline by normalizing the coefficient of $P r e _ { i t } ( - I )$ to zero.

## Stage 2: Field Experiment

Although the prior quasi-experiment provides an estimate of the causal effect of IIP-powered shelf monitoring on sales, the robustness of our finding is subject to confounding bias (e.g., unobserved time-varying, store-specific factors) because we didn’t randomly assign retail stores to either treatment or control groups. The validity of our causal inference is therefore under question (Dunning, 2008). To fundamentally address such concerns, we executed a randomized field experiment with the manufacturer to precisely estimate the causal effect of IIPpowered shelf monitoring on sales.

In addition to checking the robustness of our previous finding, we further leveraged the field experiment opportunity to investigate the effect’s persistence and thus the program’s longterm sustainability. In reality, additional monitoring effort often comes at a cost. Therefore, a monitoring program with persistence, such as that derived from the habit forming of the monitored object (e.g., Charness & Gneezy, 2009; Allcott & Rogers, 2014), indicates that a policy maker may reduce or even eliminate the enforcement of monitoring policy over time, which could minimize costs and make operations more sustainable, especially when additional costs associated with program enforcement are high. On the other hand, a monitoring program that has a small or no persistence effect or that has a negative effect once the program is discontinued (e.g., Gneezy & List, 2006; Ferraro et al., 2011; Staats et al., 2016) needs constant enforcement, perhaps even with increasing intensity.

To see whether IIP-powered monitoring necessitates constant implementation and thus requires long-term expenses, we further explored the persistence effect over the long run. In particular, we examined the product sales of retailers who were only treated by the program for a given period and then stopped using AI. On the one hand, if the retailer’s internal motivation of complying is crowded out (e.g., Charness & Gneezy, 2009; Staats et al., 2016), we could expect that removing IIP monitoring would return sales to where they were before the treatment, if not lower. On the other hand, if retailers’ sales persist yet degrade over time (e.g., Allcott & Rogers, 2014; Ferraro et al., 2011), the discontinuation of IIP monitoring should have little or no immediate impact on product sales.

Therefore, we conducted a field experiment with the cooperation of the manufacturer. Specifically, using a large network of retailers in six cities in China whose delegates are equipped with the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program, we first randomly assigned 3,808 eligible subjects (i.e., retail stores) into one of three experimental groups, as presented in Table 6. The two treatment groups were differentiated by the launch and termination of IIP-powered shelf monitoring. Throughout the experiment, 1,230 stores were assigned to a control group that used human-based monitoring and never received any IIP-powered shelf monitoring. The other 2,578 stores were assigned to the two treatment groups. In May 2019, we told the delegates of the 2,578 treatment stores to begin applying the program. In June 2019, we informed the delegates of 60 randomly selected treatment stores to terminate the IIP monitoring program and switch back to human-based monitoring, while IIP monitoring continued undisturbed in the remaining 2,518 treatment stores. The sample size of the group that terminated the IIP monitoring (i.e., Group 2) was smaller than the group that continued the IIP monitoring (i.e., Group 1) because of the manufacturer’s concern that the frequent requests for changed practices might spark resistance among delegates. That said, other than the change in shelf monitoring, everything else remained the same.

## Data

We tracked monthly sales for all 3,808 retail stores from May 2019 to August 2019. We used these data to examine how sales changed after the launch and termination of IIP-powered shelf monitoring, which indicated a change in retailers’ compliance over the long term. Here, we set one of the independent indicator variables RemoveMonitoring<sub>it</sub> equal to one if the treatment store i terminated the IIP monitoring by month t; otherwise, it equals zero. For the remaining treatment stores that continued IIP monitoring before their final observations in our data and control stores, RemoveMonitoring always equals zero. We provide summary statistics in Table 7. In total, the data set consisted of 15,232 observations for 3,808 retail stores (1,230 stores in Group 0; 2,518 stores in Group 1; 60 stores in Group 2) from May 2019 to August 2019.

## Identification Strategy

The announcement of either terminating or continuing IIPpowered shelf monitoring was made in a single day at the same time. This field experiment allowed us to create an exogenous shock to retailers’ compliance behavior and thus product sales within a very short period.<sup>8</sup> The identification of the causal effect is therefore straightforward.

## Terminate vs. Continue IIP-powered Shelf Monitoring

To estimate the average treatment effect of IIP monitoring termination on product sales, we applied the following regression model on the observations in Group 1 and Group 2:

$$
L o g (S a l e s) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \beta R e m o v e M o n i t o r i n g _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t},\tag{3}
$$

in which the dependent variable is the log of the volume of product sales for store i in month t. Our key exogenous variable is the termination of the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program, which is represented by the treatment indicator RemoveMonitoring. If RemoveMonitoring<sub>it</sub> equals one, it means that the store i terminated IIP monitoring by month t; if RemoveMonitoring<sub>it</sub> equals zero, it means that the store i continued IIP monitoring in month t. Its coefficient $\beta$ can be interpreted as the change in product sales caused by terminating IIP monitoring, compared with the product sales of the stores that continued IIP monitoring. We also included a store fixed effect α<sub>i</sub> and a month fixed effect γ<sub>t</sub> as controls for unobserved factors.

Our experimental IIP-powered shelf monitoring termination guarantees that the treatment indicator RemoveMonitoring<sub>it</sub> is uncorrelated with the residual $\epsilon _ { i t }$ and that the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator will satisfy the assumption of conditional mean independence, which is needed for convergence to the true parameter. Additionally, regressions can produce estimates with higher efficiency than those obtained from pairwise t-tests (Duflo et al., 2007).

To better capture the impact of the termination of IIP-powered shelf monitoring, we further compared product sales in treatment stores that ceased IIP monitoring activities with those in the control group (i.e., those that kept using humanbased monitoring and never launched IIP monitoring) by including all the observations of Group 0, 1, and 2. Building on Equation (1), we added an additional variable, RemoveMonitoring, to examine how product sales changed in response to the termination of IIP monitoring and then compared it with control group stores:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & L o g (S a l e s) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \beta_ {1} M o n i t o r i n g _ {i t} + \\ & \beta_ {2} R e m o v e M o n i t o r i n g _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The specification is identical to that for Equation (1) except that we include two indicator variables, Monitoring and RemoveMonitoring, to identify the effects of launching and terminating IIP-powered shelf monitoring separately. Monitoring equals one when IIP monitoring is active and zero when it is not. The indicator variable RemoveMonitoring equals one if the store was treated and is now removed from the IIP monitoring list; otherwise, RemoveMonitoring equals zero. Thus, RemoveMonitoring captures the OLS estimates for the differences in product sales between the control group stores and the Group 2 stores when their IIP-powered shelf monitoring is discontinued. The key parameters of interest in this equation are $\beta _ { I }$ and $\beta _ { 2 } .$

## Stage 3: Mechanism Analysis

Thus far, our analysis does not provide insights as to why product sales are boosted with the introduction of IIPpowered shelf monitoring. In this section, we attempt to explain several underlying mechanisms of the observed effect using four phases. First, we verified a proposed hypothetical explanation by extending our empirical analysis to find the differentiated treatment effects considering retail stores with different degrees of contract heterogeneity. Second, we utilized shelf photos to show the change in retailers’ compliance behaviors. After that, we teased out the effect of only taking photos. Finally, we adopted a qualitative research approach (i.e., interviews with delegates and retailers) to further examine our proposed mechanisms.

## Effect by Types of Retail Stores

Our analyses consistently show a positive average effect of the launch of IIP-powered shelf monitoring on product sales. We propose that this positive effect stems from delegates’ enhanced ability to monitor more heterogeneous shelf conditions enabled by the scalability of AI.

As compared to that of human beings, AI has exceptional working memory and tremendous calculation speed. Therefore, AI is capable of surpassing humans’ abilities to process routine tasks. For example, Luo et al. (2019) found that AI chatbots are four times more effective than inexperienced workers in engendering customer purchases. In addition, AI has been shown to be efficient in processing heterogeneous instances (McKendrick, 2018). In short, AI would be expected to competently manage repetitive tasks with heterogeneous instances.

Shelf monitoring can be considered routine work since the monitoring task requires simply comparing the shelf display with the contract terms. In addition, as we describe in our research context, shelf monitoring in emerging markets must respond to more heterogeneous contracts among many independent retail stores. We hypothesize that AI could exceed human performance in coping with more heterogeneous shelf conditions.

Specifically, we expect that given the time constraint and a fixed number of shelf monitoring tasks, human-based monitoring might be as effective as AI for more homogeneous contracts, whereas IIP-powered monitoring might process heterogeneous contracts more efficiently than humans. This expectation might be explained by the scalability of AI: since the AI-based model is pre-trained, the marginal cost of handling a different contract might be minimal; however, a human delegate might need more time to learn and process the information when faced with a new contract that differs from earlier ones. The marginal cost of monitoring a new contract is thus linear. As a result, when facing time constraints and heterogeneous contracts, humanbased monitoring may be coarse and inadequate, which gives retailers the opportunity not to comply with contracts. This may further lead to unmet sales targets, as stipulated in contracts. In contrast, AI empowers delegates to fulfill shelf monitoring for heterogeneous contracts in a limited time, making retailers more likely to enforce contracts, which would likely increase product sales.

According to this proposed underlying mechanism, we expect that the positive effect of launching IIP-powered shelf monitoring is mostly attributed to retail stores with more heterogeneous shelf conditions (e.g., independent stores). Because chain stores are associated with a homogeneity of contracts, human monitoring could potentially perform as well in such stores because of the lower marginal time cost for an additional (albeit familiar and unified) contract. Therefore, we might expect either an insignificant or significant effect but less magnitude for IIP-powered shelf monitoring on the sales of stores with more homogeneous shelf conditions.

To test our proposed mechanism explanation, we used data from stores in our randomized field experiment and estimated the differential impact of IIP-powered shelf monitoring on product sales in stores with different types of shelf conditions. In particular, we followed the standards determined by our manufacturer and divided stores into three general groups, based on their degrees of contract heterogeneity (i.e., low, middle, and high). The classification standard is related to certain business characteristics, such as store type (i.e., chain or independent) and the scale of stores. For example, large-scale chain stores (e.g., Walmart) or nationwide convenience stores (e.g., 7-Eleven) are considered to have low degrees of heterogeneity in terms of shelf space rental contracts. Typically, medium-sizes local stores or regional chain stores with fewer than five branches are considered to have a middle level of contract heterogeneity. Finally, small-scale independent stores (e.g., street vendors) have the most heterogeneous contracts.

According to this classification, we added two interaction terms (i.e., Monitoring×Middle and Monitoring×High) and replicated the DID model to capture the heterogeneous effects regarding the store types. Specifically, our model specification is:

$$

in which all the specifications follow those in Equation (1), except that we include two indicator variables Middle<sub>i</sub> and High<sub>i</sub>, which equal 1 when store i belongs to a specific store type (i.e., stores with middle-/high-level contract heterogeneity).

For this analysis, we only focused on stores in Group 0 (i.e., stores that never launched the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system) and Group 1 (i.e., stores that have continued using IIP monitoring since May 2019). Our study period is from Jan 2017 to Aug 2019. Therefore, we have 119,936 observations from

3,748 stores in total. We report our results in Table 12. As seen, the introduction of AI assistance has no significant impact on stores with lower levels of contract heterogeneity. More importantly, the impact of IIP-powered shelf monitoring becomes larger as the degree of contract heterogeneity increases. This result further bolsters our claim that AI is better than humans at facilitating the shelf management of more heterogeneous contracts. The main reason for this is that stores with more homogeneous contracts (e.g., chain stores) are more likely to comply with shelf space rental contracts even without AI assistance. This explanation is theoretically possible. More specifically, we believe that human-based monitoring can be as effective as IIP-powered monitoring for more homogeneous contracts. That is to say, human-based monitoring is relatively sufficient for stores like chain stores. Therefore, the introduction of AI assistance would not greatly affect chain stores’ compliance rates. However, human-based shelf monitoring may be insufficient for stores like independent stores. Since IIPpowered shelf monitoring can process heterogeneous contracts more efficiently than humans, independent stores’ compliance rates should improve after the launch of AI systems. Since the majority of retail stores in emerging markets are stores with high-level contract heterogeneity where no unified contracts can be signed, IIP-powered shelf monitoring would empower delegates in terms of scalability and expand the monitoring scope of delegates to heterogeneous retail stores, which should ultimately drive an incremental increase in sales.

## Change in Retailers’ Compliance

We believe that the observed increase in sales is mainly due to AI assistance because AI can lead to more effective monitoring by delegates; in turn, better monitoring would likely result in improved retailer compliance regarding shelf displays and thus improve sales. To better illustrate this causal chain from AI assistance to improved sales, we obtained retailers’ aggregated compliance rate prior to AI adoption from our manufacturer’s annual report. <sup>10</sup> Furthermore, we leveraged shelf photo-level data for the 2,518 treatment stores in Group 1 of the field experiment and considered the impact of IIP monitoring on retailers’ compliance behavior in terms of shelf display. To measure retailers’ compliance, we analyzed the output photos and reports of the AI system. In particular, we considered facing compliance rates, which reflect the ratio of the displayed number of products and the number required in the contract, as well as the position compliance rate, which indicates whether the position of the displayed products was carried out as required.

## Effect of Taking Photos

We further conducted a mechanism analysis to show that taking shelf photos only (i.e., not using AI) would fail to improve sales. To do so, we sought to identify the effect of a treatment wherein shelf photos would be taken, just as in the standard AI-assisted shelf monitoring contexts, but the photos would neither be processed by AI nor would the delegates receive an AIgenerated report.

Therefore, for this analyis, the delegates would monitor shelf displays without AI assistance, as they previously had (i.e., human-based monitoring); the only difference would be that they would take pictures. The difference between this treatment’s effect and that of the control group can be attributed to the effect of taking photos on shelves without AI analysis or the enabled IIP monitoring. We name this effect “the effect of taking photos only,” for brevity.

To investigate this effect, we initiated an additional field experiment with the cooperation of the manufacturer. Specifically, we randomly allocated 888 retail stores into one of three experimental groups, as shown in Table 14. The difference between the two treatment groups was whether delegates received AI-generated reports on the back end. Throughout the experiment, 772 stores were assigned to a control group that received human-based monitoring only and no IIP-powered shelf monitoring. The remaining 116 stores were split into two treatment groups. Starting in May 2021, delegates from 60 randomly chosen treatment stores utilized the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system to photograph shelves and receive AI-generated compliance reports. Delegates in the remaining 56 stores were unable to receive the AI-generated compliance reports for their IIP-powered shelf monitoring practices dating back to May 2021. As a result, these delegates were forced to monitor without AI assistance, just as they previously had (i.e., human-based monitoring), with the exception of taking pictures. We note that we were only able to use relatively small treatment groups (as compared to the control group) for this experiment because instructing delegates can be time-consuming. Also, our manufacturer was worried that running this experiment might be very costly.

We tracked monthly sales for all retail stores in this experiment and used these data to examine the effects of taking photos and using AI separately. We set one of the independent indicator variables Photo as equal to one if the treatment store i had taken shelf photos by month t; otherwise, it equaled zero. We had another independent indicator variable $A I _ { i t } ,$ with 0 indicating that no AI report was generated for store i by month t, and 1 indicating that an AI-generated compliance report of store i was shown to the corresponding delegate in month t.

## Interviews

Using a qualitative study approach, we offer supplementary evidence to help us uncover the underlying mechanisms.

## Interviews with Delegates

In August 2019, we conducted interviews with randomly sampled delegates who implemented IIP-powered shelf monitoring to draw additional insights with respect to mechanisms.

Procedure: More than 30 delegates who participated in the IIP-powered shelf monitoring program were randomly selected and interviewed over a five-day period. Before the interviews began, we carefully designed seven questions based on our discussion with the manufacturer and trained our research assistant (i.e., an employee from the manufacturer) to conduct phone interviews (Ou et al., 2014). The details of the interview procedure are as follows. First, our research assistant briefly outlined the expectations of this particular interview to participants (i.e., selected delegates). After this initial introduction, participants were asked to answer all interview questions and were also encouraged to share their thoughts by offering explicit explanations. The questions used in the interviews are summarized in Table 16. Such questions led participants to describe their opinions about AI assistance and IIP-powered shelf monitoring at work. The interviews were conducted individually, and each interview was recorded using a digital recorder and then transcribed. Finally, the research assistant sent us all completed records.

Results: We obtained responses from 28 delegates who answered either the first six or all questions in the interview. Our first question was related to the impact of AI assistance on shelf monitoring. A few delegates were unsatisfied with the current AI technology because the manufacturer had developed two separate mobile apps (one for taking orders and one for shelf monitoring) for delegates to use, which complicated their typical practice.

In their responses, 90% of delegates mentioned that AI-generated reports improved their capacity to identify retailers’ noncompliant behaviors, indicating that AI assistance does improve retailers’ compliance. This result confirms our claim that AI assistance would generate more incremental value when addressing more heterogeneous objects because of scalability.

## Interviews with Retailers

We also conducted interviews with retailers who received our treatment continuously. In particular, we physically visited hundreds of randomly chosen treatment stores in Group 1 of our main field experiment and spoke with store owners/retailers. During interviews, retailers were asked to answer several interview questions, which are summarized in Table 17.

We obtained valid responses from 112 retailers. In response to the first question, around 70% of retailers believed that the amount of time delegates spent in their stores did not change considerably as a result of AI implementation. Furthermore, nearly 80% of retailers pointed out that the amount of time delegates spent on shelf management and other tasks did not change after AI adoption. To sum up, the introduction of AI assistance did not lead to an improvement in delegates’ speed. However, this does not negate the fact that delegates are strapped for time under human-based monitoring and that AI is faster at detecting noncompliance behaviors. Although the change in delegates’ speed was insignificant, our results show that around 78% of retailers perceived an increase in delegates’ accuracy. That is to say, the improvement of monitoring efficacy can be reflected by more accurate feedback, which was made possible by AI’s accuracy in spotting noncompliance behaviors. From the retailer’s standpoint, changes in the monitoring process were imperceptible. In particular, more than 80% of retailers believed that they always received instantaneous compliance feedback from delegates. Retailers also discussed their reasons for complying. Around 80% of retailers acknowledged the important role of more accurate feedback, while 44% of retailers simply complied to avoid penalties. They also mentioned that they had learned about the importance of shelf displays.

In terms of the limitation of IIP-powered monitoring, over 85% of retailers said they did not consider the daily shelf management to be excessive. In reality, the degree of monitoring was far from excessive, as compared to that of daily shelf management.<sup>11</sup>

## Discussion

These interviews complemented our quantitative studies (i.e., quasi-experiment and field experiment) by providing evidence regarding plausible mechanisms behind the change in product sales caused by IIP-powered shelf monitoring. The use of AI assistance made it possible for delegates to provide more accurate feedback. The improved effectiveness of monitoring led to higher compliance rates among retailers, and improved product presentation can lead to improved sales. We consider this causal chain to be one possible mechanism for the observed outcome. In fact, our interview responses also demonstrated that retailers’ learning may be triggered by AI adoption, which indicates that AI assistance can improve sales by enabling human learning.

## Implications and Conclusion

## Cost-Benefit Analysis

Our findings provide important policy implications regarding shelf management in emerging markets. Our focal manufacturer, with markets in a number of developing countries, recently proposed and implemented the IIPpowered shelf monitoring system to improve shelf management. A key question is whether IIP-powered shelf monitoring is a more strategic monitoring strategy to implement in terms of profitability. To address this question, we conducted a cost-benefit analysis to present the economic efficiency of this system and provide estimates of net changes in profit.

The additional cost of developing and maintaining the IIPpowered shelf monitoring system is not small, amounting to roughly 155,000 USD per year for the use of all delegates in our focal market.<sup>12</sup> According to our estimation result from our field experiment in Table 11, the launch of IIP-powered shelf monitoring could increase product sales by 17%. This estimation is based on the DID design in a four-month period in China. Therefore, without additional assumptions, our estimates should be interpreted as the short-term average treatment effect in the Chinese market. To construct a costbenefit analysis for longer terms and for other emerging markets, we must assume that the coefficients can be extrapolated to out-of-sample time windows and markets. Because this is an untestable assumption in this study, we want to emphasize that the benefit we provide here should be interpreted cautiously. To learn how much benefit could be gained by this AI monitoring system, we must know the yearly sales of our focal product in the Chinese market. For this analysis, we used the total sales in 2020 (i.e., around 160 million USD) to obtain the total benefit of implementing this policy of 27.2 million USD. Finally, we used this number as the benefit of the policy to calculate the benefit-cost ratio (≈ 175.48). We note that our benefit estimate is likely to be a lower-bound estimate because our focal product experienced a slowdown in consumption in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, the benefit-cost ratio is also likely to be a lower-bound estimate. Our result suggests that the IIPpowered shelf monitoring system is a profitable policy, even with our lower-bound estimate of the policy’s benefit.

## Conclusion

Collaborating with our manufacturer, we use three investigative steps (i.e., quasi-experiment, field experiment, and mechanism analysis) to explore the business value of using AI-based shelf monitoring. We show that the introduction of IIP-powered shelf monitoring leads to a significant performance upgrade for delegates who manage shelves, which results in retailers’ improved compliance regarding shelf display. The improvement of retailers’ compliance gives rise to a 14%-17% increase in product sales, which we show is associated with stores with high contract heterogeneity (e.g., independent stores) rather than stores with low contract heterogeneity (e.g., chain stores). This observation indicates that the scalability of AI can expand the monitoring scope to stores with more heterogeneous contracts. In addition, we observe that, after the IIP monitoring was terminated, product sales dropped; even then, however, sales remained higher than original levels when only humans monitored shelves. These results indicate the partially persistent program effect and the existence of human learning. We provide two plausible explanations for such a positive change in product sales. Although IIP-powered shelf monitoring was terminated, the implementation of AI assistance made it possible for retailers to realize the manufacturer’s ability to validate shelf management, and delegates learned from the use of AIgenerated compliance reports.

This research makes a substantial contribution to both research and practice. We contribute to the extant literature on the business value of AI by extending the scope to shelf management and providing evidence that AI can be designed to complement human processes. We also add to the literature on human-AI interactions by identifying “AI appreciation” in the retailing industry. Further, we explore the business circumstances under which IIP-powered shelf monitoring is more appropriate than human-based monitoring, due to its greater scalability. More broadly, we explore the business scope under which AI assistance generates incremental economic value/impact. In addition, we contribute to the literature on monitoring by extending the context to retailing, and contribute to the literature on behavioral persistence by considering the change in product sales not only after the launch of IIP-powered shelf monitoring and also following the termination of such monitoring. In so doing, we help manufacturers understand how to best implement AI assistance (e.g., shelf monitoring in our context) on a longer time horizon. Lastly, our study contributes to the literature on shelf management by focusing on the manufacturer’s side.

Our research also provides several valuable managerial insights for practitioners, especially FMCG manufacturers. First, the incremental value of IIP-powered shelf monitoring indicates that practitioners could leverage AI to manage retailers’ shelf displays, especially when retailers face a high degree of contract heterogeneity. Such heterogeneous retail stores not only account for the majority in emerging markets but also exist in some developed markets as well. Second, our insights regarding the persistence of IIP-powered shelf monitoring as well as our cost-benefit analysis could help practitioners understand how to apply AI assistance correctly for long-term success. Finally, we extrapolate and discuss the broader implications of leveraging AI to cope with business challenges. Given the rise of the sharing economy, doing repetitive tasks with heterogeneous objects becomes an increasingly salient challenge in terms of scalability. For example, Airbnb must manage numerous house listings and Uber must manage a large number of vehicles. Our findings suggest that AI assistance can expand the business scope of managing heterogeneous objects.

Our work is subject to several limitations, which also present avenues for future research. First, the value of the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system in its magnitude might be underestimated since the launch of this system could potentially decrease needed labor costs. Although we did not observe any adjustments in labor costs caused by shelf monitoring (i.e., delegates’ salary) during our observation periods or our experiments, our results indicate that in the long term and under equilibrium with the IIP system, if our manufacturer could sufficiently adjust the cost of shelf monitoring, it would either decrease delegates’ working hours or the number of delegates themselves. Our paper offers a first step in comprehensively evaluating IIP-powered shelf monitoring systems. Second, future research that has access to panel data on the delegate side may be able to further reveal our logic chain from AI assistance to improved sales. Third, a larger sample size for interviews could further reveal practitioners’ perspectives and uncover why such changes occur. Fourth, IIP-powered shelf monitoring systems are merely one example of how AI assistance can be utilized. Although we believe that our findings apply to a wide range of contexts involving AI assistance, there may be additional factors that strengthen or weaken our findings.

## Appendix B

## Retailers’ Quick Behavioral Change

In this section, we explore the swiftness of retailers’ behavioral change following AI assistance. If retailers adjust their behaviors in response to the new monitoring process driven by AI, then they are supposedly more likely to comply with shelf space rental contracts after the launch of the AI. This type of change could lead to higher compliance rates and thus higher sales. Therefore, the compliance rate of retailers as exhibited in shelf photos and sales are strong indicators of their behavioral change. In our quasi-experiment, the IIP-powered shelf monitoring system was started in April 2019. According to the estimation results of the relative time model (i.e., Table 4 of the manuscript), sales grew immediately after the introduction of AI assistance, indicating that retailers quickly adjusted their behaviors after AI was implemented. In addition, our field experiment findings show that retailers’ behavioral change persists even when AI assistance is removed. We also plot retailers’ compliance rates over time in Figure B1. As observed, the compliance rate improved immediately in June 2019 (i.e., the first month following the treatment). In other words, retailers’ behavioral change occurred before June 2019. Furthermore, the compliance rate of retailer does not appear to be increasing with time. Instead, retailers’ average compliance rate fluctuates.

## Appendix C

## Excessive Monitoring

We checked the possibility of excessive monitoring by conducting interviews with retailers who received our treatment continuously in the Interview with Retailers section. In particular, over 85% of retailers said they did not consider daily shelf management to be excessive. It is worth noting that rather than shelf monitoring, delegates’ visiting schedules depend more on responsibilities such as delivering requested products to the stores. This is also supported by our available data, which shows that the number of store visits for most treatment stores remained stable month-to-month in our study period. The introduction of AI, while making monitoring sufficient, is unlikely to make it excessive because of the unchanged frequency. To further validate this point, we extended an analysis utilizing delegates’ frequency of visiting stores. In (and only in) our IIP-powered shelf monitoring system, the date of each delegate’s visit is recorded. Using such data, we then analyzed the relationship between the total number of visits and sales. Specifically, our model specification is:

$$
L o g (S a l e s) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \beta D e g r e e o f M o n i t o r i n g _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t},\tag{7}
$$

in which Log(Sales)<sub>it</sub> refers to the log-transformed focal product sales of store i in month t. We denote the degree of Monitoring<sub>it</sub> as the total number of times that store i is visited by delegates in month t. We also include the store fixed effect α<sub>i</sub> and month fixed effect γ<sub>t</sub> as controls for unobserved heterogeneity. We show our estimation results in Table C1. Specifically, we find that the coefficient of Degree of Monitoring<sub>it</sub> is significantly positive, which indicates that excessive monitoring may not be related to diminishing returns.
