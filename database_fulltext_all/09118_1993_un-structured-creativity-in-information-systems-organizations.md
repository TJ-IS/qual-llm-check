## Abstract

The subject of creativity is a neglected area in the literature of the information systems field. Yet, according to a Delphi survey of chief information officers, the field needs to be developing more creative and innovative solutions to its problems. Organizations must first be sure that certain preconditions and organizational components be in place to help individuals and teams become more creative. They can then use numerous creativity improvements techniques that have proved successful in other disciplines. In this article, six case studies show how analytical techniques (progressive abstraction, interrogatories, and force field analysis) and intuitive techniques (associations/images, wishful thinking, and analogy/metaphor) have been used in several industries to solve a variety of IS-related problems and/or opportunities. All told, some 20 creativity techniques prove especially appropriate for the IS field. Once managers understand when and where to use creativity techniques, they can move forward with implementing formal creativity improvement programs in their organizations.

Keywords: Creativity, creativity in information systems, creative climate, creativity techniques

ISRL Categories: AF0802, DA0102, DA06, DD05

## Introduction

According to a 1988 Delphi survey of SIM executives on key human resource issues in the 1990s, sixth in priority was “the need to emphasize creativity and innovation” (Couger, 1988). The Delphi survey participants confirmed “the need, in increasingly bureaucratic and complex organizations, for special emphasis on conceptualizing and developing creative and innovative approaches to problem resolution and system development” (p. 170). In today’s climate of increasingly heightened domestic and international competition, the need for creative solutions is even more important. IS personnel need to think of new ways to compete internationally and to exploit market niches. One Delphi participant commented, “The U.S. has lost its lead in almost every competitive area. Innovative software previously gave our companies major competitive advantage, and now even that area is threatened. IS must increase its creativity to help the U.S. regain its competitive edge.” The easy applications of the computer have been developed; the difficult ones lie ahead.

IS research related to creativity and innovation is embryonic. Although the terms “creativity” and “innovation” and their synonyms are mentioned frequently in the IS literature, there is rarely an elaboration on what those terms mean as related to the IS field. There are just a few papers in IS that include a discussion of creativity of more than one page in length (Couger, 1990; Elam and

Mead, 1987; 1990; Nunamaker, et al., 1987; Telem, 1988; Zmud, 1983), contrasted with over 4,000 publications in engineering, science, education, architecture, and psychology.

This paper suggests approaches intended to improve the creativity of IS organizations. Creativity is first defined, and then a model for understanding and applying creativity is presented. It is then demonstrated, contrary to popular misconception, that there are structured methods for enhancing creativity. Ironically, these “structured” methods help us “unstructure” our thinking to enable us to more freely and easily generate ideas and implement improvements. Six creativity techniques are then illustrated on a variety of IS problems and/or opportunities in several industries. This is followed by suggestions for how the environment for creativity in IS might be made more positive. Finally, an approach for implementing a creativity program in an IS organization is recommended.

## Definitions of Creativity

An examination of the literature of other disciplines has revealed some 30 definitions of creativity. From these, three themes emerge that seem to be beneficial to the study of information systems.

The first group of definitions, represented by Hausman (1975) and Morgan (1923), suggests that, to be truly creative, an innovation must demonstrate “radical newness.” Included are those situations in which the problem as initially posed was vague and ill-defined, so part of the task is to formulate the problem itself (Newell and Shaw, 1972). This has implications for systems that provide competitive advantage, as well as those that provide utility beyond present capability. To implement such creative systems requires a system development process that enables the birth of imaginative new ideas (Newell and Shaw, 1972), a subject discussed at length later in this paper.

A second group of definitions is represented by Ciardi (1956), who says “creativity is the imaginatively gifted recombination of known elements into something new” (p. 7). This theme was also expressed by the mathematician Poincare (quoted in Bruner, 1968), who defined the creative process as a “fruitful combining which reveals to us unsuspected kinship between facts, long known but wrongly believed to be strangers to one another” (p. 59). This has implications for such systems-oriented group activities as joint application development and group decision support systems, as well as module reusability at the design and code levels. This paper addresses conditions for creative environments that can enable such “fruitful combination.”

A third group of definitions is represented by Newell and Shaw's (1972) stipulation that, to be creative, a solution must have value. This is especially applicable in an era where the IS function is participating fundamentally in corporate downsizing and process redesign/re-engineering. The objectives of newness and fruitful recombination are meaningless if they do not provide value-added results.

## Origins of Creativity

What is the source of creativity? While existing theories are contradictory and fail to provide a single consistent explanation, there are two opposing possibilities. Table 1 (based upon work by Ackoff and Vegara, 1981) provides background on these alternative perspectives.

The first major view (called “origin-oriented”) asserts that creativity originates from characteristics of the individual and/or his or her environment. Some describe creativity as the natural ability of the individual, while others see it as the presence or absence of conflict in the individual’s external environment, mental environment, or both.

The second major view (called “process-oriented”) characterizes creativity less as a natural characteristic or response and more as a process. Proponents of this view assume individuals have the ability to invoke, explore, and direct cognitive processes toward specific creative goals.

This paper does not seek to determine which view(s) are correct. Rather, we proceed with the understanding that creativity exists and is part of human cognitive activity. We also assume that IS-associated individuals and teams can improve their creative process by use of specific techniques and methodologies.

## Conditions for Creativity

Individuals and teams that are otherwise regarded as equally competent do not perform equally. A primary reason is that certain preconditions must be met and certain organizational components marshalled to help individuals and teams become more creative. The preconditions were identified by Amabile (1983; 1989) in her componential framework of creativity. The organizational components are both origin- and process-oriented and are represented in the 4-Ps model of creativity (Rhodes, 1961): person, process, product, and press (the work environment). The 4-Ps model provides the basis for the remainder of this paper, which addresses the application of structure and techniques for facilitating creativity in IS.

## Preconditions for creativity: The Amabile framework

The three preconditions for creativity suggested by Amabile (1983) provide the conceptual foundation for the 4-Ps model. These preconditions are domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant skills, and task motivation. Domain-relevant skills and factual knowledge are required for performance in a given domain. Creativity-relevant skills, the second precondition, are those that enable the individual or team to “be creative” by use of innate creativity or to invoke creativityproducing processes. These skills include cognitive style, application of heuristics for the exploration of new cognitive pathways, and working style. The third precondition, task motivation, includes motivational variables that determine an individual or team's willingness to perform processes associated with creative thinking.

A key to the widespread acceptance of Amabile's model is her development of a framework for how the three components interact. In information processing terms, task motivation is responsible for initiating and sustaining the process. It determines whether the search for a solution will begin and whether it will continue, and it also determines some aspects of response generation. Domain-relevant skills draw upon prior knowledge and determine what pathways will be available during the search for a response and what criteria will be used to assess the response possibilities that are generated. Creativity-relevant skills act as an “executive controller” during response generation, influencing the way in which the search for responses will proceed.

Amabile stresses the importance of domain knowledge in achieving creative results. This fact is overlooked by many of the lay writers who suggest bringing in someone from a very different discipline to help a team produce more creative results. If the outsider does not possess the domain knowledge needed for a proper solution, his or her assistance will be nominal. Amabile's research indicates that "there is a high correlation between creativity and proficiency in the . . . domain-relevant tasks" (1983, p. 70). Possession of proper domain knowledge, possession of creativity-relevant skills, and task motivation are the combination necessary to produce successful solutions, according to Amabile's componential framework.

## The application of creativity: The 4-Ps model

Although Amabile's framework explains preconditions for creativity, it does not provide a model for the application of creativity to an organization. The 4-Ps model provides a better structure for understanding creativity and its application in IS. The model represents creativity as a dynamic phenomenon comprised of four highly interactive components: person, process, product, and press. The model's simplicity allows for the individual measurement and assessment of each component as well as evaluation of the interaction of the components. Another strength of the model is that it can be applied to a specific functional organization, such as IS, as well as the organization as a whole. In the remainder of this section, each of the four “Ps” is described and discussed in relation to IS practice.

## The First P: The Creative Person

Numerous fallacies exist regarding creativity in people. Few of us perceive ourselves to be creative, possibly because much early publicized research was confined to study of creativity in geniuses (Crovitz, 1970; Galton, 1926; Getzels and Jackson, 1962; Guilford, 1967; Lombroso, 1917; Roe, 1946) and highly accomplished professionals (Barron, 1968; Helson, 1967; MacKinnon, 1962; Roe, 1951). Most people are inclined to believe that creativity is inherited and that we either possess it or do not (Guilford, 1977; Miller, 1987; Richards, 1985). Others believe that God has either given it to them or has not (Maritain, 1930).

Research demonstrates that creativity is present in everyone (Gordon, 1961; Maslow, 1959; Rogers, 1970) and is normally distributed (Guilford, 1977; Shallcross, 1985; Tardiff and Sternberg, 1988). Nevertheless, most people utilize less and less of their native creative ability as they mature. Nationwide studies of American school children reveal progressively lower scores on creativity tests as they move through the school system, with a precipitous “4th grade slump” (Torrance, 1972).

IS management can stimulate creativity in employees by reinforcing the fact that all individuals are innately creative. Through encouragement of use of proven techniques, management can help employees restore the natural curiosity and originality that they exhibited as preschoolers. Management can also provide motivation for employees to become more creative. Amabile (1983) emphasizes the importance of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation comes from the anticipated satisfaction of generating a creative idea and putting it into effect. Management can provide extrinsic motivation by rewards, such as recognition and financial incentives, and can also facilitate a creative climate (discussed later) in which employees more easily obtain intrinsic satisfaction of creatively accomplishing a task or project.

## The Second P: The Creative Process

Considerable research has focused almost exclusively on the creative process (Guilford, 1967; Kris and Kurz, 1934; Kubie, 1958; Mednick, 1962; Patrick, 1955; Skinner, 1971; Wallas, 1926). By utilizing approaches that facilitate the creative process, people can enhance their creative abilities and creative results. Five major studies of creativity overwhelmingly show significant positive results when creative abilities are deliberately nurtured (Mansfield, et al., 1978; Parnes and Brunelle, 1967a; 1967b; Rose and Lin, 1984; Taylor, 1972; Torrance, 1972). According to Parnes (1987) “the conclusions are the same—creative abilities can be developed by deliberate programs and methods” (p. 156). Our work has substantiated these findings. Creativity improvement programs were introduced in the IS organization in two corporations, resulting in an ROI of a factor of two in the first organization (Couger, et al., 1991) and a factor of six in the second organization (Couger, et al., 1992).

Using the literature of other disciplines (education, engineering, basic science, psychology, and architecture), the authors identified a number of specific creativity enhancement techniques that have proved useful. Then, 20 of these techniques were transported over to the IS field and applied in a variety of situations. These techniques help provide the “heuristics for exploration of new cognitive pathways” that Amabile stresses in her framework. Later in this article, case descriptions of the use of six of these creativity enhancement techniques are provided. IS management can make these techniques available to employees to enable them to improve the productivity and quality of their work.

## The Third P: The Created Product

An analysis of creativity can also start with the end product, by identifying the characteristics necessary for objects to be classified as creative (Morgan, 1923; Kant, 1923). Others believe that if people are informed about their native creativity capabilities, are provided processes to facilitate creativity, and are supported through a positive climate for creativity, it is logical to assume that creative products and services will result. Nevertheless, it is helpful for employees to have ways to measure their creativity results. Couger and Dengate (1992) developed a framework for measurement of creativity of IS products/services. They propose that providing specific criteria for creative results at the specification level of system design can lead to more creative products. By adopting metrics for creative products and services, IS management can make explicit a goal that is implicit in most IS organizations.

## The Fourth P: Press (The Creative Environment)

Press is a term from the field of education that refers to the relationship between human beings and their environment (Rhodes, 1987). The importance of the work environment for encouraging/discouraging creativity is well supported in creativity research. Optimum results do not occur unless an organization has a positive climate for creativity.

There has been considerable research on the ways that work environments influence creativity (Amabile and Gryskiewicz, 1989; Cummings, 1965; Delbecq and Mills, 1985; Drucker, 1985; Pelz, 1956; Schnake, 1983). The climate for creativity comprises those factors that foster or retard creative behavior (Berlyn, 1960). “An organization’s climate for creative productivity,” according to Witt and Beorkrem (1989), “is the organization’s set of norms that communicate how the organization values and promotes creativity and innovation” (p. 33). Several studies of scientific achievement indicate that climate variables have a significant impact on the creative output of organizations (Andrews, 1975; Bailyn, 1985; Pelz, 1956; Raudsepp, 1988; Taylor, 1972). Others have found that innovative research-and-design systems are characterized by the recognition and reward of superior performance, particularly with respect to the initial exploratory stages of creative endeavors in which the individual’s need for acceptance and support plays an important role in motivating further efforts. (Abbey and Dickson, 1983; Lind and Mumford, 1987).

Managers can induce new styles of thinking by introducing a climate that encourages “kaleidoscope thinking” (Kanter, 1988). They can take an existing array of data, phenomena, or assumptions and “twist them, shake them, look at them upside down or from another angle or from a new direction” (p. 92). This permits an entirely new pattern and consequent set of actions to take place.

Five key factors to ensuring a creative climate are identified by Geis (1988):

1. A secure environment with minimal administrative or financial interference.

2. An organizational culture that makes it attractive and easy for people to discover and solve problems independently.

3. Rewards for employee performance structured to minimize the chances that intrinsic motivation will be contaminated.

4. Managerial willingness to take risks in the targeted areas for creativity and innovation.

5. Providing people with formal and informal training to enhance creativity.

## Application of Structure and Techniques for Creativity in IS

Some believe that structuring the creative problem-solving process inhibits creativity. Others, like Simon (1988), believe the process can be facilitated:

Science does not demean phenomena by explaining them. Creativity is no less challenging or exciting when the mystery is stripped from the creative process. The most beautiful flowers grow under careful cultivation from common soil. The most admirable products of human effort flourish when ordinary knowledge is nurtured by the solid process of problem solving. Creativity is understandable, but no less admirable for that (p. 23).

In the past 30 years, a number of techniques have been developed to facilitate unlocking of an individual's innate creativity in order to stimulate creative ideas. The objective is to help people move out of their normal problem-solving mode to enable them to consider a wider range of alternatives. Creativity techniques, “when applied conscientiously and repeatedly, will help awaken and strengthen . . . creative potential" (Arnold, 1962, p. 252). Use of creativity techniques "demystifies creativity and helps convince new innovators that they can build upon, modify, and combine existing ideas . . ." (Davis, 1983, p. 83).

As mentioned above, five major studies have demonstrated that creativity can be improved through creativity generation techniques (Parnes, 1987). These techniques have been classified several ways, but the most useful in our opinion is classifying creative problem-solving techniques on a continuum from analytically dominant to intuitively dominant.

The analytically oriented techniques use a structure to generate a logical pattern of thought. "They take advantage of different ways of organizing known information to help you approach problems from new angles . . . and tend to follow a linear pattern or sequence of steps" (Miller, 1987, p. 65). Intuitive techniques, on the other hand, tend to skip steps in a sequence. They "rely on a single image or symbol to provide a whole answer all at once . . . to arrive at solutions by a leap" (p. 66).

It is not surprising that systems analysts are predisposed toward the use of analytical techniques. Nevertheless, a wider range of solution possibilities can be derived if both types of techniques are applied. Both types of techniques help retain possible options, forcing divergence toward many alternatives before convergence on an acceptable solution. Even a person whom we generally believe to be the ultimate analytical thinker, Albert Einstein, used what he called “mental experiments” to stimulate new perspectives and ideas. For example, he once imagined himself as a tiny being riding through space on a ray of light, which helped him develop his general theory of relativity (Davis, 1983).

After our review of use of creativity techniques in the five other disciplines, we identified 20 techniques especially appropriate for the IS field. With the constraint on space, we will illustrate an IS application using three of the most useful techniques under each category (a summary description of the remaining 14 techniques is provided in the Appendix).

The analytical techniques to be illustrated in this paper are:

\- Progressive abstraction

• Interrogatories (5Ws/H)

\- Force field analysis

The intuitive techniques to be illustrated in this paper are:

\- Associations/images

\- Wishful thinking

\- Analogy/metaphor

Each of the above six techniques will be illustrated for a variety of IS activities. The objective is to show how the use of these techniques helps produce ideas that would not have occurred through the traditional problem-solving approaches used in IS. A description of each technique is provided, along with an illustration of its use in IS.

## Progressive abstraction technique (analytical)

## Description of the Technique

The progressive abstraction technique, developed by Geschka, et al. (1973) generates alternative problem definitions by moving through progressively higher levels of problem abstraction until a satisfactory definition is achieved. When a problem is systematically enlarged in this way, new definitions emerge that can be evaluated for their usefulness and feasibility. Once an appropriate level of abstraction is reached, possible solutions can then be more easily identified. The major advantage of the technique is the degree of structure provided the problem solver for systematically examining problem substructures and connections.

## Illustration of Progressive Abstraction: Entry-Level Employee Shortage Case

A commercial software development firm was growing so fast that it had to more explicitly plan for employee acquisition. A long-term, successful employee acquisition approach had been the hiring of new college graduates in computer science or information systems. This approach appears questionable for the future for two reasons. First, because the baby boom era is over, U.S. population growth is on the decline, and fewer people will enter the work force (a trend that started in 1992). A second factor is a reduction in the number of people entering computer science and information systems degree programs, which has been the case for the past five years. The combination of these factors is expected to produce a serious shortage of entry-level personnel. Using a traditional problem-solving approach, the problem was broken into subproblems: identifying new sources for hiring, improving recruiting effectiveness, and improving the selection process. The traditional problem-solving approach then breaks these three subareas into lower levels of detail (lower levels of abstraction). However, use of the progressive abstraction technique leads the employee acquisition planners through higher levels of abstraction as well. Figure 1 shows the next higher level of abstraction (plus-one level) to be the shortage of all professional employees, not just entry-level personnel.

The broadened problem definition was also broken to the minus-one level of abstraction. The planning team began to identify many other approaches to resolving the problem. Five subareas delineated for further abstraction were: developing paraprofessionals, delaying retirements, job sharing, use of part-time personnel, and telecommuting. This step illustrates how the progressive abstraction technique utilizes abstraction in both directions.

The problem was abstracted upward to the plus-two level of abstraction, broadening the problem to one of a shortage of human capacity, not just one of headcount. The shortage can also be counteracted by increasing productivity. The downward (minus one) abstraction at this level produces seven additional solution possibilities: improving motivation, outsourcing, simplifying processes, providing new tools/techniques, improving creativity, automating, and avoiding turnover of high performers.

## Results

By using all three levels of problem definition, 15 approaches were identified to reduce the effect of the smaller pool for entry-level employees. Stated another way, by moving to the higher levels of abstraction, solution space was increased from three to 15 subareas. The plus-one level of abstraction expanded the solution set from three to eight. The plus-two level of abstraction resulted in a problem definition that identified seven additional areas for analysis. Even though solution of problems defined for the first and second levels of abstraction were outside the area of responsibility of the employee acquisition planners, they produced a solution set useful for the entire firm. Organizations responsible for solving the problems defined at abstraction levels one and two could then utilize other creativity techniques to generate ideas for resolving their respective employee resource problems.

## Interrogatories: 5Ws and the H technique (analytical)

## Description of the Technique

The who-what-where-when-why and how questions aid in expanding an individual's or group's perspective of a problem or opportunity. The technique provides a simple, easy-to-remember framework for systematically gathering data that are relevant to identifying and solving the problem. By going through several cycles of the 5Ws/H, alternatives related to the problem/opportunity can be explored exhaustively.

## Illustration of Use of Interrogatories (5Ws/H): Determining the Feasibility of a Pilot Test

The IS department of an express mail company was considering the use of a pilot test for a new software package. The application had the potential to improve service levels significantly;

however, if it were implemented poorly it could produce the opposite result. On the other hand, a pilot test would delay the implementation of the application in a competitive market. Some team members believed a pilot test unnecessary. After an hour of discussion, the project leader remarked, “We’ve been arguing for an hour but don’t seem to be anywhere near an agreement. I suggest we use the 5 Ws/H technique to make sure we have thoroughly considered all aspects of the problem. The technique will help us better organize our evaluation so we can reach an agreement.”

Round One: Reasons for Conducting a Pilot Test. In Round One the group used the 5Ws/H to identify the reasons for conducting a pilot test.

1. Why would the pilot test be useful?

2. How would we evaluate the results of the test?

3. When should the test be conducted?

4. Where would the test be conducted?

5. Who are the key people to be involved in the test?

6. What specific things would be included in the test?

Next, the group derived answers to the set of questions. They determined that the test would be beneficial. They then attacked the question of whether the benefits of the pilot test would offset the disadvantages of delaying the implementation of the application.

Round Two: Disadvantages in Conducting the Pilot Test. Round Two questions were developed to provide a comprehensive analysis of the disadvantages.

1. What loss in competitive advantage would occur through delay in implementing the application?

2. Who in the marketing department could best provide information on the advantages of early implementation of the application?

3. How might we enhance the unit and integration tests to provide assurance of system integrity?

4. Where could we get a proven list of factors to ensure effective implementation without having to conduct a pilot test?

5. When would be the ideal time to implement the system to reduce the probability of problems?

6. Why are we assuming that this application is any more complicated than others that have been implemented satisfactorily without a pilot test?

After answering the questions developed in Round Two, the team had the basis for a complete and accurate evaluation of the pros and cons of the pilot test.

## Results

In answering the questions for each round, the team recognized the need for additional questions or subrounds. The authors' experience is that three rounds will provide the framework for analyzing the typical problem or issue being analyzed. The group believed that the forced question set caused them to take a more complete and comprehensive approach to both problem identification and resolution.

## Force field analysis technique (analytical)

## Description of the Technique

The force field analysis technique was developed by Miller (1987) from a concept originated by Lewin (1951). The name is derived from the technique's ability to identify forces contributing to or hindering a solution to a problem. The technique can stimulate creative thinking in three ways: (1) defining direction (vision), (2) identifying strengths that can be maximized, and (3) identifying weaknesses that can be minimized. The following steps are used:

1. Write a brief statement of the problem to be solved.

2. Describe what the situation would be like if everything fell apart—absolute catastrophe.

3. Describe what the situation would be like if it were ideal.

4. With catastrophic at the left of the continuum and ideal at the right, draw a center line.

5. List what forces are “tugging” right now in the situation to help make it more ideal and what forces are “tugging” now to make it more catastrophic.

The primary function of force field analysis in idea generation is to present three different stimuli for thinking of new options or solutions. Because the field represents a kind of tug-of-war, there are three ways to move the center line in the direction of the more desirable future:

1. Strengthen an already present positive force.

2. Weaken an already present negative force.

3. Add a new positive force.

Therefore, the force field provides focus for exploring possible solutions.

## Illustration of the Use of Force Field Analysis: Implementing a Creativity Improvement Program

The IS department of an electronics firm decided to implement a creativity improvement program. A workshop on creative problem solving was conducted for two units as a pilot program. One month after the workshop was conducted the units met with the facilitator to discuss progress. The discussion revealed that the climate for creativity needed improvement. The force field analysis technique was selected as a framework for determining how to enhance the environment for creativity.

The discussion on the need to improve the climate for creativity led to the following problem definition: "How to ensure that creativity techniques are utilized." The facilitator asked the group to identify what catastrophic situation would be related to the problem. They selected the statement: "Minimal use of creativity techniques even after training in the techniques and with availability of creativity resource materials." The group was then asked to formulate a statement for the ideal situation. They selected the statement: "Use of creativity techniques in everyday activities for all IS employees."

Next, the group discussed the forces “tugging” toward catastrophic results and the forces “tugging” toward the ideal result. Table 2 shows the results of the force field analysis. Each of the factors identified was placed on the organization’s agenda for determining ways to strengthen positive forces and weaken negative forces.

## Results

Every one of the factors under the ideal forces category was implemented. Creativity techniques were added to the standards manual and were among the factors evaluated in system reviews. Managers worked diligently to demonstrate the value of creativity techniques in their own activities. Meeting procedures included creativity approaches. For follow-up, employees read an assigned article on creativity each month. A discussion leader's role was rotated each month, with emphasis upon how the content might apply to the department's situation. Recognition for creative accomplishment was given within the department and also to higher management levels. Knowledge and use of creativity techniques was listed among the skills for advancement. Annual performance reviews included evaluation of creative accomplishment.

## Associations/images technique (intuitive)

## Description of the Technique

The first intuitive approach examined, the associations/images technique, builds on the natural inclination of humans to associate. The linking or combining process is another way of expanding solution space. A classic example is the Swiss hiker who was removing troublesome cockleburrs from his stockings and associated the barbs on the burrs with a new way to attach things, ultimately arriving at the idea of velcro. The procedure for use of the technique is as follows, based on an approach used by Gordon (1961):

1. The leader assists the group in identifying the problem or opportunity to be examined (if required).

2. The leader asks the participants to select a solution to the problem phrased in the form of a goal or wish.

3. The leader picks a key concept that is central to the goal/wish statements.

4. The leader asks the group to think of a world that is remote from the world of the problem. (The leader chooses the remote world.)

5. The leader requests the group to lay aside both the problem and the goal/wish developed in Steps 1 and 2 and to list associations and images that characterize the remote world.

6. The leader directs the group to relate the list of associations and images of the remote world to the world of the problem.

7. The leader directs the group to develop second generation associations and images from any one of those listed in Step 6, extracting key principles and applying them in a more realistic way without diluting the innovation.

8. The group selects and implements appealing ideas developed in Step 5.

## Illustration of the Use of Association/Images: Reducing the Highest Turnover Category

Most companies experience the highest level of turnover among programmer analysts (P/As) during the second and third year after college graduation. Exit interviews identify three principal reasons: (1) employees begin to realize their value—they are on the downward slope of the learning/orientation curve and recognize that they are making substantive contributions; (2) annual salary increases for good performers rarely exceed 15 percent, while it is not unusual to be able to attain a 20 to 30 percent increase by changing companies; (3) a variety of other factors, such as lack of recognition, politics, poor feedback, unrealistic work demands, etc.

One organization, the IS department of an insurance company, established a task force to try to reduce turnover in general and specifically in the second to third year category. The group was not excited about the assignment. Members believed the turnover problem had been worked on many times previously and that little new light could be shed on the problem. One group member commented, "Here in a city where many IS organizations are located within a two-mile radius, you can change jobs without changing your carpool. You're never going to be able to reduce turnover significantly."

Because of the group's creativity blockage, the facilitator suggested the group try the association/images creativity technique. He suggested the approach would take them far afield from their normal problem-solving modes and might provide a fresh outlook on the problem. However, prior to initiating the creativity procedure, the group decided that item two, under causes for turnover, was outside the control of the company; it couldn't give salary increases to match the increases normal for company changes. They concentrated on factors to help in items one and three. Use of the association/image procedure went as follows:

1. Problem: High turnover of IS personnel

2. Select a goal/wish: Reduce the turnover of P/As between their second and third year to the level for other points in their career

3. Pick a key concept from the goal/wish: Reduce turnover

4. Think of an example from a world that is remote from the world of the problem: Hollywood

5. Forget about the problem and the goal/wish and focus associations or images that describe the remote world: Big dollars, acting, beautiful people, divorces, agents, powerful people, Oscars, rich, drugs, big dreams, casting couch, National Enquirer, critics, parties, commercialism, silicone, lack of fulfillment

6. Use these examples to develop related ideas for the IS field:

Oscars: Statues, plaques for high performance.

Parties: Social activities to improve rapport among low social need P/As.

Divorces: Keep work demands realistic; provide counseling.

Agents: Provide mentors who help P/As plan careers, and give encouragement.

Acting: Try to diminish politics; shelter P/As from these effects. Concentrate on the politics essential to their progress.

Critics: Ensure positive feedback to temper necessary negatives in performance review. Make sure mistakes made do not remain on the employee's permanent record—some people leave because they think they could never change people's minds.

Big dreams: Develop fast-track programs for exceptional performers; help others determine realistic goals.

Casting Work to ensure no favoritism in couch: performance evaluation.

Drugs: Help P/As lessen their addiction to reading help wanted ads by doing a better job of publishing career paths available in the firm.

National Enquirer: Counteract help wanted ad lure of “greener pastures” by better delineating the strengths of career paths in the firm vs. other principal competitors for employees.

Big dollars: Help P/As understand the true cost of job change: learning/orientation curve, loss of work friends, loss of seniority, relocation costs, need to reprove oneself.

7. Develop second generation ideas from any one of the remote-world ideas, extracting key principles and applying them in a more realistic fashion without diluting the innovation. The group chose to develop ideas from each of the associations/images it derived. Although space limitations prevent recording all their ideas, those generated from one of the above, awarding Oscars, will be illustrated.

Awarding Oscars: The behavioral research on IS professionals has shown that IS managers, compared to managers in other fields, provided insufficient recognition (Couger, et al., 1979). The group identified 12 forms of low-cost yet quite effective recognition: verbal commendation at monthly staff meetings, bulletin board announcements, e-mail announcements, letters for permanent employee file, dinner for two, "ataboy and atagirl" awards, plaques, certificates, show tickets, lunch with your boss, \$50 gift certificates.

8. Pick ideas that have appeal from the second generation ideas. The task force decided that all of the ideas were useful and that the full set should be provided to management to enable recognition to occur in a variety of ways. They produced recommendations for the remaining 17 items on the association/images list, just as they had done for the one on providing Oscars. Two to three suggestions were made for each of the other 17 items.

## Results

Use of the association/images technique helped the task force break through its belief that the turnover problem had been analyzed so often that few new ideas were possible. A report on the results was provided to the CIO, who approved the task force recommendations. Meetings were held with managers to review the recommendations and to demonstrate the CIO's concern in implementing them. Turnover was reduced, both in the second and third year category and for other categories. However, other factors also had impact on turnover, such as the national recessionary period, so it was not possible to determine precisely the degree of impact of the task force recommendations.

## Wishful thinking technique (intuitive)

## Description of the Technique

The wishful thinking technique is particularly useful for people who typically take a very analytical approach to problem solving. Through the use of fantasy, analytical thinkers tend to loosen their analytical parameters to consider a larger set of alternatives than they might ordinarily consider. Van Gundy (1988) developed a procedure for use of the technique.

## 1. Develop a problem statement.

2. Open up the solution space to all possibilities, i.e., assume anything is possible.

3. State the alternatives in terms of a wish or a fantasy. Examples are: "I wish that I could be able to . . .," or, "What would happen if we tried . . .," or, "What really needs to be done is . . . ."

4. Convert each “wishful” statement to a more practical one. Examples are: “How about our . . .,” or, “Assuming that we could get around the starting constraint, what might be the advantages . . .,” or, “It may be possible to meet our wish, but first we would have to . . .,” or, “Perhaps our wish is not as far-fetched as we first thought. What if we tried . . .”

5. Move on to an analytical problem-solving approach to developing a solution.

## Illustration of Use of the Wishful Thinking Technique: Developing an Enterprise Model

The IS department of a microelectronics firm was examining approaches to develop an enterprise model for the company. While IS was in the midst of discussions with vendors and consultants about different approaches, the company's CEO attended a seminar on the Hoshin planning technique, comprising seven planning techniques for solving business problems. He conveyed his enthusiasm about these techniques to his senior managers.

Picking up on the CEO's interest, the IS manager pulled together a group of systems analysts to explore how they might respond. They decided to use the wishful thinking technique to try to combine the two needs of developing an enterprise model and a new business planning technique. In step one they chose the goal of deviating from modeling the present business and, instead, decided to concentrate on modeling the “ideal” business. From Step 2 of wishful thinking came the additional “wish” to identify opportunities to re-engineer the company’s business process flow. They developed a series of “what-if” questions related to these wishes (Step 3). Then, following Step 4 in the procedure, they converted each “wishful” statement to a practical one. Finally, they formulated an approach for integrating the techniques of Hoshin planning, enterprise modeling, and re-engineering processes (Step 5).

## Results

After receiving senior management's enthusiastic approval of their recommendation, IS organized a series of working sessions resulting in the development of an enterprise model and process re-engineering plans. Another important outcome resulted. The integrated techniques, now referred to as the enterprise model, support management's "wish" to develop a culture consistent with the specifications of the Malcolm Baldridge National Quality Award.

## Analogy/metaphor technique (intuitive)

## Description of the Technique

The development of the analogy/metaphor creativity technique is generally credited to deBono (1970). However, Aristotle spoke of the value of metaphor almost 2,200 years ago: “Ordinary words convey only what we know already; it is from metaphor that we can best get hold of something fresh” (Embler, 1966, p. 12). An analogy is a similarity between two things otherwise dissimilar (Miller, 1987). Analogies serve to “make the familiar strange and the strange familiar.” By the use of analogies, an individual or group can often find a new insight and approach to the nature of a problem and, thus, its resolution.

Developing an analogy for the creative problem solving process is not as easy as some of the other creativity approaches. VanGundy (1988) facilitated the process by originating a procedure as follows:

1. Withholding all evaluation, generate a list of objects, people, situations, or actions that are similar but unrelated to the problem. An example is a list of situations describing some form of “resistance” or “reluctance.” Some of the possibilities are:

\- Reluctance to believe politicians

\- Aversion to change

\- Dislike of (resistance to) certain vegetables

\- Hesitancy to learn a new VCR procedure

\- Resistance (of faculty) to adopt a new textbook

\- Resistance (of management) to conduct performance reviews

\- Resistance (of customers) to try a new product when the old one still meets their needs

2. Select one of the analogies and describe it in detail. Try to accomplish this step without relating to the original problem.

3. Examine the items generated and translate them into a statement that applies to the problem or opportunity being analyzed.

4. Examine the list of statements and discuss its application to the problem or opportunity.

The procedure typically produces a broadened perspective on the issue at hand and typically leads to an expanded solutions set.

## Illustration of the Use of Analogy/Metaphor: Improving Receptivity to the Use of CASE Tools

The IS department of a firm in the petroleum industry was experiencing difficulty in motivating employees to use CASE tools. The CIO assigned the technology assessment group with responsibility for encouraging increased use of CASE tools. Since other approaches had not been successful in motivating personnel to use CASE tools, the session facilitator recommended the use of the analogy/metaphor creativity technique to provide a different perspective.

Step 1. The facilitator suggested that the group use the list of analogies described above, on resistance or reluctance aspects.

Step 2. From the list of suggested analogies, the facilitator suggested “dislike of certain vegetables.” He told the group, “We could concentrate on the dislike of broccoli, which has agreement at a high governmental level, or brussels sprouts, which has larger numbers in the opposition ranks. However, I suggest we use canned spinach because of the longstanding tradition of resistance.”

The group identified a long list of factors related to the dislike of spinach. For brevity's sake, only a sample of items on the list is provided below:

\- Taste—subdued compared to green beans

\- Appearance—dull compared to carrots, squash

\- Capacity—steals space away from favorite foods

\- Cost—higher than some foods, such as rice and beans

\- Time-consuming to chew—compared to mashed potatoes

\- Not elegant—“Popeye” image compared to “beautiful people” image of those who eat caviar

\- No tradition—compared to grits for Southerners, corn-on-the-cob in summer, fried chicken for picnics

\- Healthful—get more iron from breakfast cereal

Step 3. The group examined each item listed and translated it into a statement that applied to the problem of resistance. The following translation applied to the reluctance to use CASE tools.

1. Taste—some of the pleasurable activities of CASE tools are subdued, less enjoyable than those of the existing methodology.

2. Appearance—the thought of using CASE tools is not as attractive as the favorite, proven approach.

3. Capacity—prefer some aspect of prior methodology, such as coding or programming logic chart development, to tasks imposed by CASE tools.

4. Cost—learning curve is high: manual reading, procedures learning, working out bugs in the CASE tool software.

5. Time-consuming—lengthy time required to develop data flow diagrams and data dictionary.

6. Elegance—less art, more science; loss of elegance in developing unique coding logic or algorithms because the CASE tool translates everything into common logic.

7. Lack of tradition—could go to ACM meeting and spend hours with peers discussing coding approaches and debugging episodes; with CASE tools personnel feel more like draftsmen than engineers.

8. Healthful—conversion to CASE tool use is not necessarily seen as improving one's health, that is, one's security within the department.

Step 4. The group examined each item in the list of problems and suggested an approach to counter or lower the resistance. For improved CASE tool acceptance the following approaches are examples of their suggestions.

1. Emphasize the ability to experience other pleasurable activities, such as more time to spend on designing the system in order to build a more sophisticated system, with the saving in time over the traditional development cycle. Incentives could be provided for employees who make the conversion to CASE tool use.

2. With the acquisition of state-of-the-art development knowledge, personnel are more mobile and have higher job security.

3. A loss of visibility in the final product, such as documents produced by the life cycle method, is offset by graphs of user surveys showing improved user satisfaction, and graphs of development schedule compliance and improved cost effectiveness.

4. Despite the prior preference for certain tasks in the life cycle development methodology, the higher performance ratings on design accomplishments enable faster career progression.

5. A pride in accomplishment occurs because some weak areas are strengthened, such as improved accuracy in cost/schedule estimating due to standardization of activities in development. The improved effect on the company bottom line (through more cost-effective applications) is visible to many people compared to the mere self-satisfaction acquired through the writing of an elegant subroutine.

6. Once the learning curve is overcome, the process of development is speeded considerably. Applications are not only implemented sooner and revised more easily, but the risks of not meeting specifications are also reduced. The ease in making revisions because everything is now stored saves the boring time in manually revising data flow diagrams and coding revised routines.

7. Data flow diagram/data dictionary development time is offset by reducing the time-consuming tasks of documentation and coding under the prior life cycle development methodology.

8. Rather than reduce a feeling of professionalism, the revision in time allocation to the development activities allows personnel to be primarily designers rather than programmer/analysts.

9. Designers will feel more at home at ASM and SIM meetings (compared to ACM meetings) talking about the “effect on the company of my systems,” comparing design approaches, and acquiring a management perspective. There is a de-emphasis on loyalty to the profession and a new emphasis upon loyalty to the company, which expands one’s circle of relationships from a few “coding techies” to many people within the company.

## Results

The group normally would have resorted to brainstorming as a means to accomplish its assigned task. While brainstorming assures production of a large number of ideas, it does not ensure that all facets of the problem have been addressed. The group believed that the forced question-set caused them to take a more complete, comprehensive approach to both problem identification and resolution.

## When and Where to Use Creativity Techniques

The preceding section concentrated on the “how” in use of creativity techniques. Implicit in those descriptions are the “when” and “where” of use of these techniques. “When” becomes more explicit by taking a vertical slice through the IS functions. The “where” question becomes explicit by taking a horizontal swath across IS activities. We believe that all functions within IS (vertical slice) can be improved with the use of creativity techniques. It is also our experience that all phases of an activity (horizontal swath) can utilize creative techniques. We have had the opportunity to use these techniques throughout IS and have not yet found an IS activity immune to their use.

## When to use creativity techniques

At the top of the vertical cut are the macro-level strategic activities. At the bottom are the micro-level operational activities. The example of developing an enterprise model represents the strategic area. The wishful thinking technique facilitated the team's modeling of the ideal business. An analytic technique, such as interrogatories (5Ws/H) is then useful in fleshing out the characteristics of the ideal business.

The example described above, of using analogies/metaphors to improve receptivity to CASE tools, is an illustration for intermediate, tactical functions. A second example was the use of 5Ws/H for determining the feasibility of a pilot test. To illustrate further, we have applied associations/images to help analysts better understand user needs. In addition to the “remote world” of Hollywood described earlier, we have used the “remote world” of Star Trek. Participants were asked to identify all the characters in the cast of Star Trek, to discuss their characteristics and assignments, and then to project how this framework causes one to look differently at user needs. The programmer/analysts were asked to think of the difference in approach to user responsiveness by a Commander Data or Spock versus a Counselor Troy or Guinan. Most IS personnel take the analytical approach of Data or Spock. The intuitive approach of Troy or Guinan enables a very different view of users and their needs.

Although creativity techniques are often thought of as more appropriate for broad-scope activities, perhaps their most important use is in daily activities that tend to become routine. Frequent use helps practitioners obtain a fresh perspective on frequently performed functions. For example, a metaphor could be used to force an individual to view his or her job with a “fresh eye.” We have used the nursery story of the Three Little Pigs to help programmers and analysts look at user relationships from a different viewpoint. They are asked to place users and IS personnel in the roles of the pigs and wolf. They then evaluate current relationships and generate new ways to interact with users by thinking about the relationship of the construction of the three pigs’ houses to the way they build systems to meet user needs.

## Where to use creativity techniques

Are the techniques most useful at the start of an activity or do they apply to all phases? The latter is true—techniques add value not only in “kick-starting” a project or activity but also in providing breakthrough-thinking when an impasse occurs in the midst of a project. Using systems development as an illustration, a variety of creativity techniques can be used at four key points in the development cycle (requirements definition, logical design, physical design, and program design), to ensure that a full range of alternatives is considered at each stage. Creativity techniques are useful in the initial stages of a project, such as the example of the use of progressive abstraction in problem definition demonstrates. The associations/images technique is useful to start the resolution activity for the problem of high turnover of personnel in the second to third year of employment. Creativity techniques are also useful in latter stages of a project, as illustrated by the use of force field analysis in planning the implementation of a creativity program.

Creativity techniques are commonly used in the midst of problem solving or opportunity delineation. After definition and fact-finding, the techniques are used to help generate ideas.

## Implementing a Creativity Improvement Program

Our recommended approach to implementing a creativity improvement program follows the 4-Ps structure: (1) enlighten employees about their innate creative potential (person); (2) provide employees with techniques to improve idea generation and evaluation (process); (3) improve the climate for creativity (press); and (4) inform employees of the factors that constitute a creative product or service. By addressing each of the four components of the 4-Ps model, managers can optimize creative results. Emphasis upon the proper climate for creativity proves to employees that management values creative ideas and that the ideas will be given fair and timely assessment.

One way of demonstrating managerial support for creativity is to provide training on techniques to facilitate idea generation and evaluation. Both analytical and intuitive techniques are needed to ensure a qualitative and quantitative approach to idea generation. Procedures have been established to facilitate the use of both categories of techniques. It is not surprising that procedures exist for the analytical techniques. Some are surprised to find that procedures exist for the intuitive techniques as well. These structured approaches help “unstructure” the normal thought processes, enabling IS practitioners to move beyond standard solution approaches to consider a wider range of choices and opportunities.

# Additional Creativity Techniques Transported From Other Disciplines to IS

## Attribute Association

Originated by Robert Crawford and modified by Arthur VanGundy, the attribute association technique works on the premise that all ideas originate from previous ideas that have been modified in some way. The socket wrench, for example, is an improvement on the wrench. The ratchet screwdriver, for example, evolved by changing one of the attributes of the traditional screwdriver to one of pushing instead of turning the handle. The procedure for use of the technique is as follows:

1. State the problem and its objectives.

2. List all of the characteristics of a product, object, or idea related to the problem.

3. Systematically modify the characteristics or attributes to meet the objectives.

## Boundary Examinations

The act of defining a problem involves certain assumptions about problem boundaries. Boundaries determine how information is gathered, organized, and processed. Boundary rigidity will affect problem-solving success. Boundary examinations are based upon the assumption that a problem's boundaries are neither correct nor incorrect. The objective is to restructure the assumptions of a problem to provide a new way of looking at it. The major strengths of the technique are its potential for (1) producing more provocative problem definitions, (2) clarifying often indistinguishable problem boundaries, (3) demonstrating the importance of formulating flexible problem definitions, and (4) coping with management teams that are overly precise in their problem definitions.

## Brainwriting—Shared Enhancements Variation

The distinction in brainwriting is the generation of ideas individually and recording them on a piece of paper. The advantage over brainstorming is reduction in the effect of dominating individuals; brainwriting ensures that all participants have equal opportunity to share their ideas. The other advantage comes from the rotation of ideas among participants with each person expanding and improving the idea. By the time the process is completed, everyone has ownership in the idea. It also tends to be a mature idea, ready to be implemented, compared to the results of brainstorming, where the ideas are mostly immature.

## Bug List

An excellent start for the invention process is to ask the group members to identify things that irritate or “bug” them. When bugs are common to a number of people, a need for an invention has been identified. Each person is asked to identify five or 10 bugs, and then the list is consolidated. The group is led through the list and asked to vote in order to identify the bugs most common to most people. Then the group brainstorms ways to resolve the bugs.

## Decomposable Matrices

The decomposable matrices technique was derived from the work of Herbert Simon, Nobel Laureate in decision sciences, who believed that complexity in the world has evolved from simple structures into progressively formal hierarchic systems. To understand complexity, complex hierarchic systems can be analyzed using a basic property of their structure: near decomposability. The concept of near decomposability refers to the fact that the subsystems of some hierarchic systems maintain some, although not total, interdependence from other subsystems. Decomposable matrices are especially useful for highly system-based problems. The components of each subsystem are listed and arranged within a matrix. Weights are assigned to each of the interactions. Relationships between components can then be selected as the focus for generation of problem solutions. Because this technique forces identification of problem subsystems, their components, and how they interact, it can cause the emergence of a clearer picture of important problem elements.

## Dimensional Analysis

The dimensional analysis technique is designed to clarify and explore the dimensions and limits of a problem. It examines five elements of a problem: substantive dimension, spatial dimension, temporal dimension, qualitative dimension, and quantitative dimension. It is useful in forcing a problem solver to consider the many implications associated with the various problem dimensions. The best use of the technique is as a checklist for pre-problem solving, that is, a general guideline preceding the use of some other analytical method.

## Disjointed Incrementalism

The disjointed incrementalism technique is useful for situations involving complex decisions and vaguely defined, changing objectives. The disjointed aspect of the technique refers to the way in which many policy problems are analyzed at different times in different locations without any apparent coordinating efforts and without the benefit of relevant past experience. Incrementalism refers to the prescriptions used to compare differences or increments in the consequences of various alternatives with one another and with a present (state) situation. Thus, decisions are made by evaluating the trade-offs possible between an increment of one value and an increment of another value. The technique has the ability to reduce information-processing demands made upon a problem solver. It allows a systematic way to restrict the number of alternatives and consequences requiring consideration. The technique can decrease the complexity and uncertainty of a decision environment.

## Manipulative Verbs

Sometimes people need a kick-start to help them identify alternative approaches to solving a problem. The manipulative verb checklist provides words for kick-starting. This list also helps generate ideas by taking a verb from the list and “checking” the item against certain aspects of the problem, such as implementation. The comprehensive list of verbs helps reduce the possibility that a solution might be overlooked. Users of the technique sequentially move through the following list of verbs to suggest possible solutions to the problem. ## Morphological Analysis

The morphological analysis technique is an eclectic, forced-relationship approach that consists of dividing a problem into its major parameters or dimensions and then subdividing these into different forms of the original dimensions. Various combinations of the subdivisions are then forced together and examined for possible solutions to the problem. Because of the systematic nature of the method, trial-and-error problem solving is avoided. The approach also causes ideas to be considered that might have been overlooked using a less systematic method.

## Nominal Group Technique (NGT)

The normal group technique utilizes the positive features of both brainstorming and brainwriting. It uses silent generation of ideas in writing, round-robin recording of ideas, serial discussion for clarification, and subsequent rounds of writing. This approach reduces the inhibiting factors of both brainstorming and brainwriting while retaining public sharing of ideas to stimulate new ideas.

## Peaceful Setting

The objective of the peaceful setting technique is to enable people to mentally remove themselves from present surroundings so they have access to a less cluttered, more open mental process. The goal is to try to eliminate the constraints of the normal work environment that impede full use of their native creative ability. By trying to use all five senses in this setting (taste, touch, smell, sight, and sound), they can more easily call on their sixth sense, intuition.

## Problem Reversal

The problem reversal technique is particularly valuable for defining a problem in more solvable terms and for finding solutions to everyday problems. The following steps are used:

1. Write down the problem statement in a question form.

2. Identify the verb or "action" content of the statement.

3. Reverse the meaning of the verb or action content and restate the problem in question form.

4. List answers to the reversed problem statement.

5. Reverse the answers stated in Step 4.

## SIL Method

In the SIL method, a German-developed technique whose acronym translates to “suggested integration of problem elements,” free association and forced relationships are combined to cause an integration of ideas until an acceptable solution is reached. Members of the group record their ideas in writing. Two people each read an idea. The remainder of the group tries to integrate the two ideas. Then another person reads his or her idea, and the group tries to integrate that idea with the first integrated result. The process continues, progressively adding an idea, until an acceptable solution is reached.

## Wildest Idea

The wildest idea approach tries to get people out of their normal problem-solving modes, which are usually quite conservative, by asking them to try to come up with a “wild” idea. Using this technique the leader selects (or asks the group to select) a wild idea. With this starting point the group continues to generate ideas. If no practical ideas emerge, another wild idea is used, and the process continues until an acceptable idea is found.
