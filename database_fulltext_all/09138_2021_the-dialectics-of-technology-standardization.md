# THE DIALECTICS OF TECHNOLOGY STANDARDIZATION<sup>1</sup>

Rikard Lindgren

Swedish Center for Digital Innovation, Department of Applied IT, University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, SWEDEN, and University of Borås, Borås, SWEDEN {rikard.lindgren@ait.gu.se}

Lars Mathiassen Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, U.S.A {lmathiassen@ceprin.org}

Ulrike Schultze Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University Dallas, TX, U.S.A {uschultz@mail.cox.smu.edu}

Technology standardization unfolds as a dialectic process marked by paradoxical tensions. However, standardization research has yet to provide a dialectic analysis of how tensions and management responses interact recursively over time, and with what effect. In this paper, we apply dialectics to analyze an action research study of a Swedish initiative that developed and diffused a technology standard to facilitate the integration of disparate IT systems in road haulage firms. Drawing on the technology standardization literature and our empirical analysis, we engage in midrange theorizing to capture the recursive dynamics through which standard-setters construct and respond to manifestations of three latent tensions: development versus diffusion activities, private versus public interests, and local versus global solutions. Our resulting dialectic theorizing explicates how standard-setters bring these latent tensions into being; how they construct salient tensions through the oppositional logics of polarization, complementarity, and mutuality; how they manage these tensions through splitting, integrating, and suspension responses; and how consequential functional, architectural, and organizational standardization outcomes produce a new social order in which new tensions emerge. These theoretical insights contribute to both the technology standardization and dialectics literatures.

Keywords: Technology standardization, dialectic analysis, paradoxical tensions, management responses, oppositional logics

## Introduction

Technology standards are developed to ensure technical interoperability (Zhao and Xia 2014), process integration (Guilloux et al. 2013), and industry transparency (Markus and Bui 2012) by dictating “how different technological components work together to provide utility to users” (Garud et al. 2002, p. 197). As such, these interface specifications prescribe how products that are required to connect to one another (Garud and Kumaraswamy 1993; Liu et al. 2011) or in need of complementary goods (Narayanan and Chen 2012; Liu et al. 2012) must interact to make networks like the internet (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010; Nickerson and zur Muehlen 2006), interorganizational systems (Bala and Venkatesh 2007; Markus et al. 2006), or technology platforms (Markus and Loebbecke 2013; Saadatmand et al. 2019) work effectively.

Technology standardization is challenging. It entails a dialectic process “in which a coalition of partisan actors espousing conflicting views confront each other and engage in political behaviors” (Narayanan and Chen 2012, p. 1389), which generates “seeds of self-destruction” (Garud et al. 2002, p. 196) caused by paradoxical tensions (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Markus et al. 2006) that render “outcomes antagonistic to the original aim” (Hanseth et al. 2006, p. 564). While there is some evidence of dialectic theorizing in past standardization research (Hanseth et al. 2006; Wareham et al. 2014), it does not build on dialectic analyses tracing how tensions are constructed and transformed through deliberate managerial efforts to resolve them over time (Hargrave and Van de Ven 2017). By neglecting the recursive nature of dialectics (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013), these analyses miss that the recurrence of tensions and their spillover continuously shape the organization and the trajectory of standardization efforts (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017; Sheep et al. 2017).

Dialectics is a conceptualization of social reality that reflects a relational ontology by recognizing the “unity of opposites” (Sherman 1976, p. 62). It thus acknowledges the interdependence between opposing forces that make up a contradiction, i.e., two elements that appear logical in isolation but inconsistent in conjunction (Huq et al. 2017). Hence, to theorize the ongoing transformation of social structures, dialectic analysis seeks to uncover the contradictions and limits of the present order (Benson 1977) based on the premise that there are ever-present, imperceptible binary opposites that undergird social reality and that maintain the dynamism of everyday life by operating as a perpetual motion machine (Knight and Paroutis 2017). Such analysis unpacks the mechanisms by which organizations evolve through moments of stability and change, especially in times of heightened complexity. Its potential as a system of thought is becoming increasingly recognized in management studies (Smith et al. 2017).

Our paper presents a dialectic inquiry into technology standardization to offer a better understanding of how tensions are brought into being and managed throughout this process. Accordingly, we ask the following question: How are the tensions inherent in technology standardization brought into being and managed during the production of a vertical standard? We apply foundational concepts from Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model to analyze an action research study of a twelve-year (2002-2013) Swedish initiative that developed and diffused a technology standard to facilitate the integration of disparate IT systems in road haulage firms (Andersson and Lindgren 2005; Andersson et al. 2008; Lindgren et al. 2008). In contrast to prior studies that primarily draw on archival data and post hoc interviews to reconstruct the standardization process (Garud et al. 2002; Markus et al. 2006; Wareham et al. 2014), we rely on situated firsthand accounts and experiences of the action researchers and industry actors collected throughout the initiative. This allows us to generate a dialectic analysis of (1) how underlying, latent tensions were repeatedly constructed as situated, salient tensions, (2) how the construction of the salient tensions prompted specific responses, and (3) how the responses then shaped subsequent tensions and standardization outcomes.

By theoretically and empirically situating dialectic concepts into the technology standardization context, we engage in midrange theorizing (Gregor 2006; Grover and Lyytinen 2015) to develop a dialectic model of the recursive dynamics through which tensions interact with each other and with managerial efforts to resolve them. Our theorizing explicates how standard-setters bring the latent tensions between development and diffusion activities (Markus et al. 2006), private and public interests (Garud et al. 2002), and local and global solutions (Hanseth et al. 2006 into being; how they apply the oppositional logics of polarization, complementarity, and mutuality (Bosserman 1995; Carlo et al. 2012) to socially construct manifestations of these latent tensions; how this framing of salient tensions shape their responses of splitting (Jay 2013; Smith 2014), integrating (Calabretta et al. 2017; Cuganesan et al. 2017; Jarzabkowski and Le 2017), and suspension (Möllering 2001, 2005); and how consequential functional, architectural, and organizational standardization outcomes produce a new social order in which new tensions emerge.

Our paper is organized as follows. We first present our review of prior technology standardization studies with a focus on the tensions they identify. Next, we discuss received dialectic theorizing in standardization research and outline the dialectic concepts that guide our empirical analysis. Having described our research method, we present the results of our analysis of the development and diffusion of a vertical standard in six interrelated episodes. We then draw on our empirical analysis and extant literature to engage in dialectic theorizing of technology standardization. We conclude with a discussion of the contributions, implications, and limitations of our research.

## Technology Standardization

Technology standardization can be undertaken by a variety of bodies. While standard development organizations such as ISO represent institutional arenas that compel cooperation between diverse actors (Brunsson et al. 2012), they are inadequate for complex and rapidly changing contexts (Weiss and Cargill 1992). Given the prevalence of complexity and change, industry consortia have become increasingly important means of facilitating the swift exploitation of commercial possibilities through voluntarily coordinated efforts (Uotila et al. 2017; Zhao and Xia 2014). These efforts usually endeavor to integrate the processes, resources, and technologies of the heterogeneous actors within an industry (Markus et al. 2006) by producing a vertical technology standard (Bala and Venkatesh 2007; Steinfield et al. 2005). However, the inherent competition among players in any vertical expresses itself in terms of conflicting interests, motives, and preferences (Yoo et al. 2005). These are further exacerbated when a technology standard not only codifies current integrating practices but also seeks to effect innovation by extending the beliefs, visions, and technical knowledge associated with an industry (Grøtnes 2009; Uotila et al. 2017). Such anticipatory standards, which are “developed prior to the existence of markets for compatible products” (Lee and Oh 2006, p. 185), entail a high degree of uncertainty that triggers tensions (Andersson et al. 2008; Saadatmand et al. 2019).

Our literature review yielded 34 relevant papers on technology standardization in the AIS basket of eight journals (2000- 2020) and additional six papers with influential impact on how information systems (IS) researchers have theorized development and diffusion of technology standards. These papers reveal three tensions endemic to technology standardization: development versus diffusion, private versus public, and local versus global (Table 1). All the papers deal with at least one of the three latent tensions in technology standardization, but only a handful take resolutions to these tensions into consideration.

The development versus diffusion tension concerns a struggle between effective development and widespread adoption of a technology standard (Markus et al. 2006. An exemplary study is Aanestad and Blegind Jensen’s (2011) exploration of two initiatives aimed at sharing medical records across hospitals. In both cases, the solution relied on standards for integration of patient information. The first standard, which was developed in a grassroots manner and solved a well-defined problem of relatively small scope, prioritized developing a solution rather than its broad-based diffusion. Nevertheless, the solution diffused successfully to become the industry standard because of its cost-effectiveness, i.e., the standard did not require significant changes to IT infrastructures and adopting hospitals could therefore solve their information access problem quickly and at low cost. In contrast, the second standard was abandoned, despite its top-down development by a working group that boasted broad industry representation, which was expected to ease diffusion. However, the standard was complex and required hospitals to implement it in its entirety, demanding significant changes to hospitals’ IT infrastructures and making implementation costly. These insights suggest that homogeneity and small scale are prerequisites for developing a diffusible technology standard and that a standard capable of solving users’ problems is more likely to find its requisite installed base of heterogeneous actors, albeit over an extended period of time.

The private versus public tension concerns a struggle between protection against competitive rivalry and pronation of shared knowledge (Garud et al. 2002). An exemplary study is Uotila et al.’s (2017) examination of why standard-setting is a failure-prone process. They model the uncertainty that stems from characteristics of the technology, the composition of suppliers, the level of coordination among suppliers, and the imitation costs related to IP rights. Their research shows that convergence to a single standard was in many cases facilitated by assigning powerful actors the authority for coordination and setting a low (or zero) imitation cost for IP rights. However, to serve the public interest by prioritizing the private interests of powerful actors, this resolution strategy may be limited in its effectiveness. For example, it occasionally led to an overly narrow search for alternatives and the selection of a suboptimal technological standard, which created conditions for lock-in that undermined the interests of the industry as a whole.

The local versus global tension concerns a struggle between the sought stability through standardization and the flexibility required by local use. An exemplary study is Malaurent and Avison’s (2016) investigation of the introduction of an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system across branches of a multinational corporation. They report that local system users created and implemented workarounds to facilitate its adoption and use, ultimately thwarting the potential gains of the system’s underlying global standard. Through a coordinated management initiative, however, a number of these workarounds were formalized and assimilated into the global template without compromising the standardization requirements. This response satisfactorily resolved the perceived misfit between the local requirements and the global standard, thereby enabling the successful roll-out of the ERP system. As such, it served as the starting point of deliberate efforts to balance local and global system demands and enable its use across contexts (Monteiro and Rolland 2012; Rolland and Monteiro 2002).

## Dialectic Analysis

There is considerable recognition of tensions in the IS literature on standards in the making (Uotila et al. 2017) but not all tensions are dialectic in nature (Cuganesan 2017). Dialectics implies the paradoxical relationship between opposing forces that represent contradictory yet interrelated elements that need to be pursued jointly (Huq et al. 2017). When tensions are theorized in such terms, a relational ontology that acknowledges a social order in its ongoing becoming is brought to bear (Benson 1977; Cuganesan 2017), which entails that tensions cannot be resolved once and for all (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017; Jay 2013). Instead, their recurrence necessitates continuous responses that deal with them over and over again (Svahn et al. 2017), rendering states of equilibrium temporary at best (Cloutier and Langley 2020; Smith and Lewis 2011).

A consequence of the interdependence between salient tensions and their resolution (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017; Sheep et al. 2017), however, is that a new configuration of practices affords the renewed construction of salient tensions through an inherently recursive process (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013; Van de Ven and Poole 1995). Given such a processual understanding of reality (Benson 1977), where latent tensions that lie dormant are continuously made salient through social construction in ever-changing situated practice (Jay 2013; Lüscher and Lewis 2008), actors have choices with regard not only to accepting or ignoring a perceived tension (Cuganesan et al. 2017; Stadtler and van Wassenhove 2016), but also to responding or not (Smith and Lewis 2011).

There is some evidence of dialectic theorizing in past technology standardization research. Specifically, Wareham et al. (2014) explore human intentionality in the social construction of the binary opposites that define tensions in technology ecosystems. These tensions can be framed either as a dualism that presents trade-off decisions in the form of conflicting alternatives or as a duality that presents complementary and mutually enabling attractors in a holistic system. Dualism implies that the choice between two competing options is a zero-sum game, while duality framing creates a positive-sum game where opposing poles are complements (Farjoun 2010). In contrast, Hanseth et al. (2006, p. 564) portray technology standardization as a selfdestructive process because of tensions that render “outcomes antagonistic to the original aim.” They argue that these paradoxical situations with their unintended consequences arise from the inherently contradictory properties of standards, e.g., simultaneously enabling and constraining (Garud et al. 2002). These consequences “are propagated through multiple, separate networks and finally become reflected—hence the term reflexive [standardization]—back onto what initially triggered them” (Hanseth et al. 2006, p. 566). Reflexive standardization thus signifies a process that produces outcomes antithetical to its goals, e.g., the more the standard seeks to be global, the more it needs to accommodate local variation (Braa et al. 2007).

While these two studies make the interdependence between the opposing poles that constitute a standardization tension apparent, they fall short of analyzing how tensions are brought into being and how their social construction shapes the managerial response to them. Jarzabkowski and Le (2017) suggest that such activities are key to advancing our understanding of and prescriptions for the dialectic management of tensions. Hence, our objective in this paper is to conduct a dialectic analysis that explores how emerging tensions and their resolutions are recursively produced in technology standardization. Smith and Lewis’s (2011)

dialectic model offers a strong conceptual foundation for such an analysis by recognizing the latent and enduring nature of tensions in all social life and by theorizing how these tensions interact with management efforts to resolve them in an ongoing and recursive process (Cloutier and Langley 2020). More specifically, it features foundational concepts that are cyclically related: salient tensions as empirical manifestations of latent tensions, which are triggered by the context and then socially constructed in ways that afford their temporary resolution to cause change to the existing social order.

Smith and Lewis’s (2011) tension-response cycle starts with latent tensions, which reflect the paradoxical nature of all organizational life (Van de Ven and Poole 1995). These latent tensions exist by virtue of the fact that organizing creates distinctions that are nevertheless interdependent (Smith 2014). For example, strategies of exploitation and exploration represent polar opposites but they are also interrelated—the seeds for exploration lie in exploitation and vice versa (Farjoun 2010)—and successful organizing needs to engage both. While the need to hold opposing concepts at the same time generates tensions, organizations can remain oblivious to them provided the tensions remain unperceived (Lüscher and Lewis 2008). Only if they are experienced in some way (i.e., become salient), do latent tensions pose a challenge to an organization’s coherence and integrity (Lewis 2000). We deem the three tensions inherent in technology standardization—i.e., development versus diffusion, private versus public, and local versus global— latent because they manifest in unique and situated ways across the plethora of initiatives reported in the extant literature and, as such, reflect abstractions that may or may not be brought into being in a given empirical context (Smith and Lewis 2011).

In each dialectic cycle, latent tensions become salient by contextual triggers such as change, plurality, and scarcity, and by organizational stakeholders’ adoption of paradoxical frames (Knight and Paroutis 2017; Sheep et al. 2017). Once a tension has been brought into being, which renders it perceptible to stakeholders, one possible response is the refusal to acknowledge it. Also referred to as “repression” (Lewis 2000), such nonacceptance may be based in feelings of inertia, anxiety, or defensiveness. These conditions increase the likelihood that vicious cycles of confusion and inaction are set in motion (Smith and Lewis 2011). However, a tension’s acceptance, which recognizes contradictions and ambiguities as a natural condition of organizing (Smith 2014), draws on the relationship between the salient tension’s opposing poles to identify a management response (Sheep et al. 2017).

A response of “splitting” (Smith and Lewis 2011) or “reaction formation” (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017) separates the opposing poles either temporally or spatially (Lewis 2000). “Opposing” (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013), which implies that confrontations between parties that favor one of the poles is another possible response when a tension is framed in eitheror terms. In contrast, responses like “integrating” (Smith and Lewis 2011), “transcendence” (Lewis 2000), or “adjusting” (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013; Jarzabkowski and Le 2017) are invoked when the opposing poles are framed in both-and terms, i.e., as necessary and complementary (Farjoun 2010). Splitting responses is typically a means of dealing with acute conflict in the immediate term (Smith and Lewis 2011) and selecting one pole over the other reflects defensiveness and produces fragile social structures (Cuganesan et al. 2017). When synergies between the opposing poles can be identified (i.e., a both-and framing), however, interactions among the proponents of either pole are afforded. Under these conditions, integrating responses capable of producing generative dynamics, new opportunities, and novel outcomes becomes feasible (Jay 2013).

Jarzabkowski and Le (2017) encourage dialectic analyses that zoom in on the discursive microprocesses in which actors socially construct tensions by framing their opposing poles as dualities or dualisms, as these acts of social construction determine the effectiveness of management responses to a given tension. However, despite its recognition of actors’ paradoxical cognition and rhetoric, Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model offers limited explanations of how tensions are brought into being and how the relationships between their opposing poles are framed. The technology standardization initiative in the Swedish road haulage context provides a rich empirical case for developing a dialectic theory of technology standardization, which extends Smith and Lewis’s (2011) tension-response cycle with insights into the endeavors of standard-setters to construct tensions and manage them to achieve productive standardization outcomes.

## Action Research

The first author led the technology standardization initiative to varying degrees throughout its twelve-year lifespan (2002-2013). His high level of involvement enabled us to study in situ the recursive dynamics through which tensions and management responses shaped the process. In what follows, we describe the research setting as well as the initiative, and explain our methodological choices around data collection and analysis.

## Research Setting

In the late 1990s, roughly 90% of the Swedish Road Haulage Association’s (SRHA) members were small local firms that operated five or fewer vehicles. Facing mounting competition from European logistics providers, these firms sought more effective integration of embedded, mobile, and stationary IT systems to improve their competitive position (Andersson and Lindgren 2005; Lindgren et al. 2008). Embedded systems were wired into the truck itself (e.g., CAN-bus) to collect sensor data related to fuel consumption, driving time and speed, breaking behavior, and more. Mobile systems represented technologies located in the truck cockpit (e.g., vehicle-mounted communication terminals) that afforded efficient communication between the drivers and dispatchers and between drivers and different stationary systems (e.g., route optimization). Stationary systems were conventional enterprise technologies to control the administrative operations performed by dispatchers and managers (e.g., order management).

The Viktoria Institute embarked on the Mobile-Stationary Initiative (MSI) in 2002 to design and diffuse a technology standard for integrating such systems in road haulage firms (Andersson et al. 2008). The MSI group eventually included sixteen IT vendors (Addmobile, Barkfors, Consafe, Cybercom, DPS, Halda, Hogia, IBS, NL Partner/Locus, MobiOne, Mobistics, Netlink, Pocketmobile, Transics, Transware, and Vehco), two truck manufacturers (Scania and Volvo Trucks), user representatives (the SRHA, the Regional Haulage Association in West Sweden, a number of road haulage firms, and TRB, an industry organization owned by fifteen Swedish road transport organizations), as well as ASN IT & Management (a consulting firm that offered education about ITenabled road transport solutions) and Gatespace (a telematics service provider). While these actors brought considerable ITexperience to the MSI initiative, the researchers had expertise in design-oriented action research (Frisk et al. 2014; Lindgren et al. 2004) and embedded and mobile computing in telematics (Henfridsson and Lindgren 2005; Henfridsson and Lindgren 2010). From the outset, a problem-solving dominant approach (Chiasson et al. 2009; Frisk et al. 2015; Svahn et al. 2015) was taken and the researchers sought to generate insights with both theoretical and practical significance through close collaboration with industry stakeholders. Figure 1 outlines the key activities over the twelve-year initiative.

The organizational structure of the action research project was designed to shift the distribution of power among the participants. While the researchers instigated the technology standardization initiative, authority over it was assigned to a team comprised of practitioners and researchers after the clientresearcher agreement had been signed. The first author served as project coordinator to a greater or lesser extent until the MSI standard was completed in Fall 2012.

## Data Collection

Our data collection proceeded throughout the twelve-year initiative, which allowed us to acquire a nuanced and comprehensive understanding of technology standardization as a dialectic process. We focused on the following data sources: formal interviews, participation in project meetings, work meetings and workshops, emails, and a variety of documents such as strategies, Power Point presentations, funding applications, environmental reports, press releases, popular press articles, module and standard specifications, as well as use and test cases. Additionally, in 2016, we conducted follow-up interviews with key MSI members. The objective was to gain insight into their retrospective assessment of the initiative, especially its longer-term implications. Most of the 30 project meetings and 75 working meetings, and all of the 136 interviews, were audio-recorded and later transcribed. See the Appendix for a summary of the data sources.

## Data Analysis

Our data analysis was a multiyear effort that began during data collection. After the completion of the MSI initiative, we subjected the data to numerous rounds of grounded theorizing, especially constant comparison (Glaser and Strauss 1967), to establish patterns and conceptually coherent explanations of how the standardization process had evolved. The current paper reflects one of the many possible trajectories that were identified during our grounded sensemaking. While our data analysis was initially largely deductive, it became increasingly inductive as the shortcomings of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) foundational concepts became evident.

Initially, we divided the MSI standardization initiative into episodes, each of which involved the cyclically related elements of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model. The first author, who was directly involved in the MSI initiative, made a first pass at dividing the MSI project into different episodes. He then wrote brief narratives of eight episodes each focused on a salient tension, as an empirical manifestation of a latent tension triggered by the context and verbally expressed (i.e., brought into being) in a way that it could be resolved, at least temporarily. The other two authors, who did not participate in the MSI project, then critically reviewed the candidate episodes. They asked questions and proposed alternative narratives to challenge the initial temporal bracketing. After considerable back and forth, the episode boundaries settled into six distinct tension-response cycles.

With the narrative arch in place, we drew on more detailed readings of the data (especially interview transcripts and project documentation) to flesh out the latent and salient tensions that anchored our dialectic analysis of each episode. We then identified in each episode the contextual factor that triggered the discursive production of a latent tension, the social construction of a salient tension, and the management response enacted to resolve it and why. The first author, who had the most nuanced understanding of events, drafted an initial set of tension-response cycles, which were again challenged by the co-authors. It was during these discussions that the shortcomings of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model became evident, leading us to engage in midrange theorizing to accommodate more of the empirical details into a general understanding of technology standardization.

After considerable iteration between the MSI data, Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model, and our appreciation for the microprocesses of framing and responding to salient tensions in the MSI project (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017), we settled on an empirically and conceptually coherent narrative of the dialectic cycle in each episode. To complete our dialectic analysis, we engaged in within-episode and cross-episode comparisons to identify tension-response-outcome patterns particular to technology standardization.

## Analysis Results

We now present the six episodes that constitute the MSI standardization initiative as a series of dialectic tension-response cycles. Table 2 provides a summary of our dialectic analysis.

## Episode #1: Promoting Open Platform (April 2002 – October 2003)

While stationary planning systems (e.g., logistics software) and mobile phones were integral to the operations of Swedish road haulers in the early 2000s, many haulage firms envisioned that the integration of embedded, mobile, and stationary IT systems would improve operational efficiency and help cope with increasing competition from new entrants. Fueled by the trade press and white papers, they believed system integration could streamline dispatcher-driver coordination (e.g., reduce ad hoc, verbal communication), enable real-time resource monitoring (e.g., trace driver behavior and fuel consumption), and facilitate seamless transport data management (e.g., automatically calculate the activity-based costs of a specific delivery). Technology vendors and truck manufacturers also promoted such integration to improve the environmental sustainability of road transport.

However, to tie their heterogeneous IT systems together, road haulers had to negotiate with many different technology vendors and commission them to custom-build integration interfaces. Knowing that this would lead to inherently fragile IT infrastructures and to interfaces that needed updating whenever there was a change in one of the core systems, most road haulage firms were reluctant to invest in system integration. Instead, they resigned themselves to producing the information they needed for decision-making and reporting by manipulating each system’s data manually. To address these inefficiencies around data management, the SRHA was interested in developing a technology standard on behalf of its members. This required collaboration among the diverse stakeholders constituting the Swedish haulage industry.

In April 2002, Vehco—a start-up that had been spun off from Chalmers University of Technology to develop embedded technologies for vehicles (e.g., leveraging onboard sensors to optimize a truck’s fuel consumption)—approached Viktoria to advance a research collaboration aimed at building an IT infrastructure for the Swedish road haulage industry. Vehco had developed a working prototype, EcoHauler, that integrated data from embedded, mobile, and stationary systems and generated various financial and operations reports that haulers were routinely producing manually. While Vehco owned the technology, Viktoria had multiple research partners in the Swedish transport industry—including powerful players like the two major truck manufacturers, Volvo and Scania—that would likely play important roles in a standardization initiative. Moreover, Vehco hoped that partnering with Viktoria would attract requisite funding to expand its prototype.

In fall 2002, after establishing the MSI initiative, Viktoria and Vehco held two seminars with technology vendors and road haulage firms to discuss a vertical standardization agenda. To help develop a common vision, the Viktoria researchers presented the EcoHauler prototype. As expected, the road haulers got excited about the potential value of such a solution to them. However, managers at Volvo Trucks, a prominent sponsor of Viktoria, expressed concern about the Vehco-Viktoria collaboration and its focus on embedded technologies. They regarded Vehco’s prototype, which placed truck-generated sensor data at the center of system integration for road haulage, as a threat to the truck manufacturers’ proprietary technology solutions.

They further accused the researchers of supporting an integration solution that illegally obtained truck sensor data. Because the truck manufacturers only made a minimal number of sensor parameters available to third parties under the Fleet Management System (FMS) standard, Vehco reverse-engineered the truck’s CAN-bus to get the embedded sensor data needed for its solution:

The truck manufacturers gave us a fuel consumption estimation like every 10 seconds or even less frequently … my interpretation is that the manufacturers felt they had to supply the FMS standard parameters, but they decided to do it so that no one could use them in any meaningful way. They don’t want anyone to get that vehicle data. They want to keep it for themselves, to compete. (Managing Director of Vehco)

To develop the functionality road haulers needed to compete with the new entrants, Vehco bypassed the FMS data sources. This led the truck manufacturers to raise concerns about the EcoHauler’s potential risk to truck integrity and safety:

You can sniff any network … the problem with this approach is to get the data quality you need. Is it possible to interpret these data properly? Vehco reverse-engineered the truck’s CAN bus and to be honest both Volvo and Scania as well as all members of the FMS consortium argued that if you do that, you’re voiding the warranty of the vehicle owner. (Telematics Manager at Volvo Trucks)

These disagreements over EcoHauler reflected shifts in the industry players’ respective positions (change as trigger). The prototype highlighted the market threat a small start-up like Vehco potentially posed to the powerful truck manufacturers’ telematics services. This compelled the truck manufacturers to be explicit about the conditions (e.g., limits on embedded data) under which they would participate in the MSI initiative:

The data that originates from the truck is always stored in our own systems. That’s why we get into conflict here. We feel that this data is something that we can make into a unique service. That’s something that we’re not prepared to give away ... we don’t want to leave this business opportunity to someone else. (Telematics Manager at Volvo Trucks)

Even though the Viktoria researchers recognized that the EcoHauler prototype responded well to the Swedish road haulers’ information requirements (e.g., automatically reporting on the cost of a given delivery), they had to concede that Vehco’s development approach (reengineering the CAN bus data to circumvent the truck manufacturers’ data protections) was untenable. As the truck manufacturers owned the data that was needed to integrate embedded parameters into the standard and they resisted making it public, the conflict between the collective interests of the road haulers and the proprietary interests of the truck manufacturers became salient. Framed in either-or terms, this tension manifested the private-public latent tension.

Influenced by the truck manufacturers’ position of power and influence in the road haulage industry, and the status their involvement lent the standardization initiative, the MSI group chose in favor of the truck manufacturers, forgoing the inclusion of embedded-in-the-truck parameters (splitting response). By compromising its vision of seamlessly integrating embedded, mobile, and stationary technologies in Swedish road haulage, the standardization initiative survived, despite some defections. A third-party telematics service provider, Gatespace, questioned the value of an integration standard that excluded sensor data from the connected truck:

The MSI initiative now focuses primarily on what solutions can be applied today, whereas very little attention is given to design ideas about alternative and innovative services for the future. (Managing Director of Gatespace)

Arguing that the rescoped MSI initiative substantially restricted the industry’s opportunities for digital innovation, Gatespace departed from the MSI group in September 2003. Nevertheless, the majority of MSI members who represented the various facets necessary to integrate the mobile and stationary technologies shared the belief that a standard that integrated a limited subset of the road hauler’s IT systems would still be of value (functional outcome).

## Episode #2: Evaluating Available Standard (November 2003 – June 2004)

Later in 2003, the government agency VINNOVA awarded the MSI initiative the funding for which it had applied. A key condition of the funding was that the MSI initiative would seek to leverage standards that were already in use in the road haulage industry. The MSI group therefore embarked on a study of successful integration projects within the industry. At the same time, the MSI initiative’s newfound legitimacy as a government-funded enterprise led to membership expansion. Stationary (i.e., IBS and Transware) and mobile (i.e., MobiOne, Mobistics, and Transics) IT vendors with a collectively significant installed base in the Swedish road haulage industry joined the group.

A detailed analysis of seven integration projects revealed that these had only produced modest technological innovation and no solution had fully integrated mobile and stationary systems. As all projects represented proprietary, vendorspecific interfaces, the findings reinforced the value of an open, vendor-agnostic vertical standard.

Nevertheless, the review identified Pharos Mobile as having the most potential to satisfy the integration functionality that the MSI members envisioned. It had two key advantages: several members were already familiar with Pharos Mobile, and it relied on XML (Extensible Markup Language) messaging, rendering its architecture highly flexible and lightweight. MSI therefore organized a workshop for its members where the developer of Pharos Mobile, e-Com Logistics, presented the standard.

While the MSI members recognized the merits of a messaging-based architecture, they noted that the structure and content of the Pharos Mobile schemas would require significant modifications to be usable by the road haulers. Pharos Mobile had been designed for haulage contractors (e.g., DHL and Schenker) who executed routine and predefined transport assignments where information needs varied little. This meant that the standard primarily focused on effective order management enabled by stationary backend technologies. A report summarized the workshop participants’ deliberations:

Developed and promoted by e-Com Logistics, the Pharos Mobile is an integration standard specifically intended for transport services by big logistics providers. For us, the system is too large, too detailed, and yet too small … simply put, it focuses on routinebased cargo delivery regulated through long-term contracts.

In contrast, typically executing last mile road transports, the small road haulage firms that the MSI standard was meant to serve, needed integration support for technologies that could dynamically support navigation and route planning (as the delivery addresses were highly variable), as well as the monitoring of driver behavior and performance (as margins were small):

Some of these mobile data types are important to associate with individual assignments in the transport business system. GPS coordinates are critical from a quality perspective. Odometer readings are interesting for accounting purposes. For example, when you think you’re not driving the distance you charge for … quality control is key to make sure that the driver does not deviate. (Representative of stationary system vendor)

Vinnova’s funding condition that had compelled the MSI team to perform a market analysis of extant standards, triggered the discontinuity that set this dialectic cycle (scarcity as trigger) in motion. As the stakeholders of the MSI project made sense of this disruption, they socially constructed a salient tension whose opposing poles were adapting an established standard (Pharos Mobile) that was relevant to the wider transport sector, versus developing a new one specific to small road haulers. This tension was framed in both-and terms: while the Pharos Mobile standard could not be adopted as-is, there were nevertheless a number of feasible alternatives for leveraging this extant solution (e.g., accepting some features, adapting others, and developing yet others from scratch). This tension was a manifestation of the local-global latent tension inherent to technology standardization.

After careful research, the MSI members concluded that the changes needed to adapt the Pharos Mobile standard to the road haulage industry were so substantial that it would be easier to start from scratch and develop a local, industryspecific integration solution. However, despite rejecting the schemas set by the Pharos Mobile standard, the MSI group nevertheless adopted the standard’s messaging architecture (integrating response).

By and large, the MSI members supported the decision to adopt an established, general-purpose architecture but to develop an industry-specific data schema from scratch (architectural outcome). This was deemed both technically and organizationally feasible, as some of the IT vendors in the MSI network already used XML to specify their integration interfaces and many more were following suit. The chosen integration format afforded all IT vendors an equal opportunity to earn road haulers’ integration business because any IT vendor could conceivably develop the interface between a hauler’s mobile and stationary systems.

## Episode #3: Protecting Collective Action (July 2004 – March 2006)

In July 2004, MSI succeeded at bringing users on board. They recruited TRB, an industry organization owned by fifteen Swedish road transporters. The promise of user participation and commitment energized the existing MSI members who were predominantly technology vendors. TRB was regarded as a credible representative of road haulers; it had successfully engaged in political negotiations about environmental regulations and transport policies on the road haulers’ behalf. TRB’s involvement, bolstered by the fact that the new manager of the MSI initiative was a former IT manager at TRB, however, also put the MSI group under pressure to deliver an integration solution.

To expedite the standardization process, the MSI group examined the proprietary XML-based interfaces that its members had developed. By summer 2005, the group concluded that the interface developed by Hogia, a vendor of stationary systems, was the best to emulate. It boasted an innovative XML structure, which enabled flexible ways of representing and relating different data sources from embedded, mobile, and stationary systems. As explained by one Hogia representative, adopting Hogia’s XML structure as the baseline for the MSI standard was expedient because it represented the de facto standard thanks to its large install base:

We’ve developed an XML structure ... a schema that is rather like MSI. We’ve put a lot of effort into our interface for a few years. Our view is that our own XML schema is a de facto standard because there are so many customers running it already. And the MSI interface is just another variant.

In September 2005, however, the owner of Hogia realized that the company’s proprietary XML interface was now being used as the basis for an open industry standard:

The simplest way of [developing the standard] was to use Hogia’s XML structure as the first blueprint for the MSI standard or at least parts of it. This was consistent with our agreement with them that they could contribute things to the project that then would become project property. Unfortunately, the founder and owner of Hogia wasn’t aware of it … this was apparently a middle management decision. (MSI Group Manager representing TRB)

Blindsided, Hogia’s owner called for an immediate halt to the MSI group’s work and demanded an investigation into whether the evolving standard infringed on Hogia’s IP:

We’ve always believed that the standardization initiative is a research project led by Viktoria … but now it presents itself as a commercial initiative that involves actors who compete with us. Each year we invest considerable amounts of money to develop and implement standardized interfaces together with our partners and customers. This has resulted in a situation where hundreds of firms are building their integration solutions based on our standards. The main reason why we initially shared our integration knowledge with Viktoria was because we trusted it would continue operating as a research institute. (email from the owner of Hogia)

Making sense of the disturbance caused by the differences in interpretation between Hogia’s owner and the company’s representatives in the MSI initiative (plurality as trigger), the MSI members constructed the salient tension of proprietary versus community interests. Issues of identity stood in the foreground. Vis-à-vis the end users that had just joined the MSI initiative, Hogia identified with the IT vendors whose mission was to develop an industry standard that would enhance Swedish road haulers’ competitiveness. Hogia’s willingness to share its XML schema was in line with its identification with the MSI group. However, MSI’s recognition of Hogia’s integration interface as best-in-class and the de facto standard distinguished the firm within the collective.

The conflict over Hogia’s IP rights escalated and Hogia threatened legal action and actively sought to shut down the MSI initiative by taking its case to the institutions that funded MSI (i.e., SWICT and Viktoria). Having resolved that there was no case to answer, the MSI members developed a unified position in an open letter, which they signed and sent to Hogia. At the same time, they invited Hogia to a reconciliation meeting to reestablish the collaboration. This conciliatory response to Hogia’s cease-and-desist demands was predicated on the inseparability of Hogia’s interface from its MSI derivative. It was impossible to disentangle them, making splitting—the solution that Hogia was pursuing with its efforts to shut down the MSI initiative—infeasible. Moreover, Hogia’s either-or stance vis-à-vis the MSI group made integration a challenging response in the near term.

The MSI group’s offer of reconciliation reflected its capability to endure the uncertainty it faced with respect to the standard’s and the initiative’s future, as well as its desire to resolve the conflict amicably (suspension response). Declining the MSI group’s invitation to reconcile, Hogia continued to threaten legal action. This created an impasse.

Paradoxically, the more Hogia pursued the MSI initiative’s closure, the more cohesive and unified the MSI group members became. The MSI members were increasingly convinced of the standard’s value to the road haulage industry, which meant that Hogia’s efforts to shutter the initiative backfired. Rather than being perceived as the aggrieved party that had been swindled out of its IP, Hogia came to be seen as actively hurting the Swedish road haulage industry’s efforts to improve its competitiveness. With the recent addition of TRB as the MSI representative of the actual end users (who were also Hogia’s current and potential customers), this image was potentially damaging to Hogia. To maintain its reputation, Hogia thus opted to drop its impending lawsuit. The company also withdrew from the MSI initiative.

The fact that the MSI group had overcome this existential threat energized its members and their standard development activities accelerated yet again. In light of the recent threat to its existence, however, the member organizations recognized the need for a more stable management framework and more principled development, maintenance, and diffusion practices. By the end of summer 2007, the MSI group was thus formally transformed into a standardization consortium (organizational outcome) financed via member fees. A six-member board of directors was appointed, as well as a chairman and a technical committee composed of eight members. The board of directors was responsible for the strategic management of the industry consortium, whereas the technical committee coordinated the design of the MSI standard.

## Episode #4: Building Adaptation Capability (September 2007 – February 2008)

The MSI initiative’s increasingly positive image, which was produced in no small measure through its newfound cohesiveness, made it relatively easy to attract new funding. VINNOVA offered the MSI group additional support to diffuse the integration standard among vendors and users. The group also received a grant from SWICT, which held the standardization initiative up as an exemplar of successful applied IT research. The expectation of the funding to move the MSI initiative from the development into the diffusion stage triggered a discontinuity (change as trigger) as the implementation of the new standard entailed significant changes to MSI members’ extant practices. Specifically, members had historically propagated their unique solutions for specific user needs. For example, if a provider of a stationary solution was asked to integrate its system with the mobile technology used by a given road hauler, they developed an interface that met the customer’s requirements and minimized the need for changes to their own products:

Vendors of mobile and stationary systems alike seek to be as adaptable as possible… and road haulers use their tailor-made systems in many ways. It’s fair to say that the technology vendors are simply too attentive to the local and situated requirements of their customers. This, in turn, makes it very challenging to develop an industry standard, simply because it appears almost impossible for the vendors to reject individual use patterns of road haulers that they actually built themselves over time. (IT Manager at LBC Frakt)

For IT vendors, developing customer-specific, local solutions represented opportunities for differentiation and enhanced competitiveness. Indeed, custom interfaces were expressions of an IT vendor’s customer centricity, which, paradoxically, was at odds with the MSI consortium’s interest in identifying the generic features of mobile-stationary integration in order to make IT solutions more interchangeable. With the IT vendors’ distinctiveness at stake, the negotiations over the parameters to be included in the new standard’s XML schema (i.e., content) were contentious. Most IT vendors advocated for a standard that could accommodate the peculiarities of their technology so as to maintain the design of their extant mobile and stationary systems, as well as their installed base of integration interfaces.

Despite the risk of the standard becoming an agglomeration of technology-specific parameters that satisfied the vendors but ultimately undermined the goal of achieving a vendor-agnostic interface desired by the road haulers, the IT vendors maintained that accommodating their specific parameters in the standard benefited their customers. They argued that global integration standards were likely to limit the flexibility with which vendors could respond to the road haulers’ idiosyncratic needs:

Customers usually say they would like to report this newfound business-critical information. In those situations, we add a new field to our enterprise system to allow such information to be handled, displayed, and reported … this means that the standard needs to be changed too, which requires systematic maintenance and continuous development efforts. The problem is that road haulers won’t wait half a year for that field to change, because by then they probably have already lost their customers. You know, we’re solving problems in real time. (Business Manager at Locus)

Making sense of the discontinuity generated by shifting the focus from developing the standard to actually using it for system integration brought into being a salient tension that revolved around achieving backward compatibility with the extant systems and interfaces versus designing the standard for reach. This reflected an instantiation of the latent global-local tension, which manifested as a duality, implying that its opposing poles could be reconciled by designing a standard consisting of core and peripheral elements (integrating response). Developing the architectural solution of a context schema, globally defined business terms (i.e., core) could be adapted by referring to a locally defined sub-schema (i.e., periphery):

The flexibility afforded by the context schema feature was key to keeping the actors’ commitment and lead them to continue negotiating the semantic content of the industry standard. If we instead had started the design process by identifying a given number of integration situations and then sought to define them all in a very precise way, it’s unlikely that the standardization effort would have survived. (MSI Group Manager representing TRB)

The context schema paved the way for a more gradual transformation of the industry’s IT infrastructure. While the vendors admitted that the context schema made their angstridden separation from proprietary interfaces less troublesome, it nevertheless prompted intense discussion of who was responsible for defining and managing local context specifications. After careful consideration, the MSI group assigned this responsibility to the stationary system vendors (architectural outcome).

## Episode #5: Implementing Industry Standard (Mar 2008 – June 2010)

In early 2008, LBC Frakt, a road haulage contractor and a member organization of TRB, decided to implement an IT infrastructure that applied the MSI standard. LBC Frakt’s managing director and its IT manager, both of whom had attended MSI meetings, actively sought to move the standardization process forward by requiring their integration partners to realize the promise of a vendoragnostic mobile-stationary interface.

Given the scope of the integration effort at LBC Frakt, this project represented a potentially lucrative contract for IT vendors, resulting in a spate of new MSI membership applications. In order to protect existing members, who had invested years in developing the standard, new vendors needed to apply for membership and state their potential contribution to the standardization effort. Of the six vendors that applied, only two were granted membership. Also, Hogia rejoined the MSI group as the standard gained increasing attention and the road haulers’ demand for MSIbased integration appeared increasingly inevitable.

With both established and pending MSI group members bidding on its integration project, LBC Frakt was dismayed to learn that none of the proposals would implement the MSI standard; they all relied on the proprietary interfaces that vendors had implemented in the past. The vendors reluctance to deploy the MSI standard was due to the standard’s complexity and bloatedness. For example, a core transaction, called “assignment” in the MSI standard, included an extensive set of mandatory parameters for which a typical road hauler’s order management systems did not have matching data. These mandatory parameters were thus superfluous in a typical integration. These and other design anomalies could be traced back to the context schema solution developed in Episode 4:

I’m really disappointed to be honest … we represent the users of the MSI standard and we’re frustrated and even angry. The simple reason is that we cannot use [the MSI standard] to improve our services to transport buyers. It looks like the vendors have just spent the time making sure their own concepts and terms are part of the standard … in terms of how to integrate systems there’s virtually nothing in it yet. This is far from what we expected … the hard work is left for someone else to take care of. (IT Manager at LBC Frakt)

The different perspectives (plurality as trigger) on the MSI standard, which was sparked by LBC Frakt’s new role as an actual customer rather than merely a member of the MSI group, set sensemaking in motion that brought the salient tension of maintaining extant IT vendor’s practices (i.e., inertia, stability) versus moving towards new practices (i.e., change, innovation) into being. This tension reflected the latent development-diffusion tension. While broad-based participation in the development of a uniform interface ensured that the standard was comprehensive and covered the diversity of technologies and transaction types that characterized the industry, effective diffusion relied on a lean standard that was relatively easy to implement. However, as diffusion relies on development, the tension’s opposing poles were framed as complementary.

The managing director of LBC Frakt decided to reject all bids and send a letter to the MSI group officially criticizing its members for paying only lip service to the standardization effort. Against this backdrop, the MSI leadership began pursuing a modularization strategy. Reflecting a both-and solution, modularization subdividing the MSI standard into parameter bundles needed to facilitate the completion of key business processes across multiple systems (integrating response). In other words, instead of having one standard cover all the systems that constituted road haulers’ IT infrastructures and all the transactions that needed to be processed by them, the MSI standard was arranged into three transaction sets. For example, the order management module dealt with order fulfillment, i.e., scheduling deliveries so they occurred within the customer-specified time frame and calculating the delivery costs of an order. In contrast, the resource management module processed information about the hauler’s heterogeneous array of resources that needed to be tracked and accounted for during an order’s fulfillment (e.g., driver, fuel, and truck). Such modularization reduced the complexity of the XML structures while maintaining the standard’s scope (architectural outcome).

## Episode #6: Pursuing Green Agenda (July 2010- May 2013)

Following the strategy of modularizing the MSI standard, the MSI group identified new hauler practices that might be supported by the standard. One such practice was interhauler load sharing for more environmentally friendly road transport. This appeared to signal a change in the standardization context, as transport buyers increasingly demanded green solutions. This emerging market condition revitalized the interest in embedded technologies:

Road haulers that have started to use the MSI order module now ask whether we can help them in the area of environmental sustainability. They want to calculate, for example, $C O _ { 2 }$ emissions from assignments. In terms of our current interface, we probably deal with 98% of the data needed for greener transports, but we currently miss the 10 fields with necessary vehicle data. We’re aware of the unfortunate unwillingness of truck manufacturers to engage in collaborative innovation and to share their unique resources, but let’s hope we’re able to find a way forward this time. (MSI Group Manager representing TRB)

Considering the proprietary-vs.-collective-interest tension that had previously scuttled the MSI agenda with respect to embedded technology (Episode #1), the MSI group explored alternative avenues for developing modules for green road haulage. They identified DPS, a third-party route optimization service provider, as a potential source of expertise in this space. The Viktoria researchers, who had previously backed down from including embedded systems into the MSI standard, hoped that the increasing market demand for environmental sustainability would persuade the truck manufacturers to cooperate this time around. They therefore organized meetings and seminars to spur interest in route optimization among MSI members.

While these activities led Scania and Volvo Trucks to reveal more about their business strategies, the truck manufacturers ultimately declined to share additional parameters from the trucks’ embedded systems. They justified their resistance by questioning the value of route optimization to advancing greener transport:

We believe that the order module is very valuable, and the modularization approach is generally fine … except for the emerging route optimization agenda. What does such functionality really offer? Which embedded parameters are needed here? We know Volvo is concerned too, simply because the business case for route optimization is unclear to both of us. (Fleet Management Director of Scania)

It became evident that the truck manufacturers wanted to leverage their exclusive ownership of embedded data to develop increasingly sophisticated solutions for ecodriving. The truck manufacturers’ refusal to share such data triggered a discontinuity (change as trigger), and this impasse set in motion the social construction of the following salient tension: protecting individual firms’ financial interests versus embracing social and environmental responsibility. Framed as an either-or dualism, this salient tension was a manifestation of the private-public tension inherent in technology standardization (latent tension). Once again, the Viktoria researchers decided to forgo the inclusion of embedded data into the standard, instead limiting the route optimization module to the parameters that constituted the FMS standard (splitting response). This meant that the standardization initiative could continue without further interruption. In late 2012, the Viktoria researchers were thus able to hand over the order management, the resource management, and the route optimization modules to the MSI group.

Despite the successful modularization of the standard, the decision to yet again limit the standard to the integration of mobile and stationary systems seemed to rule out any future inclusion of data from embedded systems (functional outcome). The inability to elicit full cooperation from the truck manufacturers made many MSI members wonder whether the standard would ever be able to live up to its full potential. Disillusioned, the technology vendors’ interest in the standardization effort thus began to wane. They made little to no effort to push the diffusion of the business process modules. In Spring 2013, the MSI group’s board of directors decided to terminate the standardization initiative. Nevertheless, the MSI members did not perceive the standardization initiative a wasted effort, instead claiming that their participation in this industry-wide effort had provided them valuable insights that had enabled them to improve their proprietary integration solutions and bolster their competitive position.

## Theory Development

Technology standardization is recognized as a dialectic process characterized by conflicts, contradictions, and discontinuities (Narayanan and Chen 2012). Prior standardization studies suggest its disruptive dynamics are rooted in three latent tensions (Table 1): development versus diffusion activities (Markus et al. 2006), private versus public interests (Garud et al. 2002), and local versus global solutions (Hanseth et al. 2006). Unfortunately, few technology standardization studies engage in dialectic theorizing (Hanseth et al. 2006; Wareham et al. 2014) and none of these offers insights that capture the complex dynamics of these dialectics. The objective of our paper is therefore to provide a dialectic account of technology standardization that explicates how salient tensions are brought into being and managed through a social construction process (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017; Jay 2013; Lüscher and Lewis 2008). In the context of vertical standardization (Markus et al. 2006), this social construction of tensions and responses involves a wide range of stakeholders with inherently contradictory perspectives and interests (Smith et al. 2017; Stadtler and van Wassenhove 2016).

Drawing on Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model to analyze a twelve-year initiative to develop and diffuse a technology standard within the Swedish road haulage industry, we have addressed the following research question: How are the tensions inherent in technology standardization brought into being and managed during the production of a vertical standard? We now engage in midrange theorizing (Gregor 2006; Grover and Lyytinen 2015) by identifying key patterns from the empirical analysis and combine these with insights from extant literature to develop a dialectic model of technology standardization (Figure 2) with descriptions of the activities that constitute this recursive process (Table 4). Subsequently, we discuss the contributions of this theory to the literature on technology standardization and the dialectics literature at large.

## Patterns of Triggering Latent Tensions

Our analysis of the MSI initiative shows that all of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) three contextual triggers—plurality, change, and scarcity—contributed to the creation of the six episodes (Table 2) and that these episodes resulted from the triggering of all three latent tensions evident in the technology standardization literature (Table 1). The analysis reveals a toggling between the latent tensions of private-public (Garud et al. 2002) and local-global (Hanseth et al. 2006) in the first four episodes. This pattern is interrupted by the MSI group’s effort to move from development to diffusion of the standard in Episode #5 when LBC Frakt became its first customer. In Episode #6, the pattern seems to resume, starting with the private-public tension.

The episodes in which the private-public tension was activated are characterized by the MSI group seeking to adopt a ready-made integration solution that had been developed by a third-party actor (i.e., Vehco in #1, Hogia in #3, and DPS in #4). While this strategy was pursued to expedite standard development, these technical solutions had embedded certain interests in them. For example, Vehco’s interest in working with Viktoria was to leverage the latter’s established relationships with the truck manufacturers and thereby get better access to embedded data. Similarly, Hogia’s interest was to validate its image as the developer of the industry’s de facto standard, and DPS was motivated to participate in the MSI group to expand its route management software to include truck data. Importantly, each manifestation of the private-public tension revolved around data or IP rights (e.g., Hogia’s threat of a lawsuit in Episode #3).

In contrast, in Episodes #2 and #4 where the activation of the local-global tension caused disruption, the MSI group was primarily engaged in developing the standard in a bottomup, collaborative manner (rather than adapting an extant integration solution). In Episode #2, the group collectively evaluated standards that had been implemented in road transport and arrived at a technological decision to adopt an XML architecture. Similarly, in Episode #4, the group made an architectural decision to accommodate individual software vendors’ specific needs for local adaptations by using a context schema. The relatively unconstrained decision-making implied in these bottom-up development activities triggered disagreements about members autonomy and their possibility for differentiation. These conflicts were a manifestation of the latent local-global tension (Braa et al. 2007; Hanseth and Bygstad 2015).

The empirical context might explain the toggling between these two tensions as follows: after a contest over data and IP rights had been confronted (#1 and #3), the standardization body fell back on the collective to accomplish its goal (#2 and #4). However, collaboration among competitors, which is inherently part of developing a vertical standard (Grøtnes 2009; Markus et al. 2006; Uotila et al. 2017), is challenging and raises disagreements over how members can develop and maintain a competitive position in light of standardization (Steinfield et al. 2011). Exhausted by the local-global tension over how much the collective can control individual actors, the standardizing body is likely to look for ways to expedite and simplify the process, leading them back to favoring a more top-down development process where extant integration solutions (e.g., an established interface) shape the direction of the work. However, such development opens the door to the public-private tension over IP rights (Liu et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2012).

This explanation can also help us understand why the publicprivate tension was the cause of the disruption in Episode #5. Even though individual companies bid on the LBC Frakt contract to integrate its mobile and stationary technologies, the goal of moving from development to diffusion was a collective one. The bidders’ unwillingness to implement the MSI standard painted the collective in a dismal light. The MSI group’s search for a new actor (i.e., DPS) with a readymade interface standard that would expedite the execution of its new modularization strategy can thus be understood in terms of the group’s weariness with developing the standard collectively in a bottom-up manner.

## Patterns of Constructing Salient Tensions

Our analysis of the six episodes demonstrates that bringing salient tensions into being requires the social construction of the tension, particularly naming its opposing poles and framing the relationship between them. Prior technology standardization literature identifies two frames: the either-or dualism and the both-and duality (Wareham et al. 2014). However, as we elaborate below, in Hogia’s departure over IP rights in Episode #3, there is evidence of another relationship between the poles, namely “the more of A, the more of B.” Even though Hanseth et al. (2006) provide a compelling example of such a relationship, it has thus far not been recognized and conceptualized as a distinct type of opposition in prior standardization research. Drawing on Carlo et al.’s (2012) adaptation of Bosserman’s (1995) dialectic processes, we identify three oppositional logics—polarization, complementarity, and mutuality—according to which the salient tensions in the MSI initiative were framed (Table 3).

Polarization reflects an either-or logic grounded in dualism where one pole negates the other (Wareham et al. 2014). Framing a tension in polarizing terms thus invites actors to select one pole over another. For example, in Episode #1 such framing was evident in MSI’s reaction to the manifestation of the private-public tension that arose between the interests of the incumbents (Scania and Volvo Trucks) and the smaller players of the collective (Gatespace and Vehco). Standardization research provides numerous examples of framing of tensions in polarizing terms (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2012, Uotila et al. 2017).

In contrast, the complementarity logic reflects an opposition where the poles support one another, i.e., both are needed for a desired outcome (Farjoun 2010). Framing a tension in such both-and terms invites actors to integrate based on spatial or temporal separation by vacillating between the two poles over time (Putnam 2015). Considering Episode #4, MSI members applied a complementarity framing of the local-global tension. This echoes prior standardization research (e.g., Malaurent and Avison 2016; Silsand and Ellingsen 2014).

The mutuality logic frames the opposing poles as constitutively entangled so that one pole defines the other and neither pole can exist without the other. This means that the poles are inseparable; they not only reflect one another like mirror images, but also emerge together (Carlo et al. 2012). Paradoxically, a mutuality relationship between opposing poles implies that an increase in the value of one pole simultaneously increases the value of the other pole (Hanseth et al. 2006). For example, the more a technology standard seeks to be global, the more it needs to accommodate the local (Braa et al. 2007; Malaurent and Avison 2016).

In our analysis, we present these three oppositional logics in rather discrete terms by associating one logic with each episode (Table 2). However, closer consideration of the unfolding of each episode reveals that the logics may actually operate simultaneously. For example, in Episode #3, attempts to frame the conflict over Hogia’s IP rights reflected polarization, complementarity, and mutuality. Hogia’s initial defection and its threat to sue the MSI group reflected the polarization logic: either Hogia was accorded the respect worthy of the developer of the de facto integration standard (distinctiveness), or Hogia was seen as an equal player in the MSI group (homogeneity), thereby losing its leadership position within the industry. Given that the latter pole was inconceivable for Hogia, they adopted a polarization framing that implied shutting down the MSI group’s attempt to assert ownership over the XML schema. However, the effectiveness of this framing was compromised as Hogia’s proprietary integration interface was already public. Had Hogia kept its XML schema private (like the truck manufacturers did with their embedded data), a polarizing framing might have become dominant.

Our analysis suggests that Hogia was motivated to share its proprietary standard with the MSI group as long as its members perceived its XML interface as the de facto standard in the Swedish road haulage industry. The MSI group’s adoption of its interface would thus have reinforced Hogia’s distinctiveness. Indicative of a complementarity logic, group membership (homogeneity) was crucial for Hogia to maintain its perceived leadership position (distinctiveness). However, while this duality framing drove the MSI group’s negotiations with Hogia over the standard’s ownership, it did not define the tension, presumably because Hogia was too entrenched in the polarizing logic and thus unwilling to find a both-and compromise.

In the end, as articulated in our analysis, mutuality won out in the social construction of the tension: the more Hogia asserted its IP rights and threatened the MSI initiative, the more the MSI standardization agenda rose in perceived value. MSI members who had previously been rather passive now became more actively engaged. They increasingly identified with the collective. By standing with and for the road haulage firms, the MSI collective constructed Hogia as unreasonable in its pursuit of distinctiveness within the group, especially as this came at the cost of the road haulage industry’s competitiveness. This sentiment eventually threatened Hogia’s image with its road haulage customers and the company therefore was compelled to back down and allow the MSI initiative to continue pursuing its agenda.

## Patterns of Managing Salient Tensions

Considering our empirical results (Table 2), our analysis reveals a one-to-one mapping between the oppositional logic that framed a given salient tension and the subsequent management response. The polarization logic (either-or) led to splitting in Episodes #1 and #6 as a defensive response that provides only short-term relief (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013; Lewis 2000; Smith and Lewis 2011). Splitting tends to push aside or defer the conflict instead of resolving it (Cuganesan et al. 2017; Jay 2013). This is because privileging one of the opposing poles inevitably triggers counterdemands to attend to the tension’s other side (Jarzabkowski and Le 2017; Lüscher and Lewis 2008). These challenges with splitting were evident in Episode #1, where MSI’s management sided with the interests of the truck manufactures rather than the standardization collective. The decision to push the embedded agenda aside at the beginning of the initiative merely sidelined the conflict between the truck manufacturers and the rest of the MSI group, which ultimately led to the dissipation of the collective (Episode #6).

In Episodes #2, #4, and #5, the complementarity logic (bothand) invoked an integrating response, which dialectics researchers describe as a deliberate attempt to find synergies and linkages that accommodate both poles of a tension (Calabretta et al. 2017; Smith 2014; Smith and Lewis 2011). Typically regarded as a proactive strategy in dialectics, integrating is recognized as more sophisticated and more difficult to implement than splitting (Jarzabkowski et al. 2013; Jarzabkowski and Le 2017). In all three of these episodes, MSI members pursued an architectural strategy whose coreperiphery modularity meant that the standard would be sufficiently flexible to accommodate the individual IT vendor’s specific requirements (e.g., XML-based architecture in #2, context schema in #4 and modularization in #5), while achieving a stable interface between a wide range of current and future systems. These integrating responses, all of which accommodated both local and global needs, seemed to resolve these tensions, albeit temporarily as the local-global tension resurfaced.

Our elaboration of Episode #3 above indicates that the mutuality framing (more A, more B logic) and its consequential management response were eventually effective for the MSI group. Given the severity of the conflict between Hogia and the MSI group, such framing appeared to be the group’s last resort. Nevertheless, it created the space for Hogia to rejoin the standardization initiative a few years later. Interestingly, neither the standardization nor the dialectic literatures conceptualize a response that flows from a mutuality framing of a salient tension. We suggest that the response we observed is captured well by “suspension,” which entails actors temporarily bracketing the doubt and uncertainty a tension generates (Möllering 2001). Unlike splitting, which seeks to eliminate uncertainty, suspension embraces ambiguity while containing it. It involves letting a given tension play out to stimulate learning and to create opportunities for responses that have yet to be revealed (Möllering 2005). It thus creates a workable certainty that allows diverse actors to carry on without fully grasping the complexity and potential impact of a given paradoxical situation (Lüscher and Lewis 2008).

Hanseth et al.’s (2006) reflexive standardization implicitly builds on the oppositional logic of mutuality, but the research offers only splitting as a possible management response. However, splitting denies the constitutive entanglement between the two poles. Hence, we suggest that suspension is a more appropriate response that allows insights and options to emerge by requiring participants to hold the opposing poles in tension without seeking premature resolution or closure. Holding the paradoxical forces in suspension compels participants to live with the discomfort of ambiguity.

## Patterns of Impacting Technology Standardization

Our review of the episodes that constitute the MSI initiative (Table 2) reveals that the initiative’s tension-response cycles generated three types of standardization outcomes: changes in the functionality (Braa et al. 2007; Grisot et al. 2014; Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010; Malaurent and Avison 2016) or architecture (Aanestad and Blegind Jensen 2011; Hanseth and Monteiro 1997; Malhotra et al. 2007; Monteiro and Rolland 2012) of the standard, and changes to the organization of the involved stakeholders (Markus and Bui 2012; Steinfield et al. 2005; Steinfield et al. 2011; Uotila et al. 2017).

Functional outcomes relate to the vision, scope, and purpose of the standard (Braa et al. 2007; Grisot et al. 2014). They represent the bookends of the MSI initiative as the result of standardization efforts in Episodes #1 and #6 excluded the parameters of embedded systems, which would have afforded the integration of data emanating from the trucks. Episodes #2, #4, and #5, in contrast, changed the architecture of the standard, i.e., structuring the standard into related building blocks that together achieve the requisite functionality (Hanseth and Monteiro 1997; Malhotra et al. 2007). Decisions to adopt the XML protocol (#2), implementing context schema to accomplish backward compatibility with the vendors’ various implementations (#4), and segmenting the standard into modules (#5) do not represent simple changes to the standard. Rather, they reflect overarching decisions to accomplish the paradoxical goals of stability-and-flexibility and uniformity-and-variety. In other words, all three of these episodes involve architectural changes to achieve a duality of purpose in the standard’s structure. Finally, Episode #3 reveals changes to the organization of the standardization initiative’s members (Markus and Bui 2012; Steinfield et al. 2011). Prompted by Hogia’s attempt to shutter the MSI initiative to preserve its IP over its integration interface, the MSI group sought to professionalize by becoming a consortium with a formal management structure.

In addition to these categories of standardization outcomes, we identified a potential pattern in the enactment of the changes they represent in the MSI case: The outcome was functional when the salient tension was framed as a dualism (either-or) and a resolution strategy of splitting was activated, the outcome was architectural when the salient tension was framed in duality (both-and) terms and an integrating response was enacted, and the outcome was related to the organization of the standardization body in the case of a mutuality (the more A, the more B) framing of a tension’s opposing poles, which was managed through a suspension response. Although our analysis is suggestive of the primary effect of each resolution type, it also reveals that the three standardization outcome categories seem intertwined in practice. For example, a shift in a standard’s scope (functional outcome), typically leads to structural changes in the standardization effort (organizational outcome). Considering MSI and Episode #1, the decision to restrict the standard to the integration of stationary and mobile systems led to the departure of Gatespace. In Episode #6, parameters from the truck were limited to the existing version of the MSI standard and because the integration of embedded data was again ruled out, the standardization initiative was disbanded.

## The Dialectics of Technology Standardization

Combining these patterns from our empirical analysis with insights from extant literature, we propose a midrange theory of technology standardization. As a first component of our theorizing, we offer a dialectic model of the technology standardization process (Figure 2). The model builds on the cyclical structure of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model, but it differs from their model in two principal ways. First, it identifies categories of latent tensions, management responses, and standardization outcomes that are specific to the technology standardization. Second, it adds considerable detail to how salient tensions are brought into being by emphasizing the role of oppositional logics in constructing salient tensions and responses to them.

## Discussion

There is a vast literature on the tensions that characterize technology standardization and some studies that recognize the dialectic nature of these tensions (Hanseth et al. 2006; Wareham et al. 2014). However, our paper is the first study that applies a dialectic analysis to advance theory on technology standardization. The relational ontology that underlies such an analysis highlights the recursive nature of a dialectic process, sensitizing us to the perpetual disruptions that plague standardization and allowing us to examine the relationship between the social construction of tensions, the management responses enacted to resolve them, the impacts on the standardization initiative, and the renewed activation of latent tensions. As such, our first contribution is the demonstration of a dialectic analysis of a technology standardization initiative that reveals the complex dynamics involved (Table 2).

Our second contribution is the elaboration of Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model to make the social construction of salient tensions and their resolution more evident. By offering insight into the practices of bringing salient tensions into being and responding to them, our expanded model (Figure 2) and related activities (Table 4) represent a contribution to the dialectic literature. This theorizing emphasizes the microprocesses of socially constructing a salient tension by naming its defining poles and determining the relationship between the poles through different types of oppositional logic. It further suggests that the activities through which latent tensions are made salient, are not linear but interdependent and reciprocal. A similar coevolution of the salient tension and the efforts to resolve it is evident in the response to a tension, where specific oppositional logics invite matching types of responses. Given that a dialectic analysis attributes agency to actors, stakeholders have choices regarding how to frame and respond to a salient tension.

Conceptualizing mutuality as an oppositional logic that has been illustrated but not labeled in prior research on technology standardization represents our third contribution. Mutuality highlights the highly paradoxical nature of a dialectic tension in that the more of Pole A, the more of Pole B, i.e., A’s opposite. In addition to failing to name this oppositional logic, prior standardization research also does not offer a resolution that appropriately honors the paradoxical relationship between the tension’s poles (e.g., Hanseth et al. 2006). Hence, we advance suspension as a new response type that is likely to effectively manage such mutuality tensions.

Our fourth contribution lies in insights and conceptualizations that are specific to technology standardization. These include the latent tensions that drive standardization (Table 1) and the resulting impacts in terms of standardization outcomes (Figure 2). Specifically, our dialectic analysis revealed a toggling between the latent tensions of private versus public and local versus global. Even though these recurrent tensions were brought into being as distinct salient tensions, this pattern lends support for the contention that dialectic tensions cannot be resolved but will continuously be triggered. We further suggest that repeated efforts to short-circuit the laborious work of developing a standard in a bottom-up, collaborative manner by instead adapting ready-made technological solutions (e.g., extant interfaces) are likely to drive this recurring pattern. While the search for ready-made solutions triggers conflicts over data and IP rights (i.e., private versus public), the collaborative design of the standard’s architecture and schema produces conflicts over members’ autonomy and possibility for differentiation (i.e., local versus global).

Notwithstanding these contributions, our study has two limitations in particular. First, limited to a single technology standardization initiative within a particular industry, we examine the collective work of a group of diverse actors in the Swedish road haulage industry. Our analysis is concerned with the construction and framing of specific tensions in this context, as well as the management responses invoked to produce desirable outcomes. Claims of cross-case generalizability to other empirical contexts are thus misplaced. However, through our midrange theorizing, our study achieves within-case generalizability (Lee and Baskerville 2003). By reading our data through Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model, and reading the model through our data, we achieve generalization from empirical description to theory.

Second, given the longitudinal nature of the standardization initiative we have studied, the data’s diversity and volume may raise questions about the completeness and accuracy of our analysis. To address concerns about data quality, we relied on a number of techniques, including the development of detailed timelines, comparison and contrast across the six episodes, and the application of different conceptual lenses throughout our data analysis, e.g., Benson’s (1977) dialectic principles, Carlo et al.’ s (2012) oppositional logics, and Smith and Lewis’s (2011) dialectic model of organizing.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we advance theory on the microprocesses that trigger latent tensions, socially construct salient tensions, and manage salient tensions with consequential impacts on outcomes in technology standardization. Given that prior research offers little insight into how these processes unfold and interact, we bring clarity to their complex relational nature by conducting a dialectic analysis of a twelve-year action research project that developed and diffused a technology standard. This analysis informed our dialectic theorizing of the recursive interplay between the triggering of salient tensions, the framing of salient tensions through oppositional logics of polarization, complementarity, and mutuality, the management of salient tensions via splitting, integrating, and suspension, and the generating of functional, architectural, and organizational standardization outcomes. As such, our study provides insights that can help in understanding and leading technology standardization initiatives and that speak directly to present debates in dialectics research.

## About the Authors

Rikard Lindgren is a professor of informatics at the University of Gothenburg, Sweden, and a guest professor at the University of Borås, Sweden. He is also the research director and co-founder of the Swedish Center for Digital Innovation. His research interests include action research, design science, digital platforms, and technology standards. He has published his research in MIS Quarterly, Research Policy, Sloan Management Review, and other outlets. Rikard currently serves on the editorial boards of Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and the advisory board of Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems.

Lars Mathiassen is a Georgia Research Alliance Eminent Scholar, a professor in the Computer Information Systems Department, and the co-founder of The Center for Digital Innovation at Georgia State University. His research focuses on digital innovation, Health IT, and the management and facilitation of organizational change processes. He approaches innovation and improvement initiatives with a strong focus on people skills and collaborative processes, while at the same time emphasizing the adoption of state-of-the-art technologies and methods. Lars has published extensively in major information systems and software engineering journals and has co-authored several books on the subject including Professional Systems Development, Computers in Context: The Philosophy and Practice of Systems Design, Object Oriented Analysis & Design, and Improving Software Organizations: From Principles to Practice. He has served as a senior editor for MIS Quarterly, Journal of Information Technology, and Information & Organization.

Ulrike Schultze is an associate professor in information technology and operations management at Southern Methodist University. Her research explores the complex relationship between information technology and work practices. Drawing primarily on practice theory in her early work on the implications of knowledge management and internet-based self-service technology, her more recent studies of identity work in social media have led her to sociomaterial theorizing. This philosophical stance has also guided her exploration of digital innovation, platform evolution, and the sharing economy. She has served on numerous journals’ editorial boards and is currently a senior editor at Journal of the Association for Information Systems and Information & Organization.
