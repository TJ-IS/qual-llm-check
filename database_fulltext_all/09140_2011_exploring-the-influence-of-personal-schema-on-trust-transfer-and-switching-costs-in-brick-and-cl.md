# Exploring the influence of personal schema on trust transfer and switching costs in brick-and-click bookstores

<sup>a</sup> SKK Business School, Department of Interaction Science, Sungkyunkwan University, Seoul 110-745, Republic of Korea <sup>b</sup> College of Hotel & Tourism Management, Kyung Hee University, Seoul 130-701, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 March 2009 Received in revised form 16 June 2011 Accepted 26 August 2011 Available online 10 September 2011

Keywords: Customer retention Trust transfer Switching costs Personal schema Brick-and-click

## A B S T R A C T

Due to competition, most firms try to retain and lock in customers; this is likely to ensure a greater market profit than that gained by acquiring new customers. While many studies have investigated customer satisfaction and retention in online and offline firms, scant research exists on customer satisfaction and customer retention in the context of brick-and-click firms: i.e., those that conduct business both online and in stores. Such firms encourage trust transfer among their customers. However, most studies have failed to consider the role of personal schema in trust transfer. Based on social exchange, investment, and information integration theories, we analyzed the impact of trust transfer, switching costs, and personal schema on customer satisfaction and repurchase intentions in the context of an offline bookstore that has now launched an online business. Our results indicated that trust transfer and switching costs had a small effect on customer satisfaction and repurchase intentions. Personal schema, however, affected both customer satisfaction and repurchase intention.

## 1. Introduction

Online businesses are increasingly using the Internet for to strengthen their marketing activities. E-commerce ensures rapid delivery of products, differentiated services, and lower prices. These advantages are a growing threat to offline businesses, many of which are now becoming brick-and-click oriented. The Internet reduces information asymmetry between firms and customers and the switching costs of customers; it has reduced the cost of information-searching by sharing information between buyers and sellers. Even though the price determination of online services and products is flexible, there are no significant, perceived differences in price among online services. However, in recent years, as problems of delivery and security increase the issue of trust in ebusinesses has received increased attention [15].

Online firms consider customer retention to be a critical success factor, particularly because low price sensitivity prevents price from acting as an entry barrier. Owing to the tremendous competition and costs involved in attracting new customers, companies are focusing their efforts on customer retention. Competitiveness depends on sustaining high loyalty among customers. In online bookstores, customer loyalty toward brands is quite low, resulting in customer retention becoming more important than customer acquisition. E-markets have low switching costs. Customers with strong brand loyalty are more likely to repurchase the products or services of a particular brand and attract other people to it, thereby increasing the company’s profits.

It is difficult to explain customer loyalty by relying only on the relationship between customer satisfaction and repurchase intentions. Customer satisfaction is a key component of retention strategies. The influence of other factors has been addressed in customer loyalty studies. We decided to build s a comprehensive framework to explain the factors affecting customer satisfaction and repurchase intentions in brick-and-click stores. To ensure growth, it is vital for them to determine whether customers in the offline market are retained in the online market.

## 2. Factors affecting repurchase intentions

## 2.1. Trust transfer

The repurchase intention is affected by trust, an important concept in studies on marketing, MIS, and organizational behavior [12]; it indicates the willingness to believe partners in a business contract, the credibility and honesty of business partners, and belief in the honesty of partners.

Trust has two dimensions: credibility and benevolence. Credibility is the belief that business partners will perform their role effectively and reliably, and benevolence assumes that the business partners intend to provide benefit partners in new situations. A number of studies have investigated the effect of trust on brick-and-click transactions [7], privacy and security issues, site characteristics, and the use of e-commerce. Yoon [17] showed that uncertainty avoidance and a long-term orientation affected the relationship between trust and intention to use. Trust is indispensable if the customer has to engage in a transaction or in any other kind of ongoing relationship [13]. It is critical for organizations to develop and nurture consumer trust in order to create long-term relationships.

Trust in brick-and-click transactions is hard to define because of the invisible nature of participants in brick-and-click transactions and a lack of legal policies [1]. In offline transactions, maintenance of trust ensures continuously reliable brick-andclick transactions. Trust lowers transaction risks stemming from the anonymity and uncertainty of Web environments. A lack of consumer trust in companies doing business on the Internet is a significant barrier to realizing the opportunities presented by the Web. Trust is more difficult to create online because parties do not have intense, face-to-face contact that enables trust to be built through tangible cues. One way to solve this trust-building problem is to transfer trust from a known brick-and-mortar entity to positive perceptions of the unknown online entity. Trust gained through experience with an offline company positively influences key customers’ perceptions of the same company’s online division, leading to Web site satisfaction and intention to use the site. Stewart, who called this phenomenon ‘‘trust transfer,’’ applied it specifically to B2C e-commerce, for buying computers online. The results revealed that Web sites that had a picture and street address of their land-based store generated a higher consumer intention to buy from the store.

## 2.2. Switching costs

Repurchase intention motivates a customer to engage in repeat purchases of the same brand over time. Brand loyalty generates positive recommendations for products, helping product diffusion in the market. The perceived switching costs are customer perceptions of the cost in time, money, and effort when changing service providers. Repurchase intentions are influenced by high switching costs, lack of alternative products or services, etc.

The theory of reasoned action states that behavioral intentions affect actual behavior. Thus, repurchase intentions are an important proxy for repurchase behavior and consequently consumers try to minimize switching costs. Transaction costs are those costs of acquiring and handling information about the quality of inputs, the relevant prices, the supplier’s reputation, etc. Investment theory posits that customers will not change their suppliers unless they can obtain benefits that outweigh the transaction-specific assets or costs. While high switching costs do not hurt satisfaction, perceived switching costs and satisfaction are key relationship mediators in customer relationship [14]. When customers cannot recover their initial acquisition investments, they undergo a form of lock in. Switching costs may involve search costs and learning costs resulting from the nature of many service encounters and the geographic dispersion of service alternatives there are also behavioral and psychological costs that may diminish switching tendencies; for example, Tsai and Huang [16] noted that online customers had to put in time and effort when they were given a new interface with new features, thereby increasing switching costs. Chang and Chen [4] showed that switching costs positively affected the relationship between customer satisfaction and customer loyalty.

## 2.3. Personal schema

Information interpretation requires that employees understand the conceptual schema of all units in the organization. The tacit knowledge (beliefs, viewpoints, and schemata) consists of individual frames of reference or the cognitive infrastructure [6]. Having a shared schema or common perspective helps users understand the knowledge embedded in an asset [2]. Such shared beliefs and interpretations are the schema [5].

People decide to repurchase products or services based on the rational or emotional benefits perceived through their personal schemas. These depend on experience and level of learning; it obviously affects trust transfer and switching costs. Rational aspects of transactions (the rational schema) are related to perceived ease of use, efficiency, convenience, and usefulness, while the emotional aspects of transactions (the emotional schema) are associated with desirability, fun, pleasure, and joy experienced in transactions. Thus both rational and emotional schema contribute to increase trust transfer and switching costs. A personal schema is based on an individual’s experience when exposed to technologies that make users more aware, thereby inducing them to be more accurate in their self-assessment [8].

Satisfied customers may not have repurchase intentions while dissatisfied customers may still retain repurchase intentions. It is therefore necessary to provide several factors to explain repurchase intention. We suggested that trust transfer, switching costs, and personal schema affected customer satisfaction and repurchase intention in brick-and-click firms.

## 3.1. Customer satisfaction

Customer satisfaction in our study is defined as the customer’s overall evaluation of products or services based on his or her transaction experiences with brick-and-click stores. Customer satisfaction enhances the intentions to maintain the business relationship. Thus, customer satisfaction is a most important determinant of customer retention in Internet-enabled businesses. When customers are satisfied with products or services, they have a favorable attitude toward the business providers and are more likely to have repurchase intention. Higher levels of core-service satisfaction will diminish the perceived benefits of switching service providers, thus yielding higher repurchase intention. The favorable interaction between customers and providers will reinforce positive intentions toward purchasing behavior. Thus we posit:

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ Customer satisfaction positively affects repurchase intention.

## 3.2. Trust transfer

The perceived risk of online transactions is greater than that of offline transactions; to minimize the difference, customers will use experience of offline transactions with the same firm as the basis for their decision to perform online transactions. A favorable attitude toward a store may therefore be transferred to any online equivalent of the same company e.g., Lee et al. showed that trust in an offline bank influenced structural assurance, flow, consumer satisfaction, and extent of use of its online system. Trust transfer assumes that a customer’s purchasing behavior in online channels mimics that occurring in previous offline transactions.

In online transactions, trust can be transferred from one organization to another through linked Web pages. However, we have found no papers that investigated the trust transfer from offline to online business in brick-and-click firms. In our research, trust transfer was investigated for transfers from offline to online channels or vice versa in the same company.

Recently, many bookstores have started selling books and other products through Web pages, and customers can buy these either online and offline. Such experience is considered to be brand knowledge, which creates brand loyalty and customer trust and which, in turn, affects customers’ continuance intentions, driven by satisfaction with the initial service [10].

Brand loyalty based on memories of shopping experience leads to high market share in the long-term. Thus we hypothesized:

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ The trust transfer from offline to online channels positively affects customer satisfaction.

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ The trust transfer from offline to online channels positively affects repurchase intention.

## 3.3. Switching costs

A number of companies dedicate a large amount of their resources to finding new customers; for instance, E-trade offered \$400 worth of free computer merchandise for new customers who signed up between January and March 2000. Customer acquisition costs are estimated to range from \$40 per customer for Amazon.com and over \$400 for other online brokers. Despite the critical role of switching costs in Internet-based business, there is surprisingly little empirical evidence about the presence, magnitude, or impact of these costs on customer behavior. Customers’ continuance intentions in brick-and-click stores are affected by switching costs and customer satisfaction. Switching costs are inherently low in many B2C environments with low entry barriers and limited differentiation. The Internet increases customer learning, communication and response, thereby reducing transaction costs due to a decrease in difficulty in searching and making comparisons. Online customers show brand loyalty, however, because firms have found ways to retain customers by introducing new ‘‘frictions’’, such as utilizing user profiles for personalization, frequent-purchaser programs, affiliate programs, and clickthrough rewards.

Here we suggest three types of switching costs in brick-andclick bookstores based on a study by Jones et al. [9]:

1. The cost of performance loss which includes the perceived loss of benefits and extra income that customers incur when they purchase products or goods through the Internet: e.g., mileage points in airline ticketing, special sale prices (or point accumulation), and the provision of special seats in restaurants. These offer positive incentives to retain customers in both offline and online business.

2. Costs of uncertainty including psychological uncertainty that the current relationship cannot be maintained or uncertainty about the unverified performance of service providers. This uncertainty increases when the quality of products or services is hard to evaluate and few channels and alternatives exist. With a growing number of firms engaged in Internet-based businesses, more customers feel uncertain of the quality and reliability of a provided service.

3. The cost of searching and evaluating before switching involves the perception of time and efforts devoted to evaluate the value of alternatives that customers face in order to switch. The service-related issues include geographical dispersion, channels allocated to each area, and the invisibility of the service. There are costs of time and efforts spent searching for information. In offline businesses, the cost is likely to be higher.

As long as customers perceive high switching costs, they are likely to remain loyal to their current sites. Jones et al. suggest that customer switching depends on behavioral and psychological costs and that switching costs positively affect customer loyalty and repurchase intention. Since switching service providers is likely to entail various behavioral and psychological costs, such costs should serve to reduce switching tendencies.

Jones et al. asserted that switching costs included retention costs, uncertainty costs, learning costs, and sunk cost which all influence repurchase intentions. Burnham et al. [3] classified switching costs into procedural (costs of economic risks, evaluation, learning, and setup), financial (benefits and monetary losses), and relational switching costs (from the loss of personal relations and brand relations). These are all believed to affect customers intentions to engage in repeat purchases. Thus:

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Switching costs (performance loss costs, uncertainty costs, and searching and evaluation costs) positively affect the repurchase intention.

## 3.4. Personal schema

Studies on customer attitude toward online transactions are rare. The cognitive ramifications are largely grounded in research on emotional affect (attitude). Generally, satisfactory experiences are posited to interact with other emotional cognitions and influence both future purchase intentions and post-purchase attitudes.

A personal schema is a mental map of a human being; it consists of variable and invariable components that make one schema different from that of another person. The personal schema therefore explains habitual actions and explains how a person is affected by emotional and perceptual factors. Personal schema also reflects the person’s experiences and level of learning plus his or her emotional experiences. Thus, it involves perceived ease of use, efficiency, convenience, usefulness, desirability, fun, pleasure, and joy that customers have experienced in prior transactions. Thus, personal schema can increase trust transfer and affect switching costs.

Personal schema affects the process of selection, description, interpretation, and integration of information through concentrated attention. Thus, personal schema enhances the information processing functions of users, influence the perceptual and emotional process, thus increasing customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. Therefore we posit:

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ Personal schema positively affects the trust transfer from offline to online channels.

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H _ { 6 } } .$ Personal schema positively affects switching costs.

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 7 } .$ Personal schema positively affects customer satisfaction.

Hypothesis $\mathbf { H } _ { 8 } .$ Personal schema positively affects repurchase intention.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. The measurement model

As shown in Fig. 2, the measurement model was assessed using both the first order and the second order model. Trust transfer, customer satisfaction, and repurchase intention were all measured in the first order model. The other two variables, switching cost and personal schema, were measured by sub constructs using a second order model. The sub constructs for switching cost were search, performance loss, and uncertainty costs. The sub constructs for personal schema were rational and emotional schemas.

PLS was used to test the measurement model and research hypotheses; this is a SEM approach; it tests both theoretical and measurement models at the same time. PLS adopts least-square estimation and minimizes the squared error of endogenous constructs and is better than other methods for assessing a small sample.

Reliability and validity tests were conducted for each latent variable and construct. Individual item reliability was tested for the constructs that had more than two or three items. The relationships between the items of each variable were examined to check whether they measured the same construct. Cronbach’s alpha was computed to test an individual item’s reliability. The results are shown in Table 3. All scales exhibited sufficient reliability as they exceed or are close to the acceptable reliability coefficient of 0.7. Composite reliability ranged from 0.692 to 0.951, indicating moderate to high reliability. This suggests that a significant portion of variance in the latent variable was explained by the variance of the measured variables.

## 5.2. Test of structural model

To evaluate the predictive power of the structural models’, we computed the $R ^ { 2 } s$ for customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. Interpreted as multiple regression results, $R ^ { 2 }$ indicates the amount of variance explained by the exogenous variables. Using a bootstrapping technique, the path estimates and tstatistics were calculated for the hypothesized relationships. The results suggest that distinct antecedents influenced the formation of customer satisfaction and repurchase intent. The results are shown in Fig. 2.

## 5.3. Implications

While many studies have investigated customer satisfaction and customer retention in online and offline firms, there apparently has been a lack of research on customer satisfaction and customer retention in the context of brick-and-click firms that conduct business both off- and on-line. Such brick-and-click firms naturally promote trust transfer among their customers between the online and offline channels. The results of our study provided insights into the determinants of customer satisfaction and repurchase intention in the context of brick-and-click stores.

We showed that trust in online channels was affected by trust generated in the physical stores and, thus, trust transfer from offline was important; trust in offline bookstores is actually transferred to online bookstores and has a positive influence on customer satisfaction. However, trust transfer is not significant in building repurchase intention, though it has an indirect effect because it affects the attitude of the customers in their relationships with suppliers and products or services.

Managers of online channels should try to increase the transfer of trust in offline to online stores. For example, by ensuring good site content and effective user control and its security and protection features. Physical stores should try to improve customer perceptions of the firm’s reputation and size as well as the actual and perceived offline customer service.

Personal schema, which enables the rational and emotional evaluation of products or services, is a good predictor of trust transfer and switching costs as well as customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. It is important to focus on personal schema to identify good customers. The path coefficient from personal schema to customer satisfaction is significant and larger than that from trust transfer to customer satisfaction. Further, the effect of personal schema on repurchase intention is significant, thus it is an important factor in building customer satisfaction and repurchasing intention.

Customer satisfaction affects repurchase intention, and this indicates that if customers are satisfied with the services, they are likely to regularly visit the bookstore and make purchases. The path coefficient from customer satisfaction to repurchase intention is larger than those from trust transfer, personal schema, and switching costs to repurchase intention.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Our study took brick-and-click online bookstores as target company type and analyzed the effect of trust transfer, switching costs, and personal schema on customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. Our results indicated that customer satisfaction was an important factor in influencing repurchase intention and that trust transfer and switching costs had a small effect on customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. Personal schema, however, affected both customer satisfaction and repurchase intention. Therefore, managers should focus on trust transfer, switching costs, and personal schema when an offline bookstore launches an online mode of business.
