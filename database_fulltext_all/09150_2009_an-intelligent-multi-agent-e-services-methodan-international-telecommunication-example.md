# An intelligent multi-agent e-services method—An international telecommunication example

Tung-Hsiang Chou <sup>a,1</sup>, Jia-Lang Seng <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Dept. of Information Management, National Kaohsiung First University of Science and Technology, Kaohsiung City 811, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Dept & Graduate School of Accounting, College of Commerce, National Chengchi University, Taipei City 116, Taiwan

## A B S T R A C T

The online services of B2B e-commerce have had to face a challenge resulting from the diverse services built on heterogeneous platforms that must work together in an integrated and seamless manner. Today, businesses have accumulated large numbers of online services that run and reside on a variety of environments. Furthermore, they have different workflows and operational support systems that need to interact with their legacy systems. The result has been chaotic and disruptive

We developed a new framework, artifact, and performance model with which to address this problem. A multi-agent e-service system was developed to cope with dynamics and upheaval. We used a customer-centric viewpoint with supply chains both upstream and downstream. An implementation was performed to determine problems in its development. Two simple live case studies were conducted to illustrate the model’s feasibility and validity.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Research motivation

Telecom businesses have many workflows and operation support systems (OSS) that need to interact with their legacy systems which were providing QoS management, order management, billing and customer service, resource management, etc. The consequent interface confusion has often been chaotic and disruptive. Businesses have therefore become concerned with issues of agility, flexibility, iscalability, integration, and interoperability needed to provide internal and external business-tobusiness electronic commerce.

## 1.2. Research issue

To tackle the resulting problems, business process management and web services, seemed to provide a solution, but, this solution suffered from a static and disconnected business model that failed to provide an intelligent, integrated, and interoperable solution [22]. e-Services emerged as the new way to extend web services in order to change to a customer-centric concept by extending the upstream and downstream channels of supply chains. e-Services management thus copes with the dynamics and upheaval [5,7]. Furthermore, agent techniques help e-services to use business knowledge intelligently and in an integrated fashion.

We addressed the issues faced by international telecom businesses in developing the framework and re-engineering the disjointed, ad-hoc, disconnected, and manual handling of business services today. Furthermore, current discussions of the principles of e-service have not really considered the exploration and exploitation of business knowledge. Leading international telecom businesses are now increasing their delivery needs to keep pace with worldwide telecom markets, and adapting their supply chains and the way they manage their trading partners; thus we tried to show how inter-firm telecom management could be improved.

There are two factors important in IS design: behavioral-science effects (developing and verifying theories that explain or predict human and organizational behavior) and design-science (extending the boundaries of human and organizational capabilities by creating new and innovative artifacts) [10]. We used them to incorporate novel components in a framework describing the implementation, evaluation, performance, and contribution of large IS. Its six aspects are:

\- A chaotic web client environment: In order to provide more highly functional internet-based services the telecom businesses have implemented diverse services in response to user requests.

These accumulate over time, and businesses have difficulty managing them.

\- Disruptive internet services: The telecom businesses often implemented their online services using back-end and legacy systems. Most developers did not consider the relationships between other managerial services and specific business processes and workflows: many internet services cannot function in many different ways, while loosely coupled to one another.

\- Incompatible business services: Enterprises often acquire heterogeneous platforms and systems through mergers and acquisitions. Although enterprise integration can help, it cannot provide a seamless and scalable e-service for B2B requests. Therefore, management between back-end and legacy systems now involves a variety of heterogeneous adapters that need to be managed and maintained.

\- Messy business processes: Telecom businesses have never rigorously dealt with the relationships between the B2B requests and telecom business processes. Developers have generated many spaghetti-coded internet services to link the business processes. Furthermore, the processes cannot be reused by other processes because each was built in a proprietary way. Absence of reusability prevents the internet service from being interoperable and scalable.

\- Ad-hoc handling of external and internal B2B integration: Often, when a telecom business needs to communicate and cooperate with its partners, vendors, and customers, it does not have a systematic and methodological way of handling inter-organizational process integration, interoperability, and management effectively. With fast growing B2B requests, the telecom business suffers from ad-hoc, time- and labor-consuming manual handling of external and internal business process change.

\- Lack of telecommunication standards compliance for operation support systems: The lack of an industry standard for service management resulted in many redundant applications and they complicate the heterogeneity issue.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Intelligent agent method

Artificial intelligence techniques provide an agent with autonomous, intelligent, and mobile abilities. Intelligent agents have access to the heterogeneous and operational platform where information resources can acquire, mediate, and maintain relevant information on behalf of their users or other agents. They must possess percept, action, goal, and environment (PAGE) features because the agents’ behaviors develop and operate in a changing environment. For example, when you are driving and see (perceive) a stop light at the next intersection, your action should be to stop. In other words, a change in the outside condition triggers your percept to act. Therefore, the intelligent agent provides pro-active resource discovery, resolves the information impedance of consumers and providers, and offers value-added information services and products [27]. Although Chen et al. [6,19] discussed how to build integral relationships with their trading partners by using agents, they did not integrate their heterogeneous internet services with the legacy systems. Schmidt et al. [25,26] proposed an agent-based e-services mechanism that featured web services applicable only to an MRP system.

## 2.2. Business process management

Business process management is a system whose trigger is a business process design or change, and whose function is to enact and manage business processes [1,2]. Lee and Park [21], using web services, constructed business processes according to a predefined method that allowed an enterprise to interact with its customers. Most other research [8,18] discussed how enterprises can apply business process management to cases other than the telecom business.

Telecom corporations have become concerned with the agility (a critical factor of innovation and of a corporation’s competitive edge) underlying intelligence and integration. It allows the exploration and exploitation of opportunities for the creation of new market values [13,24]. Exploration involves understanding the new options of experimentation and tracks of knowledge that might lead to new competitive advantage. Exploitation centers on using the refinements of and extensions of existing technologies and methods. Agility encompasses customer, partnering, and operational flexibility.

To accommodate user requests according to constant business processes, agility methods focus on four features: (1) interactions between processes and tools, (2) software for comprehensive documentation, (3) customer collaboration during negotiation, and (4) changes after making a plan of action. The three key roles in this are customer, partner, and operator. Each of them interact and generate four features: (1) when the customer and partner interact, they need to negotiate with each other to achieve their objective; (2) once the customer or partner interacts with the operation, they need to use processes and tools. If the key roles impact one another, they must plan to avoid an altered action.

However, the current literature has not considered the main roles to be played to allow agility occur, that is, between the customer, partner, and operation roles. Each should interact to process and pass the information. When the customer and partner interfere with each other, they need to negotiate to achieve the objective. On the other hand, once the customer or partner interacts with the operation, he or she needs to interact using processes and tools.

## 2.3. e-Services

e-Services create value services and are self-describing [3,20]. They contain open components that provide rapid and low-cost composition of distributed applications. Today, the delivery of these services depends on a combination of existing back-end systems, legacy systems, and web services.

Huhns et al. [12,16] used a standard data format to connect, integrate, deliver, and intermediate other e-services. Their standard format was XML. They also investigated several information tools to illustrate how to use them to solve e-services issues. Casati et al. [4,9] developed an eFlow model that supported the specification, enactment, and management of composite eservices, modeled as processes that are enacted by a process engine. Jr et al. [14,23] used of a hub-and-spoke connection model, in the form of an intermediate entity; this provided, a number of advantages both for end-users and business clients. The modular architecture of the service aggregator allowed integration and support of a larger number of heterogeneous e-services from many business clients.

Kapsalis et al. [15] described a context-aware access control architecture for the provision of e-services based on an end-to-end services infrastructures. The architecture was required to control access permissions to distributed e-services through an intermediary server, in a completely transparent way for both clients and protected resources. The access control mechanism incorporated dynamic context information. Casati et al. [4,29] noted that the new e-service paradigm took advantage of the inherent nature of the online environment to feature information flows and computation as a means of learning more about customers and building long-term relationships. An e-service orientation was seen as taking advantage of the electronic environment and technology advancement to stay competitive, nimble, and customer-focused in a turbulent business landscape.

However, the literature did not consider the exploration of business knowledge in the resolution of chaotic and disruptive business processes. Furthermore, they did not provide agility and integration of e-services, especially in large-scale enterprises.

## 2.4. Enterprise Application Integration and Enterprise Service Bus

In order to simplify service integration in a large-scale enterprise’s environment, software vendors have provided an Enterprise Application Integration (EAI) and Enterprise Service Bus (ESB) architecture to simplify integration of the software components [11]. The EAI/ESB integrates service flow, service logic, data, and existing applications. It also centralizes control and distributes processing [28]. It consists of tools and an architecture that uses web service technology to communicate with other services. However, much effort is required to provide the kind of support available in established middleware platforms such as the Object Management Group’s Object Management Architecture. Kim and Lim [17] details its essential metadata and capabilities and define an integration model for EAI/ESB, but provided no empirical evidence of the model’s efficiency. Many vendors sell implementation tools that an enterprise can use to develop EAI/ESB, but these tools are very complex and do not allow users to modify their settings. However, ESB faces significant complexity such as management change, migration costs, and implementation costs. Hence, this paper deals with the vendors’ methodology so that the research framework meets the needs of the telecommunication industry.

## 3. Research approach

Our method supported web-based management and the computing environment, provided the agility-enabled and intelligence-embedded B2B integration, and combined e-services with the back-end telecom operational support systems. Web users submittal of requests to the method then trigger a multi-agent eservices management system and communicate with the back-end operational support system.

## 3.1. The multi-agent e-services architecture

The multi-agent method was developed with intelligent interfaces that play the following management roles: data management, mediator, and transformation. Business rules and system heuristics were created for these roles.

(1) Data management: When the users submit their requests into the mechanism, the data management agent will transfer these data into a specific layout in an XML format. All the agents coordinate their behaviors following this specification.

(2) Mediator: This coordinates the workflow of business processes and starts an exchange of information with agents; for example, the agent determines the back-end legacy system’s workload situation and decides whether delivery of a user request is possible. If the legacy system is working, the agent will send the user request to it, and when it receives the user data, it will acknowledge the system status to the agent as confirmation of completion. Otherwise, the agent will hold the user request in a temporary database from which the agent continuously resends the user request system until it is confirmed and acknowledged.

(3) Transformation: This role returns e-services to the original requesting agent and determines whether they should be returned to the back-end legacy system or the temporary database.

Fig. 1 depicts the intelligent multi-agent method in telecom B2B integration and management. Fig. 2 shows the components of the framework, including the multi-agent, the EAI service bus, and the back-end legacy systems. Each of these not only supervises the different components of the business process but also drives the changes, communications, and controls. The EAI service bus is responsible for integrating the back-end legacy systems and the ecommerce business processes. The legacy systems are managed by e-services enabled by agents.

The roles are represented by business knowledge to the data management agent, mediator agent, and transformer agent as shown in Fig. 3. The agents are:

(1) Data management: This incorporates the web agent (WA), which collects all user information from the e-services portal and then uses the data-transformation agent (DTA) to encode them into the XML format.

(2) Mediator: This has two tasks: (1) to coordinate the process of an agent and the (2) to dispatch the service between agents. The coordinator (CA) is responsible for supervising the output of the DTA. And the monitor (MA) follows the interaction processes of the multi-agent mechanism. The other function of the MA is to supervise the flow of information and to monitor the legacy system status through the service-provider agent (SPA), which is in charge of transferring requests from the transit agent so that they can be mapped onto an e-service. The MA has a ‘‘plug-in’’ property. It extends the storage agent (SA) and the trigger agent (TA) to detect availability. When the legacy system fails, the MA receives the status message from the TA and triggers the SA to store the user request in the temporary database. When the back-end recovers, the MA will tell the TA to retrieve the user request and to resend the result to the user.

(3) Transformation: This moves the user request to the e-service. Owing to the importance of information requests, we added two new agents: a distribution agent (DA) and a transit-service agent (TSA). The DA receives the user request from the DTA in XML format, decodes it, and then maps e-services onto the TSA.

## 3.2. The multi-agent e-services repository

An e-services repository collects the user request, the dispatch decision, the rule execution, and the result. Therefore, the web agent, after receiving the user request, will convert raw data into an XML document; the DTA will parse this and check the legacy systems’ availability. If the situation is clear, the DTA will send the user data to the DA. But if the situation is not, the DTA will send the XML document to the CA and trigger the MA. When the DA receives the document, the DA will activate the mapping service and send it to the TSA, which will send the service information to the SPA, which will, in turn, process the user request under the MA. Once the SPA responds with a ‘‘time out’’ or a ‘‘busy’’ to the TSA, it will trigger the MA to send the original user request to the SA, which will keep the original user request in the database and notify the TA, which then notifies the user and restarts the process when possible.

(1) Online business process management executes the normal process while every resource is working in normal status and operation work, see Fig. 4.

(2) Offline batch business process management executes unusual legacy systems and sends out user information, see Fig. 4(b).

## 4. Research implementation

## 4.1. The prototype system

The proposed method was implemented in Visual Studio .NET 2005 and SQL Server 2005. The EAI/ESB was implemented in the .NET Framework and Tibco. A Windows Sever 2003 using IIS and .NET Framework SDK was used as an application server to support the development of web services and a general e-services gateway. The intelligent multi-agent e-services had the following features:

(1) validating and collecting user data;

(2) verifying and transforming user information;

(3) integrating inter-firm internet services;

(4) managing a portal to drive diverse services;

(5) deploying business services;

(6) streamlining heterogeneous service interfaces;

(7) accelerating the web application development, maintenance, and reuse.

Fig. 5 is a simplified illustration of the infrastructure of a largescale telecommunication company. The implemented infrastructure is web-based and has five roles in an e-commerce environment: clients, certificate-authority policies, web servers, application servers, and back-end and legacy systems. In order to illustrate the business process of web request, we consider the five steps that characterize a website’s processing:

(1) Clients are the actors of the e-commerce model. Each user browses the telecom company website from any device. Through the internet, the customers inquire and submit their requests.

(2) Certificate-authority policies are used to prevent internet crime; e-services expose sensitive information outside the customer premises. Therefore, authority policies have to be incorporated, to protect information from unauthorized access. The authority policies provide several kinds of certificates such as Mobile Codes; Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA); GCA (Government Certificate Authority); MOICA (Ministry of Interior Certificate Authority); and Non-CA (verification only by customer DB).

(3) Web servers are at the forefront of e-commerce operations. They provide the web page service and receive the customers’ requests. Web servers are located behind the external firewall. After customers pass the certificate-authority policies, they can use a browser to navigate the services.

(4) Application servers control all telecom electronic services. They involve the business logic of a telecom business. The application-server operations receive the user requests and dispatch instructions to the back-end and legacy systems. Their operations include inquiring into the equipment-installation status, examining the equipment status, submitting any customer complaints or requests, generating the reports to clients, and completing the customers’ requests.

(5) Back-end and legacy systems represent the core of operations in a telecom business. They are responsible for managing the user requests for service.

## 4.2. The system metadata

We created a data model to define the system metadata in the eservices. It contains six tables in three data areas: the functionality, processes and e-services. Fig. 6 shows the data model. Each area has a specific purpose, as follows:

(1) Functionality of Function\_Type and Function\_Category: This describes all services in the telecom business. Most of it is derived from the customer requirements. The Function\_Category describes each function, and the Function\_Type records the general telecom service types.

(2) Processes of Process\_Logic and Process\_Category: These define the business logic of the telecom business and adopt the database to extend the telecom services. The Process validates the input information [customer requests] and describes the process active status. The Process\_Category classifies the telecom processes.

(3) e-Services of Process\_Flow and Function: These describe the outbound telecom services and inbound business process management. The Function records the inbound operation and information about its creator. The Process\_Flow records the sequence of business processes and associates it with the Function table.

## 6. Conclusions

We presented a telecom intelligent multi-agent method: a framework, approach with evaluation. A novel, systematic, and methodological method to manage an integral, interoperable, and interchangeable B2B value chain. It is an on-demand business service.

The agent-based and service-oriented method can prevent point-to-point chaotic connections and provide B2B with flexibility and simplicity: the distinct features of the method are:

(1) All services have a clear boundary that identifies the purposes and portal of each.

(2) No service depends on an outside system.

(3) The metadata and XML-based system; e-services use common data schema and data validation to facilitate information exchange.

(4) The policy-driven connectivity; e-services can use the business knowledge to state connection-port, data, function, and security rules.

(5) Interoperability that allows trading partners to gain access to data or business functions via e-services wrappers.

This is a promising approach that can enable real time telecom business communication and collaboration to deliver services. Such method could provide a viable method for ubiquitous connectivity without technology barriers imposed by closed or proprietary technology.

The case studies addressed the physical situation with such intelligent agents and revealed several tacit management issues. Hence, the method achieves the desired properties of telecom eservices management: (1) loose coupling, with business processes assembled from reusable components, (2) implementation neutrality, (3) flexible configurability, (4) persistence, and (5) granularity.
