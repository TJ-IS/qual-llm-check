## Abstract

Mobile user switching has become a critical issue facing mobile service providers. This study examines the switching behavior of mobile users who are not under any contractual obligations to stay with a provider. Drawing upon the literature on relationship marketing and switching costs, we examine if the relational investments made by mobile users in a user-provider relationship and demographics influence their switching behavior. Based on data on over 30,590 mobile users, we examine our research questions. Statistical analysis supports significant associations between mobile users’ service usage, service bundling and their switching behavior. Support was also found for the influence of age and gender on mobile user switching. European Journal of Information Systems (2006) 15, 269–276. doi:10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000616

Keywords: mobile user behavior; switching; relational investments; user demographics; technology acceptance; m-commerce

## Introduction

In the last few years, the demand for mobile telecommunication services has grown exponentially (Bloom, 2005). The growth in consumers’ use of mobile services has been accompanied by an increase in the sophistication of mobile technology devices (Balasubramanian et al., 2002). Mobile users increasingly use these devices not only for voice communications, but also for computing purposes including internet access, e-mail, text and multimedia transmissions (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005). Surging demand for mobile services and proliferation of service offerings has resulted in rampant switching behavior among mobile users. This has resulted in intense competition, severe price wars, promotion campaigns, attractive calling plans, and a host of customer retention strategies among mobile service providers.

Customer switching has become a critical issue facing mobile service firms. Customer turnover is as high as 40% in the U.K. (Precision Marketing, 2004). In the U.S.A., mobile customer defection has significantly increased, especially after introduction of wireless number portability act in 2003 (Braff & Laogue, 2004). Switching behavior of mobile users causes great concern as mobile operators spend about \$300 to acquire every new customer (Brown, 2004).

Customer switching refers to migration of users from one provider to another. In most service contexts, customer switching is associated with negative consequences such as declining market share and poor profitability (Keaveney, 1995). Switching assumes additional significance in mobile context as it has become widespread among mobile users. To control switching, mobile service providers are increasingly relying on contracts that would lock-in customers for a definite time period. However, with changing competitive dynamics, contracts are not being favored by many users (Braff and Laogue, 2004). Therefore, it becomes important to understand the fundamental drivers of switching behavior under noncontractual conditions.

There is an emerging, yet growing body of research on user behavior in mobile environments. Several scholars have examined users’ intentions to adopt and use mobile services (Bruner and Kumar, 2005; Kim et $a l . ,$ 2005; Lu et al., 2005). Although these studies shed light on specific factors that influence the adoption of mobile services, there is only limited understanding on postadoption attitudes such as switching behavior. Our study addresses this important gap. In this paper, our goal is to examine the switching behavior of mobile users who are not under any contractual obligations to stay with a provider. We seek to identify key user-related constructs that influence customer churn. Specifically, we examine if relational investments made by mobile users in a user-provider relationship and user demographics influence their switching behavior. This research is an initial, exploratory study that seeks to understand the influence of key user-related factors on mobile users’ switching behavior.

## Theoretical background

Customer switching is a central concept in the relationship marketing paradigm. According to Morgan & Hunt (1994), relationship marketing is oriented towards ‘establishing, developing, and maintaining successful relational exchanges’ (p. 22) between a firm and its customers. It advocates viewing user-provider interactions as relational exchanges rather than as discrete, arms-length transactions (Peterson, 1995). Relationship marketing focuses on enhancing firm-customer relationships as a means to reduce customer switching, increase loyalty and overall firm performance (Sheth & Parvatiyar, 1995).

Considerable research in relationship marketing has examined switching behavior. Prior literature has proposed conceptual typologies of switching (Burnham et al., 2003), compared switchers and stayers (Ganesh et al., 2000), and has outlined process models (Roos, 1999). Previous research has also examined switching behavior in different service contexts such as credit cards, long-distance telecommunications (Burnham et al., 2003), online brokerages (Chen & Hitt, 2002) and Internet services (Keaveney & Parthasarathy, 2001). Further, researchers have sought to identify key antecedents to switching (Keaveney, 1995). Factors such as prices, quality, brand name, customer service and competitive offerings have been identified as possible antecedents to customer switching (Ganesh et al., 2000; Keaveney & Parthasarathy, 2001; Bienenstock et al., 2004). Most of these factors pertain to the service providers with relatively limited focus on user-related attributes. However, some recent studies suggest userrelated factors to play a significant role. For instance, Burnham et al (2003) identified user factors (e.g.: their domain expertise) to be significant antecedents to switching. Similarly, Chen & Hitt (2002) found both firm and user-related attributes to be associated with customer switching. Studies have identified a strong association between customer dissatisfaction and switching (Bolton & Bronkhorst, 1995; Bansal & Taylor, 1999; Lee et al., 2001). Among the user-related variables, research on relationship marketing and switching costs (e.g., Gwinner et al., 1998; Bolton & Lemon, 1999; Keaveney & Parthasarathy, 2001; Burnham et al., 2003) suggests two important constructs that potentially influence the switching behavior of customers: users’ relational investments and their demographics.

Building upon prior research, we seek to contribute to the literature in three distinct ways: (i) Our focus is on mobile users and their switching behavior. While prior studies have focused on other service settings, there is only limited examination in the context of mobile services. (ii) Many studies on switching behavior rely on self-reported intentions to repurchase or switch (e.g., Lee et al., 2001). In contrast, this research examines the actual switching behavior of mobile users. (iii) Recent studies on switching behavior (e.g., Chen & Hitt, 2002) as well as mobile technology adoption (e.g., Wu & Wang, 2005) have identified user-attributes to be important in influencing mobile user behavior. In this paper, we extend the work on mobile user behavior by examining two userrelated attributes that is, users’ relational investments and demographics and their associations with switching behavior of mobile users. Our research model linking the key constructs is shown in Figure 1.

## Users’ relational investments and switching behavior

Relational investments refer to investments that are specific to the user-provider relationship (e.g., learning about products, services, procedures, transactions, proprietary systems etc) (De Wulf et al., 2001). These investments influence customers’ perceptions of the costs of switching between providers. Switching costs include economic costs as well as range of non-economic costs related to searching, learning, cognitive and emotional adjustments that are incurred as a part of the switching process (Klemperer, 1995; Burnham et al., 2003). Further, since the investments are specific to the relationship, they are lost when the relationship is terminated (Klemperer, 1995). Relational investments increase switching costs and discourage users from migrating to a different provider, thus enhancing loyalty. Studies have shown that loyal customers tend to spend more and bring a steady stream of future customers by spreading positive word of mouth (Reichheld & Teal, 1996). Based on prior research in service marketing (Gwinner et al., 1998; Bolton & Lemon, 1999; Keaveney & Parthasarathy,

Service usage refers to the extent to which a user utilizes the services offered by a mobile service provider. It reflects the users’ intensity and frequency of consumption of services offered by the mobile service provider. The expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver, 1980) suggests that higher and frequent usage of mobile services should provide users with adequate performance expectations, thereby potentially reducing disconfirmation as well as switching intentions. The relational investments made by users in searching for a provider, forming expectations and evaluating the service performance could drive the switching costs, making them less prone to defection.

Studies on customer satisfaction also suggest a possible linkage between usage and switching. Intense, frequent and continued users of a service are likely to develop a strong and presumably positive attitude about the service. Bolton & Lemon (1999) found strong associations between prior service usage, customer satisfaction and subsequent usage. Users who had positive evaluations of their prior usage experienced higher cumulative satisfaction; higher satisfaction in turn was associated with increased subsequent service usage. ## H1: Service usage will be negatively related to switching behavior of mobile users.

Relationship duration refers to the tenure of the relationship between a user and service provider. Bolton (1998) argues that the relationship duration is contingent upon the users’ perceptions on the expected value from the relationship. As the duration extends, users become more experienced with the provider and the services. They are also likely to become more aware of the market including competitive services, pricing practices and industry trends. Higher duration of the relationship results in increased user confidence about what they can expect to receive from the provider (Gwinner et al., 1998). Based on the experiences during the course of the relationship, the level of satisfaction and the expected value from a relationship varies, which ultimately determine the switching decision of users. Therefore, relationship duration reflects the cumulative experience and satisfaction levels experienced by users during the entire course of relationship.

Longer association with a service provider could imply experiential knowledge that the users develop over the period of relationship. Users tend to gain considerable knowledge and expertise when they increase the duration of their relationship with a service provider (Park et al., 1994). Having invested time and energy to learn and use a provider’s mobile services, users could perceive higher switching costs to defect to another provider. Therefore,

## H2: Duration of user-provider relationship will be negatively related to switching behavior of mobile users.

Service bundling refers to aggregation of multiple products or services as a single package (Tellis, 2002). Service providers often assemble bundles that customers could accept, modify or reject. Marketing literature has investigated how product and service bundling influence a consumer’s decision process and the ultimate buying decision (Yadav, 1994; Johnson et al., 1999). MIS researchers have also examined the economic aspects involved in optimally bundling online services and information goods (e.g. Bakos & Brynjolfsson, 1999). In the context of mobile services, providers typically offer several complementary services such as voice mail, multimedia messaging, online games, web access, email, and so on. Customers could choose a standard bundle created by the mobile service provider, or selfassemble a service bundle. We expect a larger service bundle to diminish a user’s switching tendency for at least two reasons. First, the theory of consumer learning (Hoch & Deighton, 1989) suggests that a customer using a large bundle of services would accumulate providerspecific skills and knowledge, which would be difficult to transfer if users switch. The time and energy invested to learn and use a service bundle might discourage customers to defect. A second, related argument is that the larger number of items in the service bundle would enhance the customer locking-in to the service provider. Hence,

H3: Service bundling will be negatively related to switching behavior of mobile users.

## User demographics and switching behavior

Recent research on mobile computing suggests user demographics to play a dominant role in influencing mobile user behavior. Lu et al. (2005) argued for looking beyond behavioral beliefs and examining personal traits and user attributes to better understand mobile user behavior. Individual user demographics have been found to influence user attitude towards mobile services (Okazaki, 2006). Studying Singaporean mobile users, Gilbert & Han (2005) found user characteristics to be prominent in influencing mobile consumers’ behavior. Based on these prior studies, we focus on two specific demographic variables (age and gender) and examine their possible association with switching behavior of mobile users.

Carroll et al. (2002) found that young mobile users (16–22 year olds) use mobile services to satisfy their social and leisure needs, reinforce group identity and add value to their lifestyles. They also found that young mobile users view mobile devices as lifestyle-related tools rather than as task-oriented technologies. This attitudinal shift might influence their switching intentions as well. In fact, market research studies report young users to be heaviest users of mobile services as well as highly prone to defection (Myring, 2003). Therefore,

H4: Age will be negatively related to switching behavior of mobile users.

Several scholars have reported that female users tend to experience higher levels of anxiety than males in using technologies (Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990; Brosnan & Davidson, 1996). Some studies have even suggested that the differences in anxiety-levels among genders are due to technological devices being viewed as more ‘masculine’ entities (Brosnan & Davidson, 1996). Leung & Wei (2000) found significant gender differences in mobile phone usage. In the context of mobile internet technology, Gilbert et al. (2003) found gender to be a key variable: they found females to exhibit more technophobia and anxiety towards mobile technologies. This anxiety is likely to prevent them from switching from one provider to another. Hence,

H5: Female users will exhibit lesser propensity to switch than male users.

## Methods

## Dataset

Our dataset came from the customer database and call log files provided by a mobile services provider firm (which we refer to as MOBCO). MOBCO was among the top ten mobile service providers in North America and provided multiple service plans and features such as textmessaging, multimedia messaging and web surfing to their users. MOBCO also offered their customers an option to enter into a 1 or 2-year contract in return for free phone equipment and a lower-fee. MOBCO’s service plans were based around a ‘bucket’ of calling time minutes. Customers paid a fixed monthly fee based on their choice of service plan. Each service plan specified the total calling time minutes, with specific limits on peak time minutes and those that were available only in the nights (non-peak period) and weekends. Customers were charged an additional fee on per-minute basis if they exceeded the minutes allotted by their service plan. While some of the expensive service plans included services such as text and multimedia messaging for no additional costs, customers with other service plans had the option to include specific services in their plan for an additional fee.

MOBCO’s customer data files provided information on the profile of the customers – including their demographics as well as the date of their service activation. In addition, the company also provided the call log files for these customers for a period of 6 months. These files contained detailed information on the calls, durations, and timing of the calls. After removing missing data points, we had a final usable data for 30,590 customers. Of these, about 46% had churned the mobile service, while the rest continued with MOBCO. It should be noted that the customers included in our analysis were under no contractual obligations to stay with MOBCO. Profile of mobile users in our dataset is shown in Table 1. Our dataset had a fairly heterogeneous mixture of mobile users in terms of age as well as gender.

## Measures

MOBCO offered different service plans, which differed in the amount of free call minutes during the peak hours (peak hours were typically in the day time on weekdays, say from 0700 until 1900 hours), off-peak hours or during the weekends. For assessing service usage, we used four indicators – (i) total usage ratio, which was measured as a ratio of total minutes used and the total free minutes that was available to the user as per their service plan. (ii) peak time usage ratio, total minutes used in peak time period as a proportion of total free minutes, (iii) off-peak time usage ratio, total minutes used in off-peak time periods as a proportion of total free minutes available, and (iv) weekend usage ratio, total weekend minutes used as a proportion of total free minutes available. The duration of user-provider relationship was measured by the number of months elapsed since the time of service activation. Service bundling was assessed using the number of additional services subscribed by the user. Age and gender information were directly obtained from customer profiles. Mobile user switching behavior was modeled as binary variable indicating if a mobile customer continued with MOBCO or had disconnected the service.

## Analysis

Since our dependent variable was dichotomous (user switched the mobile service provider or otherwise), we chose binary logistic regression as the analytic technique to examine our hypotheses. Logistic regression estimates the coefficients of a probabilistic model of independent variables that best predict the value of a dichotomous dependent variable. Further, this technique does not have limiting assumptions surrounding data normality. The model is specified as:

$$
\text { Prob. } (Y = 1) = 1 / [ 1 + \mathrm{e} ^ {(- (\alpha + \Sigma_ {i} \beta_ {i} X _ {i}))} ]
$$

where Prob. (Y ¼ 1) is probability of a user switching the mobile service provider; $X _ { i }$ are the independent variables such as service usage, relationship duration, service bundling and user demographics; $\beta _ { i }$ are the estimated coefficients; $\mathscr { X }$ is a constant; and e is the base of the natural logarithm. Since our dependant variable is defined as probability of customer switch, a positive coefficient of an independent variable implies that, with a unit increase in the independent variable, the probability of switching also increases by the amount determined by the above equation.

Logistic regression produces unstandarized coefficient estimates (shown as B in Table 3), which represent the degree to which log odds of the event occurring are changed for unit increase in associated variable. The odds ratio $\mathsf { \bar { ( i . e . , e ^ { B } ) } }$ indicates increase/decrease in the odds of the event happening for each unit increase in respective independent variable. The Wald statistic is commonly used to assess the significance of the coefficients. While there is no statistic in logistics regression that can be interpreted analogously to R-square in multiple regression, statistics representing ‘goodness-of-fit’ of the overall model could be computed. The model chi-square represents the increase in predictability of likelihood of the event occurring when all effects of independent variables are considered. Further, Hosmer and Lemeshow (H–L) goodness-of-fit test also gives an assessment of the overall model significance.

## Discussion and contributions

In this study, we addressed two major research goals. First, we examined if relational investments made by a mobile user are linked to their switching behavior. Results provide support for association between relational investments in the form of service usage and user defection. Second, we sought to examine the influence of user demographics (age and gender) on mobile user switching. Our findings indicate that gender and age does make a difference in switching tendencies of mobile users.

Our results show a strong association between users’ relational investments and their switching behavior. Mobile users with considerable usage levels and multiple services bundled into their service plan were less likely to defect to other providers. Higher levels of usage could indicate potential customer satisfaction with the services, and this satisfaction is likely to discourage mobile users from switching.

Our results from Model 2 provide some additional insights on mobile user behavior. We found a significant, positive association between higher levels of weekend usage and switching behavior. A one unit increase in weekend usage was associated with the odds of switching increasing by a multiplicative factor of 1.78 (Model 2 in Table 3). Users with higher levels of weekend usage are likely to be those who use mobile services for more social, non-work related purposes. Past research has reported such users to be more interested is using mobile technologies as fashion and status devices, rather than for work-related or functional purposes (Leung & Wei, 2000). Therefore, it is not surprising that these mobile users are more inclined to switch providers. These users could seek more trendy devices or services, such as multimedia messaging, and are likely to defect to other providers offering new services.

Our study makes some important contributions to research. Grounded in the theories of relationship marketing and switching costs, this study provides some interesting insights into mobile user behavior under non-contractual conditions. It provides evidence that relational investments made in the user-provider relationship strengthens the ties, thereby decreasing user migration. Our study extends the extant IS research on mobile users by throwing light on post-adoption switching behavior. Our findings also supplement the marketing literature on service switching. Though researchers have examined tactics used by service providers to generate relational investments (e.g.: reward programs, loyalty clubs, discounts, service bundling, etc.) the issue of user-initiated relational investments has not been adequately examined. Our study underscores the importance of relational investments that are made by users.

For practitioners, this research has important implications for designing and offering mobile services to consumers. Mobile services industry is struggling to identify ways to reduce user defection. Our findings point to the importance of identifying ways to promote service usage and improving service bundling in order to decrease user defection. Mobile service providers could offer different promotional plans, provide additional calling minutes that would increase the usage levels of their customers. Service providers could also benefit by bundling different services such as text messaging, internet access and multimedia transfer that could further strengthen user-provider ties. Moreover, our study also stresses the need to distinguish mobile users based on their patterns of usage. Mobile users who use the services more for functional and work-related needs have to be treated differently from those who use it more for social and lifestyle purposes.

Though this study used fairly objective data from a large dataset, a key limitation is that all the users were customers of one service provider. We did not examine multiple providers and user migration among these providers. However, despite this caveat the dataset allowed opportunities to examine the research questions we sought to explore. Second, we did not examine a comprehensive set of factors that influence switching behavior of mobile users. Past research has identified several provider-related factors (e.g. prices, branding, service quality, and satisfaction) and other market-related variables. As our study is one of the initial studies on mobile user switching behavior, a fruitful extension of our work would be to examine a more comprehensive model of mobile user switching. Third, we did not examine user traits such as education level, self-efficacy, and income, etc. Future research could include these additional demographic variables as well. Finally, we did not examine mobile users who were under contractual obligations with the service provider. Long-term contracts are common in American mobile telecommunications market, and contract-bound users would incur high financial costs to move to a different provider. Contractual concerns might be more overbearing that the other variables, we enumerated in this study. A possible future research study could compare the salient factors under contractual and non-contractual conditions.
