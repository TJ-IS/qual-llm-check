# EXPLAINING BROADBAND ADOPTION IN RURALAUSTRALIA: MODES OF REFLEXIVITY AND THEMORPHOGENETIC APPROACH<sup>1</sup>

Philip Dobson, Paul Jackson, and Denise Gengatharen

Centre for Innovative Practice, School of Management, Edith Cowan University, 270 Joondalup Drive, Perth, Western Australia 6027 AUSTRALIA {p.dobson@ecu.edu.au} {p.jackson@ecu.edu.au} {d.gengatharen@ecu.edu.au}

Universal fast broadband is currently being implemented by the Australian government. It is the largest single project in Australia’s history. Represented as a nation-building exercise by the government and many public and private promoters, it is vilified by others as a massive waste of taxpayers’ money. Ultimately the target of successful universal availability will require that metropolitan installations subsidize rural adoption. The take-up of these facilities, particularly in regional and remote areas, constitutes a complex, multi-factorial scenario in which political, personal, and organizational decisions are shaped by physical, cultural, economic, and ideological elements. Critical realism is proposed here as an aid for examining the complex reality of rural adoption for communities and small businesses in the regions. This article highlights the importance of considering individual reflexivity in explaining the adoption decision and potential adoption barriers.

Keywords: Critical realism, broadband adoption, internal conversation theory, morphogenetic approach, technology adoption

## Introduction

Australia has been somewhat late in implementing super-fast broadband. Numerous studies have highlighted the poor performance of broadband technologies within Australia compared to comparable OECD countries (for example, Bowles and Wilson 2012). One of the major policy initiatives of the current Labor government is the implementation of a national broadband network (NBN) that will be available to all Australians. The majority of coverage will be provided by fiber optic cable (94 percent) and the remainder by a mix of wireless and satellite. Under the NBN, a basic service offering of a minimum peak speed of 12 mbps will be available to all Australian premises, with fiber initially providing speeds of up to 100 mbps and rising to 1 gigabit per second in 2012 (NBN Co. Ltd., 2011). The project is currently estimated to cost on the order of \$40 billion to \$50 billion Australian and will take six to eight years to implement. It is the largest single project in Australia’s history and has played a pivotal role in national politics over the last five years. The incumbent Labor government survived a recent election through an alliance with rural-focused independent politicians and the Greens, who see the benefit to rural regions consequent on a universal fast broadband network. This shaky ruling alliance is threatened by the Liberal–National (Rural) Party opposition who strongly criticize the level of government involvement and the use of fiber optic as the primary medium for the broadband network, arguing that a greater mix of wireless and satellite will provide a more economic alternative.

The functionality provided by broadband depends to a large extent on the medium by which it is provided. Transmission over the existing copper wire telephone network currently has performance limitations in terms of speed, bandwidth, and latency. Fiber optic cable is seen by promoters as the best long term solution as a transmission medium, however the costs associated with installation and maintenance throughout Australia are huge and difficult to justify on a straight cost– benefit analysis. The sheer enormity of the cost of connecting all households has removed the project from private hands and necessitated public involvement as an infrastructure project. The provision of the NBN throughout metropolitan and rural Australia is presented as a nation-building exercise. Cost-justification of provision in populous compact metropolitan regions will prove to be much easier than for rural implementations. The nation-building commitment to provide access to almost all Australians means the project can only be justified on altruistic grounds due to the enormous cost of rural provision.

The ultimate success of the network depends very much on how many users sign up for the new technology. Many of the social justifications for fast broadband, such as remote medical care or access to education, assume ubiquitous access. Yet, international experience suggests that despite strong promotion, reasonable pricing, and obvious social and economic benefits, adoption has been disappointing worldwide, particularly in rural areas, where adoption rates have tended to lag behind those of urban regions. A recent American study finds that 50 percent of rural Americans do not use broadband, perhaps reflecting the higher age profiles and lower incomes of the rural population, but also the simple lack of availability (Horrigan 2011). Katz et al. (2012) highlight the reduced broadband connection of ethnic groups and argue for the importance of targeted community support and local ethnic media in explaining to ethnic groups “why broadband matters” (p. 81). The U.S. National Broadband Plan (Federal Communications Commission 2010) sees ubiquitous broadband as “means, not ends. It is what those connections enable that matter” (as quoted in Katz et al. 2012, p. 79).

As Perlgut (2011) suggests, much of the rationale behind building the Australian National Broadband Network has been dependent on a “build it and they will come” model, yet it is eminently clear from international experience that this is not necessarily so. In particular, a portion of society remains most at risk, particularly the “poor, indigenous, elderly, disabled or those living in rural or remote areas of Australia” (p. 1). Even among those with the resources and capability, engagement is not guaranteed. Cultural factors can also impact adoption levels as Gregg (2010, pp. 163-164) highlights when she describes the negative attitude in rural regions toward the new possibilities provided by the NBN:

Much of the anger surrounding the plans for the national broadband network stems from the perception that city policy makers fail to notice the different priorities that exist in rural areas, which affect the kind of services that are seen as valuable.… Think, for instance, of the civic institutions that have flourished across Australia’s diverse landscape over the past century—from Rotary to the Returned and Serviceman’s League (RSL), churches to parents’ and citizens’ groups, and volunteer groups like the Country Women’s Association (CWA). These affiliations question fundamental assumptions about the benefits of the digital economy, particularly the notion that online connection is the best form of social networking.

It is suggested that rural populations traditionally have thrived on face-to-face interactions and the promise of the Internet is less attractive to rural people. Understanding the attitudes of rural communities to broadband and the NBN in particular is an important precondition to achieving adoption.

This paper describes a recent research project where we seek to define broadband adoption issues for a rural region of Australia. As a part of this study, we examine in detail a semigovernment organization that adopted broadband and promoted a social web application with initial enthusiasm, before backing away. Archer’s (1995) morphogenetic approach (MA) and considerations of personal reflexivity (2003, 2007a, 2012) are used to explain the unexpected outcome and suggest new ways to look at the complex broadband adoption process.

## The Research Context

The city of BigTown has been nominated as one of the first centers to receive the NBN. The coastal city borders desert regions and has a population of 60,000. Employment, economic, and education options available in the region surrounding the city have caused a population shift from inland shires to BigTown and other smaller towns on the coast. Farming plays an important role in all communities in the region. Because of the borderline farming capacity of the land, average farm size is 3,500 hectares and farmers are generally big technology users due to the broad-acre farming style which utilizes global positioning systems, geographical information systems, and sophisticated tracking devices. As one participant remarked, “They’ve been putting GPS across country for over 10 years, mapping soil types, grain and fertilizer distribution, herbicides…because it’s a cost thing. They have to.”

Many ancillary businesses depend on farming for survival. This group of people tends to be conservative; selfsufficiency, hardiness, sociability, and helpfulness are highly regarded. Events and activities that support socializing and the building of community spirit are cherished, whether it is in the form of supporting the local Australian-rules football club, the school dance, or cleaning up a local park. The mechanism for generating and reproducing these structures is overwhelmingly face-to-face contact, with the land-line telephone being the other universally accessible means of communication (mobile telephone coverage is erratic and limited). The population is highly dispersed and difficult to reach. As farmers retire, or as their farms become unviable, they move to more pleasant coastal areas and the population in inland areas (100 kilometres or so) is dwindling, leading to farm consolidation. The material and natural landscape is vast, arid, and hot, and beset by environmental problems such as drought, land degradation, feral animals, species diversity reductions, and the consequences of climate change. Different farmers in different areas confront contextual issues; for example, land degradation requires specific remedies such as sand farming, revegetation to address wind erosion, or rehabilitation from salinity.

In early 2010, our team of three university researchers decided to initiate a research project to examine broadband adoption in the region. Fast broadband (ADSL 2+) had been introduced into the area in 2008 and it was felt that this rollout would allow us to gain an understanding of some of the adoption issues involved in the forthcoming NBN rollout. Initial interviews provided some unexpected results for the interview team. We were surprised at the level of animosity toward the national broadband rollout. Past research experiences in examining Internet adoption in rural regions prepared us to expect a degree of scepticism but not at the level we experienced, as the following quotes demonstrate:

We have other priorities….we’re living two days from disaster here—if electricity goes off and generators go down, the telephone’s off, water’s off, mobile phones are off. (A public meeting in a small country town)

They [government agencies] just use it as an excuse NOT to have people on the ground. (A public meeting in a small country town)

There is no clear business case for the NBN. It is ridiculous that the government is only now investigating what the potential uses of the NBN are. I am not against it, but it needs to be very clearly explained and propagated. Our members are wide ranging, some have very sophisticated IT systems, and some are very basic. But none really understand what the benefit will be of the ultra-fast broadband. (Head of a local industry group)

I only use e-mail once a day; I don’t really need it. (Interview with a shop owner)

I have a vague understanding. I ask my teen how to do things…I am happy with my current knowledge of the Internet. (Interview with a small business operator)

After many interviews and group meetings, we decided in mid-2010, that to better understand the complex relationship between broadband provision, adoption, and use, we should target the semi-government organization, A-NGO, which was using existing broadband to host an innovative social web application and develop a GIS system for farming. A-NGO was held up as an exemplar for Internet usage in the region and we were interested to understand the conditions that supported such innovative application in a difficult adoption environment.

As detailed below, the initial examination of this organization inspired us; the promised applications were highly innovative and the designer and driver of the projects was charismatic, competent, and committed. To our amazement when returning 12 months later, we found the designer had left the organization, the innovative social web application was scrapped, and future Internet development funding was in jeopardy. The promised GIS application that had wide potential to address environmental degradation was languishing. We felt it was important to understand the reasons for the changes, particularly given that the innovative implementation was held up as an exemplar for the region and that the proposed GIS application was to be one of the largest and most significant NBN applications in the region. Given the background of all members of the team in critical realism, critical realism was an obvious choice for examining the case in more detail. In searching for mechanism-based explanations for the observed happenings at A-NGO we came to learn much about broadband adoption in general.

## Critical Realist Underpinnings

Raduescu and Vessey (2008) suggest that the most widely used explanatory frameworks within critical realism are Archer’s (1995) morphogenetic approach, Danermark et al.’s (2002) staged model (see Figure 1), and Pawson and Tilley’s (1997) realistic evaluation framework. We used Danermark et al. to guide the methodological development, with Archer’s morphogenetic approach providing the basic social theory for examining the intimate relationship of broadband adoption and use with rural social dynamics. Archer’s MA is used to support the contention that broadband adoption needs to be accepted as an important individual life project for encouraging social inclusion and, ultimately, realizing the social identity of each individual.

The Pawson and Tilley framework, with its focus on the evaluation of social programs, provides support for the argument that the NBN rollout should be seen as a social program rather than a technology project and that context is vital in understanding the particularities of broadband adoption. The focus by Pawson and Tilley on context, mechanisms, and outcomes (CMO) as a backdrop to determining what works for whom in what circumstances is useful in analyzing the A-NGO case study presented later in the paper. Yet as Kontos and Poland (2009, p. 6) suggest in examining social health care improvements, there is a need to reflect agency along with context in examining social programs:

More is at stake here than an exhortation to be mindful of context as a kind of general backdrop for interventions. In seeking to understand how mechanisms play out in a particular setting, with particular agents at a specific time, we must also take account of how reflexive agents perceive, negotiate, unwittingly reinforce or selectively resist the effects of these broader trends and influences in the context of their own life biographies, socialization, and the micro-social context of peer relations in the workplace.

Danermark et al. provided the basic methodological framework for the study. In proposing a critical realist method, Danermark et al. (p. 109) describe six stages in explanatory research with the first two stages involving moving from the concrete initial description to analytical resolution, whereby the components of interest are identified and separated. Abduction is involved in the third stage, where the components are reinterpreted using different theories and frameworks. Retroduction is then applied in stage 4, where the abstractions are then made “real” within a critical realist frame by hypothesizing the necessary structures and mechanisms consistent with the previous abstraction. These two stages of abduction and retroduction are in practice closely related as the theoretical reframing is matched against consequent realist outcomes. The fifth stage examines the relative merit of each abstraction/theory in explaining the observed happenings. The final stage, termed concretization and contextualization, is primarily focused on testing the applicability of proposed mechanisms to specific contexts (Johnston and Smith 2010; Miller and Tsang 2011) and involves examining the manifestation of different structures and mechanisms in specific concrete situations. In terms of our broadband study, we describe the initial five stages of the model as we scoped the project and developed and refined our proposals regarding the role of reflexivity in technology and associated broadband adoption.

The scoping stages led us to focus on community and small business adoption practices since international experience with fast broadband rollout had continually emphasized that building community demand was essential to ensuring satisfactory adoption (Crabtree and Roberts 2003). A number of small businesses in the region were interviewed concerning their views on broadband adoption and the NBN. These initial interviews prompted the development of an initial model emphasizing the importance of individual reflexivity in the adoption decision. This model was then applied retroductively in stages 4 and 5 to a single organization (the socalled A-NGO) to examine its explanatory power.

Other organizations apart from A-NGO were also examined but in less detail; ultimately, the limited funding available for the study providing only tentative confirmation of conclusions. Thus, stage 6, testing and contextualization, is left largely to subsequent research. This further stage would involve contextualization of the theory for various situations (including, perhaps, urban contexts) in order to examine the effectiveness of the reflexivity-based explanatory theory.

In Stage 1 of the explanatory framework, the complex situation studied is described in everyday concepts, and incorporates interpretations of the situation by the persons involved using qualitative and quantitative methods. In our study, this initial stage was very time-consuming and resource intensive as we came to understand the issues within the region and the nature of the personnel involved. This process was made easier by using a local researcher who was well known and respected in the region. Similarly, our relationship with the local development commission helped progress the study. The development commission was strongly committed to developing broadband capacity in the region and specifically included within its strategic plan the need to identify and grasp opportunities to enhance broadband network capacity. Having access to such involved and knowledgeable research partners provided significant benefit in terms of access to relevant personnel and contextual understandings, yet, the initial scoping stages of the research were still quite overpowering as the inherent complexities became more evident. The initial search for alternative explanations of observations was also complex and was assisted through the varied background of the research team. The previous individual research of team members on regional portal development, use of social software, and technology adoption provided different avenues for examination. Similarly, team members’ contrasting interest in both constructivist and realist approaches to research led to many alternative perspectives on the research.

We conducted structured and unstructured interviews and focus groups with relevant stakeholders in the region, totaling some 40 individual community members and organizational staff (see Appendix). Our initial description of the domain in everyday terms led us to use various methods, including soft systems methods, dialogue mapping, and mind maps, to initially scope the project and to arrive at an understanding of the important components of the phenomenon under study. Figure 2 reflects our initial efforts to scope the research domain as we completed a soft systems “CATWOE” analysis to define one of our target groups.

Figure 3 shows an extract of a dialogue map that recorded the unfolding views on NBN opportunities. The focus group was made up of representative officers from three local government authorities in remote regions. Dialogue mapping is a group interaction methodology supported by a software tool called Compendium. It is a facilitation technique developed to assist the development of shared understanding and emergent learning (Conklin 2006). In the Figure 3, we see the discussion uncovering the benefits of fast broadband for medical services as, for example, inexperienced doctors can “work under supervision,” a registrar can be linked electronically to support all doctors, and possibilities of amalgamation opportunities appreciated. As detailed later in the paper, we came to appreciate that a large part of building a commitment to broadband was teasing out innovation opportunities—encouraging projective, future-oriented visualization to replace often iterative, past-focused orientations.

In line with Danermark et al.’s stage 2 arguments, documents and interview transcripts were coded separately by each of the three researchers to identify the various structures, agents, and mechanisms impacting broadband adoption. Stage 2 of the framework, termed analytical resolution, involves separating or dissolving the composite and the complex by distinguishing the various components, aspects, or dimensions in order to divide the phenomenon (adoption and non-adoption) into “a number of imaginable causal components” (Danermark et al. 2002, p. 111). The initial complexity of these first two stages of the broadband study is difficult to represent in a relatively short journal paper. The research team used all available means, within the financial limitations of the study, to afford insights into understanding the attitudes of stakeholders in the region and likely causal impacts on the broadband adoption decision. Local, national, and international surveys on broadband were accessed as well as local regional development commission surveys, government documents, broadband submissions, and telecommunications plans. This stage 2 process depended heavily on critical realist representations to tease out appropriate elements (in our case, Archer’s morphogenetic approach provided the basic framework for initiating and focusing the analysis), encouraging a search for preexisting structures that had likely causal effects on agents interactions and thus subsequent transformation possibilities.

Stage 3 of the Danermark et al. explanatory framework reflects the core of the critical realist method in that abduction is used to propose likely theories (i.e., explanations) for actualities identified. In the movement from surface phenomena to a deeper, perhaps non-observable causal thing, the critical realist depends heavily on theory to propose possibilities. Such a perspective is consistent with a deep realism where explanation is not about prediction but about the steady unearthing of deeper levels of structures and mechanisms. This abductive process is difficult and time consuming and requires a non-traditional approach to research as theories are frequently examined and discarded based on their explanatory power. The process entails theoretical redescription and interpretation of the different structures and relations; the new contexts allowing the development of original ideas regarding the objects under study. As Smith (2010, p. 52) suggests from his own study on e-government adoption, this is a timeconsuming process:

In particular, the approach proposed here is heavy on front-end theorizing and theory-integration…. Furthermore, the result of theorization for the case presented here was a very broad set of potential causes (social systems are complex!) on which the author tried to gather data. The end result was breadth rather than depth, and plenty of time was spent on particular causes that, in the end, were not of any importance.

Our search for explanatory theory to examine broadband adoption provided limited results. Broadband research, particularly in the IS field, is surprisingly scarce. Peronard and Just (2011), in particular, decry the very limited research available on rural adoption of broadband. Dwivedi et al. (2008) suggest that the predominant model used in examining consumer adoption and diffusion of ICT has been the technology acceptance model (TAM) and diffusion of innovation (DOI). Recent TAM supported articles particularly focused on broadband adoption include Irani et al. (2009), Maarop et al. (2011), and Schilke and Wirtz (2012). Basically, TAM proposes that an individual’s intention to use broadband technology is jointly determined by their perception of the technology’s usefulness (PU) and of its ease of use (PEoU). Irani et al. extend traditional TAM concepts by replacing “usefulness” with “utilitarian outcomes” (the extent to which technology use enhances routine household activities) and “perceived ease of use” with “self-efficacy” for consumers perceived skill to operate the computer and access the Internet. Their study suggests perceived resources, being the availability of suitable computer equipment and skills, directly affect both broadband adoption and the intention to adopt broadband, and that broadband adopters will be influenced by positive messages from their existing social networks.

It is the contention of this paper that such TAM-focused analyses provide an overly simplistic representation of the individual’s adoption decision and cannot reflect, for example, the continual trade-off that prospective broadband users follow in determining the attractiveness of various plans; considerations as to cost and so-called utilitarian outcomes are intimately connected. Similarly, the TAM reflects an old-fashioned view of technology in that it does not reflect the importance of the increasingly pervasive “app” in adoption decisions. It is not solely the ease of use, or perceived usefulness of broadband, that drives broadband adoption; the usability, relevance, and context of the intended applications also play a significant role.

In a similar vein to TAM, broadband adoption surveys also tend to break down the adoption decision into separate impacting factors. For example, Horrigan (2011) found that non-adopters in America (making up approximately 35 percent of the total population) gave lack of own digital literacy, cost, lack of perceived relevance, and non-availability of broadband as primary reasons for not having broadband. Such surveys and TAM-focused studies helped to focus this study on particular general material constraints to adoption but provided little guidance in understanding the complexity of the individual adoption decision.

Given that the productivity benefits of superfast broadband depend largely on ubiquitous adoption, it is important to gain an appreciation of the reasons for any non-adoption, our focus being community non-adoption. As Dailey et al. (2010) suggest, comparative pricing is an important consideration as families never make decisions regarding broadband in isolation. They suggest that broadband adoption is “one of the key decisions made at the margin of household budgets—one of the first to be accommodated once other core expenses have been covered” (p. 28). Interestingly, broadband is rated as a second priority behind the cell phone. They also highlight the high numbers of what they term “unadopters,” previous users who drop their broadband primarily due to budget pressures.

Understanding and addressing individual reasons for nonadoption is essential to encourage ubiquitous adoption. In our case, we were particularly surprised that some interviewees rejected the NBN and its potential when they clearly had the economic means to afford the NBN, they had the requisite computer knowledge, and the transformative potential seemed self-evident. It was felt that it was important to understand possible causes behind this surprising rejection, as without such understanding there was little chance of achieving ubiquitous adoption. To address this non-adopting group, we need to know more than “what most people do most of the time” (Archer 2007a, p. 17); we came to feel that we needed to dig deeper to understand more clearly the mechanisms through which individuals made their subjective choices. As discussed below, Archer’s work on reflexivity provided a basis to support this examination.

In terms of Danermark et al.’s model, stages 3, 4, and 5 tended to merge in our study as we came to focus on community and small business adoption practices. Our initial use of the MA led us easily toward highlighting the important preexisting structural impositions on broadband adoption. For example, our early examination of broadband adoption focused on the way that the federal government set the structure of the NBN rollout: it was relatively easy to focus on the broadband adoption impacts of using a mix of fiber optic and satellite with associated cost and market implications. Similarly, the proposed wholesale only monopolized NBN marketing arrangements, prompting speculation about possible service shortcomings. This initial structural focus is in line with Reed (2005, p. 1668) who argues that there is

an undoubted risk that CR-based explanation will overemphasize structure at the expense of agency because it gives so much explanatory weight to the manifold way in which social structures shape and constrain the opportunities for action.

Similarly, Archer (2007b) berates critical realist argument as sometimes “better at conceptualizing structure than agency” (p. 39) and having too little to say about “persons.” Our initial structural concentration, focused on first-order emergent outcomes from governmental policy decisions, did not help us to explain why individual comments on proposed

NBN adoption were often so unexpectedly negative. Our consequent incorporation of individual reflexivity is in line with de Vaujany (2008) and Czerniewicz et al. (2009) who use reflexivity to examine groupware usage and student use of technology respectively.

## The Morphogenetic Approach and Modes of Reflexivity

The morphogenetic approach (MA) is described by Carter and New (2004) as probably the major development in realist qualitative empirical research. The approach reflects the fundamental critical realist assumptions:

(i) structure necessarily pre-dates the action(s) leading to its reproduction or transformation

(ii) structural elaboration necessarily post-dates the action sequences which gave rise to it (Archer 1995, p.15).

It proposes that ensuing structural elaboration can be either morphogenetic (structural change) or morphostatic (structural reproduction). A consequence of the approach is the suggestion that there are three basic mechanisms (Astbury and Leeuw 2010, p. 371):

(a) situational mechanisms (macro-micro)….This type of mechanism shows how specific social situations or events shape the beliefs, desires, and opportunities of individual actors.

(b) action-formation mechanisms (micro-micro)…. This type of mechanism looks at how individual choices and actions are influenced by specific combination of desires, beliefs, and opportunities.

(c) transformational mechanisms (micro-macro)…. [This type of mechanism] shows how a number of individuals, through their actions and interactions, generate macro-level outcomes.

The morphogenetic approach suggests three morphogenetic cycles operating in different domains—the structural, cultural, and agency—each having relative autonomy as they interact and intertwine with each other:

However, whilst structural and cultural antecedence means that they exert conditional influences upon agency, predisposing toward different courses of action, the former in no way determines the latter.… no conditional influence works as a hydraulic pressure, but is subject to reflective (if often imperfect)

evaluation by agents who weigh it against their other concerns, due to their own emergent properties of self-consciousness and self-monitoring (Archer 1995, p. 184).

The fact that the morphogenetic cycles have relative autonomy from one another and therefore may be out of synchrony leads to the possible situation where one cycle may foster morphogenesis and another morphostasis. As described below, when the structural cycle and the cultural cycle are out of synchrony, particular situational logics impact the personal domain and affect the options individuals see and respond to.

As de Vaujany (2008, p. 55) writes, the MA suggests that structures influence agential action by proposing a conditioning process:

Structures would determine a social cost (material or psychological) and would give a premium to certain actions. Actors could then conform to the conditioned action, diverge and accept the cost, or even display an inventive capacity and circumvent the constraints posed by structures.

According to de Vaujany, such explanation is incomplete in that it does not fully describe the means by which structures affect individuals’ beliefs and underlying reasons for consequent actions.

Archer’s later publications (2003, 2007a, 2010, 2012), in particular, address this issue by examining how an agent’s internal reflexivity interacts with imposing structural conditions to potentially create change. Archer describes reflexivity as

the regular exercise of the mental ability, shared by all normal people, to consider themselves in relation to their (social) contexts and vice versa. Such deliberations are important since they form the basis upon which people determine their future courses of action—always fallibly and always under their own descriptions (Archer 2007a, p. 4).

These reflexive deliberations are held internally by the individual in a question/answer format termed by Archer as an internal conversation.

Archer (2007a, p. 17) proposes a three-stage model for incorporating reflexivity:

(1) Structural and cultural properties objectively shape the situations that agents confront involuntarily, and inter alia possess generative powers of constraint and enablement in relation to (2) Subjects own constellations of concerns, as subjectively defined in relation to the three orders of natural reality: nature, practice and the social. (3) Courses of action are produced through the reflexive deliberations of subjects who subjectively determine their practical projects in relation to their objective circumstances.

The concerns at stake are respectively those of “physical wellbeing” in relation to the natural order, “performative competence” in relation to the practical order, and “self-worth” in relation to the social order. Developing a modus vivendi (“way of living”), and subsequently a social identity, involves weighing up between these concerns and developing projects consistent with these concerns. Confrontation with emergent structural and cultural properties is triggered as active agents pursue projects in line with their life interests. Archer suggests that it is stage 3 that is often neglected in social analysis; without this we settle for empirical generalizations about “what most people do most of the time”—in our view, TAM exemplifies such an approach.

Personal reflexivity is seen by Archer (2012) to be a necessary condition for social advancement in the present uncharted territory where variety spawns more variety and new opportunities supersede the old routine ways of doing: “In place of habitual guidelines, subjects become increasingly dependent upon their own personal concerns as their only guides to action” (p. 65).

Archer suggests four primary modes of reflexivity—communicative, autonomous, meta, and fractured—the modes basically shaped by the degree of contextual continuity evidenced over their personal growth and development of personal identity. The social relations within which a person is enmeshed are largely seen as having “the prime mediating (and mutational influence) upon how they develop and sustain a particular mode of reflexivity” (Archer 2012, p. 127). Communicative reflexives develop in an environment of contextual continuity with close family support. This continuity and close relationships with family and friends encourage them to complete their internal conversations by talking through with others and getting their opinions. The practice of this reflexivity mode reinforces contextual continuity as morphostasis is encouraged by “thought and talk” with “similars and familiars” who reinforce social solidarity (Archer 2012, p. 21). The rural community under examination tends to reflect this contextual continuity, being relatively close-knit and supportive of each other with the desire for external face-to-face communication confirming and supporting conventionalism: “In other words it enmeshes the subject in local custom and practice, it drags flights of fancy down to earth, it valorises the familiar over the novel, it privileges the public over the private, today over tomorrow and certainty over uncertainty” (Archer 2012, p. 33). This inherent conservatism, individual self-reliance, and the commonly held rural belief that city-based government bureaucracy remains out of touch with the concerns and needs of the rural producer is represented in this paper as a cultural “country way of life” (for a more detailed discussion of socalled “country mindedness” in rural Australia, see Gray and Lawrence 2001). According to Archer, the situational logic consequent from contextual continuity (where socio-cultural morphostasis is in line with cultural morphostasis) is protection as vested interests seek to maintain the contextual continuity on which they depend.

However, where structural change emerges in an unchanging cultural system, contextual discontinuity is promoted. Such discontinuity promotes what Archer terms as a situational logic of competition, where the predominant reflexive mode is the autonomous reflexive, as individuals follow a degree of instrumental rationality to make the most of competitive chances to achieve their own personal goals. Autonomous reflexives do not need the opinion of others to test out their own internal conversations; they tend to be self-motivated, socially mobile upward, and often undertake risky, innovative, or ruthless courses of action. Following from their lack of dependence on the similars and familiars to confirm their internal conversations, the autonomous reflexive depends more on their own counsel to be more open to unconventional ways of doing, tending toward progressive opportunities and new vistas in line with their personal goals. The autonomous reflexive is

not a very political animal. He would rather try to circumvent the constraints of his societal environment than attempt to change the social structure itself—because circumvention is an individualistic exercise, whereas structural change is a collective enterprise (Archer 2003, p. 299).

This form of reflexivity is encouraged by contextual discontinuity and characterized by social mobility.

Progressively, as rapid morphogenesis ensues at the cultural level and at the socio-cultural level, opportunities open up more and more, thus creating a contextual incongruence which Archer sees as providing a situational logic founded around opportunity. Such a situation encourages the adoption of more innovative modalities favoring the meta-reflexive. As opportunities further open up and variety generates more variety, a greater degree of self-critique and inner reflection is required to chart a pathway through personal concerns, projects, and practices—thus making a way for the metareflexive. As for the autonomous reflexive, meta-reflexives also do not need the confirmation of external parties and are characterized by constantly looking inward, reflecting on the reflection process itself. Meta-reflexives tend to aim to live up to their ideals, often becoming subversive toward constraints and enablements, being prepared to be make adjustments to fulfill their life purpose. They can be quite political in their actions to achieve their social equity goals and tend to display lateral social mobility and thrive in contextual incongruity and social volatility.

The fourth modality, fractured reflexivity, is the mode evident for the passive agents; they depend little on internal deliberation to chart courses of action. Their focus is on dayto-day survival, being seen as the casualties of today’s societies. Unable to accommodate the needed “reflexive imperative” so strongly argued for by Archer (2012), they regularly “admit to huge difficulties in making decisions, in defining courses of action to be consistently pursued and, above all, in engaging in anything more than the survivalist’s day-to-day planning” (Archer 2012, p. 249).

The four reflexivity modes make up the dominant reflexive modes for all of Archer’s studies, such reflexivity being determined via in-depth interview and her developed instrument ICONI. An indicative breakdown, based on her study of a Coventry community in the United Kingdom, provides an estimate of the distribution of modes as varying from 21 percent to 27 percent for each mode (Archer 2012, p. 319).

## Reflexivity, Agency, and Action

Archer suggests that a major preoccupation of reflexivity and the internal conversation is for individuals to chart a pathway through their considered life chances and to define a modus vivendi that they can practically follow; she is less concerned in her examination with the potential for the internal conversation as a guide to immediate action. In contrast, Wiley (2010) examines the way that inner speech can support agency which, for the purpose of the article, he simply describes as “the process of conscious and purposive human action” (p. 23). He sees this deliberate process as being supported by a series of processes involving a mental construction of a possible action (defining), the actual choosing and the behavioral carrying out of the action (enacting). All of these processes are seen to be supported by inner speech.

Emirbayer and Mische (1998, p. 962) also provide a more action focused representation of agency as

a temporally embedded process of social engagement, informed by the past (in its “iterational” or habitual aspect) but also oriented toward the future (as a “projective” capacity to imagine alternative possibilities) and toward the present (as a “practicalevaluative” capacity to contextualize past habits and future projects within the contingencies of the moment).

They see agents emphasizing different components at different times and contexts:

there are times and places when actors are more oriented toward the past, more directive toward the future, or more evaluative of the present; actors may switch between (and reflexively transform) their orientations toward action, thereby changing their degrees of flexible, inventive, and critical response toward structuring contexts (p. 1012).

Emirbayer and Mische briefly mention the importance of the “ontology of the self” (p. 974) and the internal conversation in understanding agency but do not specifically develop this issue.

In terms of envisaging broadband possibilities, we feel that Emirbayer and Mische’s three components of agency can play an important role in understanding the adoption decision. As Wiley suggests, individuals move through defining, choosing, and enacting prior to action; in particular defining an action involves considering “the consequences of the act for oneself” (p. 26). Using Archer’s terms, they weigh up the consequences of action with respect to their ultimate concerns. Such an argument fits well with that proposed by Katz et al. (2012) when they see broadband as fulfilling communicative options to achieve personal goals:

individuals engage the communicative options available to them in their environments to achieve their goals; and furthermore, that the connections individuals make vary according to the goals they are trying to achieve (p. 82).

They suggest that communities with low broadband adoption rates adopt only “if they are able to understand how online resources can help them accomplish goals that are important to them” (p. 82). We contend that this defining stage is particularly important in broadband adoption as individuals demonstrate a differing capacity to envisage iterative, practical, or projective opportunities for broadband to support the projects they see as important. Such an argument raises the importance of the broadband “project” in achieving the modus vivendi of individuals.

Increasingly, as our interviews and observations progressed, we could see one of the three components being emphasized. The shop owner who could not see a need for more than a single e-mail a day and the business owner who was happy with his current Internet knowledge can be seen to be informed by the past in their iterative focus. Similarly the country person who focuses on potential electricity disruptions and water shortage cannot see the significance of broadband, given their grounding in the present and their focus on practical concerns.

## The Search for Mechanisms

The above explanations of adoption mechanisms are proposed as a useful basis for examining broadband adoption by individuals. The reflexivity mode, along with the capacity for agents to visualize iteratively, practically, or projectively, describing the mechanism by which agents interact with relevant cultural and socio-cultural structures in morphogenetic or morphostatic sequences as they adopt broadband or not. This aggregate model is proposed as a generalizable mechanism for explaining, for example, the (surprising) observation that often people with the means, capacity, and purpose do not adopt broadband—the mechanism in this case being proposed as a communicative reflexivity bound together by an inability to envision practical or projective broadband opportunities. Yet such a model is provisional and fallible as Danermark et al. (2002, p. 81) describe when they suggest that there are no “fixed criteria from which it is possible to assess in a definite way the validity of an abductive conclusion.” It is the purpose of research to progressively increase the confidence in the mechanisms suggested by applying the model to different contexts (the stage 6 within their methodological approach). Similarly, in Pawson and Tilley’s (1997, p. 77) words “the task of a realist evaluation is to find ways of identifying, articulating, testing and refining conjectured CMO configurations.”

The search for deep causal generative mechanisms is fundamental to critical realist practice. Pawson and Tilley specifically examine the importance of mechanisms in evaluating social programs, where social programs are loosely seen as interventions aimed at social change, a program being seen as a spiral of new ideas and transforming conditions. For them it is not programs that “work,” it is the action of stakeholders in taking up program features and initiatives that make them work, “the causal potential of an initiative takes the form of providing reasons and resources to enable program participants to change” (p. 215). In other words, programs are said to work (have successful outcomes) only in so far as they introduce the appropriate ideas and opportunities (mechanisms) to groups or individuals in the appropriate social and cultural conditions (contexts). Pawson and Tilley see a “social program” as a social system which comprises “the interplay of individual and institution, of agency and structure, and of micro and macro social processes” (p. 63). While they focus on evaluating social programs, it is useful to examine the way they represent the social environment within which programs are implemented. They propose the adoption of social realism to provide a standard set of concepts for examining social systems (and evaluating social programs). Similarly the examination of NBN adoption and usage can benefit from such an approach.

The NBN promotion can be presented in a similar light to that of health promotion programs, in that the successful intervention requires human behavioral change. Under this view,

interventions need to address not only the cognitive or psychosocial elements of behavior change, be that lifestyle behavior, organizational behavior, or the behavior of policy-makers, but also the social environments in which these behaviors are shaped or maintained (Poland et al. 2008, p. 302).

The context within which a program is implemented is critical to understanding the impact of program mechanisms; context is not to be regarded as a contaminant, or a nuisance, to be addressed only where it threatens to seriously compromise implementation or program outcomes. Mechanisms fired by broadband provision are expected to be heavily dependent on the particular context in which the provision takes place; in our case, we suggest that the communicative reflexive mode is dominant in rural communities and thus needs to be recognized in encouraging acceptance. The outcomes of programs are seen to be the intended and unintended consequences of programs consequent from the activation of different mechanisms in different contexts.

Astbury and Leeuw (2010) argue the concept of a “mechanism” is poorly understood with, for example, research by Mahoney (cited in Astbury and Leeuw, p. 367) identifying 24 different definitions. They define mechanisms as “underlying entities, processes, or structures which operate in particular contexts to generate outcomes of interest” (p. 368). In the context of their paper on program evaluation, they particularly argue against conflating the term mechanism with program activity. For example with respect to broadband adoption, it may be observed that basic Internet skills training leads to improved adoption statistics. It is tempting to argue that the skills training caused the increased adoption, but as Astbury and Leeuw suggest, such argument may be too deterministic;

the underlying mechanism that causes the change may not be the program activity of basic skills training, but may be the improved individual’s belief in their capability to use broadband effectively, along with the capacity to envisage useful opportunities consequent from broadband adoption. In line with our reflexivity arguments, we would propose that basic skills training should also include an associated strategy to highlight possibilities, these possibilities tailored to particular reflexivity modes (these might be determined via an instrument similar to Archer’s ICONI instrument).

As Astbury and Leeuw suggest, mechanisms are some deeper “thing” that have three common characteristics:

1. Mechanisms are usually hidden;

2. Mechanisms are sensitive to variations in context; and

3. Mechanisms generate outcomes (p. 368).

The remaining part of the paper investigates whether our proposed mechanisms can provide useful explanation in a particularly interesting case example. The A-NGO case describes a not-for-profit organization that developed an innovative social media application SOCNET that was released to the local community. The platform was held up as an exemplar of what could be done with newly introduced broadband technologies. We discovered over the period of our research that the software had been abandoned and, in line with Danermark et al.’s framework, saw the case as a good opportunity to retroductively examine the usefulness of our model. We thus seek to answer the retroductive question: To what extent can considerations of Archer’s individual reflexivity modes and Emirbayer and Mische’s capacities for visualization help explain the conditions under which a seemingly successful broadband application at A-NGO be abandoned?

## Case Study Description

We reached the primary case study after a long series of interviews and group meetings with individuals, industry representatives, and government agencies over a period of about 6 months. When we entered the case organization, personnel were chosen who were most familiar with the innovative applications and potential broadband capabilities. The designer of the application was interviewed twice and the CEO once. A number of the social media end users were also interviewed. All meetings were recorded and transcribed, and individual researchers took their own notes. A team of four researchers participated or listened to the recordings and analyzed the data individually to code important elements. We then came together and discussed the validity of using our suggested model. As detailed below, we felt that the model provided a useful means for explaining actual happenings in depth. The following describes the initial interviews during which the innovative broadband-dependent applications were introduced, and later interviews where we found the media application had been abandoned and the GIS application languishing.

## A-NGO: An Autonomous Reflexive Collides with Structures

A-NGO is a not-for-profit organization with the objective of coordinating environmental stewardship of the region. The macro-level social structures within this context are those of the federal government which, through its “Enviro” funding program, determines the goals, performance indicators and funding to improve environmental and water catchment outcomes. A-NGO sees its purpose as improving and maintaining the natural environment; its main goal is to deliver activities and programs, in combination with community groups, business and industry, nongovernment agencies, individuals, and all levels of government, to conserve and enhance natural assets and advance sustainable commercial outcomes.

A-NGO is based in BigTown and establishes management processes, job roles, environmental programs, and technologies that assist it in achieving its goals to protect fauna, flora, and soils within its catchment areas. It is not a policing body or a “doing” organization, however, being constrained by funding rules and its terms of reference; its only viable strategy is to distribute responsibility for land management activities to the inhabitants of the area, who are either directly funded to conduct (for example) particular programs such as fox eradication, or are supported to act largely in a voluntary capacity. The mechanism it employs to do this is to raise awareness of the problems, develop programs for environmental improvement, establish and promote the framework for processing funding applications, and encourage voluntary participation in the community.

## Conditions in the First Encounter

Within the context of these vast distances, information technology provides important capabilities to manage and share information pertaining to environmental management. About 20 natural and social scientists and technology specialists work at A-NGO. The cost of printing and distribution of paper has led to a minimization of printed matter in favor of digital content on the A-NGO web site and usage of e-mail lists (there are 3,000 subscribers to the newsletter and 90 percent of subscribers have e-mail). The web site is a valuable content provider, containing all information pertaining to the A-NGO, the programs it runs (in wind erosion, coastal care, biodiversity, and so on), and how to become involved.

Going beyond communication via e-mail and the web site, A-NGO envisaged two significant information systems to support its goals through the Internet. The first system is a geographical information system (GIS), the purpose of which is to allow the collation of multiple layers of information about specific locations. Gathered from various sources, A-NGO might provide information on terrain, water courses, rainfall, vegetation, and nature reserves via the GIS on the Internet. Users, such as farmers, environmental groups, and mining companies, might add to this by providing information about local histories, photographs, weather observations, their own rain records, and fertilizer or harvesting histories. These would be made available to anyone who might use maps and data about land in their vicinity to develop their own land management strategies. At the beginning of the research project, this system is in the design phase.

The second system is a new social networking site, SOCNET, which is structured to allow community groups to create projects themselves (for example, to care for a residential street or rehabilitate a particular beach or watercourse). As the responsible senior communications officer (SCO) says, it would “save you knocking on every door.” This system has been developed at a cost of AUD \$30,000 and is proprietary to A-NGO. However, the functions are familiar social media functions and easy to use: start an interest group, create, post, and discuss a theme; upload a picture. No further structures were predefined; the software allows a wide corridor of usage. Three of the researchers easily obtained a sign-on and walked through the software, finding it straightforward and “intuitive” but, at that time, empty of any content.

A-NGO is a technologically sophisticated group with a clear understanding of technology and of how it can be used for collating information (the GIS) and community building (SOCNET). The data intensity of the GIS requires highcapacity networks and relatively sophisticated, purposive users and powerful end-user devices. As the SCO said, “This absolutely needs Broadband.” SOCNET, on the other hand, implies an always-on, ubiquitous presence so that users can communicate through the social network. It is important to note the divergent socio-materiality of these IT artefacts. Where a GIS consists of structured data, supported by clear functions and protocols for upload and retrieval, SOCNET is discursive unstructured text, highly malleable, not predefined, and personal. It relies on the user to define purpose and theme and imprint themself in some way into the online conversation.

The dominant agent in charge of SOCNET and the web site is the SCO, a highly experienced public servant with a theoretical and practical understanding of ecology, social networking and technology. She describes the origins of SOCNET:

It was an intuitive thing of having been chairman of everything since I was 16….I started a newspaper and started noticing that farming and mining people didn’t communicate…then women came in to write articles for the newspaper (about gardening) and we saw a zipper effect in the community and that started me thinking… common interest is what brings people together.

She has strong opinions about community building and the role of technology and a clear chain of reasoning as to why technology is important: “The most fundamental need of human beings is to belong, more important than food and water,” she says. Not surprisingly, she considers always-on, ubiquitous, high-speed broadband to be essential for these relatively communications-intensive forms of application to succeed: “An integrated view of managing the catchment and the only way we can do that is with high broadband speed.” The NBN is a necessary condition for the success of both of these systems as a foundation technology to support the various mechanisms designed to service the aspirations of A-NGO.

## Situation after One Year

A year after our initial interviews, further investigations at A-NGO show an organization with new plans and priorities. The need to address climate change issues has become more important: a decision was made by the CEO to redistribute the limited resources and create a new climate change officer role, although no new funding has been received for this. The position of senior communications officer was downgraded to a junior role and the incumbent has left the organization.

The SOCNET system, the development of which was funded by government grant under the National Resources Management program “Enviro,” has fallen out of favor and has been closed down. As the new CEO said,

[SOCNET] was not consciously part of [A-NGO’s] vision. It was [the senior communication officer’s] interpretation of how to achieve it…her commitment to SOCNET meant that there were some difficulties…no formal authority was provided by management or the board to advocate for SOCNET in the way she did.

Indeed, the new CEO was experienced in managing technology projects and had worked extensively with youth and natural resource management online networks in rural areas. He said that online networks have not worked for the National Resource Management programs or youth groups throughout rural Australia, citing his own experience and those of the previous manager. There had been poor uptake by farmers and other country groups, and “it will be a long time before that changes…they are outdoors, riding horses and playing hockey, not sitting in front of a computer….And this was the issue with SOCNET.” The money spent on SOCNET is euphemistically seen as “not having been used to its maximum potential” and, it was stated, social interaction via the Internet was not a function the natural resource management community required. In trying to encourage take-up of SOCNET, A-NGO had conducted workshops on SOCNET, executed marketing programs, “and there was no interest—not one bite on the environmental side.” Certainly, A-NGO’s view of the farming community that should be using SOCNET is that it is more conservative than most: How to crack this group is a key question for A-NGO.

On the other hand, the CEO said the organization was still very keen to get funding for the GIS application. There is a perceived strong demand for a map-based information system which integrated all information, from images to photos to structured data about rainfall, harvests, historical events, and climate, “and we need broadband for that.” But in contrast to the previous vision of “crowdsourcing” facilitated by direct upload of data by farmers and other individuals or community groups, uploading in the new design would be controlled and managed by a system administrator: “Not everyone can be uploading anything, it needs verification first….Farmers and environmental groups are looking for reliable, useful information and quick, easy access.” But efforts to gain funding for this sophisticated and expensive system and the associated specialist administrators have been unsuccessful, so this application does not exist yet and languishes in “pending” status. In the meantime, because of the need for communications media which tie together various sources for information, the CEO tells us that A-NGO will return to a “newsletter type strategy that would be pushed to members.” As the CEO summed up, “it’s not about social media, it’s about getting information about soil types.”

This was a considerable departure from the organization’s initial position. So at this point, we examined data from outside A-NGO for confirmation of the existence of a general resistance in the rural community to social media, one of the very affordable avenues for tapping into community energy and knowledge: recall that the basis for A-NGO’s environmental rehabilitation success was the participation of a wide cross-section of the rural community.

In our first set of interviews, we had conducted a focus group with 5 individuals from TS Shire, a small shire of 600 people covering 8,000 square kilometres about 200 kilometres east of BigTown. It consists mostly of farmers and farming service workers and is both declining and aging. Community activities, such as dances, concerts, fetes, and sports, which involve face-to-face contact, are very important and provide critical opportunities for social contact and community building. Participants in this focus group said there was no substitute for being together and that broadband would not be very useful to them: “Most people are isolated so they like to meet and talk to each other in person rather than use IT to replace it.” They had little understanding of how social media with broadband might complement face to face meeting and facilitate organizing and preparing a concert, for example. When this was explained to them, they came to appreciate that many of their smaller planning interactions would usefully be supported by social media. When asked to what extent this was a generational issue, they replied,

We have an ageing population. We also have an emerging youth demographic not participating in the current offerings in the community. We may have an opportunity to engage them using IT. Older people may not be able to do all the jobs needed to run the community. Even though we want their participation in decision making we discourage them from getting involved, in a way.

In the course of this focus group we came to realize the role of informed, nonconfrontational conversation in changing attitudes. As opportunities for broadband to support current activities were highlighted, the participants moved from a fairly robust position of not being interested in broadband to appreciating that maybe it could play a practical role for them that did not threaten their face-to-face encounters. In hindsight, it is perhaps not surprising that such nonconfrontational “thought and talk” in the company of familiars and similars worked well with the predominantly communicative reflexive group.

We subsequently interviewed employees of a large farmers’ group who had been responsible for implementing social networks with their members. This very substantial group has members over the entire state and is highly visible, both politically and within rural communities. They claimed,

[Many farmers are] tech savvy, bringing laptops with them to meetings and using social media since 2008. Facebook and Twitter didn’t really take off then for us; we didn’t really know what to use it for…70 percent of people coming to our Facebook site just came to pick up information…like about links to a wild dog control forum.

Within political contexts, social media has since become a major tool for social influence. After a live meat export scandal in 2011, animal rights activists and cruelty prevention groups immediately applied political and social pressure using social media such as Twitter and Facebook to stop the trade. Until that point, the group’s and its members’ use of social media had been modest and linked to practical discussions (e.g., the wild dog control); then it exploded, as they had a practical threat to their livelihood to resolve. Within one week, they went from 150 to 800 Twitter followers, and they started to link from their Facebook site with other farmer groups with names like “Save Australian Farming” and “Save Live Exports.” After this issue had subsided, farmers continued to use it regularly, mostly for work, but also to tweet the local radio station or drop a Facebook comment on cricket results. According to these sources, even though there was initial reluctance and conservatism, the catalyst for use was the practical political threat of restrictions to live meat exports followed by the realization that the software was in fact very simple and cheap. Further impetus is given by mobility and smart phones:

A farmer can use them anywhere at any time: they get home from work at 8:00 at night, the last thing they’re going to do is sit down and do social networking—now when I e-mail or tweet someone, I get responses straight away.

These two pieces of evidence suggest that without any particular cause or event, farmers and rural people have little interest in exploring social media technology: there is a “country way of life,” that is both conservative and consuming, that is physically demanding, “close to the edge,” and occurs over a wide space and during long days in the field, but this evolves as generations change and norms coevolve with technology. When there is a practical need, they can be rapid adopters. And the country way of life is mutable: members of the farmers’ group within the 40 to 55 year age bracket are now keen users and the norms that led to reluctance to express themselves publicly are changing. This can be seen as a second order emergent consequence of their initial virtual foray on the live meat exports trade issue—the practical action consequent from live meat export concerns has led to an ongoing positive change in their attitude to social media.

The additional data indicates that while the country way of life is a dominant cultural structure for the farming group, with the preference for face-to-face contact being very strong, they are open to adoption of technology when they can see there is a practical component and it can be integrated into their mobile lives. Whether this change in technology usage can be tied back to a lasting change in the cultural system is questionable and difficult to assess. As Horrocks (2009, p. 53) argues,

Maintaining this example of analytical dualism is much more difficult when analyzing contemporary or near contemporary data from the position– practice system of an organization than it is with historical data.

Only with further detailed and extensive study can such a proposal be verified comfortably. In our view this “countrymindedness” culture is enduring and ever-present; for example, both the CEO and SCO acknowledge that it was something that had to be allowed for in their software design and promotion. This belief structure would not have been changed by the failure of SOCNET—in fact we view it as being reinforced. Yet, case examples such as the live meat trade example, where farmers embrace social software for practical purposes, suggest a potential for cultural change, particularly as the NBN develops and mobile availability increases.

Returning to A-NGO, we see the narrative thread binding budgetary constraints and information system outcomes as important. A core principle of A-NGO is community participation and activism, as this is the only way to affordably achieve environmental outcomes. A-NGO obtains government funds and, instead of executing projects itself, passes on these funds to community groups and individuals who essentially provide the knowledge and the labor to get the projects done. This devolution of natural resource management (NRM) responsibilities to communities was a deliberate policy of the central government to make the regions more accountable for resource management issues (Wallington and Lawrence 2008). This devolution was supported via the “Enviro” funding program, under which SOCNET was funded. Acting as a visionary meta-reflexive, the SCO introduced SOCNET as an effort to continue this logic and engage the community to organize themselves using a designed social media platform to allow community members to create communities of practice whereby groups could collectively address particular environmental projects, in effect encouraging primary agents to become corporate agents (that is, acting as a part of a collective with social change targets; Archer 1995). The lack of community interest in

SOCNET could be partly explained as a lack of desire by the farming target group to act as the corporate agents that the software required (although this proposed mechanism would need further investigation). Perhaps more thought needed to be given by the software developer as to ways to encourage this rather momentous change from primary agent to corporate agent, particularly for the targeted communicative reflexive group who seek external community ratification. Such desires could have been supported within the software via diary components to arrange frequent offline meetings, for example, or perhaps via the incorporation in the software of blogging capability to “talk through” prospective actions.

Whatever the case, the software was rejected, despite our own views that the user interface seemed user-friendly and not unduly complex. We felt that the most likely explanation for failure is that SOCNET was not in keeping with the desire for face-to-face interactions, an important cultural requirement from the targeted predominant communicative reflexive group. The lack of clear and immediate practical benefit would also have failed to entice them further. The rejection can be taken to support Archer’s suggestion that the communicative reflexive farming group followed a situational logic of protection, as the farming group seeks to maintain their cultural desire to support existing ways of operating.

As a consequence of the low enrollment, SOCNET was abandoned by the current CEO and the GIS scaled back. In terms of the NBN rollout, the new stance embodied by A-NGO is a backward step: usage of the Internet for crowdsourcing into the GIS and using social networks to create participation and affordable momentum have been abandoned in favor of a more conservative model of information provision. But this conservative model is more expensive, requiring hands-on administrators and expensive software, and as the funding is not available, the GIS project is inactive. The NBN capabilities of high interactivity, high data transfer rates, always-on provision, and ubiquity of access are unnecessary for the planned e-newsletter push approaches, thus diminishing reasons to adopt broadband.

## CMO Analysis

Our analysis of the case makes use of Pawson and Tilley’s (1997) proposal for realistic evaluation to find ways of identifying, articulating, testing, and refining conjectured CMO configurations. As demonstrated in the tables presented later in this section, we use their tabular method of demonstrating context (C), mechanism (M), and outcomes (O). These elements were identified in independent coding by the research team and agreed upon in a collaborative process. The separate tables for the different societal layers can be seen to reflect Archer’s “analytical dualism” in that the separation is not ideal but is necessary for analytical reasons to represent a complex duality.

The separation can also be seen to be justifiable because of the fact that the layers tend to be largely independent in their day-to-day instrumental interactions. The lack of integration between the governmental and organizational layer is a consequence of the fact that much of the work being done at the organizational layer is a result of the predominant use of tendering and contracts; there is little direct integration (apart from performance monitoring) between government and rural day-to-day “goings-on” in this case (Productivity Commission 2010). This lack of integration between the layers suggests that the higher-level government layer does not play a significant role in the subsequent micro–micro mechanisms at the middle level; it provides the preexisting context or structure via contractual arrangements but little day-to-day micro-level instrumental input. Again also the separation between the organizational layer and the community layer works well in this case because A-NGO is not a farmer group; it is predominantly made up of engineers and scientists. The farmer group is largely separate from the internal decision-making of A-NGO. This separation would perhaps not work so well if farmers played a more direct role in the workings of A-NGO.

Similarly, in terms of technology representation, the separation between community, organization, and government again works quite well for the technology-in-use activity we are examining, primarily due to the fact that the layers do not interact significantly with respect to usage. In terms of package use, the community level stands on its own reasonably well as there is little ongoing structural imposition from the organizational level as it is not a required adoption or subject to ongoing monitoring. In effect, we can simply represent the interactions as a layered morphogenetic model with the output from one layer being the preexisting structural condition for the layer below. The government layer had virtually no impact on use, nor, given the tendering arrangements, on the actual design.

One difficulty with this analytical separation is that the time periods over which the CMOs operate are necessarily different. The governmental layer is over the greatest time as the contractual funding for “Enviro” is longer but has definite limits. The middle organizational layer refers to the SOCNET and GIS funding regime timing, the lower-level community layer only the time period relevant to the SOCNET implementation. As Horrocks (2009) suggests, it is important when describing change cycles that the timing over which the change ensues is clearly defined as it bounds the “problems” to which the analysis relates.

Another consequence of the separation is that we largely bypass the consideration of “second order emergent” (the results of the results) since our analysis is nearly all representable as “first order emergent” in that the outcome of the top governmental layer is the structural context for the middle layer, mechanism-based outcomes at the middle layer being first order emergent from the context so displayed, rather than second order or third order emergent if represented as a single CMO model. This analytical device does help with understanding but perhaps does not fully reflect the ultimate complex consequences of prior structural and cultural conditions.

## The Governmental Regulatory Level

As detailed in Table 1, the aspiration of land care in a country as ecologically and agriculturally fragile as Australia results in structures that involve both intellectual and emotional commitments. The federal government, the public service agencies, and the funding programs constitute key high-level structures with impacts at the organizational policy level and for individuals who fill roles within that organization.

## The Organizational Level

As detailed in Table 2, A-NGO is a structure called into being by the funding program “Enviro.” The terms of reference, the board of directors, the CEO, and various positions and procedures are structures invested with particular roles and responsibilities. The ultimate existence of these structures depends on funds being provided on an ongoing basis via governmental funding programs. In line with Pawson and Tilley, the funding programs themselves cannot be seen as mechanisms; it is the extent to which stakeholders use the inbuilt features of funding programs that is important, such adoption providing potential change outcomes. This subtle difference in emphasis encourages a focus on the social adoption mechanisms in evaluating programs rather than the actual program implementation.

Stakeholders at the organizational level are heavily impacted by structures and mechanisms initiated at the higher governmental level, largely under contractual arrangement. Within these agreed time and budget limited arrangements, A-NGO then initiates structures designed to engage the farmers and consumers at a community level. Enrollment and usage of SOCNET was hoped to be an important mechanism to engage and drive effective environmental behavior at this community level. Yet the hoped-for mechanism for change ultimately was unsuccessful as individual stakeholders at the community level did not take up the opportunity.

SOCNET had been initiated and developed by the senior communication officer (SCO) who had a strong background in the use of social media for organizational communication. The SCO can be seen to be acting predominantly as an autonomous reflexive in that she pushed ahead with SOCNET implementation based upon her own projective vision of the importance of communities of practice for bringing about change, this vision lacking full organizational backing and without due thought to funding constraints. As Mutch (2007, p. 1130) describes,

[Autonomous reflexives] are more likely to strike out on their own in the pursuit of personal projects. In pursuing such projects, they are likely to be aware of the constraints and enablements that existing structures afford, and to seek to work with and change these to suit their own requirements. This does not mean that they will be successful in their endeavors: “to call ‘autonomous reflexives’ strategists no more implies that they possess great strategic virtuosity than does the fact that someone is called a military strategist” (Archer 2003:253). However, it does mean that they are more likely to be aware of the broader features of the environment in which they operate, and seek to manipulate such features as potentially afford them success in their projects.

This was evident in the A-NGO case: the SCO, as an autonomous reflexive, underestimated the communicative reflexivity of the targeted farmer segment and their lack of interest in becoming the corporate agents that the social software SOCNET required. The CEO, perhaps representable at times as a fractured reflexive, by virtue of power structures and constrained by organizational objectives and budgets, kills off the vision. The CEO can be seen as someone who appreciates the vision of the autonomous reflexive and the transformational potentialities of the technology but who is caught in the existing ontological web of structures and powers, budgets, and lines of command—perhaps himself less powerful than the person who was demoted and whose project was stopped. As Wiley (2010, p. 28) suggests, “most inner speech is for the voluntary side of life,” and we can represent the CEO’s behavior as largely unreflexive, thus suggesting a new brand of reflexivity where individuals demonstrate a reflexivity conditioned by the roles they fill (this suggests a possible subject for further study as detailed in the discussion section below).

## The Community Level

Although the SOCNET application was targeted at the community in general, allowing all members to create communities of practice, the farmer segment was the predominant target since it was considered to have the most significant potential impact on environmental change. We considered that farmers exhibited the characteristics of communicative reflexivity generally, their conservatism consequential on their strong sense of community and their close personal interactions with members of their group. Our interviews with various farming supply groups indicated that they are certainly not technology poor or averse to technology, but see technology as a practical and necessary tool for addressing the harshness of their environment and the large scale farming required. SOCNET, as a typical manifestation of social software, offered no clear linkage to everyday praxis of farming; it was a communicational greenfield, where one could start groups and dialogues about anything, but what in particular? A mechanism for breaking this ice would be at least to provide a purpose. Corroborating evidence for this suggestion was provided by the example of take-up of social media to campaign for live meat exports—in this case, social media adoption followed a clear practical purpose. In the absence of such an event, cultural considerations predominated and worked against SOCNET enrollment.

The reluctance of rural people to embrace technologymediated communication at the community level led to changes at the organizational level: A-NGO changed its information delivery strategy back to a conventional information push approach in the light of SOCNET’s failure to gain traction and mounting experience in other jurisdictions that country people did not use social networking.

Figure 4 describes the A-NGO context overlaid on Archer’s morphogenetic model, enhanced with a consideration of individual reflexivity. We argue that micro-level interactions between time T<sup>2</sup> and T<sup>3</sup> led to both morphogenesis within the A-NGO organizational structure and technologies, and morphostasis in terms of the confirmation of the enduring A-NGO perception of the so-called country way of life.

The failure of an exemplar broadband application, within an organization made up of largely professional scientists would be expected to have undesirable effects in terms of growing broadband adoption, but these effects can only be surmised at this stage pending further investigation.

## Retroductive Analysis and Discussion

As the above discussion indicates, we came to appreciate that our mechanism-based proposals concerning reflexivity could not stand alone. The argument for reflexivity is intimately bound up with Archer’s MA and the critical realist representations underlying it. Thus, along with the reflexivity suggestions, we need also to retroductively examine the unified explanations (such as the relevance of situational logistics) provided by Archer’s arguments.

As the study developed, it was becoming clearer that the consideration of reflexivity modes was useful in explaining broadband adoption. The underlying issue for the study was moving away from “how should broadband adoption be encouraged in rural regions” toward “how should broadband adoption be encouraged for particular reflexivity modes in a rural context” and even more particularly, as we examined A-NGO and the farming group, “how to encourage communicative reflexives to adopt broadband in rural context.” This realization provides an important focus for further research: to examine how reflexivity might affect the adoption decision in other contexts.

It is interesting to speculate as to the underlying situational logic constraining agents’ interaction possibilities. The rural environment is changing as substantial rural funding initiatives develop, initiatives such as the “Royalty for Regions” program, which commits to investing 25 percent of the State’s mining, oil, and gas royalties in needed rural infrastructure, is having important effects on rural infrastructure developments. Mining developments and national resource management initiatives are promoting investment in the regions. The NBN program, focused as it is on rural development, is expanding in the region and promoting variety, opportunity, and new information sources. All of these increasingly change the way people reflexively monitor their life chances. Consequently, for our study, it is tempting to apply Archer’s situational logic of competition to the area, as structural changes ensue and cultural systems remain the same, yet we see this as difficult to justify given the strong conservatism we encountered. As young people move to urban regions to make the most of opportunities, we would expect the remaining older demographic would continue this inherent conservatism and reinforce the perceived predominant communicative reflexive mode. Archer’s proposal concerning the increasing predominance of the autonomous reflexive and meta-reflexive was not supported in our rural-based study. The predominant attitude in the region seemed to be still quite focused on maintain existing “ways of doing.”

Given our lack of evidence for wide-ranging structural or cultural change, we accept Archer’s proposal that the situational logic of protection is most appropriate, whereby institutions are mutually reinforcing and disruptions are discouraged as “everyone has something to lose from disruption” (Archer 1995, p. 220). As Archer (2012) suggests, communicative reflexivity encourages a situational logic of protection:

It protects and protracts the micro-life world of, at most, a small community and, increasingly today, a handful of significant others. Such protectiveness towards (usually) the subject’s family has been shown to involve objective self-sacrifice, a refusal by subjects to avail themselves of accessible forms of advancement and a willingness to settle for “living within their means” (p. 33).

Whether such situational logic is maintainable in the face of ensuing change in the region is perhaps questionable and merits further study.

With respect to the role of technology within the community, the selection of A-NGO as a primary case example was useful for understanding the issues involved in community adoption practices, particularly given its promotion of a social web application. The recognition that SOCNET is actually a tool for encouraging corporate agency was an important insight as it allowed a clearer appreciation of the social requirements of the software.

Within the organization itself, the proposal that the SCO is operating predominantly from an autonomous reflexive mode proved a useful representation and allowed considerable depth of analysis—this, however, only being possible from completing three quite lengthy interviews. We did not have such access to the CEO and have correspondingly less confidence in our reflexivity conclusions for the CEO. As Archer demonstrates, examining reflexivity is no easy task and requires deep understanding of the personnel involved.

This lack of confidence is also tied in with the fact that Archer’s reflexivity arguments are less developed in examining the ways that reflexivity impact collective action, particularly organizational actions. For example, in the decision to scrap SOCNET, it is difficult to represent the extent to which the CEO is subverting his natural reflexivity to accommodate the important organizational role he is fulfilling. It is similarly difficult to tease out the mechanics by which the aggregated farmer group of predominantly communicative reflexives surprisingly adopt social media. Certainly we can argue individually for the importance of practical evaluation in explaining individual farmer’s adoption of social media, but it is difficult to follow through how this individual behavior impacts the collective. At this stage, we propose that the farmer “group” response to the live meat scandal was an aggregation of individual behaviors, but we wonder to what extent it should more accurately be represented as an outcome of the relational properties of the farmer “collective.” Certainly the social media adoption seems so rapid and extensive that we suspect there is a relational emergence that intervenes in the process. Further research focused on this and other case examples would be needed to develop this argument.

As Vandenberghe (2005) suggests, Archer’s reflexivity arguments would benefit from addressing collective agency in more detail. This is a suggestion that Archer (2007b) accepts:

This trio of books on intrapersonal deliberations about society will still leave the morphogenetic project unfinished. They will have dealt with the aggregate effects alone of the dialectic between changing human subjectivity and the objective transformation of society (p. 44).

Yet as Mutch (2007, 2010) demonstrates, reflexivity does have important things to say about the organizational behavior of actors. In particular, in examining the role of the institutional entrepreneur, Mutch (2007) warns against placing too much emphasis on the “willed and conscious change of practices” by institutional entrepreneurs and suggests, rather, seeing change as consequence of an individual’s pursuit of projects. This subtle difference in emphasis raises the relevance of reflexivity and can be usefully applied in our case where the SCO stubbornly pushes on with the doomed SOCNET project, even to the extent of losing her job. Of course, it is only certain types of organizations and organizational contexts that would provide the individual this freedom to act; it is where this can occur that Archer’s current representation of reflexivity would be most likely to be most useful.

Such an argument suggests that small business would be a good domain for the adoption of reflexivity arguments. Small businesses are generally more agile and responsive, with a degree of voluntarism that the larger organization lacks. The usefulness of reflexivity arguments was confirmed as we interviewed other small businesses in the area, but there were contradictions. For example, we would have expected that small businesses in the region would have tended to be iterative in focus since we felt they were characterized as communicative reflexive in mode, being very small, community involved, and family run. Yet, based on interviews, they were generally not particularly iterative in their envisioning of broadband possibilities, tending more toward practical evaluative. For example, a small local media and printing company were strong supporters of fast broadband but only to the extent that they would not pay beyond a certain monthly fee. This fee was directly linked to the savings in terms of speed and time in completing their current media practices; they were open to using broadband to better complete their current practices, but projective possibilities for broadband did not play a significant role in their adoption arguments.

Similarly, a local education provider was supportive of fast broadband but had difficulty engaging with the planned rollout, largely due to lack of consultation from the NBN Co. They already had fiber optic in place and were focusing on improving their own use of this. They can see that the NBN will be useful, if it is ubiquitous, since they will always need to plan offerings for the lowest connection speeds. Again, their focus is largely practical evaluative, with a recognition that the NBN may completely change the way that they do business if it encourages the advent of new players in the region.

## Contribution and Future Research

Archer (2012), in presenting her historical development of reflexivity and the increasing need for reflexivity in today’s world, suggests that,

At the end of the day, it will not be incontrovertible, but must remain what Weber (1964) called a “peculiarly plausible hypothesis.” Like his own, it would be open to further substantiation, to some extent historical, through use of diaries, “confessions” and novels, and today through investigation of those vast tracts of the Southern world where morphogenesis has not yet engaged (p. 17).

In a similar vein, the arguments presented here, based as they are on Archer’s reflexivity series, must be open to further substantiation or amendment. Certainly as Archer suggests, the study of rural areas, where structural and cultural morphogenesis is just starting, provide rich opportunities for important research that can be carefully extended to explain urban developments and perhaps help to influence and respond to coming changes in the developing world.

Archer (2012) suggests that as structural and cultural morphogenesis increasingly impinge on agents, the importance of reflexivity in charting a path through the consequent complexities increases proportionally. In particular, reflexivity will play an increasingly important role in answering the following questions:

1. Why do people act at all? What motivates them and what are they (fallibly) trying to achieve by endorsing given courses of action? This entails an examination of their personal concerns and inner reflexive deliberations about how to go about realizing them.

2. How do social properties influence the courses of action that people adopt? This involves a specification of how objective structural or cultural powers are reflexively mediated.

3. What exactly do people do? This requires an examination of the variability in the actions of those similarly socially situated and the differences in their processes of reflexivity (Archer 2007a, p. 6).

All of the above questions are particularly relevant to examining community adoption of broadband and associated Internet applications. We must be able to explain the processes by which individuals make the decision to adopt in order to properly address perceived barriers; we must understand the particular contextual cultural factors that are likely to impact adoption in order to modify our support and promotion strategies; we must address the surprising broadband outcomes of those “similarly socially situated” in order to seek the ubiquitous adoption needed to fully realize the benefits of broadband.

This paper describes the first steps in the examination of broadband from a reflexivity perspective, not as a prescription for describing a positivist social hydraulic whereby social forces constrain and enable passive agents (Archer 2012, p. 7), but more as a recognition that individuals have an active role that is always finally determined by the fact that they can always decide differently. The adoption process is represented as intimately social in nature as, prior to adoption, agents examine the extent to which broadband and associated applications support their ultimate concerns and consequent personal projects.

The integration of Emirbayer and Mische’s (1998) agency representation was useful as it allowed us to explain more clearly action behaviors in response to broadband and associated technologies; specifically, the agent’s power to envision is seen to be an important part of the adoption process, thus providing a possible lever for broadband promoters. We could see that communicative reflexives, while focused on iterative behavior, may see the opportunity to act out practical possibilities if broadband is consistent with the projects and practices they have mapped out, and they receive the external corroboration they need. Similarly, the autonomous reflexive, while instrumentally focused on practical possibilities for own advantage, can perhaps be moved toward broadband and novel Internet projects provided clear advantage is demonstrated. In line with Archer (2012), the meta-reflexive promises to be the mode with the most potential for projective envisioning and would be expected to grasp the opportunities provided by broadband and associated applications as they seek to support their life projects to break out of undesirable day-to-day social situations and perhaps open new worlds.

Emirbayer and Mische’s representation is important as it describes different possibilities for agents as they examine broadband as a possible support for their considered personal projects. If they are unable to envision iterative, practical, or projective possibilities for broadband they will be unlikely to include the important broadband project among their existing life projects. The importance of the broadband project is exemplified in the U.S. National Broadband Plan (Federal Communications Commission 2010), where it is argued that “the poor, the elderly, some racial and ethnic minorities, those who live in rural areas and those with disabilities” (p. 21) lag in their broadband connection; this neglect having major social implications as residing in the “analog world” disconnects them from the vast opportunities provided by broadband.

As Katz et al. (2012) suggest, awareness campaigns need to focus on promoting broadband and need to make the most of existing communicative avenues. Identified information sources (within the so-called similars and familiars valued by the communicative reflexive) should be provided with sufficient information and resources to be able to appropriately encourage an understanding of the opportunities provided by broadband. Such a strategy suggests that the first step in promotion is identifying suitable communication avenues.

This paper suggests that efforts to promote broadband must become far more nuanced and targeted than currently, needing to specifically address agents’ reflexivity and the actual contexts and life concerns of those involved. The arguments made in this paper are in line with comments made by the CEO of Australia’s largest telecom organization, Telstra:

It is not going to be about the rollout of fibre to 93 per cent of homes which will be the issue. It is what the hell we do with it….and I’m not yet convinced that there is enough focus on the innovation and the different ecosystems that we need to put around that, even from educating people about how to use it… most people say gee they are happy with what they have today it’s all about how structures need to change to take advantage of it (Liddington-Cox 2012).

This view is supported by Pawson and Tilley (1997) who suggest programs themselves do not provide a mechanism for change; it is the way that stakeholders embrace program opportunities that provides change. As the SOCNET example demonstrates, just providing the opportunity is not enough— build it and they may not come!

In fact, if we are looking for the modes most likely to not grasp opportunities, we cannot go past the communicative reflexive and the fractured reflexive. As Archer (2003, p. 316) suggests, the response of the communicative reflexive to constraints or enablements is evasion, unlike the autonomous reflexive or meta-reflexive whose response is strategic and subversive, respectively. The communicative reflexives are not challenged to move beyond their similars and familiars. They are conservative and focus on local friendship and family; they are perhaps more likely to be under cost pressures as they become socially immobile. In short, they would be the most likely group, along with the fractured reflexives, to be non-adopters. Further, the aged demographic for rural regions would also suggest that rural communities tend to be less familiar with information technology and the Internet. All of these factors suggest that targeting communicative reflexives for broadband promotion seems the option most likely to give the best return, particularly in rural regions. However, given the importance of ubiquitous adoption, no reflexive mode can be ignored; in particular, the enrollment of the fractured reflexives presents perhaps the greatest challenge as there would seem to be initially little avenue for leverage, given their separation and disillusionment. As Archer (2012, p. 56) indicates, constraints need something to constrain and enablements need something to enable:

In other words, for anything to exert the contingent power of a constraint or an enablement, it has to stand in a relationship such that it obstructs or aids the achievement of some specific agential enterprise as subjectively defined. The generic name for such enterprises is “projects”—i.e., any end, however inchoate, that can be intentionally entertained by human beings.

The fractured reflexive needs first to be encouraged to develop coherent life projects prior to being able to envisage broadband enablements and thus weigh their relative merit against the cost constraints involved in enrollment.

## Conclusion

Broadband technologies and their associated applications are inherently social in implementation and in their effects. As Yoo (2010) suggests, in his call for increasing the IS domain toward experiential computing, today’s “user” interacts with computing technologies in much wider social contexts than in the past. There is no technology for which this is more evident than broadband. We cannot understand broadband adoption and use without understanding the social, everyday context within which it operates. Archer’s graphical morphogenetic model (Figure 4) provides a useful means for representing the extraordinary complexity of this context. Yet to call Archer’s arguments a model does not seem to do the underlying social representations justice; Archer’s approach incorporates much more. Her careful arguments and steady unearthing of the dynamics of social change and the increasing role for reflexivity provide an opportunity to support deep analysis and provide considerable analytical power.

Reflexivity arguments suggest that an often unacknowledged element in broadband adoption is the subjectivity of the individual agents themselves. It is not enough to complete research focused on what people do most of the time. This induces analysts to search for the silver bullet, the critical success factors, that explains what most people will do most of the time—perhaps focusing on price, speed, availability, ease of use, and so on. Yet, if broadband is to achieve the ubiquity desired, we must also understand and address the reasons behind surprising adoption practices. The consideration of individual reflexivity, supported as it is by personal biography, context, and personal concerns, can provide the necessary focus to address how individuals uniquely engage with broadband technologies as they evaluate their potential in the light of their concerns and personal life projects. Reflexivity cannot be considered as a homogenous phenomenon which when applied in similar circumstances would lead to the same outcomes. Inner dialogue can be conducted from different modes, dependent on the situational context and the projects and life concerns of individual persons, often leading to unexpected divergent actions as the individual can always decide to act differently.

This paper argues that consideration of modes of reflexivity is important in understanding broadband adoption decisions. Further, we suggest that promotion and training will be more successful if information is provided in a way that suits particular ways of thinking. Agents will need to be given some skills in conceptualizing their own situations such that current and potential applications become apparent to them, and we expect that the manner in which such information is delivered should, by and large, provide opportunities for conversations in which barriers and preconceptions can be dialogically explored.

The development and rollout of a national broadband network is a large, socially complex, technology-related project that has the potential for substantial social and economic benefit. It should be a fertile area for the information systems researcher, yet there is limited research in the area. As Yoo (2010) suggests, there is a need within the IS community to extend their domain of interest to the examination of computing in everyday life experiences. In order to address this target, there needs to be a wider dissemination of methods within the IS field to adequately reflect the social realities of community-based technology adoption and use. There is considerable potential for critical realism, particularly the morphogenetic approach, to provide an important foundational philosophy to examine and address the complexity of such examination. As critical realism becomes more widely known and disseminated within the IS discipline, practitioners and researchers can be encouraged to play an increasing role in the examination of such socially complex technology projects.
