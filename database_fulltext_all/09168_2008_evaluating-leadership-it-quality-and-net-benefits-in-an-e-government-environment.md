# Evaluating leadership, IT quality, and net benefits in an e-government environment

Victor R. Prybutok <sup>a,\*</sup>, Xiaoni Zhang <sup>b,1</sup>, Sherry D. Ryan <sup>a,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Technology & Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of North Texas, Denton, TX 76203-5249, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Business Informatics, Northern Kentucky University, Highland Heights, KY 41099, United States

Received 23 June 2006; received in revised form 10 October 2007; accepted 29 December 2007 Available online 10 March 2008

## Abstract

US Government organizations are creating value for their citizens and businesses by improving their public service delivery through good websites. Our study examined leadership and IT quality, and their effect on positive delivery outcomes in an e-government environment. We first developed a theoretically based model using elements of the model developed for the Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award (MBNQA) and DeLone and McLean’s IS Success model. To test this model, we conducted a field survey at a municipal city government. The results supported our hypothesis that the MBNQA leadership triad (leadership, strategic planning, and customer/market focus) had a positive impact on the IT quality triad (information, system, and service quality). We also found that both leadership and IT quality increased the benefits. <sup>#</sup> 2008 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Leadership; IT quality; Organizational net benefits

## 1. Introduction

Technology helps governments achieve some important goals through quality websites that provide convenient and accessible information and e-government services to citizens. Technology also assists in decentralizing public administration and enhancing the government’s ability to oversee key activities.

E-government provides services to business, government employees, and citizens. In general, it requires a vast volume of information to deliver effective services. We initiated this study to examine e-government effectiveness. In addition, although the importance of leadership is well recognized, there is a need for a quantitative assessment of leadership and IT quality contributions in the public sector.

Nickerson and Zenger [26] proposed that an organization should structure itself in a manner that enhances information generation and handling and, consistent with this goal, the City of Denton decided to evaluate its current IT organization so that it could increase the benefit of its e-government capabilities. To assist the City of Denton, we embarked on a study to identify the relationship between leadership, IT quality, and organizational net benefits in an e-government environment.

The Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award (MBNQA) was intended to provide a framework that could help organizations achieve performance excellence and reap organizational benefits. It is given annually to a set of businesses and public institutions by the US Government through NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology) of the Department of Commerce (see: http:// www.quality.nist.gov/). The assumption has long been held that there is causal relationship between leadership and organizational net benefits and that IT is an important means of connecting them.

## 2. Theoretical background

The Malcomb Baldrige Award documentation states that leadership is the driver for the whole system. IS is an integral part of most organizational systems and leadership is deemed essential to the effective use of IS and quality outcomes. Given that our research objective was to test these relationships in e-government, we selected the leadership constructs from the Baldrige model and incorporated the quality and net benefits constructs from a well-known IS Success model (DeLone and McLean’s IS Success model [10]) to form a theoretically grounded model by which we could examine the benefits the organization derives from these interrelationships.

## 2.1. MBNQA and the leadership triad

The MBNQA is a well-known quality improvement award recognized in industry, education, healthcare, and the US governmental systems. The general hypothesis underlying the award is that leadership drives the system that creates results.

The MBNQA has evolved over the years but most studies that tested the MBNQA model were published before 2000. The leadership triad became a part of the 2000 MBNQA Performance Criteria and is used today. The leadership (category 1), strategic planning (category 2), and customer/ market focus (category 3) dimensions form the leadership triad. According to the 2006 Criteria for Excellence [25], these three categories are placed together in the leadership triad to recognize their collective importance. Leadership supports organizational and employee learning, performing regular performance assessments of the business, and supporting and strengthening the relationships with the community. Strategic planning involves determining the short and long term goals and making plans to enhance relationships with customers, suppliers, and partners. Customer and market focus emphasizes formal methods for determining product and service expectations of customers, identifying customer groups and market segments, and measuring customer satisfaction levels. Based on these three, senior leaders must set an organizational direction and seek future opportunities for the organization that are sensitive to the customer’s needs and desires. Prybutok and Spink’s [29] study in the healthcare industry provided evidence that proactive leadership can enhance organizational decision making.

## 2.2. E-government and quality

Providing quality e-government services to citizens makes interactions between citizens and government agencies smoother, easier, and more efficient. This, in turn, has the potential to facilitate better relationships between the government and the public.

Poor quality, however, can have a detrimental effect and can be a barrier to e-government service usage. Gilbert et al.

[15] investigated factors related to the evaluation and usage of an online e-government delivery mechanism. They found that trust, financial security, information quality, time, and money were predictors of e-government failure or success. As the Web becomes increasingly important in communication between the government and its citizens, it is essential for government agencies to provide quality e-services that minimize adoption barriers and maximize benefits.

Aspects of e-government offerings such as information quality and service interaction quality have also been used as measures for assessing e-government offerings. Barnes and Vidgen [3] used a survey to assess the quality of the Inland Revenue web site provided by the UK government. de Jong and Lentz [8] took a scenario evaluation approach to appraise the quality of e-government offerings. However, we found no studies that quantitatively assessed the relationship between leadership and quality in an e-government environment.

## 2.2.1. E-government and its relationship with leadership and IT quality

On a national level, many countries are recognizing the importance of leadership and IT quality in providing successful e-government offerings. For example, the U.K. Cabinet office’s largest unit, the e-Government Unit, has the following responsibilities: ‘‘formulating IT strategy and policy, developing common IT components for use across government, promoting best practice across government, and delivering citizen-centred online services’’ (http:// www.cabinetoffice.gov.uk/e-government). E-government incurs costs but provides faster, more efficient, and more convenient information access and services to citizens. The US Department of Labor claims that their advanced position in technology has increased the quality of customer services and operational efficiencies [1].

## 2.2.2. E-government and leadership

Typically leaders that champion e-government play an important role in the development of their applications, providing guidance about information resources and technology management. Leadership exerts a positive impact because it can provide direction, promote knowledge sharing and transfer, and mobilize resources.

However, there have been few empirical studies that have investigated the contribution of leadership in an e-government environment. One study on the adoption of municipal egovernment found that managerial innovativeness, managerial orientation, and city size were the most compelling determinants of municipal e-government adoption [23].

Several other studies investigated leadership as it relates to various organizational outcomes. In a study of local law enforcement organizations, Schildt et al. [32] found that senior leadership’s support and understanding of the dynamic nature of the underlying technologies produced a greater level of success in e-government implementations. Irani et al. [16] conducted in-depth case studies on e-government and recommended that senior executives become more involved in the evaluation of e-government initiatives to achieve better outcomes. Kim and Kim [18] identified e-government values as perceived by public officials in South Korea. They concluded that successful e-government development requires leaders who develop strategic plans and clarify the relationships between e-government services, evaluation criteria, and effectiveness. In a case study of the state of Tennessee, visionary leadership, in conjunction with strategic and tactical planning and management, was credited as instrumental for creating an e-government application that allowed citizens to renew or update their driver’s license online [19].

## 2.3. DeLone and McLean’s IS Success model

DeLone and McLean [9] attempted to combine measures from various IS Success categories to create a comprehensive model containing six constructs: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. Since then, many have validated components of this model [34]. In 2003, recognizing the important role of information services, DeLone and McLean refined their model by including service quality. In addition, in an effort to make it more parsimonious they combined individual impact and organizational impact into net benefits.

The model includes information, system, and service quality. Information quality has involved attributes such as: accuracy, relevancy, precision, reliability, completeness, and currency [20]; system quality has referred to perceived ease of use, user friendliness, system flexibility, usefulness, and reliability [12]; service quality can be assessed as the overall support delivered by the service provider [11].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

We combined the leadership triad from MBNQA and net benefits constructs from the success model. As shown in Fig. 1, two second-order constructs were used. The leadership triad is reflected by three first-order constructs: leadership, from the MBNQA model. The three quality constructs were included from the IS Success model; these are independent variables and are related to IT systems. We formed a second-order construct IT quality that included all three. While the IS Success model has Intention to use/ Use and User satisfaction as IS Success constructs as mediators between the quality variables and net benefits, Teo and Wong [35] found a direct relationship between the quality variables and organizational impact. DeLone and McLean also stated that every study would not use all IS Success constructs, but should only incorporate those relevant in the context under investigation. In our study, we choose to focus on the direct influence that IT quality has on net benefits.

## 3.1. Leadership and IT quality

Interrelationships within the leadership triad are evident in the strategic management literature. Developing strategic plans requires extensive knowledge of the relevant customers, markets, suppliers, competitors, etc. [5]. Similarly, to make CRM initiatives successful, organizations need to consider strategy, processes, systems and change management.

We proposed that leadership that embraced strategic planning and a customer/market focus would also emphasize the critical role of quality IT. Also, effective leadership promotes customer focus and demands that IT deliver superior results [4]. Such organizations depend on leadership to drive continuous improvement.

Numerous MBNQA studies confirmed the relationship between leadership and overall quality; for example, Flynn and Saladin [13] in the manufacturing industry, Pannirselvam et al. [27] in government operations, Prajogo [28] in the service industry, etc. In our study, we examined a subset of overall quality–IT quality. If the relationship between leadership and quality holds, then leadership’s commitment should enhance IT quality. Hence:

Hypothesis 1. The leadership triad is positively related to IT quality.

## 3.2. Leadership and net benefits

Leadership is important in influencing groups of people and mobilizing resources. Effective leadership promotes knowledge sharing to shape organizational structures and process and to leverage the resources to facilitate change induced by e-government. The National Assembly for Wales [24] asserted that strong senior management sponsorship for e-government was critical for e-government success. Hence:

Hypothesis 2. The leadership triad is positively related to net benefits.

## 3.3. IT quality and net benefits

IT quality leads to improved organizational performance. Saeed et al. [31] found that quality e-commerce applications were the key driver for organizational performance. Others have tested the relationship between outcome measures and subcomponents of IT quality and found significant results. Seddon and Kiew [33] tested the indirect relationships between information quality and individual impact/organizational impact, and system quality and individual/ organizational impact. Teo and Wong validated the direct relationships between system quality and organizational impact, and information quality and organizational impact Service quality was also identified as an important variable that affected positive IT outcomes [17]. We used a higher order construct, IT quality, to encompass the three quality components and hypothesized:

Hypothesis 3. IT quality is positively related to net benefits.

## 4. Methodology

As part of a comprehensive study of IT practices, we examined the City of Denton’s (CoD) e-government initiatives, including an assessment of current plans and implementations. A web-based survey was used to gather data and test our hypotheses.

## 4.1. Survey development

We developed and extensively piloted a web-based field survey to test our research model empirically. First, we reviewed the literature, identifying previously validated multi item scales that could be used or adapted to measure our constructs. CoD managers reviewed the instrument and made written and oral comments on clarity, format, and appropriateness for the CoD environment. After several rounds of evaluation and incorporation of their suggestions, the modified version was pilot tested at a meeting of the Dallas chapter of the American Society for Quality. Because of their expertise, members were asked to evaluate the instrument for content and clarity. Modifications were made based on their comments and the analysis from the pilot study data. Table 1 shows the items used in the study for data collection at the City of Denton.

## 4.2. Measures

Leadership, strategic planning and customer and market focus measures were based on Prybutok and Spink’s instrument. We used 9 items to measure leadership, 8 items to measure strategic planning and 10 to measure customer and market focus.

Information quality was adapted from Bailey and Pearson [2] and Rai et al. [30]. Seven items involving content, accuracy, timeliness, conciseness, convenience, availabilit and an overall measure of information quality were used.

System quality was adapted from Mason [22]. Six items capturing ease of use, accessibility, flexibility, usefulness, reliability and an overall measure of system quality were used

In our survey, length of the questionnaire limited us to more general questions and this resulted in our defining and limiting the number of elements in the IS service quality construct. It was therefore measured with six items: perce ptions of responsiveness, reliability, empathy, competence, communication, and attitude/commitment.

Net benefits were measured with three items incorporating satisfaction and individual and organizational performance [7]. All scales used a 1–7 Likert scale with anchors ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

## 4.3. Data collection at the research site

Denton is a city located 35 miles north of Dallas, Texas. It has a population of 80,000 with an annual growth rate of 6%. The city government employs about 1400 people and has an annual budget of about \$300 million. Prior to our study the city government had already successfully implemented several e-government applications. Subsequent to the survey completion, it continued expanding its e-government offerings and received several prestigious e-government awards.

We surveyed city employees because they have a better understanding of the government and IT operations, information creation, storage, and utilization processes as they screen complaints and maintain the systems. They are also more likely to have informed judgments about the city’s leadership and initiatives. We emailed 1100 employees, asking them to participate in the on-line survey. A total of 339 responses were received and stored in our database over a period of five weeks, representing a response rate of 30.82%. After deleting respondents with a large portion of missing or ‘‘not applicable’’ responses the remaining 178 useful responses were available for data analysis, yielding a 16.18% usable response rate. Of the respondents available for analysis 50.6% were male and 49.4% female. Non-response bias was examined by comparing the demographics of the respondents with those of the population. No significant differences were found on the demographic variables: age, gender, educational level, and years of experience. ## 5.1. Measurement model

PLS Graph Version 3.0 was used; with the Bootstrapping methodology, we determined the composite reliability, factor loadings with t-statistics, and average variance extracted (AVE). Acceptable values for composite reliability should exceed 0.70 and Table 2 shows that the value of the composite reliability for all the constructs were from 0.81 to 0.98. Also the average variances extracted were greater than 0.5 for all the constructs and these results.

Convergent validity is shown in Table 3, where the loadings for leadership, strategic planning, customer and market focus, information quality, system quality, service quality and net benefits have loadings are greater than 0.70 except for one item used in measuring the leadership triad with a value of 0.68.

Discriminant validity refers to how well a measure actually measures the underlying constructs and does not correlate highly with the measure of other constructs. The off-diagonal values in Table 4 show the correlations among the three constructs: leadership triad, net benefits, and IT quality. Table 4 shows square root of variances extracted for the constructs. Each of them has an average extracted variance greater than 0.5 and greater than their correlations with any of the other constructs. According to Fornell and Larker [14] this is consistent with the criteria needed to establish discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Structural model

Chin [6] stated that the $R ^ { 2 }$ and substantial/significant structural paths were needed to assess the model fit in PLS. In our model, we included two second-order constructs— the leadership and IT quality triads. The convergent validity of the first-order factors were supported by examining the strength of the paths connecting to the second-order constructs: leadership and IT quality triads. Fig. 2 shows the loadings for the first-order constructs: leadership, strategic planning, and customer on the leadership triad were 0.94, 0.95, and 0.92, respectively; the loadings for the first-order constructs information, system, and service quality on the IT quality construct were 0.97, 0.94 and 0.84, respectively. The paths from first-order constructs to second-order construct were greater than 0.70. Such strong loadings of the first-order constructs provided evidence of an adequate model fit and supported the existence of the second-order construct.

All the path coefficients were statistically significant. Table 5 shows that the $R ^ { 2 }$ was 19% when the leadership triad was used to predict the IT quality triad. The coefficient for the leadership triad was 0.44. Thus, Hypothesis 1 was strongly supported. In the equation to predict net benefits, the coefficient for the leadership triad was 0.15, the coefficient for the IT quality triad was 0.73, and the $R ^ { 2 }$ for the model was 65%. Hypothesis 2 stated that the leadership triad would be positively related to net benefits and it was supported at the 0.05 level of significance. Hypothesis 3 stated that IT quality was positively related to net benefits and it was supported at the 0.001 level of significance. The leadership triad, had both a direct relationship to net benefits and an indirect relationship, mediated by the IT quality triad.

## 6. Discussion

A major contribution of our research was the formation of a theoretically based model, the LQS, which combined elements of the MBNQA and the IS Success models. Its empirical testing highlighted the importance of leadership in producing IT quality and in generating net benefits from egovernment initiatives. The statistical significance of the LQS model strongly supported the relationship between the leadership triad and the IT quality triad, the leadership triad and net benefits, and the IT quality triad and net benefits.

## 6.1. Leadership triad—IT quality triad

The LQS model shows that the leadership triad contains three elements that are important to e-government: leadership, strategic planning, and customer/market focus. While this triad is composed of three elements, each can be considered individually to determine the strengths on which the organization capitalizes and shows where they need to make improvements. By focusing on one or all of the areas, organizations can strengthen their leadership, which will then drive quality. In practice, this might be achieved by government leaders initiating directives to regularly review quality and performance capabilities, providing feedback for improvement and innovation opportunities, seeking and benchmarking best practices, and setting goals and objectives.

## 6.2. Leadership triad—net benefits

The leadership triad is related both directly and indirectly to net benefits. As with the private sector, leadership has a direct link to organizational performance. This means that the goals and directives of e-government initiatives should be clearly articulated to the employees. The organization’s strategic plan and that the IT department’s plans are appropriately aligned. In addition, the organization must become citizen-focused. Managers should reevaluate business processes to leverage e-government’s personalization, timeliness, and delivery capabilities.

## 6.3. IT quality triad—net benefits

Our findings suggest that IT quality is a driver to enhancing performance so that government agencies can achieve their mission of serving the public. In practice, IT quality should be a primary focus of e-government initiatives. The importance of information quality is well recognized. Nevertheless, organizations continue to struggle with information and data quality. The expanded access to information that e-government provides highlights the importance of data integrity, timeliness of information, elimination of inconsistencies, and standardization of data and information formats.

System quality refers to the e-government application’s ease of use, accessibility, flexibility, usefulness, and reliability. Citizen-focus suggests that citizens external to the agency, as well as internal users, be involved in requirements determination and development to achieve the usability and relevance characteristics that high quality applications provide.

Service quality includes not only responsiveness to problems and competency in handling them but also in the attitude and degree of professionalism by which they are resolved. Supporting a broader clientele, who may have less computer competence can be daunting.

## 7. Conclusion

In our study we proposed a theoretically based model, Egovernment leadership and quality success (LQS) combining elements of the MBNQA) and the IS Success model. A field survey was conducted in the US to test the model. All three of our hypotheses were supported. Our analyses show that the leadership triad [leadership, strategic planning, and customer/ market focus] had a positive impact on the IT quality triad [information, system, and service quality]. Both leadership and IT quality triads are significant in predicting net benefits.

The CoD’s success provided some additional supporting evidence that leadership impacted IT quality and produced positive outcomes. This success also supported the contention that leadership drives better utilization of IT and can create many benefits for organizations.

Other organizations can use the measures in self-assessment, identifying areas that need improvement. Government agencies need to provide good leadership and IT quality as fundamental driving forces. Our instrument was designed to assess the broad and detailed aspects of these issues. Thus, other government bodies can benchmark their services. Such information allows decision makers to take corrective actions where needed and develop initiatives to address problems.
