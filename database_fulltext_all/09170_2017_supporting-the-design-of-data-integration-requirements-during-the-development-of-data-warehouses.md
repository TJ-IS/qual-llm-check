# Supporting the design of data integration requirements during the development of data warehouses: a communication theory-based approach

Christoph Rosenkranz<sup>1</sup>, Roland Holten<sup>2</sup>, Marc Räkers<sup>3</sup> and Wolf Behrmann<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

Data warehouses (DW) form the backbone of data integration that is necessary for analytical applications, and play important roles in the information technology landscape of many industries. We introduce an approach for addressing the fundamental problem of semantic heterogeneity in the design of data integration requirements during DW development. In contrast to ontology-driven or schemamatching approaches, which propose the automatic resolution of differences ex-post, our approach addresses the core problem of data integration requirements: understanding and resolving different contextual meanings of data fields. We ground the approach firmly in communication theory and build on practices from agile software development. Besides providing relevant insights for the design of data integration requirements, our findings point to communication theory as a sound underlying foundation for a design theory of information systems development.

## Introduction

Many companies have implemented data warehouses (DW) during the last decades to enhance decision making, risk management, or regulatory reporting (Narayanan, 2008). DW-based analytical applications use reporting, online analytical processing (OLAP), data mining or ‘business intelligence’ tools to support decision making (Watson & Wixom, 2007). DW projects require huge capital spending, consume a good deal of development time, and have a high possibility of failure (Wixom & Watson, 2001; Hwang & Xu, 2008).

One of the biggest challenges in DW projects is data integration, the consolidation of data from disparate sources into one consistent body of data (March & Hevner, 2007; Watson & Wixom, 2007). Getting data into a DW requires up to 80% of development time and effort, and generates more than 50% of the project’s costs (Watson & Wixom, 2007). Data sources are often heterogeneous, disorganized, or even inaccessible because many business systems are developed independently of each other (Halevy, 2005). Differences in data <sup>fi</sup>elds that need to be integrated can be as simple as naming conventions or units of measures. Frequently, however, the differences are more complex. Consequently, semantic heterogeneity is high in most scenarios of data integration (Halevy, 2005; March & Hevner, 2007).

In DW development projects, data integration requirements specify the data integration design from a logical and physical perspective (Sherman, 2014). This includes detailed information about all data sources and targets, along with the transformations required to deliver the necessary information to meet business requirements. Owing to semantic heterogeneity, data integration requirements are far from trivial, and require a detailed and costly manual pro<sup>fi</sup>ling of source data and a description of target schemas (Halevy, 2005; Sherman, 2014). Current approaches lack support in overcoming semantic heterogeneity.

In this paper, we introduce a development approach that supports the design of data integration requirements in DW development and mitigates the problem of semantic heterogeneity. We suggest building on a perspective focusing on language and communication processes in information systems (e.g., Beynon-Davies, 2010; Holten & Rosenkranz, 2011; Mingers & Willcocks, 2014) and information systems development (e.g., Corvera Charaf et al, 2013; Rosenkranz et al, 2013; Mastrogiacomo et al, 2014). We follow a design science methodology (Hevner et al, 2004). As a theoretical basis for our approach, we make use of Clark’s (1996) communication theory of ‘common ground’ and language as action (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Clark, 1992; Clark, 1996), a theory that provides the rationale for three design strategies. These design strategies lead us to propose an approach for designing, managing, and communicating data integration requirements, lessening semantic heterogeneity. Our approach is supported by a software tool and tested using multiple case studies. Besides making practical and empirical contributions, we draw theoretical implications for a design theory of information systems development (ISD).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First, we analyze related work on DW development and describe our research method. We then introduce design rationales for supporting the design of data integration requirements during DW development that build on communication theory and agile software development. Following this we present our approach and its design components before evaluating the approach using multiple case studies and a user survey. Finally we discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclude with an outlook on future work.

## Related work and problem awareness

## Data warehouse development – The backbone of data integration

Numerous DW development approaches have been presented by practitioners (e.g., Kimball & Caserta, 2004; Inmon, 2005) or academics (e.g., Holten, 2003; Giorgini et al, 2008; Mazón & Trujillo, 2008). Most are based on development approaches for transactions-based applications, advocating a sequence of development steps and relying on formal mechanisms of communication such as speci<sup>fi</sup>cation documents or data models. Central to these approaches are sequential phases with release plans including speci<sup>fi</sup>c milestones and deliverables as outputs (Couture, 2014; Sherman, 2014). For example, data integration requirements are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed based on business requirements and form the basis of data models for the target DW (see Figure 1).

The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of data integration requirements includes the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and analysis of necessary data sources and the mapping of sources system schemas to DW schemas, leading to designs for data extraction, transformation, and loading (ETL). These phased models result in releases ranging from 1 to 6 months or more (Couture, 2014). No industry standard for DW development exists (Sen & Sinha, 2005), however, and academic approaches are too complex for real-world settings (Malinowski & Zimányi, 2008) or feature de<sup>fi</sup>cits (Mazón et al, 2007).

DW development is traditionally guided by the analysis of operational data sources, thus overlooking an explicit design phase in which business information requirements of decision makers are gathered alongside data integration requirements, and then jointly mapped to data sources (Mazón et al, 2007) – which is evident in the usual separation of specifying business information requirements and identifying data integration requirements in separate phases (see Figure 1). Thus, semantic heterogeneities and instance level data integration continue to pose enormous challenges for DW developers (March & Hevner, 2007).

## Data integration tools and semantic heterogeneity

The key to the successful design of data integration requirements is reconciling semantic heterogeneity. Several tools support the design of data integration requirements, but overcoming semantic heterogeneity is an ‘AI-complete’ problem (i.e., as hard as reproducing human intelligence, Bernstein et al, 2004; Halevy, 2005), and therefore inherently a non-automatable, heuristic process done by humans. Therefore, the preparation of semantic mappings – a part of the design of data integration requirements specifying how to translate data from one data source into another – is laborintensive and error-prone (Halevy, 2005). As a consequence, the practical bene<sup>fi</sup>t of ontologies (Simperl & Tempich, 2006), which offer the potential for mapping heterogeneous data sources, is limited because robust domain ontologies need to be established <sup>fi</sup>rst (Guarino, 1998; Ram & Jinsoo, 2004). However, domain-wide ontologies are idealistic because of the continuous business dynamics and underlying human learning processes (Honkela et al, 2008). Experience shows that standardized schemas such as domain ontologies have limited success, and depend on very strong incentives to agree on standards (Halevy, 2005).

Schema matching is another technical solution for supporting the design of data integration requirements (Rahm & Bernstein, 2001; Bernstein et al, 2004; Shvaiko & Euzenat, 2005; Do & Rahm, 2007). Schema matching relies on discovering correspondences between similar elements in a number of schemas from different source systems. In large-scale scenarios, where data sharing involves a huge number of data sources, such techniques are not suitable; even sophisticated approaches cannot guarantee that the mappings are 100% correct and rely on human judgment for selecting the mapping candidates (Saleem et al, 2008).

## Context and communication of data integration requirements

Fundamentally, overcoming semantic heterogeneity requires understanding the context, which determines the semantics and meaning of data (Agrawal et al, 2009). People have to collaborate and share their knowledge, to <sup>fi</sup>nd the appropriate data for a given situation, to convince data owners to share it, and to address their concerns about sharing (Halevy et al, 2006). Many different stakeholders own speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge that is necessary for data integration in DW development (March & Hevner, 2007). This results in involved business users and IT experts communicating and negotiating questions along the lines of ‘What does this data <sup>fi</sup>eld mean?’, ‘Is this attribute the same as one of those?’, or ‘What does this model actually show?’ For example, does the term ‘redemption’ for a bond mean the same thing as for a fund? Are the meanings of the term ‘redemption’ in both contexts related or are they completely different? And which data <sup>fi</sup>elds of source systems capture those meanings? Methods are needed that support communication and ‘meaning negotiations’ with cost-effective means to successfully deal with semantic heterogeneity during the design of data integration requirements (Halevy et al, 2006; Honkela et al, 2008).

Techniques originally used for the development of transactions-based applications may not be the best choice for DW development because the data is often less precise, extremely heterogeneous, less predictable, and sensitive to the conditions under which it was captured (Agrawal et al, 2009). Moreover, in complex or rapidly changing environments, it is hard for formal mechanisms of communication such as models and documents to react quickly enough (Herbsleb & Mockus, 2003; Vidgen & Wang, 2009). Normative and practice-driven approaches such as Joint Application Design (JAD) (Wetherbe, 1991) or agile software development (Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2009) build on collaboration and communication, but so far miss theoretical rationales for why they are useful, why they work, guidelines on how they could be used during the design of data integration requirements, or evidence that the application is effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (e.g., cf. Davidson, 1999 for a comprehensive discussion of JAD).

To sum up, current approaches for the design of data integration requirements do not consciously address the problem of semantic heterogeneity and they insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently support communication and negotiation between stakeholders mostly by formal mechanisms of communication. There still is a gap concerning method and tool support to determine the context of data and to negotiate a mutual understanding of the context for both business requirements and corresponding data <sup>fi</sup>elds of source systems in the form of data integration requirements.

## Research method

By conducting design science research (March & Smith, 1995; Hevner et al, 2004), we aim to create artifacts that support the design of data integration requirements by addressing semantic heterogeneity. Appendix A summarizes how our study relates to the guidelines for design science proposed by Hevner et al (2004). We followed the methodical procedures proposed by Vaishnavi & Kuechler (2008) and the framing recommendations by Gregor & Hevner (2013). The rationales and kernel theory of our design and the <sup>fi</sup>nal con<sup>fi</sup>guration of our approach, as presented in the section ‘Design rationales and framework’, emerge as the result of a sequential series of phases, with iterative feedback loops between the phases (see Hevner et al, 2004; Peffers et al, 2007; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2008; Baskerville et al, 2009). In the sense of proof-by-demonstration and similar to ‘Action Design Research’ (Sein et al, 2011), we evaluate our artifacts in multiple case studies. In the section ‘Application and evaluation’ we report how the artifacts have been tried out in real-life scenarios. Appendices C to E give details on the case study design, data collection, and data analysis, and Appendix F provides information on a survey design.

## Design rationales and framework

## Agile software development

Agile practices from approaches such as Scrum or Extreme Programming (XP) have complemented traditional approaches for software development over recent decades (Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2009). Agile development methodologies build on incremental and user-centered approaches such as rapid application development (Martin, 1991) or JAD (Davidson, 1999). They suggest that business customers and developers must work together daily and that project information should be shared through informal communication such as face-to-face conversation (Black et al, 2009), together with principles such as iterative cycles or incremental, early delivery of working software (Beck et al, 2001). Agile development methodologies emphasize small iterative phases, encouraging constant change and frequent interactions (Cockburn & Highsmith, 2001; Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). Methodologies such as Scrum or XP each propose speci<sup>fi</sup>c agile practices that should be followed and applied during development (e.g., holding daily stand-up meetings in Scrum or programming in pairs in XP). In industry, <sup>fl</sup>exible combinations of these practices are carried out (Abrahamsson et al, 2002) to tailor the methods to speci<sup>fi</sup>c project settings (Fitzgerald et al, 2006).

Some practitioners (Hughes, 2008; Corr & Stagnitto, 2011; Couture, 2014) as well as academics (Goede & Huisman, 2010; Golfarelli et al, 2011) have begun to explore the use of agile practices in DW development. Nevertheless, no approach that we are aware of focuses on data integration requirements or gives theoretical rationales for why some practices are adopted and others are not. For example, Corr & Stagnitto (2011) provide no theoretical argument why agile data modeling works. The same holds for speci<sup>fi</sup>c ‘text-book’ approaches such as Scrum or JAD, which evolved in industry, and where the literature is largely anecdotal and prescriptive, lacking empirical evidence and theoretical foundation to support the choice of speci<sup>fi</sup>c practices (Lee & Xia, 2010). For example, Couture (2014) gives no theoretical rationale why Scrum should be more useful than XP in DW development. As a consequence, it is hard to decide how and why agile practices might be suitable to support the design of data integration requirements.

## Theory of common ground and grounding in communication

We build on communication theory to provide a theoretical rationale for selecting agile practices for incorporation into a DW development methodology to support the design of data integration requirements. Contemporary research on agile development identi<sup>fi</sup>es communication as one of its most fundamental aspects (Korkala et al, 2006; Pikkarainen et al, 2008; Karhatsu et al, 2010; Hummel et al, 2015), and suggests that ‘theories of communication, collaboration, cognition, and sense-making’ (Hummel et al, 2013) could provide a starting point for designing and improving ISD approaches.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, psycholinguistic research by Clark and others (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Clark, 1992; Clark, 1996; Clark & Krych, 2004; Clark, 2005) on human language has provided a useful theory of communication. We use Clark’s ‘theory of common ground’ for three reasons. First, because it is derived from detailed experimental and <sup>fi</sup>eld studies (Hanna et al, 2003; Brown-Schmidt, 2012; Frank & Goodman, 2012; Gupta et al, 2012). Second, its explicit focus on language as a mechanism for sharing situation models and action plans to manage joint activities (Tylén et al, 2010) is compliant with our understanding of designing data integration requirements, which relies on two or more people coming together to share perceptions, engage in the design as a joint collaborative tasks, or otherwise coordinate and align their understanding of terms and states of affairs in the world. Third, the theory was previously used in the IS discipline, for example, for language-based IS evaluation (Ågerfalk, 2004) or to improve real-time coordination in ISD projects (Mastrogiacomo et al, 2014).

Unfortunately, Clark’s theory does not offer a set of concepts or prescriptions so that it can be directly used in design (Mastrogiacomo et al, 2014). However, three well-established principles follow from it (see Clark & Brennan, 1991; Clark, 1992; Clark, 1996; Clark & Krych, 2004; Convertino et al, 2008 for a detailed summary). We draw on these principles to deduce consequences for the design of data integration requirements (see Table 1).

Principle 1: communication is a joint activity Communication is a joint activity of at least two interlocutors (Clark, 1996). It takes coordination for people to do things together, and it takes communication to achieve that coordination (Clark, 2005). What makes an activity a joint one is the coordination of both content, what the participants intend to do, and processes, the physical and mental systems they recruit in carrying out those intentions (Clark, 1996). A crucial element of joint activities is mutual alignment of expectations and sharing of higherorder situation models, action plans, and goals (Tylén et al, 2010). Successful communication as a joint activity requires active involvement and constant veri<sup>fi</sup>cations by all participants, and joint activities cannot be accounted for without understanding the interplay between content and process, and their place in overall joint activities; conversations cannot work without coordination of both content and process (Clark, 1996).

Principle 2: communication depends on a grounding process As a result of principle 1, content and process are interdependent: the more complicated the content, the longer the process (Clark, 1996). Moreover, a minor misunderstanding at the beginning might snowball into major ones in the end (Clark, 1996). The basis for successful communication is ‘common ground’ between interlocutors, a concept that refers to mutual knowledge, mutual beliefs, and mutual assumptions (Clark, 1996). Common ground is background knowledge that is shared between interlocutors in a conversation (Clark & Marshall, 1981). Common ground re<sup>fl</sup>ects what can reasonably be assumed to be known to interlocutors on the basis of the evidence at hand; this evidence can be nonlinguistic (e.g., if both know that they come from the same city they can assume a degree of common knowledge about that city) or it can be based on prior conversation (Pickering & Garrod, 2004). This does not mean that their understanding needs to be identical, but they must have some same understanding of what they are discussing in order to disagree about a particular aspect of it (Pickering & Garrod, 2004). For example, if two people are arguing the merits of Microsoft vs Oracle database software, they must agree about, for instance, what the names refer to and the two products’ characteristics so that they can disagree on their evaluations (Rosenkranz et al, 2014). Common ground is incrementally built on the history of joint actions between interlocutors, as common ground is continuously updated in dialog (Clark & Krych, 2004). This grounding process of establishing common ground and mutual understanding is always adaptive to the current context of communication (Clark, 1996).

In general, two types of common ground do exist – process common ground and content common ground (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Convertino et al, 2008; Convertino et al, 2009; Convertino et al, 2011). Content common ground includes ‘I know that you know that I know what’, while process common ground encompasses ‘I know that you know that I know how’ (Convertino et al, 2009). Content common ground is the mutual understanding on the subject and focus of work, resulting from exchanging content and mutually checking and signaling understanding (Convertino et al, 2008). Process common ground is the mutual understanding of the rules, procedures, timing, and manner in which the interaction will be conducted (Convertino et al, 2008).

Empirical research on expert-layperson communication (e.g., business expert as IT laypersons and vice versa) found low content common ground between interlocutors (Bromme & Jucks, 2001; Jucks et al, 2008). Therefore, the expert must assume the knowledge of the layperson. This is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult because the reference frameworks of the interlocutors are mainly determined by the interlocutors’ respective backgrounds and their speci<sup>fi</sup>c education, and reference frameworks of laypersons are partly resistant against changes (Bromme & Jucks, 2001). This may cause an illusion of evidence (Bromme et al, 2005), which means that experts overestimate the understandability of transferred facts (Bromme & Jucks, 2001).

Principle 3: communication is a multi-modal process Communication involves more than just words or verbal conversations (written or spoken). The participants use speech and gestures in parallel. Indeed, the term ‘common ground’ suggests how important physical co-presence and shared physical setting may be to establishing mutual understanding (Cramton, 2001). Less conventional forms of language and modes of communication such as vocal gestures, facial expressions, eye gaze, and postures help people to make signals and exchange messages (Clark & Brennan, 1991; Clark & Krych, 2004). In the case of written communication without face-to-face contact, the probability of misunderstandings and illusion of evidence increases rapidly and failed understanding will often not be signaled in practice (Garrod & Anderson, 1987; Bromme et al, 2005).

## Design strategies: matching theoretical principles with agile practices

The three principles provide theoretical rationales to identify and combine agile practices in order to tailor the practices to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c setting of designing data integration requirements. This is the justi<sup>fi</sup>catory, explanatory knowledge that links the goal with the artifacts’ shape and properties (Gregor & Jones, 2007), and which leads us to propose to follow three design strategies that are intended to support the design of data integration requirements during DW development: (i) Improve requirements specification with regard to development of common ground; (ii) Detect and signal remaining communication defects and misunderstandings early; and (iii) Reduce scenarios with potential for illusion of evidence. Building on the consequences described in Table 1, Table 2 describes the rationale and particularities of each strategy in detail. The strategies identify and leverage a set of agile practices (see Vidgen & Wang, 2009 for an overview of enabling agile practices).

To summarize our theory-based argument for the three strategies: to overcome semantic heterogeneity during the design of data integration requirements in DW development, continuous face-to-face communication between stakeholders about the context of data integration requirements and data <sup>fi</sup>elds is required to establish common ground and mutual understanding of the meaning of data <sup>fi</sup>elds and to avoid the illusion of evidence. A practical consequence of this is it will speed up the development process while simultaneously enhancing the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nal product. Consequently, if our theoretical argument is true and we enforce continuous face-to-face communication then we should be able to observe increased common ground and mutual understanding as well as reduced semantic heterogeneity and faster projects of higher quality.

## Artifact descriptions: specification of the approach The design strategies are implemented within three artifacts:

● A template for jointly building and specifying both business information requirements and data integration requirements (e.g., schema mappings) bottom-up and for discussing the context of data <sup>fi</sup>elds.

● A procedure building on agile approaches and comprising process steps, roles, and responsibilities.

● A software tool that helps to implement the template and that supports the procedure, providing views to different stakeholders and the ability to discuss and negotiate the context of data <sup>fi</sup>elds.

## Template for context information and schema mappings (speci<sup>fi</sup>cation format)

On the basis of our theory-driven design strategies, the way of building semantic mappings was considerably redesigned during the design and search process. For example, we used usual data models and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation documents initially, but discarded them early on as an alternative because they do not take context into account and are not minimal with regard to documentation (design strategy 1, see Table 2). As a trade-off between legal necessity for documentation (BITS, 2005) and following theoretical principles and agile practices, we suggest building the documentation of data integration requirements and the schema mappings incrementally in small steps and bottom-up. We propose creating necessary but minimal documentation for each data <sup>fi</sup>eld. What is necessary and minimal needs to be determined for each case and situation, and this allows for trial-and-error in the design process (Wetherbe, 1991). Data integration requirements are modeled and added not just once up-front but just-in-time, becoming more detailed over time as data <sup>fi</sup>elds become the subject of discussion among stakeholders.

Furthermore, we suggest letting stakeholders annotate each data field with information about the context. Such semantic annotations are supposed to add detail to the data <sup>fi</sup>elds in a ‘pay-as-you-go’ fashion and to enable a better understanding of the meaning of a data <sup>fi</sup>eld in its context. We propose that this helps to build content common ground between stakeholders: in order to discuss the meaning of concepts and data <sup>fi</sup>elds, we need to explicate and share our knowledge about the context <sup>fi</sup>rst. Only if we try to formalize and codify the meaning of a thing or concept do we begin to notice that different meanings exist for this concept, and we can engage in discussions of those meanings. The stakeholders have to be able to jointly specify and to incrementally model (a) business requirements (e.g., functional requirements that are annotated with information about their context), (b) corresponding data <sup>fi</sup>elds in the target data model (e.g., attribute definitions). and (c) schema mappings to data <sup>fi</sup>elds of source systems (e.g., mappings that match to the requirements as well as meta-data needed for ETL processing that are annotated with information about the context of the source data <sup>fi</sup>elds).

## Procedure model

We decided from the start not to use plan-driven, sequential procedure models. Instead, we propose a sequence of iterative development phases inspired by agile ISD methodologies, with explicit forward and feedback communication mechanisms between stakeholders, and output objects of every phase (see Table 3). The activities in each phase follow from our design strategies (see Table 2) and are intended to support face-to-face communication, minimal documentation, and early releases and quick changes of software.

The procedure model is cyclic in nature, where the last phase is followed by the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase but logically introduces the next iteration of the design of data integration requirements (see Figure 2). Each cycle is short compared to traditional DW development, lasting between a few days and a couple of weeks. Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rst release of the DW is deployed early. Sprints-like stages are used and are coupled with constant forward and feedback communication loops, data quality checks, and a focus on communication for detecting schema mapping errors near the beginning through early deployment and quality testing of schema mappings and ETL designs that are stored according to the template. After a couple of cycles of discussions, negotiations, and experimentations, the meaning of data <sup>fi</sup>elds and the data integration requirements and schema mappings become more stable, and the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations are frozen in this state.

## Software tool (data requirements tool)

To support documentation and codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of data integration requirements and context statements, we developed the Data Requirements Tool (DaRT). This software tool emerged from the evaluations during the case studies, which revealed a need for managing the template data, guiding team members and connecting the different stages of the procedure model (see Figure 2). Figure 3 is a screenshot of the user interface. DaRT implements the template, linking business requirements to data integration requirements. Its navigation tree supports navigation of data integration requirements for a data <sup>fi</sup>eld (left side of Figure 3); and a structured user interface for discussing and editing details of data <sup>fi</sup>elds, including context statements (right side of Figure 3).

Users can create a business view that contains a selection of target data <sup>fi</sup>elds for the DW database tables. A combination of those views is grouped under business areas. Views can be set as mandatory or not in every business area. Figure 3 shows the DW table ‘Deals – External rating deal’ within the business area ‘Standardized Approach’. The details pertaining to the selected target data <sup>fi</sup>eld ‘RATING\_AGENCY\_ID’ are shown on the right side. Every user can add comments on data <sup>fi</sup>eld attributes. This supports discussions and negotiations of contextual meanings of data <sup>fi</sup>elds between DW developers, business experts, and IT experts for the source systems.

The schema is documented and semantically annotated, and the schema mappings gradually improve in a ‘pay-as-you-go’ fashion. Users can add contextual statements to document the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed source data of a data <sup>fi</sup>eld in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c business area according to the data integration requirements (source system, table, and field name). Additional free text <sup>fi</sup>elds (comment, functional mapping, and technical mapping) allow further documentation (e.g., meeting minutes, involved persons, time references).

Data <sup>fi</sup>elds are discussed, negotiated, semantically annotated, and the mappings changed if necessary. Table 4 shows an excerpt from this negotiation process.

## Application and evaluation

Our design strategies translate into an expectation about observable behavior in real world projects: the use of the artifacts supports the development of process common ground and content common ground during the design of data integration requirements, and leads to early detection of communication defects and data integration errors (P1), it enhances project performance (the development process) (P2), and it enhances the quality of the final product (the DW) (P3).

For our evaluation, we follow the recommendation that any evidence of the value of the artifact should be given (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). We present results from case study research that we carried out to iteratively evaluate the artifacts during the design process, and from survey research that provides a descriptive evaluation for a stable con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the artifacts.

## First phase: evaluation during development

Research strategy and design We employed an embedded multiple case study design (Yin, 2003) in order to iteratively examine the usefulness of the artifacts during their development. We selected six <sup>fi</sup>nancial DW projects in banks that are typical for recent projects in the European <sup>fi</sup>nancial services sector. The trigger for all selected projects was the supervisory requirements known as Basel II. These regulations became law in most member states of the European Union in 2007; they demand speci<sup>fi</sup>ed risk calculation and treatment processes. Table 5 gives an overview of the cases. Appendix C gives details on the research design, Appendix D illustrates the used coding scheme and data analysis, and Appendix E summarizes aspects with regard to validity of the research.

All projects were conducted with the help of the IT consultancy zeb (https://www.zeb.eu/). The <sup>fi</sup>rst set of cases (Bank A–C) used traditional DW development approaches (including formal requirements speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, dimensional modeling for logical data models, data loading interfaces, data <sup>fl</sup>ow modeling, and software tool support for ETL and data integration, see the section ‘DW development – The backbone of data integration’). The second set of cases (Bank D–F) used some or all of our artifacts in different states of their development (Table 5). We used direct observations, interviews, surveys of project members, project documentation, and e-mail exchanges to generate data. The collection of the data was started in March 2007 and ended in December 2008. Coding techniques and checklists were then used to analyze the data (see Appendix D).

Cross-case summary We contacted seven project managers involved in all six projects to invite them to participate in a focus group discussion with us. The purpose was to discuss the projects and to establish a ranking scheme of project performance on the basis of their expert opinions. These professionals had an average experience of 5 years with <sup>fi</sup>nancial DW development and created two to four <sup>fi</sup>nancial DW each during this period, indicating a considerable level of expertise.

During the workshop, the researchers gave a presentation explaining each project. Next, all participants were asked to rank the six projects with respect to the projects’ performance, understood as the quality of the process, the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nal product, and the project’s ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Nidumolu, 1995) using a scale of 1–6. For this scale, a rank of 1 indicates that a project is perceived as having the highest relative performance; a rank of 6 indicates the other end of the scale, representing the lowest relative performance. The participants were asked to give a full ordering, that is assigning an equal rank to two or more projects was not allowed. Afterwards the single rankings were presented, discussed among all workshop participants, and consolidated into one <sup>fi</sup>nal ranking (presented in Table 6).

In summary, all six projects are comparable in scope and complexity. The latter projects that used some or all of the components of the presented approach are ranked higher in performance than the <sup>fi</sup>rst three projects that did not; the quality of product and process of the latter three projects were stated to be superior. During the subsequent discussion, the project managers also ascribed this difference in performance at least in part to the artifacts. On the basis of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings we cannot completely rule out that, as all projects have been conducted with the help of zeb, the consultancy’s abilities on DW development will be increased having experienced the previous projects.

Although we tried to employ natural controls such as interviewing project team members for both single and across projects, and although most of the projects were still ongoing at the time of the interviews, we cannot completely eliminate all such factors.

## Second phase: evaluation of stable con<sup>fi</sup>guration

To evaluate the utility of the stable con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the artifacts we conducted a questionnaire-based user survey, which is an accepted methodology for software or design evaluation (Henderson et al, 1995). We collected data by distributing an online survey to 69 members of <sup>fi</sup>nancial DW development projects in 10 different organizations, which constituted all known users of the artifacts at the time of the survey (complete population count). We recruited our participants by distributing our survey via e-mail to all known users. A total of 28 users from 10 different organizations answered the questionnaire completely (response rate of 40%). Appendix F gives details on the survey and summarizes the survey results. We note that this approach is not to be confused with the standards of quantitative survey research. Because of the very limited sample size, a questionnaire-based survey for software or design evaluation can seldom reach the statistical power required in more behavioral research settings.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the users <sup>fi</sup>nd the procedure model and DaRT easy to use (means of 5.41 and 5.13). Perceived usage (means of 4.56 and 5.43) and self-reported intention to use (means of 4.84 and 4.95) suggest that the users are interested in the artifacts and will use them. The participants are quite positive about the procedure model with regard to its ability to reduce illusion of evidence (mean of 5.35), to improve interaction (mean of 5.58), and to support early detection of communication defects (mean of 5.18). For DaRT, the questions for improved speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (mean of 5.55) and early detection of communication defects (mean of 4.75) also score high. The majority rates performance expectancy (means of 4.99 and 5.00) and system quality (means of 5.10 and 5.33) of the artifacts as high. (Note: system quality relates not to the quality of procedure model or DaRT but to their effect on the developed DW.)

## Summary of evaluation

Our assessment of validity and utility shows that the use of the artifacts supports the development of process common ground and content common ground in the design of data integration requirements. Our expectations about observable behavior in real world DW development projects were con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed in the following way: the case studies showed that our artifacts (template, procedure model, and DaRT) led to early detection of communication defects and data integration errors (support for P1); furthermore case studies and survey revealed that the artifacts enhanced project performance, since overall the required communication time in development projects could be reduced (support for P2). Finally, survey and case studies support the notion that project shareholders judge the quality of the end products, which are the resulting DW solutions in the different projects analyzed, to have increased because of the usage of the three artifacts in the development process (support for P3).

## Discussion

## Empirical contribution

Our study makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant empirical contribution in the form of the description of our artifacts and their rationales as well as an exploration and evaluation of their actual use (Ågerfalk, 2014). Our main practical contribution is a new solution for dealing with semantic heterogeneity to overcome a root cause of problems in the design of data integration requirements when developing DWs. We included essential justi<sup>fi</sup>catory knowledge, and explain why the artifacts are constructed as they are and why they work (Gregor & Jones, 2007). We provide new knowledge in the form of theory-grounded design strategies and operational principles for a useful method as well as three implemented artifact instantiations, mixing both improvement research and exaptation research (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). First, we improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness compared to existing DW development approaches. The reason for this improvement is that we address the problem of semantic heterogeneity in the design of data integration requirements, which existing approaches do not or do so insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently. We evaluated our approach in real-world scenarios and provided evidence of improvement in the form of reported positive changes in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness for the design of data integration requirements as well as the quality of the product. Second, we extended known solutions from agile software development to new problem areas: artifacts that help deal with problems in the design of data integration requirements. We thereby ‘exapted’ effective solutions from agile software development practices (Gregor & Hevner, 2013).

In summary, we make an important empirical contribution to the knowledge base in the form of situated instantiations (our three core artifacts; Level 1 contribution) and a method (with corresponding design principles; Level 2 contribution) (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Our design strategies may be generalized to other scenarios of data integration, where semantic heterogeneity is a prevalent factor.

## Theoretical implications: towards a design theory of information systems development

Our results have theoretical implications (Ågerfalk, 2014) for a future design theory (Level 3 contribution according to Gregor & Hevner, 2013) because our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are generalizable to a broader set of classes of problems: to ISD in general, regardless of application domain. In this sense, our design strategies are ‘general solution concepts for a class of <sup>fi</sup>eld problems’ (Sein et al, 2011).

Since we argue that linguistic communication is a fundamental cornerstone of information systems, and accordingly of ISD (see the section ‘Introduction’), our insights should be applicable beyond the design of data integration requirements to all ISD efforts, and the theoretical principles of Clark’s communication theory should hold for other contexts and scenarios of ISD. All collaborative work in ISD is essentially a joint activity in the sense of Clark’s theory of language as action, with the need to build common ground and to coordinate contents and processes. Consequently, our insights can be expanded in future studies to build a design theory for ISD in general based on Clark’s communication theory. Clark’s theory provides concepts, components, propositions, and design principles given speci<sup>fi</sup>c boundary conditions of ISD situations. Such a general design theory for ISD could enrich other models and theories on ISD as a social process such as, for example, socio-technical systems theory (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008).

## Practical implications and limitations

The most promising aspect of our approach is that semantic heterogeneity is overcome by intensive communication and development of common ground between all stakeholders. Our approach can and should be used in conjunction with other tools such as schema matching (e.g., Saleem et al, 2008) or ontologies (e.g., Ram & Jinsoo, 2004). For example, the approach can aid communicating, negotiating, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cying an ontology.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are also important in putting recent technological advances into perspective such as column-based data storages or in-memory data management (e.g., Färber et al, 2012; Plattner & Zeier, 2012; TDWI, 2014). These technologies speed up data processing to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree and have been argued to considerably alter the landscape of data integration, enabling the uni<sup>fi</sup>cation of operational transaction processing systems and analytical applications such as DWs and OLAP systems (e.g., see Plattner & Zeier, 2012). While the material uni<sup>fi</sup>cation of operational and analytical systems is physically possible and useful for data organization and storage, we contend that the fundamental problems of designing data integration requirements such as semantic heterogeneity will not be solved with these technologies.

Our case studies only provide an initial understanding of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the approach. Since the cases took place in real-life environments, we cannot completely rule out that better performance was achieved because of other causes than because of our approach. The existing evaluation results and the additional information gained in the construction and running of the artifacts have to be brought together and fed back to another round of suggestions in future research.

## Conclusion

Business intelligence and data warehousing are important application scenarios in industry, where the problem of semantic heterogeneity in the design of data integration requirements is still an important issue to be studied. We provide an approach based on communication theory to deal with semantic heterogeneity. Our approach makes use of agile development practices, and we show how and why our approach helps to mitigate the problems induced by semantic heterogeneity in the design of data integration requirements. ## Appendix C

## Case study design

## Overview

Natural controls that were already in place were utilized for the research design (Lee, 1989). For example, focusing on persons who were participants in more than one of the cases held constant the people factors while comparing projects. Other examples of natural controls are the scope of the project and the parallel implementation of a new core banking system.

Two of the authors are practitioners that have worked for more than 7 years for CONS. They participated in all three of the selected projects that were conducted without the approach (Bank A–C). Afterwards, both participated in the design of the artifacts but not in the subsequent projects (Bank D–F). The other two authors were not involved in any project. During the evaluation of the artifacts they acted as observers and interviewers in the case studies. The analyses of the case studies in Bank D–F evaluated the artifacts and led to re<sup>fi</sup>nements of the design (e.g., DaRT was a response to a need for managing the template).

Direct observations by the <sup>fi</sup>rst two authors, unstructured and semi-structured interviews, structured selfestimation surveys of project members, project documentation, and e-mail exchanges were used to generate data and collected in a case study database. We interviewed both project managers and project workers (see Table C1). In addition, project documentation helped to check interview statements. For example, simple and complex DW architectures in the cases became comparable based on data <sup>fl</sup>ow models. ## Interviews

Since at the time of the interviews most of the selected projects or related successor projects were still ongoing, this minimized the risk that participants displayed retrospective bias or that they had already forgotten something in their interactions with other project members. We employed a variant of the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954). We used an interview guideline to maintain the interview <sup>fl</sup>ow in interviewee-directed interaction, asking for examples and trying to get to speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations or events. The interview guideline was not shared with interviewees and was only used as a general outline, with lots of room for deviations and probing questions. For example, we asked about situations of bad data quality, a circumstance leading to this, or what people did that was helpful in resolving this. The interviews were either conducted by phone if the informants worked at the time in geographically remote locations or in face-to-face sessions at the organizations’ site.

The interviews were conducted by two of the researchers. They lasted between 45 and 120 min and were all recorded and transcribed. This resulted in about 130 recorded transcript pages. Follow-up e-mails were sent to request clari<sup>fi</sup>cations and to offer informants the possibility to provide feedback and comments. After we had conducted and analyzed 16 interviews with 11 project members, we jointly posited that data saturation had for the most part occurred (Guest et al, 2006): most interviews yielded rich data, new themes did not emerge, and the objective was to evaluate the usability and utility of the artifacts. In related cases such as user testing, up to <sup>fi</sup>ve users can be enough (Nielsen & Landauer, 1993). Finally, a focus group meeting of all involved project managers discussed similarities and differences between the projects. This discussion was moderated and observed by two researchers.

## Coding scheme

Categorical codes based on Clark’s communication theory were used to analyze the case study data (Miles & Huberman, 1994). The coding scheme was developed a priori to the analysis and is illustrated along with examples in Table D1.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst analysis occurred in parallel to the data collection. We wrote down <sup>fi</sup>eld notes while conducting the interviews, transcribing the tapes, and reading the transcripts and feedback of the interviewees. These <sup>fi</sup>eld notes were subsequently discussed by the interviewers after each case was completed regarding different impressions and interpretations of patterns in the data. In the following phase, the emerging interpretations were connected and matched to the artifacts.

MaxQDA (http://www.maxqda.com/) was used for coding the interview transcripts and linking additional documentation from the project diary. The <sup>fi</sup>rst and third author coded and interpreted the data independently before discussing and resolving differences. After each interview cycle concerning a single case, phone and face-to-face sessions among the researchers and both instant messaging and e-mail conversations were used to discuss the cases in order to resolve differences in interpretation. For the cases A–C, this was also matched with impressions from direct observations of the two researchers that participated as consultants in these projects.

## Construct validity

A case study investigator must (1) select the speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of changes that are to be studied (in relation to the original objectives of the study) and (2) ensure that the selected measures of these changes do indeed re<sup>fl</sup>ect the speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of change that have been selected. For satisfying the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we studied changes in communication within <sup>fi</sup>nancial DW development projects using categories based on concepts from communication theory (cf. Appendix C). For satisfying the second step, we followed three tactics to increase construct validity. We used multiple sources of evidence (multiple key informants) and established a chain of evidence (with the help of the case database/project diary) during data collection. Furthermore, key informants reviewed draft reports of the case study.

## Internal validity

Internal validity is a concern for case studies, where an investigator is trying to determine whether pattern X led to pattern Y. Basically, a case study involves an inference every time an event cannot be directly observed. Thus, an investigator ‘infers’ that a particular event resulted from some earlier occurrence, based on interview and documentary evidence collected as part of the case study. In the data analysis of this case study, pattern matching became possible by linking the categories of the coding scheme to data from the case study diary. This was supported by explicit explanation-building in the case study analysis and narratives, cross-checked against the results of the focus group discussion (cf. section ‘Cross-Case Summary’).

## Survey and descriptive results

The survey includes users from CONS (consultants) and users from client organizations (decision makers, business experts, and IT experts), as well as both project workers (17 respondents) and project managers (11 respondents). The empirical evaluation of the artifacts is based on this sample of 28 users, which equates to a large degree of the total population of users from which they are drawn (26 male, 2 female; age between 26 and 51 years, with a mean of 34.5 and standard deviation of 5.4; job experience in current position between 1 and 12 years, with a mean of 4.4 and standard deviation of 2.5; and IT experience between 0 and 24 years, with a mean of 8.0 and standard deviation of 6.4). (Note: This includes (a) the procedure model and (b) DaRT. The template is not included because in the stable con<sup>fi</sup>guration the template is implemented in DaRT.)

We constructed the survey instrument based on novel scales and existing questionnaires to measure common ground, performance, quality, and use. (The speci<sup>fi</sup>ed items, organized by construct, and corresponding statistics are shown in Table F1 below.) The questionnaire includes 55 scales, including items for self-reported usage, intention to use, ease of use, reduced illusion of evidence, improved speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, early detection of communication defects, performance expectancy and system quality, as well as a series of demographic and other control items. First, we compiled groups of questions from validated instruments to represent each construct, and we modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the wording to <sup>fi</sup>t the situation. Second, we developed some novel items relating to our three strategies based on existing studies. Each question was measured on a 7-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Before implementing the survey, the instrument was reviewed by academics and practitioners with knowledge of survey design and DW development. We made minor changes based on their suggestions. Owing to the limited number of users we did not pilot test the resulting survey.

Totally 26 of 28 participants stated that they had worked in their project(s) with the procedure model and answered the corresponding questions. Some people, especially decision makers and business experts, were only confronted in discussions and workshops with the information that is managed by DaRT. Moreover, 16 of 28 participants stated that they had actively worked with DaRT and also answered the corresponding questions. Inspection of the data showed that the two participants who stated that they use DaRT but not the procedure model are working on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c project that deals with a very narrow focus, where DaRT is only employed for managing requirements. Each construct showed internal consistency levels exceeding 0.7, as measured by Cronbach’s α.
