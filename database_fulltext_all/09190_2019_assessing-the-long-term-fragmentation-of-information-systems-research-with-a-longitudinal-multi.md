# Assessing the long-term fragmentation of information systems research with a longitudinal multi-network analysis

Gohar Feroz Khan<sup>a</sup> and Matthias Triers<sup>b,c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management & Marketing, Faculty of Management, University of Waikato, Hamilton, New Zealand; <sup>b</sup>Department of Business Information Systems, Paderborn University, Paderborn, Germany; <sup>c</sup>Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg/Copenhagen, Denmark

## ABSTRACT

Over the decades, the evolving information systems (IS) research community and its academic output has greatly expanded. This paper o<sup>f</sup>ers an integrated analysis of multiple dimensions of network interconnectedness of the growing IS discipline. In line with the social and intellectual dimensions of the underlying theory of science, we synthesise multiple network views on authors, institutions, journal outlets, citations, and themes into a multidimensional knowledge network infrastructure analysis of collaborative networks of IS researchers and their academic output. We further introduce two fragmentation types to better address the dynamics of the IS discourse discussed in previous research. Based on a corpus of all 3587 AIS basket of 8 journal articles over 20 years, we use the analytical framework to study whether the fast growth of the IS discipline resulted in a reduced coherence of the overall academic collaboration and the research themes. The analysis reveals that the sampled IS researcher community developed a large core component with in<sup>fl</sup>uential bridging people who mitigate fragmentation and centralisation. This IS community structure constitutes a valuable asset to cope with fragmentation tendencies in the intellectual dimension (research topics) resulting from many short-term topic bursts and from centralisation of conceptual terms.

## 1. Introduction

The information systems (IS) research discipline is re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon in various scientometric studies that focus on general properties, maturity stages and the overall development of the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Banville & Landry, 1989; Bernroider, Pilkington, & Cordoba, 2013; Liu, Li, & Goncalves, 2016; Taylor, Dillon, & Wingen, 2010; Xu, Chau, & Tan, 2014). Methods such as cocitation, citation, or keyword network analysis are used to elicit the IS discipline’s intellectual identity and to assess long-term challenges, such as the threat of disintegration and incoherence through fragmentation in the research topics (Bernroider et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2016; Taylor et al., 2010). The initial concern that IS researchers “get diverted almost daily by new research ideas” in a fragmented discipline with low internal dependency (Banville & Landry, 1989) was more recently contrasted by reports of a growing interdependence and density within central topic clusters (Liu et al., 2016; Taylor et al., 2010).

While these <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest a maturation towards a more cohesive IS topic landscape with several manifesting themes, the focus on thematic core paradigms has been criticised as being too narrow to assess the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Banville and Landry (1989) stress that the widespread reference to Kuhn’s (1970) notion of research paradigms as the underlying theory of science should not dismiss that Kuhn also emphasised the community structure of science as a basic unit of analysis (Kuhn, 1970, 1977). Banville and Landry (1989) further emphasise Whitley’s (1984) theory of science as an appropriate theoretical foundation that encompasses an intellectual (thematic) and a social dimension of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>eld. These two dimensions are interdependent as academic output is mostly socially produced through collaborations (Moody, 2004; Xu et al., 2014) and co-authoring is linked to increases of the contributions’ impact (Gallivan & Ahuja, 2015). Unlike the positive recent studies on the intellectual dimension, the few previous assessments of IS research’s social dimension show rather mixed results: co-authorship patterns exhibit very diverse degrees of fragmentation inside four di<sup>f</sup>erent sub-communities (behavioural, organisational, computer, and economic sciences) with very limited collaboration across the sampled communities (Oh, Choi, & Kim, 2005). While two-thirds of the researchers became embedded in a large core cluster of authors, the number of researchers grows rapidly and “the IS community cannot be deemed structural e<sup>fi</sup>cient” (Xu et al., 2014, p. 848).

To advance beyond the existing isolated monodimensional studies and arrive at a more integrated and nuanced re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s intellectual and social dimensions and their mutual synergy, we propose knowledge network infrastructure (KNI) analysis as a multidimensional approach. It merges the analysis of interdependence of collaboration and production structures among authors (ie, Whitley’s social dimension) and the assessment of interdependence among the produced academic outputs such as topical clusters or citation patterns (ie, Whitley’s intellectual dimension) into one approach that is based on one consistent dataset. In this way, multidimensional KNI analysis can, for example, identify whether fragmentation e<sup>f</sup>ects through <sup>fl</sup>uctuating and disconnecting subthemes are indirectly compensated through a tightly knit group of core academics.

A further limitation of existing research pertains to the conceptualisation and measurement of fragmentation and coherence. The concepts derive from Whitley’s (1984, p. 111) qualitative evaluation of coherence as a strong mutual dependence among researchers and their research goals (topics). The absence of coherence is fragmentation. In the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, the network measures centrality and density have been utilised as a more formal way of assessing the level of fragmentation or coherence in the intellectual dimension (eg, Liu et al., 2016). While increasing centrality has been interpreted by IS scholars as an indication of growing coherence (Liu et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2014), network theorists such as Moody (2004) or White and Harary (2001) debate that centrality is the opposite of coherence (and density), as centralising nodes (with structural holes around them; Burt, 2001) and emerging hierarchies re<sup>fl</sup>ect a pattern of “star production” (Moody, 2004, p. 216) instead of a pattern of cross-fertilising collaboration in a densely connected dynamic community (Merton, 1968). To better link IS research with network theory, we regard cohesion as a structural pattern that is well interlinked (dense) but does not heavily depend on a central elite. Based on this view, we go beyond previous IS research by introducing an extended framework with two opposing fragmentation types, (1) lateral fragmentation as a lack of interdependence and (2) hierarchical fragmentation between a manifest central core and a disconnecting periphery (the two types and their measures are introduced in more detail in the section on fragmentation types). Conceptualising lateral and hierarchical fragmentation as two opposite poles highlights that attaining disciplinary coherence in a dynamic discipline such as IS involves a balancing act, where the rapidly evolving research <sup>fi</sup>eld is constantly navigating “between Scylla and Charybdis” with the threat of dissolution through lateral fragmentation of collaboration patterns and topics on the one end, and a very manifest centralisation of stable core themes and researcher groups that are not well integrated with emergent processes in the dynamic periphery.

To assess the two fragmentation types for the social and intellectual dimension of the IS discipline systematically, our KNI approach adopts a social network analysis (SNA) perspective (Wasserman & Faust, 1994) and its metrics. SNA is frequently used for re<sup>fl</sup>ections on the discipline (Choi, Yi, & Lee, 2011; Khan & Park, 2013; Liu, Bollen, Nelson, & VAN DE SOMPEL, 2005; Liu et al., 2016; Oh et al., 2005; Trier & Molka-Danielsen, 2013; Xu & Chau, 2006) and is suitable for observing changes over time (Trier, 2008). Across the KNI dimensions, we use one single and consistent dataset of all articles (N = 3,587) published in the 20-year period from 1995 to 2014 in the Association for Information Systems basket of eight journal outlets (AIS, 2011; from now on referred to as the “basket journals”). The KNI is investigated at multiple levels (eg, author vs. institution, or topic vs. journal levels) and considers fading, emerging, and central themes and keywords in the IS domain, with a main interest in assessing the interconnectedness among the diverse IS research sub<sup>fi</sup>elds and the impact of peripheral changes. We focus on the following two research questions (RQ):

RQ1 – Social Dimension: To what extent is the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS research, as measured by its collaborative production structures, marked by lateral and hierarchical fragmentation?

RQ2 – Intellectual Dimension: To what extent is the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS research, as measured by its collaborative academic discourse output, marked by lateral and hierarchical fragmentation?

In brief, the KNI analysis will systematically explore how the centralisation, density, and clustering of coauthorship structures, co-author prominence in the network, the di<sup>f</sup>erences between the core and periphery of the network, the interconnectedness between institutions, as well as the patterns of fading, emerging, and central themes can be brought together to answer the RQs and shed light on IS research as a long-term collective contribution in a rapidly evolving environment. Positioning IS research in our comprehensive fragmentation framework enables to identify actionable insights and recommendations for strengthening, incentivising, or reconsidering conducive IS research collaboration practices and topical research attention.

The rest of the article is organised as follows: The next section provides an overview of the previous attempts to understand the IS knowledge infrastructure and introduces our approach of KNI analysis as a systematic investigation of several interconnected network views. We then explain the methodology adopted for our study and present our results in the form of a series of network analyses to gather evidence that sheds light on our RQs. Finally, we discuss the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings and derive a series of recommendations.

## 2. Prior research of knowledge network dimensions

Researchers have applied a plethora of approaches to study the IS knowledge infrastructure including systematic literature reviews (Hamilton & Ives, 1982; Kitchenham et al., 2009), scientometrics (Cunningham & Dillon, 1997; Katerattanakul & Han, 2003), bibliometrics (Raghuram, Tuertscher, & Garud, 2010; Subramanyam, 1983; Vessey, Ramesh, & Glass, 2002), webometrics (Ortega & Aguillo, 2008, 2009; Vaughan & Thelwall, 2005), and frequently also SNA techniques (Khan & Park, 2013; Oh et al., 2005; Swar & Khan, 2013; Vidgen, Henneberg, & Naude, 2007). Existing studies are carried out from di<sup>f</sup>erent views (eg, topic, journal, author, country, region, or method) and are either cross-sectional or longitudinal (Xu & Chau, 2006). They often have the main purpose of describing key players (eg, authors and journals) or emerging topic trends to contour the identity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## 2.1. Coherence and fragmentation analysis

A special thread of related research debates the health and progress of the IS discipline by assessing its coherence and fragmentation (eg, Banville & Landry, 1989; Liu et al., 2016; Taylor et al., 2010). Related studies ground in Whitley’s (1984) work on the intellectual and social organisation of sciences. Whitley conceptualises a multidimensional space that includes the thematic research focus (knowledge) and the academic community that through their work organises the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The main proposed characteristics of a discipline are (1) mutual dependence and (2) task uncertainty. The <sup>fi</sup>rst aspect points to the degree of a well-established interdependence of the involved researchers and their knowledge contributions, while the second aspect considers the interrelation of the addressed research subjects and the absence of unconnected schools of thought. On a more abstract level, both these properties suggest the important role of the degree of interdependence among researchers and contents as a descriptor of the development of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. The decline or disruption of this interdependence is called fragmentation (Moody, 2004; Whitley, 1984).

Most empirical studies of the social dimension of IS research describe structures of diverse conference communities (Stein, Galliers, & Whitley, 2014; Vidgen et al., 2007; Xu & Chau, 2006) or assess aspects of co-authorship (Gallivan & Ahuja, 2015; Trier & Molka-Danielsen, 2013). Studies on a more aggregated level focus on listing key institutions (Ortega & Aguillo, 2008, 2009) or how countries and regions are contributing to the output of the IS research domain (Galliers & Whitley, 2007; Pouloudi, Poulymenakou, & Pramatari, 2012). From the few papers that explore the fragmentation of the social dimension, Oh et al. (2005) note a very limited collaboration of researchers across the main sub-communities. Xu et al. (2014) <sup>fi</sup>nd small increases in the researchers’ average number of collaboration partners, but on the other hand, the uneven distribution of contact networks results in a few central, prominent authors. While the largest component connects about two thirds of all authors, Xu et al. (2014) also note a growing number of small peripheral groups. The authors’ conclusion that an increasing amount of collaboration ties suggest growing levels of mutual dependence appears very positive, but attenuates the study’s data on parallel adverse e<sup>f</sup>ects of network growth, low density, or centralisation.

Another branch of fragmentation studies concentrates on the intellectual dimension. Taylor et al. (2010) identify that the co-citation patterns suggest a stable topical focus on three main IS research themes (development, implementation and use of systems; IS strategy; group work and decision support) despite also <sup>fi</sup>nding constant disintegrating influences of rapid topical changes. Liu et al. (2016) investigated a sample of key topic clusters with a keyword co-occurrence (co-word) analysis. Coherence and fragmentation were operationalised and measured using the network metrics of centrality and density (see Appendix A1 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions). The authors identify a “high-centrality, low-density structure of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld” (p. 15), where diverse topic clusters with a rather low internal density evidence the absence of strong internal cross-references between concepts. Further, the clusters of the two studied decades were found to di<sup>f</sup>er by more than 80%, marking IS as a “story of change” (p. 14). Small increases in average centrality and density (from 0.04 to 0.08) within the sampled key topic clusters are suggested as evidence of growing topical coherence (and thus reduced levels of fragmentation), despite the noted rapid changes and the focus on selected top keyword clusters.

A theoretical shortcoming of previous research is that considering increases in centrality as a coherence improvement is contested by network theorists (Moody, 2004; White & Harary, 2001), who consider the manifestation of critical central nodes and hierarchies (star production) antithetical to a process of wide cross-fertilisation among larger cliques with densifying collaboration. Consequently, centralised hierarchical network structures are conceived as the opposite of coherence (also see Burt, 2001, on this debate) and thus can be regarded as a fragmentation risk. This notion is further supported in a widely recognised study by Merton (1968). He identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that a <sup>fi</sup>eld’s long-term centralisation and hierarchisation of a permanent core can, in combination with the limited attention resources of the researchers, point to a “rich-get-richer” dynamic, also known as “Matthew e<sup>f</sup>ect”. Here, a manifesting small elite of prominent authors or papers gets most attention while new in<sup>fl</sup>uences of a broader base at the periphery are getting marginalised and ignored. Such a dynamic can reduce the discipline’s capability of absorbing topical diversity and growth in the long run. This hierarchy formation risk is not addressed in previous research despite its relevance for more dynamic research <sup>fi</sup>elds with shifting and permeable disciplinary boundaries (Moody, 2004), such as the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, which was found to be entangled in dynamic ways (Bryant, 2008) with many related disciplines (Bernroider et al., 2013) and subject to a considerable temporal <sup>fl</sup>uctuation in the topic landscape (Larsen & Levine, 2005). We react to the misalignment of IS research and network theory by adapting our concept of coherence to the network-theoretical view and by conceptualising an extended framework of two fragmentation types (a detailed introduction follows in the KNI methodology section below).

## 2.2. SNA and IS knowledge network infrastructure

Starting with the early quantitative studies of citation networks between papers by Price (1965), SNA (Wasserman & Faust, 1994) has become a very widespread approach to investigate knowledge network structures (Moody, 2004). SNA is based on graph theory (Biggs, Lloyd, & Wilson, 1986) and treats a network as a function of nodes and links. Nodes can represent various model elements such as individuals, organisations, topics, or other entities, and links represent the relationship among these nodes, for example, friendship, similarity, or citations. Based on the conceptual elements, SNA o<sup>f</sup>ers a large range of di<sup>f</sup>erent measures to evaluate node, link, and network properties (de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of key SNA metrics are listed in Appendix A1). The SNA technique is frequently applied to investigate the IS discipline, for example, to assess co-authorship structures (eg, Oh et al., 2005; Vidgen et al., 2007; Xu & Chau, 2006) or topic clusters (Liu et al., 2016). Most existing studies, however, consider either only sub-communities, selected outlets, or core clusters. They further focus on only one or a few network views of the complex and dynamic collaboration and publication patterns.

In this article, we utilise SNA methods and measures in our assessment of Whitley’s (1984) social and intellectual research dimensions in the dynamic IS discourse (the measures are further introduced in the methodology section). We will now introduce and apply a comprehensive framework of KNI analysis that comprises di<sup>f</sup>erent network views and considers the two types of lateral and hierarchical fragmentation. Based on this approach, we develop a more nuanced fragmentation profile of the journal output production in the information systems research <sup>fi</sup>eld. Grounded in network theory and the theory of science, our KNI analysis helps to reassess previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings and enables a deeper insight into the multidimensional and complex collective production of the IS discourse and its long-term challenges.

## 3. KNI analysis methodology

The KNI of IS research is a complex pattern comprised of interconnected sub-networks that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the academic work cycle starting with interconnected meta-data on collaborating authors, their academic output in the form of journal papers, the publishing outlet and the community’s reception of academic work. The network views are (1) the authors’ coauthorship network, (2) the institutions’ co-authorship network, (3) the countries’ co-authorship network (not assessed in the study presented in this article, due to space restrictions), (4) the papers’ keyword co-occurrence network, (5) the journal-keyword a<sup>fi</sup>liation network, (6) the journals’ co-citation network, and (7) the co-author-citation network.

Table 1 provides an overview about the KNI network views and how they link to the Whitley’s (1984) two key dimensions of a research discipline. The collaboration analysis begins at the author level and is then aggregated to institutions and nations to reveal patterns in academic research interactions. The intellectual dimension concerns the academic output, which we analyse with regards to citation patterns as well as with regards to evolving key themes and their relationships.

## 3.1. Network views

We will now explain each network view of the KNI analysis framework and then outline the fragmentation types in more detail, followed by a short introduction of the relevant network measures.

Co-authorship Network (Researcher Level). The nodes in the author co-authorship network represent authors who published in the basket journals. The link between the nodes represents the co-authorship relationship (ie, for each joint publication the tie strength increases by 1). The co-authorship network reveals the network structure of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c collaborations among individual researchers (Liu et al., 2005). A single collaboration between two authors is considered su<sup>fi</sup>cient for preventing fragmented communities, as the collaboration on one paper is very intense, strategic, and long-term. Network fragmentation results from various author clusters that are not linked with each other. Few active clusters with high activity in the centre of the network can indicate hierarchy tendencies and a small dominant author elite (Merton, 1968).

Co-authorship Network (Institution Level). In this network, nodes represent the institutions (eg, universities, industries, government, research centres, etc.) publishing in the basket journals and links represent co-authorship relationships among the institutions. Due to the large number of institutional relationships, heat maps were used to visualise the data (see Appendix A4). Multiple a<sup>fi</sup>liations to institutions are considered via a weighted link (the detailed matrix multiplication approach is described at https://www. leydesdor<sup>f</sup>.net/software/instplus/). Institutional collaboration networks reveal critical information <sup>fl</sup>ows among institutions (Khan & Park, 2013) and thus indicate the degree of organisational fragmentation.

Co-authorship Network (Country Level). In this network (which is not reported in this article due to space restrictions), nodes represent countries publishing in the basket journals. The links represent aggregated co-authorship relationships among the countries. Constructing the co-authorship networks among countries can show collaboration patterns among nations (Khan & Park, 2013), hence providing a further layer of fragmentation analysis of the social dimension of the IS discipline.

Keyword Co-occurrence Network. Two keywords co-occur if both appear in one de<sup>fi</sup>ned text element, which is in our case the title of a published article in our dataset. The two keywords then each become a node in the keyword co-occurrence network and their co-occurrence is resulting in the creation of a relationship between the two nodes with a tie strength of 1. Each further co-occurrence of the same two keywords in another article title increments the relationship strength by 1. With a focus on dense stabilising keyword links over time, the keyword cooccurrence networks provide insights into the semantic fragmentation or integration of the intellectual dimension of IS research (cf. keywords of fragmentation versus keywords of integration, noted in Choi et al., 2011). To emphasise topical dynamics and longevity, we further analysed the temporal patterns of keyword use in the journal article titles with burstanalysis (we introduce this software-based analytical technique in more detail in the section on data collection).

Journal-Keyword Afiliation Network. In this twomode network (ie, a network with two types of nodes), the <sup>fi</sup>rst type of node represents the authors manually provided keywords and the second type of node represents the journals. The links represent a<sup>fi</sup>liation relationships among the keywords and journals. For example, a link is present when a keyword appears in the title of a paper published in a journal. The journal-keyword network helps to assess the degree of fragmentation between publication outlets as this view elicits journal speci<sup>fi</sup>c research themes and integrative themes shared by journals.

Journal Co-citation Network. The nodes in this network represent the sources (eg, journals and conferences) that get cited in the article’s reference section. A link is instantiated between two journals, if they were cited in one single publication (unlike citation networks that reveal ties between cited and citing papers). Tie strength indicates which journals are often cited together. In this way, co-citation networks reveal interconnections in the long-term research discourse when contributions from di<sup>f</sup>erent journals are becoming semantically integrated into a discourse through the (citing) follow-up article. Semantic relationships and similarities among journals are revealed (Ding, Chowdhury, & Foo, 2000; Tsay, Xu, & Wu, 2003). Journals may either form fragmented attention clusters that host certain special topics or the discourse is agnostic of particular journal pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Due to the large volume of the co-citations, heat maps (see the Appendix A1 for a de<sup>fi</sup>nition and A7 for the visualisation) were used to emphasise the most frequent relations as bright zones in the network.

Co-author Citation Network. A <sup>fi</sup>nal network view combines received citations with the author centrality in the co-authorship network. Nodes are authors, links are co-author collaborations and the size of the node represents the received citations. This view is derived from the co-author network and sheds light on potential hierarchisation e<sup>f</sup>ects (Merton, 1968), where the core of the network is consisting of a cluster of central researchers, who also received most attention for their work.

Based on the di<sup>f</sup>erent KNI networks, we can contribute a nuanced multi-dimensional assessment of the network fragmentation that may have evolved over time in the growing IS discipline, in order to reveal structural long-term challenges.

## 3.2. Fragmentation types

To address the noted lack of alignment between previous IS research and the conceptualisation of coherence by network theorists (Burt, 2001; Merton, 1968; Moody, 2004), we introduce an extended framework the two opposing fragmentation types (Figure 1) lateral and hierarchical fragmentation. Based on Whitley (1984) and in line with previous IS research by Taylor et al. (2010), Xu et al. (2014) or Liu et al. (2016), lateral fragmentation results from low levels of interdependence between topics or academics (eg, a pattern with disconnected clusters). Opposite to the lateral disconnect among the authors and themes, we draw from the work of Merton (1968), White and Harary (2001) as well as Moody (2004) and introduce hierarchical fragmentation. With a dedicated focus on centrality and hierarchy formation processes in the network, this second fragmentation type pertains to the manifestation of a centralising core (in top hierarchical network positions) and a marginalised and fragmentising periphery (at the bottom of the hierarchy). By conceptualising hierarchical fragmentation opposite to lateral fragmentation, we can advance previous research by not equating increases of centrality with increases of coherence (Moody, 2004) and by amplifying possible negative implications of centralising structures (Merton, 1968). For assessing the fragmentation types, we utilise network measures that are introduced in more detail in the next section (and in Appendix A1).

Based on our conceptualisation, we can now assess, if the rapidly changing IS research discourse (Bryant, 2008; Larsen & Levine, 2005) shows a sustainable disciplinary development that is balancing between lateral fragmentation as the lack of interdependence and hierarchical fragmentation between a manifest central core and the disconnecting periphery. This notion of balancing has been noted by Whitley (1984, p.100) as a tension between normative standardisation and fragmentising innovation as two equally important elements of dynamically evolving scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>elds. It further better corresponds to the recent calls to consider IS as a dynamic and evolving discourse (Bryant, 2008).

As Figure 1 illustrates, the lateral and hierarchical fragmentation types can be assessed for the social as well as for the intellectual dimension (Whitley, 1984) of IS research. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension is pertaining to the separation or integration of di<sup>f</sup>erent author sub-communities and their collective action, the latter aspect emphasises the coherence of the resulting intellectual IS discourse of the mutually referencing journal contributions that address the diverse topical themes within the information systems discipline.

## 3.3. Network measures

Initially, the assessment of fragmentation was based on a qualitative evaluation (Whitley, 1984) or categorisations (Banville & Landry, 1989). In this article, we additionally identify lateral and hierarchical fragmentation tendencies of the KNI with quantitative network measures (Wasserman & Faust, 1994). We provide an overview of utilised SNA measures in Appendix A1.

For our assessment of lateral fragmentation, we use measures such as the number and size of network components, the network diameter, network density levels, the clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient, the average degree of the networks, and tie strengths (but not centrality). In this context, a network component is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an isolated sub-network that is connected within so that all member nodes are reachable. It is disconnected from other sub-networks (Hanneman & Riddle, 2005), that is, there is no path to a node outside of the component (Wasserman & Faust, 1994). The network diameter is the largest shortest path between any pair of nodes in a graph (Wasserman & Faust, 1994). It indicates how long it would take for some information to pass through the network. The density of a network is expressing the degree of interconnectivity in a graph and is calculated as the number of links present in a network divided by the number of all mathematically possible links between pairs of nodes in the network (for an undirected network, the number of all possible links can be calculated as n(n-1)/2 where n is the number of nodes in a network). A fully connected network, in which each node is connected to every other node, will have a density of 1. In a co-authorship network, the clustering coeficient of a network is a local density measure that “tells us how much a node’s collaborators are willing to collaborate with each other” directly (Barabási et al., 2002, p. 296). Finally, the average degree is the average number of links of the nodes in the network and tie strength refers to the number of papers that two authors co-authored.

The assessment of hierarchical fragmentation utilises centrality measures (Friedkin, 1991), such as degree-, betweenness-, and eigenvector centrality. The degree centrality of a node measures the number of links a node has to other nodes, for example, the number of co-authors or co-occurring keywords. Betweenness centrality is measuring to what extent a node lies on the paths between other nodes. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects the ability of a node to control or facilitate collaboration and information <sup>fl</sup>ow. Eigenvector centrality measures the global in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a node based on the number of links it has to other nodes, but also factoring in the centrality of these neighbours as a weighting. An eigenvector-central node thus has many relationships with other central nodes (also note the explanation in Appendix A1). Such a view on centrality identi<sup>fi</sup>es elite networks, where prominent actors are linked primarily with other prominent researchers. Further, the analysis of hierarchy formation processes adopts Borgatti & Everett’s (2000) method for di<sup>f</sup>erentiating the network’s core from the periphery in connected structures. In a recursive process, the network is divided into a core group with members who are more densely tied to other core nodes (and optionally to a few peripheral nodes). The periphery comprises unconnected smaller groups or members with a de<sup>fi</sup>ned small number of connections to one or a few core members but not to other members of the periphery, that is, they are located “outside” the core cluster, either isolated or with very few critical inward links to the core (Borgatti & Everett, 2000, p. 378). Based on the division between core and periphery, we conduct a comparative assessment using SNA methods to analyse di<sup>f</sup>erences in the degrees, group sizes, or tie strengths of core and peripheral nodes. A further indication of a dominant core (Merton, 1968) is the existence of the process of preferential attachment to core nodes that leads to a power-law distribution in the network, where very few central nodes attain over-proportional numbers of links (Barabási & Albert, 1999; also see the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in Appendix A1).

## 3.4. Data collection

The total of 3587 articles that appeared in the basket journals during 1995–2014 were retrieved from the Web of Science (WoS) database (the Journal of the AIS joined in 2000, that is, 5 years after the beginning of our period, the data does not include editorials; Appendix A2 lists the number of papers per journal). The dataset was then manually inspected to correct for errors.

In order to extract, analyse, and visualise the networks, several tools were adopted, including Pajek (DE NOOY, Mrvar, & Batagelj, 2005), NodeXL (Smith et al., 2010), Science of Science tool (SCI2TEAM, 2009), VOSviewer (VAN ECK & Waltman, 2010), and GUSS (Adar, 2006). Previous studies have extensively used these SNA tools to study the knowledge infrastructure, particularly using WoS data (Leydesdor<sup>f</sup>, 2004, 2006a; Vidgen et al., 2007; Xu & Chau, 2006). Pajek was mainly used to investigate the structural holes and hubs present in the network. NodeXL, GUSS, and VOSviewer were used for the data visualisations and other network level analysis. We also used the IntColl.exe routine for studying collaboration (available at http://www.leydesdor<sup>f</sup>.net/software.htm). The burst detection algorithm implemented in the Science of Science Tool (SCI2TEAM, 2009) was used to identify rapidly emerging and fading themes in titles of the articles (not the list of provided keyword). According to Leydesdor<sup>f</sup> (2006b), keywords in the titles of the articles are well suited to study trends in a domain. The burst detection is based on Kleinberg’s (2003) work on bursty and hierarchical structure in streams of events and identi<sup>fi</sup>es short periods of increased target events in a stream of events, based on minimising a cost function. The strength of the burst re<sup>fl</sup>ects the relative keyword occurrence, measured as a factor of the baseline keyword occurrence probability as described in Kleinberg (2003). We use the method to identify the topics that increased in usage, were more active for a period of time, and then disappeared (SCI2TEAM, 2009). This helps us to address the IS discipline as a dynamic discourse (Bryant, 2008) and capture the changing zones of topical coherence (Larsen & Levine, 2005).

Before applying the burst detection routine, the terms in the dataset were lowercased and tokenised by the applied Science of Science software tool, that is, text strings are simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed and disaggregated into small meaningful elements so that all alphabetic characters within contiguous strings are part of the token, removing white spaces, line breaks, or endings. Further a common list of stop words (such as “a”, “all”, “about”, or “the”) was removed. Previous studies have extensively used the burst detection technique to identify emerging trends in other research domains (Chen, 2006; Chen et al., 2009; Guo, Weingart, & Borner, 2011; Mane & Borner, 2004).

## 4. Fragmentation analysis of the social dimension

A <sup>fi</sup>rst insight about the development and fragmentation of the IS research collaboration patterns can be gathered by investigating the evolution of the core network of co-authoring researchers. The primary focus of this data analysis is on the degree of the network interconnectedness and how new academics have been able to either form relevant groups or to connect to a main community. The presented results address RQ 1 (fragmentation of the social dimension of IS research).

## 4.1. Researcher level co-authorship analysis

Figure 2 shows the evolution of the IS co-authorship network over the observed 20-year period. The emerging collaboration network structure is composed of many components that are strongly internally connected but have no paths to other components. Larger clusters show chain-like structures. The core cluster of the KNI has grown considerably to almost 5 times its initial size, from 946 to 4477 authors who established 7554 co-authorship links. The density of the overall network is very low with 0.002 (ie, only 0.2% of all possible relationships are established) in the largest cluster indicating an overall fragility of the quickly growing but sparsely connected community structure. We will now discuss the various measures and the overall evolution of the network structures over time in more detail to arrive at more nuanced insights about possible fragmentation tendencies. A summarising overview of the metrics across the time periods is presented in Table 2.

From 1995 to 1999, the KNI consisted of 946 researchers clustered together in 233 separate components. Several important but unconnected core groups coexist in that time period. The largest network component comprised 50 nodes (representing actors) closely followed by the second largest component with 42 nodes. Researchers had the relatively small average number of 1.91 contacts in the network (ie, average degree). In the most central component this number was slightly higher with 2.72. The small peripheral components were each well interconnected as a clique, indicated by a high clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.79 (ie, 79% of direct links among actors in the clique component). The very low density of 0.002 and the large diameter of 11 path steps point to rather exclusive relationships of the authors. Their interaction with only two or three neighbours led to an absence of short-cuts and long fragile chain patterns, in which each author is critical to keep up the collaboration chain. The overall pattern evidences a low level of structural interdependence.

During the next 5 years (2000–2004), the network size almost doubled with 907 new authors entering the studied publication outlets. Circa 55% (502) of them attached to one main core that converged from the previous era’s two big components, but there was also a large share of 45% (405) who linked to peripheral components. The fast growing core now assumed a much more central role in the overall collaboration pattern. The average degree of this central component (3.41) indicates a small increase in the average number of co-authors. The many small network components connected internally (clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient = 0.78) but the overall density remains on a very small value of 0.001, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the absence of many possible relationships between the various researcher clusters. The longest collaboration chain diameter increased to 21 authors. The new authors were hence not adding to the interconnectedness of the network and fragile and long collaboration chains persisted.

From 2005 to 2009, the network evolved further and added 70% more authors (1325). About 80% of these new nodes (1111) linked with the main component, indicating that the strong growth of the discipline coincided with a good integration in this 5-year period. The average degree was growing slightly to four collaboration contacts per author in the core group (and 3 contacts in the periphery). The clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient (0.77) also remains stable on a high level, indicating relatively stable author groups. The diameter increased to 23 steps. Together with the low density (0.001) this evidences the continued development of a fragile chain-dominated network structure during the strong network growth (few shortcuts or redundant links; see Figure 2(c)).

From 2010 to 2014, the number of people that entered the KNI was similar to the preceding period (1299). The share of new authors that attached to the core group rose to more than 90% (1186) and less than 10% (113) attached to the periphery. Overall, the whole network accumulated 4477 nodes and 7554 links. The researchers’ average number of co-authors showed a marginal growth to 3.37 (4.26 in the centre), and thus constitutes a rather stable long-term property of the KNI, with the vast majority of authors not developing notable hubs. The stable and high clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.76 re<sup>fl</sup>ects that linked authors in the network tend to cluster together, yielding a more robust network with some group-internal redundancy of direct relations between authors’ contacts. Outside these interlinked clusters, however, the co-authorship network is remaining very sparse, indicated by the combination of a very low density (0.0008) and long chains that require many steps in the network to get from one author to another (diameter = 20; also see Figure 2(d)). Together with the increasing number of separate components (from 233 almost doubling to 453) these network measures point to a lateral fragmentation tendency outside the core clusters. In this peripheral zone, authors primarily work with very few contacts that either built longer chains without redundant paths (eg, when an author’s contact only worked with one further researcher, etc.), or established a small separated group.

On the other hand, the large core group built considerable gravity and is now uniting almost two-thirds of the whole author network (2849 authors) in a well interconnected network (6082 collaboration links). The growing core assimilated an increasing share (from 55% to 90%) of new researchers. The core’s stable average number of contacts per author, the central component’s large size, as well as the author’s increasing preference for attaching to the core component all suggest that the growing network’s lateral fragmentation tendencies at the periphery and the establishing chain structures are contrasted by a broad integration tendency in the large core.

The increasing importance of a main core and the simultaneously fragmenting periphery could further point to hierarchical fragmentation resulting from the formation of centralisation and elites. Looking at the degree distribution, the network exhibits a power-law distribution (curve <sup>fi</sup>t: $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 8 7 )$ . Following Barabási and Albert (1999), this suggests a preferential attachment pattern with centralisation tendencies, that is, the more popular authors are, the more likely they form collaboration relations with others.

While power-law distributions are fairly common in interdependent co-authorship networks (eg, Appendix A10), centralisation e<sup>f</sup>ects are further indicated by other network measures such as the strong correlation between the degree and betweenness centrality $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 4 , r = 0 . 8 )$ , highlighting that authors with most contacts also developed very central positions on other researcher’s networking paths. This suggests a hub and spoke architecture where a limited group of central prominent authors has a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the collaboration structures (compare Figure 2(c) for a visual illustration). Such a pattern can be considered as a barrier for the whole network as the established stars in the network are crucial for connecting the network, ideas are less likely to spread over the entire network, and theoretical integration is reduced (Moody, 2004, p.217).

The formation of a dominant central elite is further evident if prominent researchers collaborate mostly with other central authors in an internally focused group. To examine this, we measure the share of a person’s links to other prominent and central coauthorship contacts with eigenvector centrality and compare the resulting author ranking with the author betweenness centrality ranking (see the table in Appendix A3). This approach reveals if the most prominent people also primarily link to each other and thus form an elite. We found only 2 coincidences between betweenness and eigenvector centrality rankings for the 50 ranked authors. This implies that even though the best performing authors are very central to the network, they are not all primarily connected to the other central people in the network. The lack of overlap in the two centrality rankings further reveals that IS research seems to be shaped by two diferent types of central academics, integrators and elite-forming researchers. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type, integrators, relates to prominent but not eigenvector-central co-authors who formed many connections to people from the periphery and thus created opportunities for less well embedded authors to enter the core component (ie, the main collaboration structure). These integrators are conducive for coherent academic production in a dynamically evolving and growing network. As a second type of important core researchers, we <sup>fi</sup>nd elite-forming actors. These individuals did not accumulate the largest number of contacts but their high eigenvector centrality indicates that these researchers are very well connected to other senior and central people (also compare Appendix A3). This second collaboration pattern is relating to the Matthew e<sup>f</sup>ect (Merton, 1968), that is, the formation of a smaller elite group focusing on internal interactions and increasing hierarchical fragmentation. However, in the IS research community, this elite-forming subgroup’s potential fragmentation in<sup>fl</sup>uence is balanced by the outreach of the <sup>fi</sup>rst group of central integrators.

We further note that the top 10 authors in the di<sup>f</sup>erent centrality rankings appear in the top ranks for about 10 years before other researchers take over in the ranking (Appendix A3). This is suggesting a long but limited life span of central positions and a corresponding slow long term change at the core, as the central nodes are continuously replaced by new researchers. As in a network with a power-law distribution a few central actors have an over-proportional importance, such a rejuvenating core balances out prospective hierarchical fragmentation of the social dimension of the IS discipline.

## 4.2. Institutional level co-authorship analysis

We further studied the collaboration patterns on the organisational level. Overall, 7397 institutions have been retrieved from the WoS database of which 3563 were unique (the large reduction results from many di<sup>f</sup>erent spellings and infrequent use of abbreviations). Appendix A4 shows the heat map of the largest component with n = 1144 institutions). The resulting network is sparse with a low density (0.005) and the diameter of 10 steps is shorter (more e<sup>fi</sup>cient and robust) than on the actor level. Together with the average of 6.4 connected partner institutions (ie, average degree), the network is better integrated on the institutional level than on the actor level (average degree = 4.26). The average clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.40 further suggests that the institutions generally tend to build stable but not too internally dense clusters in an otherwise sparse network. Together, these properties point to only a small lateral fragmentation tendency and reveal the potential structural contribution of institutions for integrating the discipline.

When looking at the hierarchy formation in the network, the top 20 institutions show a large overlap in terms of degree, betweenness and eigenvector centrality rankings (see Appendix A5 for the full list). A total of 15 of the 20 top institutions with the most collaboration contacts (degree) also primarily link to other central institutions, leading to a high eigenvector centrality. This overlap suggests hierarchical fragmentation tendency with an emerging group of central active organisations that build the backbone in the institutional network of research collaboration. We further <sup>fi</sup>nd that the institution’s degree shows a power-law distribution. This supportive evidence further corroborates a hierarchical fragmentation and a centralising elite of core institutions.

## 5. Fragmentation analysis of the intellectual dimension

Next to studying the lateral and hierarchical fragmentation of the collaborative production of journal contributions at two di<sup>f</sup>erent levels, we investigated the fragmentation of the academic output, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the intellectual dimension of IS research (compare the overview in Table 1). In contrast to the collaborative dimension, this part of the analysis involves a more diverse combination of topical analysis, co-citation analysis (indicating thematic relationships among cited sources), and citation analysis.

## 5.1. Bursting topics and topic evolution analysis

For our topical analysis, we <sup>fi</sup>rst apply the keyword burst method (Kleinberg, 2003). It helps to shed more light on the dynamics of di<sup>f</sup>erent thematic clusters of the discourse, which are not surfacing in previous cumulative analyses of extended periods. The bursting topics in the titles of the articles from 1995 to 2014 are shown in Table 3. In this method, topics are represented by meaningful word stems. For example, “evalu-” summarises the occurrences of “evalu-ation”, “evalu-ate”, and other variations (for better reading an example ending is provided in the bracket in Table 3).

A closer examination of the bursting topics reveals the evolution of main themes of IS research, starting from the mid-1990 when “EDI” and “systems reengineering” were the main research focus. The latest observation period from 2010 to 2014 features the bursting topics of “social media”, “open”, “networks”, “virtual world”, or “multimedia”. The topic of “ERP” has the longest burst (2000–2006) in the observation period. Beyond the arbitrary and large time windows of past topic studies, our analysis reveals a pattern of many short topic bursts, that often relate to technologica innovations (eg, “social”/“media”, “virtual”/“world”, “open” (technologies), “website”, “recommender” (systems), (IT) “security”, (IT) “standardization”, “internet”/“network”, “mobile” (devices), “computing”, “erp”, (e-) ”banking”, “group” (-ware), “expert”/”system”, etc.). These bursts demonstrate that the IS research discourse is exposed to a fragmentising in<sup>fl</sup>uence of fast-paced technological developments.

A total of 9265 keywords (2.58 keywords per article) were assigned by the authors of the studied journal articles, pointing out a very large breadth of diverse topics in the IS research <sup>fi</sup>eld. In the network of the top 500 co-occurring keywords (Figure 3), the term “case study” most frequently co-occurred with “IS implementation” and “information technology”, “Trust” co-occurred with “electronic commerce/business”, “virtual teams”, and “privacy”. “Knowledge management” co-occurred with “knowledge sharing”, “social capital”, and “collaboration”.

While in Figure 3, the top keywords appear very integrated, the density of the top 100 keywords is showing only minor <sup>fl</sup>uctuations on a low level and a small decline in the last period (top 100 keywords density: 1995–1999: 0.077; 2000–2004: 0.061; 2005– 2009: 0.099; 2010–2014: 0.093). Additionally, the network’s weak tie strength of 1 for 97% of the links shows that the majority of the ca. 42,000 keyword combinations appear in only one paper. These results stand in contrast to the density increase of ca. 0.04 reported in the study of top thematic clusters (Liu et al., 2016), suggesting that the reported growth of coherence may be not representative for the whole domain but is limited to a few central topic clusters (also note Appendix A6).

When comparing the co-occurrence network with the temporal burst analysis, we further note that in contrast to the burst analysis, the co-occurrence network centre contains many conceptual terms and few technology terms (except very big themes such as web2.0), suggesting that the conceptual terms gain their weight and embeddedness over longer periods while fast-paced new technological in<sup>fl</sup>uences are not getting well-embedded in the network. Further, technology keywords are not very frequently related to each other.

## 5.2. Further fragmentation e<sup>f</sup>ects in brief

Due to space limitations, we just brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarise the other network views of the KNI that add to our fragmentation pro<sup>fi</sup>le (compare Table 1). The pattern of the journal-keyword afiliation network reveals that keywords are in about 9 out of 10 cases not used in more than one journal. The fragmentation of the periphery is thus not reduced on the journal level.

The analysis of the journal co-citation network helps to investigate, whether the low topic interdependence leads to a fragmentation of the evolving discourse (see Appendix A7). The network shows an alternating mix of basket journals and several other journals from neighbour disciplines (eg, C. ACM, M. Sci, Org. Sci, etc.) that is well interconnected with a relatively high density (0.22), a high average clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient (0.59), and a very short diameter (3). This points to a highly integrated discourse without lateral fragmentation on the journal level: authors draw from various journals in each of their publications without forming any special clusters. However, there are hierarchical fragmentation tendencies, as the journals’ eigenvector centrality ranking is largely overlapping with the betweenness ranking, indicating a centralising discourse concentrated in the prominent core journals. The network’s power-law distribution $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 9 7 )$ , further supports the view of a strong long-term elite-formation e<sup>f</sup>ect (Barabási & Albert, 1999), in which the core journals with many co-citations increasingly take the researchers’ full attention, manifest their position and marginalise more peripheral journals.

The citation network analysis (see Appendices A8 and A9 for more information) suggests that a large number of articles is cited and therefore many articles are well integrated into the IS research discourse. Nonetheless, linking article citation data with the co-authorship network, shows that the more central authors are, the more citations they receive (compare Figure 2(d), where node size represents the number of citations). The discourse thus shows hierarchical fragmentation tendencies, where cited authors move to the centre, get most attention and by that collect even more citations (Merton, 1968). ## 6. Discussion and implications

Based on the overview about our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the di<sup>f</sup>erent KNI network views in Table 5, we will now <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss in more detail the social dimension of the underlying theory of science (Kuhn, 1970; Whitley, 1984) and its fragmentation tendencies (RQ 1), followed by the intellectual dimension and its fragmentation tendencies (RQ 2). We then re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the abstract implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the discipline and derive helpful recommendations for advancing the structural interdependence of the discipline (Whitley, 1984).

## 6.1. Lateral and hierarchical fragmentation of the social dimension

With regards to the degree of lateral fragmentation of the social dimension of IS research (RQ1), our network data and visualisations (Figure 2) reveal very long paths that result in a chain-like structure of the co-authorship network. This pattern re<sup>fl</sup>ects a low density and almost no redundancy and illustrates how few research connections individual researchers develop. Together with the increasing number of small groups in a larger peripheral zone (compare Figure 2(d)), this underlines the fragile nature of the co-authorship structure and the critical relevance of each established collaboration tie for the discipline’s interdependence. Similar patterns found in studies of IS conference communities (Trier & Molka-Danielsen, 2013; Vidgen et al., 2007) suggest that IS collaboration networks are not developing the “shortcuts” among otherwise distant authors that characterise the small-world property usually associated with large social networks (Watts, 1999; see Appendix A1 for a de<sup>fi</sup>nition). Our network analysis further reveals a high local clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of the IS researcher network, which together with the low network density and the increase of unconnected smaller components implies that many author groups are in an internal clustering process with a limited attention to ties outside their local neighbourhoods. While previous research reported this increasing density inside selected author clusters as increasing coherence of the discipline (Xu et al., 2014), our extended analysis beyond such small focal groups accentuates that the overall sparse academic collaboration structure faces the inherent risk of ongoing lateral fragmentation between more peripheral co-authorship groups.

We also <sup>fi</sup>nd, however, integrative processes that mitigate the above fragmentation tendencies. For example, similar to Xu et al. (2014), we note a small increase of the average number of the academics with whom a researcher collaborates (degree). This is a positive achievement as theoretical network studies suggest that the average degree is likely to remain constant as a network grows (Barabási et al., 2002). Another balancing e<sup>f</sup>ect that alleviates lateral fragmentation risks of the community is the growth of the largest connected component of the co-authorship network to include about two-thirds (63.64%) of all publishing researchers. After an initial period of high growth in the periphery, the core hence developed a large and well-integrated structure, which is also supported by the average degree that is slightly higher within the largest connected component than outside it. The achievement of a large core component of network actors is further noted in other studies of IS conference community patterns (Vidgen et al., 2007; Xu & Chau, 2006; Trier & Molka-Danielsen, 2013; compare Appendix A10) and of IS journals (Xu et al., 2014).

Beyond previous reports that categorised centrality increases and structural holes as an improvement (Xu et al., 2014), our grounding in network theory calls for a more critical assessment of such centralisation tendencies of the academic community. We address this issue by assessing the hierarchical fragmentation of the networks. In line with Xu et al. (2014), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the degree distribution shows power-law properties (see Appendix A1 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions). Following Barabási and Albert (1999), the emergence of this typical network distribution suggests a process where new researchers and collaboration links were preferentially attaching to the authors that are already well-linked. This creates centralisation e<sup>f</sup>ects that point to hierarchical fragmentation challenges (Merton, 1968). Beyond previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the low correlation of the researchers betweenness and eigenvector centrality reveals that the collaboration structures are marked by two types of central academics. While one group is primarily networked with other central researchers, thereby increasing the centralisation risks via manifesting elites, the second group of prominent but not eigenvector-central integrators plays an important role by reaching out and integrating less well connected researchers. Next to these brokers, a further dynamic that limits hierarchical fragmentation tendencies in the long run is that most central actors maintain their core position just for a limited period of about 10 years before new actors take these positions, indicating a continuous slow change and rejuvenation at the core of IS productivity.

Beyond the author level, we additionally assessed the role of research institutions. We noted that the network properties of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld do not simply translate to this more aggregated level. Compared to the author networks, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a better integration with shorter paths and higher degrees among institutions, leading to a low risk of lateral fragmentation. Apparently, the researchers of an institution complement each other’s personal networks well and as a group hence attain a stronger embeddedness, suggesting a network bene<sup>fi</sup>t of larger academic institutions. However, our framework of two opposing fragmentation forces also reveals via the eigenvector centrality analysis that the higher cohesion resulted in a central core and decreasing connections to outside institutions, which points to hierarchical fragmentation tendencies. Linking this to the noted integrative processes at the author level, it appears to be important for new authors (such as PhD candidates) to enter the discipline via a central IS research institution with central academics, as the noted integration e<sup>f</sup>ects do not transcend beyond the author level. For IS researchers, thus, provenience matters.

In summarizing the results on the social dimension, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the notion of social interdependence (Whitley, 1984) or fragmentation is going beyond a general degree or level. It is better understood as a complex interwoven process with various concurrent fragmentation and integration tendencies at di<sup>f</sup>erent levels. The fragility of the researcher network is most strongly noted in the large peripheral zone of the network. Integrative central actors and a slow rejuvenation at the core, however, suggest that the social network is balancing the fragmentation risks and acts as the engine of a dynamically evolving IS discipline.

## 6.2. Lateral and hierarchical fragmentation of the intellectual dimension

Our second RQ concerns the fragmentation of the intellectual dimension of the IS discipline, that is, the resulting research topics published in the basket journal outlets. While aspects of lateral fragmentation of research topics have been explored extensively (Bernroider et al., 2013; Larsen & Levine, 2005; Liu et al., 2016; Sidorova et al., 2008; Taylor et al., 2010), we contribute to a more nuanced fragmentation analysis by adding novel insights about topic bursts and the interplay of conceptual and technological keywords. We further extend the view beyond the frequently studied top keyword clusters by identifying di<sup>f</sup>erences between the core and the vast periphery of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and by elaborating on potential restrictions through hierarchisation tendencies.

The keyword co-occurrence analysis emphasises the discipline’s large topical diversity (also compare Robey, 1996) with over 9000 adopted keywords that formed a fragile network structure with a low density and long chains. The vast majority of keyword combinations occur only in a single article, implying that even the constituted network links are mainly weak ties.

Beyond the coarse time window comparisons in previous studies, the keyword burst analysis further surfaced a fast-paced dynamic, where the fragile IS discourse is exposed to many short topic bursts that frequently relate to terms that reflect emerging technologies. The rankings of the most prominent keywords across di<sup>f</sup>erent time periods did not indicate a dominant role of theoretical concepts, supporting the assumption of a strong influence of short-lived technology waves (also noted in Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). However, a comparison of co-occurrence and burst results shows that conceptual keywords attain more stable central positions in the cumulative cooccurrence network, indicating that theoretical terms attain embedded positions and gain centrality over longer time periods despite the topical dynamics.

Taken together, these results augment previous research on core topic clusters over time (Sidorova et al., 2008; Taylor et al., 2010) by suggesting two di<sup>f</sup>erent zones of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, a central core with many conceptual keywords and a more dynamic periphery with terms that are only weakly interrelated and are subject to the noted topical bursts. The centrality of the core conceptual terms is primarily resulting from linking with other core conceptual terms, while the fast-paced periphery with many technology-related keywords is subject to lateral fragmentation processes.

The di<sup>f</sup>erences between the core and the periphery also surfaced in our assessment of hierarchical fragmentation. The analysis of the complete keyword network revealed a power-law distribution (also noted in Liu et al., 2016), suggesting that only few popular keywords are frequently used by follow-on studies (Choi et al., 2011). The very low density and high clustering coe<sup>fi</sup>cient implies that dense clusters of closely related keywords built few external links to other more remote terms. While various smaller centres of increased coherence may have been found in previous studies on top keywords (eg, Liu et al., 2016), our core-periphery analysis suggests that these increases only develop inside the core clusters. Beyond their narrow boundaries, large peripheral regions are subject to fragmentation as the majority of the IS keywords is not getting well connected (low density, weak ties). This core-periphery diferentiation points to an ongoing hierarchical fragmentation tendency with an internally densifying core and a sparse dynamic periphery. This pattern may have the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect that several conceptual keywords can mitigate fragmentation via binding selected technological concepts to the core (in line with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Taylor et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2016). However, the hierarchisation e<sup>f</sup>ects and the prominence of a few manifesting core concepts also points to the risk of a Matthew e<sup>f</sup>ect (Merton, 1968), where much attention is on the prominent terms while relevant new phenomena are not optimally recognised and integrated into a dynamically evolving discourse.

When looking at other network views of the KNI, we note that the diverse and fragmenting keyword landscape is not getting more interconnected at the journal level, where it is rare that keywords di<sup>f</sup>use beyond a single journal. The analysis surfaced strong centralisation e<sup>f</sup>ects with an increasing inward focus of the 8 basket journal outlets that can marginalise outlets outside the core (including new outlets). While this situation can contribute to narrowing down the attended topical landscape, it also implies less attention to outside developments. We further <sup>fi</sup>nd a hierarchisation of prominent authors who, in line with Merton’s (1968) Matthew e<sup>f</sup>ect, attract most citation attention. Together with their noted active role in integrating the social dimension of the discipline, this shows how strongly the trajectory of the IS discipline is dependent on a few prominent senior actors, who’s orientations and contributions become critical in ensuring a diverse and dynamically evolving discourse (Robey, 1996) that develops or even replaces stable theories in the face of the constantly changing stream of new technological concepts.

In summarising the results on the intellectual dimension (Whitley, 1984) of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, we note again how the evaluation of interdependency reveals various concurrent processes. The di<sup>f</sup>erentiation into lateral and hierarchical fragmentation helped to recognise the contrast between the laterally fragmentising periphery with its dynamic topical bursts and the narrow centralising core that shows small increases of internal coherence, but also causes hierarchical fragmentation that can marginalise the periphery. Future research can target at a more detailed core-periphery analysis that further disentangles applied technological themes from abstract conceptual themes and explores the interactions between the periphery and the core. In particular, this invites more critical assessments of challenges for existing concepts arising from changes in the technological environment.

On a more general level, our study contributes to the emerging research stream that strives to understand the IS knowledge infrastructure from a network perspective (eg, Liu et al., 2016; Trier & Molka-Danielsen, 2013; Xu & Chau, 2006). While previous research considered only individual facets of the research process (either actors or topics), the multidimensional KNI analysis approach addresses the interrelated intellectual and social dimensions of the theory of science (Banville & Landry, 1989; Kuhn, 1970; Whitley, 1984). The various related network views provided a comprehensive re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the discipline covering the whole academic work cycle with its collaborative production of IS research contributions that are then published in interconnected journal outlets and gets integrated into the evolving IS discourse through citations in further collaborations.

Through grounding the assessment of fragmentation and interdependency in a network theoretical conceptualisation we recognised that centrality needs to be considered as an opposite of density (Moody, 2004). In contrast to previous IS studies that reported increases in centrality as a positive change, the theoretical foundation emphasises negative implications of centralisation and hierarchy formation (Merton, 1968), where strong but inward-looking interdependence in subgroups can imply unsustainable elite formation in a research discipline. The methodological recti<sup>fi</sup>cations proposed in this article are particularly relevant, as the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is conceived as a rapidly evolving discourse with changing core zones of coherence (Larsen & Levine, 2005), an adaptive identity (Robey, 2003), and a moving target in a changing environment (Bryant, 2008).

The multifaceted results surface that coherence of a research <sup>fi</sup>eld pertains to complex concurrent processes and fragmentation risks that are not necessarily aggregating into one generic measure and degree. Our conceptualisation of two opposed fragmentation types elucidates in more detail to what extent the IS discipline achieves active and ongoing balancing processes between lateral dissolution tendencies (lateral fragmentation) in the KNI network periphery and manifesting hierarchies and elites at the network core (hierarchical fragmentation) that restrain the important accommodation processes of new in<sup>fl</sup>uences in the rapidly evolving and dynamic IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Bryant, 2008; Merton, 1968). In this context, our study showed that previous reports of growing coherence apply to top clusters in the core, while the fragmentation in the large remainder of the network is often not su<sup>fi</sup>ciently addressed. The analysis suggests that we should consider the core and the periphery as two di<sup>f</sup>erent but intertwined zones, that each play an important role in the evolving the IS discipline. Studying the core helps to shape a shared (ex-post) identity, while we also need to understand how new subjects that emerge in the periphery get accommodated. Future research could explore in more detail how fragmentation in the periphery perhaps constitutes an evolutionary process for the dynamic IS discipline and its special research interest in the moving target of technological innovations.

## 6.3. Deriving recommendations

With the increased awareness of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s inner workings and more detailed insights about the di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of balance or fragmentation, we can now derive several implications and recommendations that appear helpful for the ongoing development and sustenance of the dynamic IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

In line with Trier and Molka-Danielsen (2013), our results emphasise the critical role of each established collaboration link between researchers. It represents a very exclusive interaction encounter that contributes to the overall collaboration network as a valuable longterm asset that the IS community requires to attain interdependence and react coherently to the special topical dynamics of the discipline. With the awareness of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s very fragile network structure and the tendency to maintain internally dense clusters, a resulting recommendation is that the IS community can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from increasing social interdependence by developing a more pro-active stance on incentivising and encouraging a layer of research ties among previously unrelated researchers (also note Robey, 1996). New links between experts could be supported by positioning more cross-thematic (grand) challenges subjects in conference tracks or special journal issues, for example, on multi-level research, comparisons of multiple perspectives on one subject, discussing conceptual links between di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies, or facilitating conceptual synthesis through debates or curations. Such activities should particularly focus on integrating less central actors in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (eg, young researchers).

Further, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings point to the need to better acknowledge the important role of brokers that limit hierarchical fragmentation through their involvement in diverse collaborations that reach out to the periphery but also broker ties between their contacts (also note the related concept of “brokering activity” introduced in Trier & Bobrik, 2007). In this context, IS departments were found to be more interconnected than the authors, suggesting an important (invisible) bonding role of local institutions that host several IS researchers. The important brokering role of departments should re<sup>fl</sup>ect in explicit strategies that leverage their group size. The current “inward-looking” preference for other central institutions could be augmented with more activities that reach out to interesting peripheral institutions that are currently not so well-established in the IS community. This process could be triggered through global and intercultural studies, or the attempt to explore new local themes of general interest. Publication outlets (including conferences) could encourage such behaviours through emphasising political, cross-cultural, or global IS aspects in their tracks, or by supporting researcher exchanges.

All these integration e<sup>f</sup>orts in the social dimension create most value when they are oriented towards catalysing the abundance of topical changes and in<sup>fl</sup>uences that we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the intellectual dimension of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Whitley, 1984). Here, the discipline should seek constructive ways of attaining a more interdependent topical periphery with a more critical process of topical selection. For that, the community organs could encourage research e<sup>f</sup>orts (eg, by senior researchers) that make sense of general long-term patterns, waves, or connections in the fast-paced topic <sup>fl</sup>ow in order to elucidate more abstract underlying phenomena. A more pronounced debate culture may also foster the synthesis of related or alternative concepts. Conference organisers who determine new track proposals could, as gatekeepers and catalysts, exercise more strategic selection and a deeper integration of new topics with a likely impact on theories into the existing IS discourse.

Further, the emphasis should be on ensuring the permeability and adaptability of the topical core zone rather than aspiring a stable manifestation of permanent themes (such a discourse view is further elaborated in Bryant, 2008). This involves balancing the di<sup>f</sup>erent dynamics of established conceptual and transient technological topics. The long-term centralisation of certain conceptual core terms should also be critically assessed as it can point to a hierarchical fragmentation challenge that can limit the absorption and theorisation of novel developments. Further, the process of integrating emerging subjects should not encourage a simple application of established theory to yet another technological domain, as this would also lead to further manifestation of traditional topics (and theoretical lenses) without much theoretical advancement. One approach to cope with the changing technological environment could be to encourage more critical contributions that challenge and debate the continued relevance of traditional theoretical explanations in the changed environment.

## 7. Limitations

Before we conclude, we want to point out potential limitations of our study. Despite the focus on the most relevant publication outlets, we only studied the eight key IS journals, while the IS domain is a vibrant community with many further journals and conferences and also tightly interwoven with neigh bour disciplines. While we do not explicitly consider the other outlets in all views, we included them in our journal co-citation network heat map (Appendix A7). We further compared our results to studies of conferences (Appendix A10). While the adopted network analysis methodology helped us to attain a systematic account of the structures of the disciplines across di<sup>f</sup>erent views, it is just one possible view on the discourse, which may attenuate facets that other methods amplify. Further, caution should be exercised while generalising the results, as network measures are highly aggregated and may not reveal special individual contexts of the authors or their topical focus. Solo-authored articles are further not shown in co-authorship networks, but our other views on the discourse consider such contributions in alternative ways. Finally, the longitudinal dataset naturally involves also papers and outlets that only join the discourse at a later stage or towards the end, which gives these elements less time and chance to move to a central position in many of the views, that is, network embeddedness is essentially also a function of time. We address this concern by making use of smaller time windows and burst analysis, where applicable. Finally, due to space restrictions this article does not contain network analysis on the country level, but this will be part of future research.

## 8. Conclusion

In this article, we introduced a comprehensive conceptual framework and a related method for re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the progress of IS research published in the AIS basket journals. Our focus on the balance between two opposing types of lateral and hierarchical fragmentation is better aligned with network theory and helps to address the dynamics of the rapidly evolving and growing IS discourse (Bryant, 2008). Beyond previous research, the presented study systematically covers the full cyclic academic process (collaboration, publication and citation) and integrates the previously partial views on social collaboration and the resulting intellectual outputs. This better corresponds with the underlying theory of science that emphasises that a discourse is created by a community (Banville & Landry, 1989; Kuhn, 1970; Whitley, 1984).

Our research allowed us to advance beyond static and broad classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the discipline (eg, Banville & Landry, 1989) by elucidating the dynamic network mechanisms that balance or unbalance the IS discipline in the various network dimensions. From our detailed analysis above, we conclude that the IS community of collaborating authors has evolved to an important asset (also note Desanctis, 2003) that helps to cope with the continuous fragmentation challenges of a very dynamic discipline. This ampli<sup>fi</sup>es that instead of searching for a persistent thematic identity in an ever-changing topic <sup>fi</sup>eld, we should engage in better understanding the important social identity and role of the IS researcher community, which emerges as the motor of the discipline. Elaborating the social dynamics of the <sup>fi</sup>eld may eventually also help to transform the challenge of fragmentation in the periphery into an innovative scienti<sup>fi</sup>c process that e<sup>f</sup>ectively accommodates fast technological changes into a sustainably evolving IS discipline.

Appendix A1. Glossary of terms.

<table><tr><td>Number</td><td>Term (Domain)</td><td>Brief explanation</td></tr><tr><td>1</td><td>Degree Centrality</td><td>The number of links a node (eg, an author, an institution, a keyword) has to other nodes.</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td>Betweenness Centrality</td><td>The centrality of a node in a network. It measures the extent to which a node lies on paths between other notes in a network.</td></tr><tr><td>3</td><td>Eigenvector Centrality</td><td>The importance of a node in a network based on the number of connections it has to other nodes, weighted by the centrality of these neighbour nodes. An illustration of the computation approach is shown here: http://djjr-courses.wikidot.com/soc180:eigenvector-centrality</td></tr><tr><td>4</td><td>Density</td><td>The ratio of actual links to the number of all possible (or potential) links in a network. A fully connected network has a density of 1.</td></tr><tr><td>5</td><td>Diameter</td><td>Largest shortest (most efficient) connection between any two nodes, indicating the maximum of steps it takes in the network to get from node to another</td></tr><tr><td>6</td><td>Local clustering coefficient</td><td>The degree to which nodes in the network of a focal node tend to cluster together (ie, have direct relationships with each other).</td></tr><tr><td>7</td><td>Small World Property</td><td>A network has a small-world property if it has a high clustering coefficient and a small characteristic path length.</td></tr><tr><td>8</td><td>Heat Map</td><td>A network heat map is a graphical representation of the network where the individual values contained in a matrix are represented as colours/shades, resulting in brighter hues for zones with higher levels of interdependence.</td></tr><tr><td>9</td><td>Burst Analysis</td><td>A burst is a period of increased activity and burst analysis is used to identify sudden increases in the frequency of words in a text corpus (eg, articles, abstract, keywords, and titles).</td></tr><tr><td>10</td><td>Power-Law Distribution</td><td>A scale-free network distribution where numerous small-degree nodes coexist with a few highly connected hubs. This structure results from a rich-get-richer preferential attachment process, where new nodes prefer to connect with nodes with a large contact base.</td></tr><tr><td>11</td><td>Citation Analysis</td><td>Analysis of the number of times that author, article, or publication has been cited by other works, as a measure of impact.</td></tr><tr><td>12</td><td>Co-citation Analysis</td><td>Analysis of the frequency (tie strength) with which two articles (nodes) are cited together in the reference section of other articles, as a measure of similarity.</td></tr><tr><td>13</td><td>Lateral Fragmentation</td><td>Low levels of interdependence between topics or academics (eg, in a pattern with disconnected clusters), measured with density levels, number of disconnected groups, tie strengths</td></tr><tr><td>14</td><td>Hierarchical Fragmentation</td><td>A low interdependence between a dominant network core and a marginalised network periphery, assessed via the existence of preferential attachment to core nodes and differences in the degree, group size, or tie strength between core and peripheral nodes.</td></tr><tr><td>15</td><td>Inter-connectedness</td><td>General condition that nodes in a network are reachable via stable, redundant (density) and short path lengths in a dense network structure.</td></tr></table>
