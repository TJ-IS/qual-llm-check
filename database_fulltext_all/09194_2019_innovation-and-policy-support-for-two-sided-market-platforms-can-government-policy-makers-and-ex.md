Abstract. Information technology (IT) can transform societies, governments, and businesses alike. Technological innovation requires difficult but critically important strategic decisions. Making good decisions becomes even more challenging for platforms tightly linked to network externalities that are inherent in two-sided markets. Inspired by the driverless car industry, which is now receiving green signals from available technology and government policies, this study introduces a model of competing IT platforms tha use a complementary physical medium. The aim is to understand the possible incentives for technological innovation to inform decision makers from both industry and govern ment. Although the existing literature on two-sided markets focuses on platform pricing strategies for network expansion, it pays little attention to decisions that involve high levels of innovation. For this reason, the study investigates how platforms can strike a balance between these two decision alternatives, namely price and improved quality from innovation. This study examines the conditions under which platforms receive incentives to innovate in the presence of network externalities. We also examine how equilibrium is influenced by technological asymmetry between the competing platforms, and consumers and service providers’ multihoming (i.e., choosing between multiple options) behavior We extend focus to a government policy perspective by studying how to subsidize in novations. Three different policies are considered: (1) subsidies for consumers, (2) subsidies for service providers, and (3) innovation-based subsidies for platforms. The scholarly and practical implications of our findings are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Technological innovations can transform economies, both industrialized and developing (Meso et al. 2009). Government policy makers regard information technology (IT) innovation as an important area for enlightened regulation but often, are at a loss to know which policies are best for economic development (Claffy and Clark 2014). C-level corporate executives, however, can decide how much to invest in innovations vis-a-vis rivals and where to set their price\` points, but it is often not clear a priori what outcomes would emerge from such strategic policies.

These issues are perhaps more evident in two-sided markets, where values are generated through interactions between two distinct groups of users, such as consumers and service/software providers. In this type of market, an intermediary termed a “platform” enables these groups of users to interact and transact business and provides everything that users require, such as hardware, software, after-sales support, and even protocols (Eisenmann 2007). Many modern IT businesses share platform characteristics, including intermediaries like Google, Expedia, and Airbnb as well as social networking services like Facebook and Twitter. These internet-based platforms prioritize net work expansion to achieve momentum and thereby, fully utilize network externalities over a long period because of the nature of digital products that can be easily fixed and improved through updates and patches (Arora et al. 2006). Presently, most academic studies offer guidance for strategic growth while paying little attention to the quality side of the value proposition. However, unlike purely digital platforms that offer an online playground for users, other platform businesses form an ecosystem through a physical medium. Essentially, the subject of this study is such an ecosystem.

In the literature on strategic management, this kind of product is formed by “complementary” products, where complementarity means that these products are valuable only when both are present and offered as a package to customers (Wernerfelt 1984, Barney 1991). Together, they are operationally effective and create a competitive advantage for all of the partners (Ross et al. 1996, Melville et al. 2004, Bhatt and Grove 2005). The extension to resource-based view (RBV) theory, which takes complementarities into account, is known as the RBV complementarity perspective, and it has resulted in several important contributions related to cospecialization in the information systems (IS) literature (Clemons and Row 1991, Benjamin and Levinson 1993, Melville et al. 2004).

An extremely interesting and emerging example is the driverless car (more broadly, vehicle), which is a physical platform that depends on IT coupled to the physical parts as the medium of delivery: that is, a metal-based automobile or any other critical hardware, such as sensors. After driverless cars are fully commercialized, passengers of driverless cars, one side of the two-sided market, will enjoy a variety of in-car activities or services that are not available in traditional cars. For example, passengers could enjoy movies and music, play games, rest, and be involved in activities similar to in-flight entertainment. Diversity in service/software provider offerings (the other side of the two-sided market) could cast a long shadow on consumers’ decisions, because service/ software providers need to warrant a sufficient size of installed base before users get on board, forming the conventional “chicken-and-egg” dilemma for the driverless car ecosystem. Thus, the driverless car technology manifests platform characteristics that form its ecosystem with passengers (consumers) on one side of the market and service/software providers<sup>1</sup> on the other side.

In this context, it is important to note that the conventional strategy for digital platforms (namely, network expansion first followed by later quality enhancement) may not be applicable to the driverless car ecosystem. Compared with the pure digital platform cases, the quality (that is, how well and safely the driverless car functions as intended) is critical for consumers who want to adopt the innovative technology. The reason for this is that failures in driverless car quality can lead to fatal outcomes, such as serious injury or death.

Meanwhile, it is clear that firms possess different levels of knowledge and technological capabilities as argued by the advocates of RBV (Barney 1991). In reality, competition is observed between firms at different levels of technological capability. The emergent and dynamic real-world driverless car industry is no exception. Waymo and Cruise, the driverless car business units of Google and General Motors, respectively, have been competing head to head.<sup>2</sup> Waymo is believed to lead, having the most advanced driverless car technology after Google unveiled its driverless car project in October 2010 (Perkins 2018). Google has recorded the most driven miles on public roads while reporting the fewest accidents. Cruise, the biggest competitor, is closely behind Waymo, and both firms are now signaling that their driverless cars are getting closer to the commercialization stage for a robot taxi service (Welch and Bergen 2018).

Broadly, a firm desiring a higher market share should pursue technologically superior products, because consumers are attracted to quality. Existing studies on product quality have stressed the strategic intent of quality improvements (Garvin 1984, Flynn et al. 1995, Nair 2006). However, quality improvements implemented through elaborate innovation processes can lead to massive additional expenditure. In such a case, price levels may be under pressure despite the expected benefits of innovation during the time when it is still a problem for the firm to grow its ecosystem. Thus, at times, policy makers<sup>3</sup> must intervene in the market by promoting innovative products or technologies delivered through innovation.

Inspired by the emerging driverless car industry, this study focuses on IT innovations that involve platforms combined with a physical medium and aims to understand if the platforms have an incentive to create better quality while simultaneously pursuing growth. In such a case, it is possible to model optimal levels of platform quality through innovation and price. Therefore, this study investigates the equilibrium behavior of the competing platforms via the characteristic of technological asymmetry, which is done to capture the difference in technological capabilities between platforms.

The study further explores the implications of policy alternatives by comparing three different types of subsidy policies: (1) subsidies for consumers, (2) subsidies for service providers, and (3) innovation-based subsidies for platforms. These three policy options have potential for adoption. For example, the United States, China, the United Kingdom, and many other countries are planning to subsidize self-driving car manufacturers and/or consumers. In January 2016, the U.S. administration proposed to invest \$4 billion to subsidize driverless cars (Spector and Ramsey 2016, Street 2016). China is also considering providing subsidy to consumers of driverless cars in the form of tax incentives (Bloomberg News 2016). In this emerging context, this study offers policy makers meaningful insights into optimal policies for the future driverless car market. In fact, platforms, policy makers, and executives can use these findings as a guideline to optimize investment decisions and policy choices.<sup>4</sup>

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows. The next section presents the literature review followed by the analytical model. Then, the results of policy comparison are presented. A numerical study is included to illustrate the efficiency of different policies. Finally, the study contributions are discussed, and avenues for future research are presented.

## 2. Literature Review

Two-sided markets have two distinct groups of users as well as an intermediary. This intermediary is called a platform. These platforms facilitate interactions between market players. Moreover, a user’s utility of joining the platform depends on the size of the other group (Evans 2003, Rochet and Tirole 2008, Rysman 2009), a concept that is classically modeled by the five competitive forces model of Porter (2008). Because users connect through the platform, decisions about its operations affect the equilibrium manifest in platform competition.

Numerous research streams are concerned with optimal pricing in two-sided markets. Rochet and Tirole (2008) studied platform fee policies, showing that the optimal fee in a two-sided market is not determined by elasticity of demand or marginal cost. Armstrong (2006) explored platform-pricing strategies and concluded that platforms charge a higher price to the group that enjoys more benefits. Focusing on cross-sided network externality, he found that this subsidization is mitigated when the platform implements a per-transaction fee policy. He further argued that platforms extract more profit from a group that participates in multiple platforms (multihoming) when the other group joins only one platform (single-homing). Choi (2010) extended this model by analyzing the effect of “tying,” which has also been investigated and proposed by Rochet and Tirole (2008) and Amelio and Jullien (2012). All of these scholars offered indirect ways of rebalancing demand in the two-sided market. Choi (2010) found that tying reduces social welfare in the absence of multihoming, whereas it can be welfare enhancing in platform competition when all of the users are provided options to multihome.

Although it is clear from Sethi (2000), Tellis and Johnson (2007), and several other studies that strategic thinking about technological innovation significantly influences firm success in the market, relatively little research has been done on quality issues, such as the degree of technological innovation, despite its eminently practical value. Lin et al. (2011) raised quality concerns regarding innovation in two-sided markets and investigated a seller’s quality choices.

A few prior studies have also investigated innovation in platforms. Tellis et al. (2009) showed empirically that innovation boosts market share, albeit with a time lag. They found that both network ex ternalities and innovation affect market share, and enhanced quality produced by such innovations is also critical in high-technology product markets. With regard to other studies that focus on quality and innovation in platforms, Zhu and Iansiti (2012) argued that network size is not sufficient to defend a platform’s market position under conditions of strong network externalities. They used evidence of empirical estimates combined with analytical prediction to argue that platforms must have quality of a level comparable with that of competitors.

Despite the practical value of such work, technological innovation has been studied only lightly in the context of a platform and a two-sided market. More attention has been paid to pricing strategies faced with network externalities. These studies tend to focus on resolving the so-called chicken-and-egg problem of how platforms can expand their network, where users are willing to join a platform only when other groups are of sufficient size (Caillaud and Jullien 2003).

Because platform quality is heralded as a subject for additional study, the merits of quality have been vigorously debated. Tellis et al. (2009) stressed the relative importance of quality in the context of a hightechnology products market; Anderson et al. (2014) argued that it would be better to provide greater content availability than to invest in platform quality, especially in content-driven markets. Zhu and Iansiti (2012) examined the relative importance of platform quality, network externality, and consumer expectations as drivers of platform success. Mantena and Saha (2012) studied collaboration and cooperation between platforms and found that even small differences in technology can result in a massive change in two-sided market competition. Although this work is an extension of research streams that examine such platform quality issues, the focus is on ecosystems formed by IT platforms that require a physical medium, one where quality matters a great deal to consumers.

In terms of analytical modeling, our study has enriched the research of Choi (2010), which considers multihoming users and assumes the same given value of exclusive content for both platforms. In other related work, Hagiu and Lee (2011) analyzed the exclusivity of endogenously determined content, but they only modeled consumers’ single-homing behavior. The framework proposed in this study is novel in the literature, because it models endogenously determined user behavior on both sides, namely multihoming, while exploring the platform’s choice of quality level.

## 3. Model

Before the model setup is introduced, the main findings are previewed to help readers understand the flow of logic. After an examination of incentives for innovation on competing platforms, where consumers and service providers are allowed to join both platforms (i.e., Proposition 1, which argues that both platforms have incentives for innovation with multihoming users), the impact of a technology gap on competing platforms’ equilibrium decisions about additional innovation and changes in segmentation of users on both sides are also examined (Proposition 2). Next, three different subsidy policies are examined, namely a consumer-based subsidy (Proposition 3), a service provider-based subsidy (Proposition 4), and an innovation-based subsidy paid directly to the platform (Proposition 5). Then, the equilibrium levels of innovation achieved under each policy are compared to determine which is most effective (Proposition 6).

## 3.1. Benchmark

In this section, a duopolistic competition case is presented as a benchmark. Based on Armstrong (2006), a model of a two-sided market, where a platform earns profits through interaction between the two user groups, is introduced. Although Armstrong (2006) considers single-homing consumers and multihoming product providers in his supermarket competition exemplar, the model is extended to account for the realism that users can choose freely between single-homing and multihoming on both sides. Thus, our modeling also contributes to the platform literature by capturing multihoming on the part of both user groups. Choi (2010) considers a similar situation in the internet content market but treats the proportion of exclusive content as exogenous. In this model, the configuration of users on both sides is endogenous. The study adds further relevance to the literature by examining the impact of asymmetric technological capability on competing platforms’ choice of level of quality and compares different subsidy policies to determine their effectiveness in driving innovation.

It is assumed that there are two user groups; consumers (C) and service providers (S). Each user group is assumed to be uniformly distributed in [0, 1]. The two competing platforms are labeled as $i = A$ and $B ,$ and they reside at the end points of a line with unit length. The two platforms compete for market share by enabling the two user groups to interact with each other. Both user groups, consumers and service providers, are allowed to join either A or $B$ (single-home) or join both platforms (multihome). Let $n _ { C i }$ and $n _ { S i }$ represent the numbers of consumers and service providers, respectively, who single-home on platform $i ,$ whereas M indexes the multihoming users: that is, $n _ { C M }$ and $n _ { S M } ,$ respectively. Finally, consumers and service providers choose the platform to join after assessing platform quality and price levels. In sum, the model assumes that a consumer’s utility depends on the number of service providers that join the same platform as well as platform quality and price. It is obvious that a higher level of incentive stimulates more service providers to multihome; however, it can be a burden for value generation by the platform. Therefore, three types of user segments occur on both sides in equilibrium given the moderate consumer’s preference for a platform and the appro priate level of incentive for service providers.

## 3.2. Quality Competition Under Cost Asymmetry

As RBV (Barney 1991) argues, firms differ in their levels of knowledge and technological capability, and winners of the competition between firms depend on such factors. In this section, the benchmark is extended to the duopolistic competition, where two platforms at different levels of technological capability compete with each other.

Compared with a superior platform with sufficient technological capability, it requires greater effort, more time, and higher costs for an inferior platform lacking skills and resources to attain superior quality. In this sense, this technology gap is captured by modeling cost asymmetry. Suppose that $c _ { D }$ and $\omega c _ { D }$ are the costs of innovation for platforms A and $B ,$ respectively. The parameter $\omega \in ( 1 , \infty )$ represents that platform A has a cost advantage over platform B. That is, the technologically superior platform (A) incurs lower costs to extract the same level of quality from innovation than the inferior (B). The model timeline remains the same as in the benchmark case, whereas the business value functions are as follows:

$$

Although its cost-advantaged rival makes effort to the provision of quality, the technologically inferior platform has no choice but to offer a lower platform quality at a lower price to attract consumers. This result implies that, under duopolistic competition with technological asymmetry, the technology gap creates differentiation of innovation outcomes and that the larger gap results in a sharper contrast between platform qualities.

Technology gap also affects the behavior of consumers and service providers. Given the differentiated innovation outcomes led by the technology gap, some consumers still prefer the inferior platform in equilibrium, because not all consumers are motivated by premium quality. Also, it is true that more consumers choose to single home on the superior platform when it becomes evident that its offering is on the cutting edge of innovation. However, the increase in number of single-homing consumers on the superior platform creates an incentive for the service providers to multihome. More service providers will join both platforms to reach a greater mass of consumers given the larger technology gap between the platforms. Consequently, the entire consumer (service provider) size decreases (increases) owing to the choice with respect to multihoming.

From the above discussion, it is evident that a clear contrast between platforms plays an important role in deriving equilibrium but in different ways. Although the technology gap places a heavy strain on the inferior platform, the superior platform benefits from competitive advantage during innovation. Eventually, the technology gap reduces the entire consumer market size by resulting in a distinct polarization on the consumer side through differentiated platform quality and price.

Regarding the downside of polarization, it is worth considering policies that can invigorate competition while incentivizing competing platforms to innovate. Given the above discussion, the following question emerges. What if external support is provided to promote innovation whenever there is technological asymmetry between platforms? In the next section, different policy support scenarios for platforms are compared, and practical implications are further explored.

## 4. Policy Support and Innovation Quality

Recall that, in this study, “policy makers” are government officials who are responsible for public policy actions that encourage innovation. At the early stage of innovation, the future remains uncertain for players, including consumers, service providers, and platform intermediaries. It is rarely convincing that innovative improvement will successfully create a new economic ecosystem before it actually materializes. Thus, in the real world, policy makers often intervene in the marketplace and implement various policies to encourage innovations while sharing these economic burdens with stakeholders.

Once again, the driverless car industry provides a ready case. It is often argued that optimal policy support is essential for new technologies to thrive (Tillemann and McCormick 2016). In their Fortune article, Tillemann and McCormick (2016) identified impediments for driverless cars, including costs and policies, thus underscoring the need for optimal governmental policy support. In terms of choosing the most desirable use of limited resources, policy makers need to develop far more extensive and effective ways of balancing meaningful demand while still encouraging innovation. To achieve this, policy makers need to define rules for fair distribution of resources and clearly understand what drives platforms to invest in innovation. A priori, it would seem desirable that only a platform with serious techno logical potential should be deemed eligible to receive policy support, which generally is in the form of a subsidy. Therefore, for a platform to be selected as a target of policy support, it has to overcome administrative hurdles, such as strict assessment by policy makers, rigorous examination by professional systems groups, or third-party rating bodies. This could (as in the past) take the form of differential treatment of firms that offer technological products that are, for instance, beneficial for the environment or transform cyberspace, such as in the case of virtual firms operating in a tax-free internet setting.

Even when all of the platforms satisfy policy makers standards, it is reasonable to assume that policy makers can differentiate subsidy levels based on the platform’s technological capability. Note that policy makers can realize each platform’s technological capability in the administrative process and by comparing them, determine which is superior to the other.

Indeed, technological capability will likely be a key factor in the imminent driverless ecosystem. Some governments are already designing and proposing policies to encourage adoption of driverless cars. As noted earlier, in 2016, the U.S. administration proposed to spend \$4 billion to subsidize driverless cars (Spector and Ramsey $2 0 1 6 ,$ Street 2016). The UK government plans to invest more than £22 million for research and development projects related to the driverless car technology (Perkins 2016). In fact, the People’s Republic of China has been considering tax incentives for driverless car buyers, aiming to open a new era in the car industry and accelerate business breakthroughs (Bloomberg News 2016). Such governmental support mechanisms, directly or indirectly, provide assurances that innovations are indeed valuable to consumers and service providers.

Consequently, it can be worthwhile for platforms to bear the burden of early and risky innovation. Widely adopted forms of subsidy include those for consumers $( \mathrm { e . g . } )$ , tax incentives for consumers), which China is currently considering (Bloomberg News 2016, Kerlin 2017). Another type is innovation-based subsidy that is offered directly to the platform. Examples include research and development (R&D) support and/or tax cuts for environmentally friendly car manufacturers.

Given the above discussion, policies that incentivize platform innovation and the various conditions are investigated. Reflecting the realities of this emerging ecosystem, three different types of policy innovations are considered: (1) subsidies for consumers, (2) subsidies for service providers, and (3) innovation-based subsidies for the platform itself. These three forms of subsidy policies have been considered (if not yet implemented) for the driverless car industry as discussed above. Accordingly, the study investigates and compares these three subsidy policies for effectiveness in promoting innovation.

## 5. Policy Comparison: A Numerical Study

This section extends the policy analysis. In this section, the policy maker’s policy choice is further explored by comparing the two policies to determine which platforms achieve higher levels of innovation: that is, the consumer-based subsidy and the innovationbased subsidy. In the previous section, the levels of innovation quality garnered under each policy are compared. Specifically, the level of quality extracted from innovation chosen by the platforms is examined given a certain subsidy level. However, it is clear that the total subsidy that a government offers is not necessarily the same under different policy choices, because the total expenditure changes according to factors, such as the number of consumers. In this section, a fairer comparison of policy effectiveness is undertaken by examining the varying levels of innovation quality while holding the total cost of subsidies constant across different policy options.

Thus, to undertake this analysis, the total amount of subsidy under each policy is made constant, and the two aforementioned policy alternatives are compared. Although policy makers set the supporting scale and eligibility, all of the agents in the market— consumers, service providers, and platforms—make their decisions amid complicated and ambiguous cir cumstances. In the case of consumer-based subsidy platforms set their quality and price levels, desiring to be the first choice for consumers who are influenced by the subsidy level. Thus, the total amount of subsidies that the government incurs under the consumer-based subsidy is given by

$$
S T _ {C} = \delta s n _ {C A} ^ {*} + (1 - \delta) s n _ {C B} ^ {*} + 2 s n _ {C M} ^ {*}.\tag{28}
$$

However, under the innovation-based subsidy, whereby policy makers directly support the platform itself, subsidies are given to platforms based on their innovation levels. Therefore, the total amount of subsidy spending becomes

$$
S T _ {I} = \delta s Q _ {A} ^ {*} + (1 - \delta) s Q _ {B} ^ {*}.\tag{29}
$$

With respect to policy choices, the total amount of subsidies is computed by assigning values to the level of subsidy that an agent (i.e., consumer or platform) receives. Next, the level of quality achieved through innovation in equilibrium is examined while holding constant the total resources allocated for both policy choices: that is, $S T _ { C } = S T _ { I }$ . Figure 6 illustrates this and shows the changes in equilibrium innovation level with respect to the total amount of subsidy spent.

As can be seen from Figure 6, it is clear that both policies—subsidies for consumers and platforms— encourage platforms to innovate more. Interestingly, numerical analysis shows that innovation-based subsidy can outperform subsidizing consumers, because the policy results in a higher level of innovation on a fixed budget. Although firm-level (platform) subsidy can be substantially more than the subsidy for an individual consumer, the total amount of subsidy spending can be the opposite based on factors, such as the unit subsidy level, the total number of subsidized consumers, and the level of innovation achieved.

## 6. Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, very few studies have examined quality as an outcome of innovation in platforms. Studying how a platform’s innovativeness for bringing improvements can be an effective and competitive weapon contributes in several ways. When IT platforms build the ecosystem through a physical medium involving severe risk in the real world, the study suggests that platforms seeking pole positions cannot ignore the lessons of product innovation found in the literature, even if the market exhibits network externalities. It further suggests important guidelines for policy makers and executives desiring to promote continuous improvement via innovation.

This study contributes to existing literature on twosided market platforms by underscoring the impact of innovation on platform competition in the presence of network externalities. Methodologically, IT platform users’ multihoming behavior in the two-sided market is modeled, reflecting real-world conditions. The foundational condition for how a decision to innovate influences the business value of a platform and the role of technology gap in the competition between platforms is articulated. Under conditions of competition based mainly on quality considerations, technological capabilities determine the competitive advantage of each platform, because technological excellence enables the platform to attract more consumers and thus, engender a robust ecosystem.

Finally, the impact of policies supporting platform innovation is formulated, which is a crucial approach given that policy makers often have trouble preparing and implementing support for such policies. The final analysis focuses on social effectiveness pertaining to policies that directly affect platform innovation. Interestingly, it is observed that a finer application of subsidy policy invigorates the market, and this is considered socially desirable.

Practically, the most straightforward findings can be used as a guideline for policy makers and stakeholders, including platforms, consumers, and service providers. The results also direct a compelling message toward executives and provide specific guidance. The study findings show how the strategic use of government resources can be effective in incentivizing platforms to innovate. If the subsidy recipients are not chosen appropriately, the overall efficiency of social resources will suffer. Therefore, when policy makers attempt to stimulate platform innovation, they first need to recognize who the best recipients of policy benefits are and what the appropriate levels of subsidy are. Resource distribution should be clearly designed with respect to market conditions.

This study has several limitations that deserve consideration. Although this model reflects the market expansion effect to a certain extent (by allowing consumers to join more than one platform), the limitations generated by the assumption of fixed market size cannot be completely ignored. It is also assumed that, after a platform invests in quality, it improves immediately without any lag or possibility of failure in the process of innovation. A similar assumption is applied to the policy maker’s intervention effect. Although sudden failures are expected in both cases, it is not a stretch in reasoning to infer that innovation outcomes generally improve with greater investment. Prior studies, for instance, uncovered the positive relationship between input in innovation and output from innovation in various contexts (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Brouwer and Kleinknecht 1997).

Indeed, it may be desirable to consider the detailed aspects of subsidy policies in future research. For example, offering differential tax cuts to different consumers could provide additional insights. Also, a uniform distribution of consumers and service providers is assumed. Because most of the results hold even under violation of this distributional assumption (an assumption widely found in the literature), other researchers are encouraged to extend the model with alternate assumptions. Finally, there are many factors that may play into the competitiveness of multisided markets and their concomitant regulation, discriminatory and predatory pricing, and blocking content from complementors (Greenstein 2001, Evans 2003, Eisenmann 2007, Rochet and Tirole 2008). These could be natural extensions of this study, and active scientific research in this tone is welcome.

Lucas et al. (2013) raise the question of whether the IS field has much to offer to both business executives and government policy makers, and the essential question is “Are we impactful?” They challenge the field when they say the following:

What is less clear to us, however, is the extent to which our research is having an impact that extends beyond the circumscribed confines of academia. Does the IS field have insights to provide to decision makers who are confronted with a major transformation in their industry or firm? Can we advise government policymakers about technology issues facing societies? (Lucas et al. 2013, pp. 371–372)

This study helps to address this challenge by providing pragmatic advice for both C-level executives and government officials. Furthermore, working in the IS field can offer insights into the real world, and this is an upfront, defined goal of this study.
