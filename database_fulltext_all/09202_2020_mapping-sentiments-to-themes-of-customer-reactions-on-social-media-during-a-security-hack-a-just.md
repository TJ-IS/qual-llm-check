## Abstract

As social media continues to transform firm–customer interactions, firms must leverage customer reactions to generate actionable insights, especially in contexts (e.g., crisis events) where customer reactions are critical. Using the justice theory, we categorize customer reactions of two firms, Home Depot and Target, during the time-frame of a security hack to understand key themes/topics. We then map the themes/topics to customer sentiments in those reactions. We found that customers associate justice with simple procedures than the experience of dealing with the firm. In addition, it is critical for firms to carefully assess and control customer sentiments on social media during crisis events.

Keywords: sentiment analysis, justice theory, security hack, customer reactions, brand management

## 1. Introduction

Usage of social media (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, etc.) has grown exponentially over the last few years. Recent Pew internet research reports that in 2016 close to eight in every ten online Americans (\~79%) use Facebook, an increase of 7% points compared to 2015 (Greenwood et al., 2016). Unsurprisingly, firms have embraced social media by increasing their presence to leverage the advantages of being within easy reach of their customer base (Barnes, 2015; Kumar et al., 2016). A 2015 study shows that 74% of the Fortune 500 companies have a Facebook page while 93% have a LinkedIn account and 78% have a Twitter account (Barnes, 2015). Equipped with sophisti nication mechanisms (e.g., posts, responses, and emotional expressions), social media has enabled firms to understand customer reactions to their upcoming or released products and to any service issues (Yang & Kent, 2014).

Although firm-customer relationship has always been critical for firms, social media has enhanced the visibility and importance of this relationship (Yang & Kent, 2014). For example, customers are now able to get the attention of the firm’s customer service department easier than in the past via the firm’s official Facebook page, which is an online space typically managed by the marketing and customer service department. In addition, volume, speed, and nature of responses from a firm increases visibility into the firm’s customer service strategy (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). In addition to firm–customer interactions, social media creates an additional dimension, that is, customer–customer interactions. These interactions are valued by firms because it enables word-of-mouth promotion, which is known to be the most persuasive form of promotion (Kumar et al., 2007).

While organizations can largely benefit through customer reactions on social media, there are potential negative effects. For example, while customers’ positive word-of-mouth promotion can help firms enhance their reputation, it is possible that customers engaging in negative word-of-mouth promotion could hurt the firm’s reputation (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). The problem exacerbates in a social media setting where it is difficult to control the negative narrative a firm experiences, especially during crisis events such as security hacks (Dijkmanset al., 2015). Similarly, firms not showing empathy, concern, or not being responsive to a dissatisfied customer could be negatively perce (Parasuraman et al., 2002). As such, firms cannot be content by just creating a social media presence for the sake of it. Firms need to actively monitor and engage with their customers through social media.

Although extant literature on managing customer reactions suggests the importance of maintaining constant, fair, and unbiased response (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010), there is little research that studies this in a social media context. Specifically, there is little research that explores different social media communication strategies that firms can use during crisis events such as security hacks to maintain reputation among customers and avoid backlash from those affected by the event. Security hacks are particularly damaging for firms and are found to be on the rise in recent years (Andoh-Baidoo et al., 2010; Kharif, 2017). For example, if a firm does not respond or produce an adequate response to customers reaching out through its social media spaces, it might be perceived to be indifferent. On the other hand, firms can’t respond to every customer’s post, making it critical for firms to systematically structure their response strategy on social media.

We posit and investigate the research question – How can firms develop intelligence by strategically analyzing customer reactions during crisis events such as security hacks? – Consequently, this research adds value to firms thinking about investing in social media in two important ways. First, firms need to strategically choose posts to respond to, to balance the needs between customer response and resources handling social media after crisis incidents such as security hacks. This study will help firms build intelligence around responding to the right posts. Second, firms need to get a handle over how customers perceive their brand. During a crisis event such as a security hack, it is likely that brand will be perceived with a negative sentiment. Firms should, therefore, be able to preempt such situations and control the narrative to contain negative brand sentiment.

To achieve this research objective, we analyze customer reactions on Facebook for two retail firms, Target and Home Depot, which faced security hack incidents during 2013-2014. We used a qualitative analysis to identify topics or themes and linked them with the sentiments of the comment to create a unique form for social media intelligence (Zeng et al., 2010). Our findings show that after a security hack incident, customers often engage in topics relevant to policies and procedures related to service recovery. Our findings also show that how a firm manages its responses during a security hack event could have an impact on the company’s aggregate sentiment toward the firm.

Rest of the paper is organized as follows: In section 2, we discuss the motivation for linking sentiments and topics on social media and then discuss the theoretical background for justice theory and sentiment analysis. In section 3, we describe security hack incident cases at the two firms – Target and Home Depot. In section 4, we discuss data collection and analysis approach in detail. We also discuss about the qualitative coding mechanism we followed with a detailed description of the two cases. We then present our results and key findings. Finally, in section 6, we discuss theoretical contributions, practical implications, and limitations of our paper along with potential future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

The aim of social media intelligence is to help amplify positive messages about the firm, address issues in a timely and fair manner, and to be able to mitigate any damage to the firm’s reputation (Fan & Gordon, 2014). Although th e are existing tools that help build such intelligence through techniques such as a sentiment analysis or opinion mining, these techniques lack specificity and context. For example, even though a sentiment analysis provides an understanding of the polarity of the reactions from customers (positive, negative, and neutral), it does not provide insights on the context for which such reactions are expressed to get any actionable insights. The missing piece is a more granular level qualitative analysis of textual data that form the core of customer reactions.

To address this gap, we first used qualitative thematic coding to identify specific themes of customer complaints that emerged related to the hack. For this, we used the justice theory as our theoretical lens (Colquitt et al., 2001). The justice theory is an appropriate lens for this work as it explicitly explores how customers react to firms’ actions in response to service complaints. Then, we used sentiment analysis to identify different customer sentiments that emerge after a security hack event by analyzing all customer reactions on the firm’s official social media page. The combination of these techniques (qualitative thematic coding and sentiment analysis) affords us the ability to investigate sentiment contents of each customer reaction along with the justice theory classification of that reaction. This provides firms the intelligence needed to understand specific customer concerns they can address to mitigate the negative reactions. The context of security hacks is particularly suitable for such an investigation because it can be expected that customer concerns and service requests would elevate after such events.

In this section, we first discuss the justice theory along with its three key dimensions – distributive, procedural, and interactional justice. We describe the specific components of the theory that are particularly suitable to the context of security hacks. Next, we discuss sentiment analysis, the other key component of the methodology we developed for the analysis of sentiment contents of customer reactions.

## 2.1 Justice theory

The justice theory explains how individuals react to a firm’s decisions and whether these decisions are perceived to be fair. Decisions that are perceived to be fair are found to have a significant impact on customer satisfaction and loyalty (Cropanzana et al., 2007). As our aim was to unpack customer reactions to a security hack incident by identifying themes or topics discussed by customers regarding the firm’s actions related to the security hack, we believe the justice theory is an appropriate lens for this investigation.

The central theme of the justice theory is based on the notion that individuals are increasingly being influenced by the concept of “fairness of procedures and outcomes” rather than whether they received the most “favorable outcome” (McFarlin & Sweeney, 1992). Rooted in social psychology literature, the justice theory has been used as a lens to study social and criminal justice (Jost & Kay, 2010). In the management literature, the justice theory has been used to study several human resource issues such as pay gap and pay equity (Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Folger & Konovsky, 1989), impacts on employee markers such as performance, productivity, and citizenship behaviors (Cohen-Charash & Spector, 2001; Cropanzana et al., 2007; Moorman, 1991). In marketing literature, several studies have used the justice theory to evaluate service experiences and to build customer relationships (Lin et al., 2011; Tax et al., 1998; Wetsch, 2006).

The proliferation of social media has played an instrumental role in shifting the bulk of modern day service delivery to the online realm (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). This presents an opportunity for IS researchers to analyze online services using the justice theory. However, there are only a few studies in IS that have adopted the justice theory lens to study technology-based contexts and none of them, to our knowledge, study online services. For example, Liang et al. (2017) use the justice theory to study the impact on doctor–patient relationship and patient satisfaction after the implementation of a patient accessible health information system. Chou et al. (2013) use the justice theory to study motivation of information systems personnel to be committed to their projects. In another study, Glassman et al. (2015) study the concept of cyber loafing through the justice theory lens. The extant literature indicates that fairness assessments are measured along three dimensions – distributive justice, procedural justice, and interactional justice (Cropanzana et al., 2007; Tax et al., 1998).

2.1.1 Distributive justice: Distributive justice refers to the level of fairness associated with allocations, provisions, or outcomes that customers expect to get (Folger & Konovsky, 1989; McFarlin & Sweeney, 1992). The central tenets of the justice theory are based on the idea that victims place more weight on the fairness of the process of justice than the actual outcome. Fairness is primarily perceived to be a comparison of ratio of one’s contribution (input) to the outcome (output) to some other person’s ratio (Van den Bos et al., 1998). This aligns with the idea of “equity”, which is a core rule used to allocate distributive justice (Cowherd & Levine, 1992). Every working relationship such as employer–employee, vendor–client, etc., is governed by a psychological contract that implicitly establishes certain standard trust and fairness expectations (Robinson, 1996). When such contracts are violated it will impact levels of trust between the parties eventually leading to changes in behaviors, such as loyalty towards the other party and may also eventually lead to an ending of the relationship (Turnley & Feldman, 1999). As such, the absence of distributive justice might lead to unintended negative consequences. For example, Tepper (2000) suggested that subordinates of abusive supervision will feel disenfranchised compared to peers if their supervisors spend more time berating them than providing mentoring functions. Similarly, the literature on racial profiling and bias also provides evidence that lack of fairness in justice outcomes for black people in comparison to white people led to a trust deficit (Engel, 2005). Given the context of our study, we look for breaches in this psychological contract as evidence of distributive injustice. For example, Target and its customers share a contract that states Target will protect sensitive personal and financial information. However, when a security hack that puts all that sensitive information at risk occurs, customers might feel that Target has not kept their end of the bargain and consequently perceive that the contract has to be breached. Accordingly, we look for two specific negative outcomes as evidence of distributive injustice – (1) Loyalty break – one that we define as loyal customers ending their loyalty with the firm because of the security hack and (2) Trust deficit – one that we define as a gap in the level of trust shared between the customer and the firm.

2.1.2 Procedural justice: Procedural justice refers to ‘means’ by which outcomes are allocated (Folger & Konovsky, 1989). The difference compared to distributive justice is that focus is on policy and procedure that is followed to decide on allocation of a specific outcome. Extant research has shown that when a just process is followed, it can mitigate negative effects that are associated with unfavorable outcomes (Cropanzana et al., 2007). This effect has been termed as “fair process effect”. Procedural justice is composed of five dimensions – (1) procedural control – extent to which one has control over procedural steps, (2) decision control – extent to which one can dispute a decision, (3) response speed – speed with which firm responds to customers concerns, (4) flexibility – extent to which procedures can be tailored to individual situations, and (5) accessibility – ease with which one engages in a process (Tax et al, 1998). In addition, research shows that customers tend to give more importance to process control over decision control (Kumar et al., 1995; Van den Bos et al., 1997). Given the context of the study, i.e., customer reactions to a breach, we look for evidence of procedural injustice and accordingly, in our analysis, we use the opposite definitions of the five dimensions discussed above.

2.1.3 Interactional justice: Interactional justice is the perceived quality of interaction between two parties (Colquitt et al., 2001). If interaction between two parties is deemed to be cordial, polite, and dignified then there is interactional justice. However, if one of the two parties treats the other party in an impolite manner, then there is a lack of interactional justice. Extant literature points to five dimensions to identify interactional justice – (1) politeness, (2) honesty, (3) empathy, (4) explanation, and (5) effort (Tax et al., 1998). Politeness, honesty, and empathy are interpersonal traits that form the sub-theme interpersonal justice. While effort and explanation showcase the extent to which the firm goes to resolve a conflict in a timely manner. Effort and explanation together form the sub-theme informational justice (Greenberg, 1993). We use the composite theme of interactional justice with the above noted five dimensions because it enables an appropriate comparison across other two dimensions (i.e., distributive and procedural) and appropriate granularity to analyze specific customer reactions. Again, given the context of the study we looked for evidence for the existence of the opposite of the five dimensions discussed in the literature to identify interactional injustice i.e., (1) impoliteness, (2) dishonesty, (3) lack of empathy, (4) bad explanation, and (5) low effort (Tax et al., 1998).

## 2.2 Sentiment Analysis

Individual opinions on web-based platforms such as blogs and social media have recently become a valuable resource for organizations because it can be analyzed via text mining techniques to understand customer reactions, manage customer relations, and to channelize targeted product offerings (He et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2012; Taboada et al., 2011). Opinions expressed on social media are known to influence individual and group opinion and behavior across areas including products purchasing behavior, understanding advertising effectiveness, predicting stock markets movements, and voting trends for an election (Eirinaki et al., 2012; He et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2012).

##

An opinion is a statement in which the opinion holder makes a specific claim about a topic using a certain sentiment (Eirinaki et al., 2012; Bose et al., 2017). The opinions expressed can be analyzed using sentiment analysis, a natural language processing technique that uses computational linguistics and text mining to identify the text sentiment, typically as very positive, positive, neutral, sarcastic, negative, or very negative. This technique is also known in the text mining literature as the emotional polarity analysis (EPA), opinion mining, review mining, or appraisal extraction (Mostafa, 2013). Thus, sentiment analysis is regarded as an automated knowledge discovery technique that can be applied to find hidden patterns in a large number of reviews, blogs, or social media posts. Recent extensive literature reviews suggest that the sentiment analysis has been applied across four major categories: product (or brand) reviews, movie reviews, political orientation extraction, and stock market predictions (He et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2012; Mostafa, 2013). For this research, as the focus is on a comparative analysis of customer reactions to a specific crisis event at two popular retail firms, we build on prior research in the context of brand reviews. We elaborate on the sentiment analysis process that we employed in section 4.

## 3.. Case-Site Descriptions

To understand customers’ reactions to a crisis, we performed a case-site analysis of two retail firms dealing with a major security hack event. The specific type of security hack that is relevant to our cases is credit card hack, which involves theft of customer’s credit card-related information including credit card number, security codes, customer names, and address information. We selected retail firms with both online shopping and physical store location presence because such firms have direct customer interactions enabled through the website or physical stores. Identifying emergent themes in customer reactions in light of a major hack of its internal technological infrastructure for any firm is a complex phenomenon because it involves multiple stakeholders, affected customers who are geographically dispersed, and sophisticated technology setups. Case study methodology allows to systematically analyze such complex phenomena that require in-depth investigations (Benbasat et al., 1987). As noted earlier, we use the justice theory (Colquitt et al., 2001) as our guiding theoretical foundation to analyze the cases. As such, we employ a soft positivist approach in analyzing and interpreting our findings (Dubé & Paré, 2003). Our goal in analyzing the case studies was to first understand the nature of credit card hack at the firms and then analyze nature of interactions of the retail firms with affected customers enabled by the social media platform, Facebook.

An important aspect of case study research is to decide on the number of cases that will be included as part of the investigation. Analyzing multiple cases has inherent advantages. For example, multiple cases imply more degrees of freedom and rigor (Lee, 1989). Second, an increasing number of cases is akin to replicating an experiment and comparing results. For example, Yin (1994) describes literal and theoretical replication as two main types of replication. Cases are selected for theoretical replication when the cases come from different contexts and are expected to produce different outcomes, while cases selected for literal replication are expected to produce similar outcomes. In addition, using multiple cases offers the inherent advantage of deriving specific business insights by employing a crosscomparison of the cases.

reasons for selection of the two firms. First, both firms suffered a major credit card hack at around the same time period (2013-2014). Second, both firms were similar in many ways. Both are large multinational Fortune 100 retail firms, have a significant number of franchisee stores and an online presence, and float a branded credit card service. Third, both firms have active official Facebook pages, which are firm-managed spaces on Facebook used as a marketing channel for products and as an avenue to engage with customers. Customers make use of this avenue to get direct responses from the firm regarding their concerns (Champoux et al., 2012). When Target and Home Depot suffered a major credit card hack and the news became public, disgruntled and panic-stricken customers used the firm’s Facebook page to get in touch with the firm directly to understand how the hack occurred, know the actions taken by the firms, and to express their concerns. They also used the official Facebook pages to interact with each other and to share information about the hack. This interaction on the official Facebook pages of the firms provides the core data for our analysis. Next, we describe the Target and Home Depot credit card hack cases in detail.

## 3.1 Case details - The Home Depot

The Home Depot is an USA-based retailer of home improvement and construction products and services. Home Depot operates many big-box stores across the United States, Canada, as well as in many parts of Mexico. The company is headquartered in the Greater Atlanta Region. As of May 2017, Home Depot had 2,274 retail locations and \$88.5 billion in annual revenue (Home Depot, 2017).

The credit card hack at Home Depot first came into news on September 2, 2014 when security news reporter Brian Krebs reported that evidence of credit card numbers linked to Home Depot purchases being sold online was found (Pagliery, 2014). On September 18, 2014, Home Depot released a statement confirming that hackers obtained 56 million credit card numbers from purchases made at any Home Depot store from April 2014 to September 2014 (Hardekopf, 2014). In addition, separate files containing approximately 53 million customer email addresses were also compromised (Stone, 2014). Home Depot used their Facebook page to keep customers informed about the actions the company took in the aftermath of the event. On November 6, 2014, Home Depot said in a press release that hackers used a third-party vendor’s username and password to enter the perimeter of its network (Kerner, 2014). From there, hackers acquired rights that allowed them to navigate portions of Home Depot’s network and to deploy custom-built malware on self-checkout systems in United States and Canada (Kerner, 2014).

In the year preceding this hack, Home Depot suffered at least two other smaller hacks (Elgin et al., 2014). Afterward, Home Depot security contractors urged to strengthen the firm’s cyber defenses by activating a key unused feature of its security software that would have added a layer of protection to the retail terminals where customers swipe their cards (Elgin et al., 2014). However, the malware used in the hack during April 2014 to September 2014 was not seen in any of the previous hacks Home Depot experienced and was designed to evade detection by anti-virus software (Elgin et al., 2014).

As a follow-up to the hack, Home Depot offered affected customers free one-year credit monitoring service from AllClear ID (Turner, 2014). Home Depot also rolled out new encryption technology for their cash registers and self-checkout systems to protect customers (Home Depot, 2014). In early 2015, the firm was estimated to pay \$62 million to recover from incursion, including costs for call-center staffing and legal expenses (Turner, 2014). Home Depot implemented enhanced encryption of payment data in all US stores. The new security protection locks payment card data, taking raw payment card information, and then scrambling it to make it unreadable to hackers (Home Depot, 2014). Home Depot’s encryption technology, provided by Voltage Security Inc., was tested and validated by two independent IT security firms (Home Depot, 2014). This project for enhanced encryption implementation, which was initially launched in January 2014 as part of a strategic plan to enhance security measures, kept getting delayed because of internal reasons. However, after the hack, implementation of the project was accelerated and completed in all stores in the United States by the end of 2014 and in Canada in 2015.

## 3.2 Case details - Target

Target is a chain of large retail stores primarily based in the United States and headquartered in Minneapolis, Minnesota. As of 2017, Target operates in 1807 store locations throughout the United States (Target Corporation, 2017)

Credit card hack at Target occurred during November 2013. In the days prior to thanksgiving in 2013, hackers installed malware in Target’s security and payments system designed to steal information on every credit or debit card used at company’s stores. The malware was designed to capture on each card swipe the information on a card’s magnetic strip including card number, expiration date, and security code and store it on a Target server commandeered by the hackers (Riley et al., 2014). Cumulatively, the malware affected 40,000 credit card swipe machines at Target store locations, leading to details of 40 million credit and debit cards exposed to the hackers. Customers who shopped at Target stores in United States between November 27, 2013 and December 15, 2013 had their credit or debit card details exposed to the hackers (Target Corporation, 2013). Cumulatively, the hack took place over a 19-day period that began on the day before thanksgiving (November 27, 2013) and ended on December 15, 2013 (Target Corporation, 2013). Through the hack, the hackers gained access to customer names, credit card or debit card numbers, card expiration dates, and card security codes (Newman, 2013).

In a press release after hack, Target informed some key details about the hack, some of which are noted in the following (Target Corporation, 2013). Initial indicators of the hack were detected on November 30, 2016 by security specialists at Target’s office in Bangalore, India. Six months prior to the hack, Target invested \$1.6 million in installation of a malware detection tool made by the computer security firm, FireEye. Target also setup a team of security specialists at the Bangalore office to monitor its systems and the tool’s capabilities around the clock. It was the job of the team in Bangalore to immediately inform Target’s security operations center in Minneapolis if any suspicious activities were detected by the tool.

On November 30, 2013, hackers had set their traps and planned to move stolen credit card data first to staging points spread around United States to cover their tracks and then into their computers in Russia (Target Corporation, 2013). However, the malware detection tool traced this activity. At this point, the team in Bangalore alerted the security team in Minneapolis. However, the team in Minneapolis did not take any action at that point because Target’s security policy recommended watching and waiting, which was later criticized by security experts in the press (Klausner 2013). Target Chairman, President, and Chief Executive Officer, Gregg Steinhafel, issued an e-mail statement regarding this matter stating, “We are conducting an end-to-end review of our people, processes, and technology to understand our opportunities to improve data security and are committed to learning from this experience. While we are still in the midst of an ongoing investigation, we have already taken significant steps, including beginning the overhaul of our information security structure and the acceleration of our transition to chip-enabled cards” (Riley et al., 2014). The company announced in February 2014 that more than \$100 million have been invested to ensure that new chip-enabled store credit cards were issued to customers and in-store registers were updated to read new chip-enabled cards (Harris, 2014).

On March 5, 2014, Target’s Chief Information Officer, Beth Jacob, resigned and promised that Target Corporation is ensuring that appropriate measures are taken to avoid such incidents in the future (Harris, 2014). Even after proactive actions were taken after the incident, customers and banks filed more than 90 lawsuits against Target for negligence and compensatory damages. By February 1, 2014, Target had spent \$61 million just for customer relations, legal, and press activities in response to the hack, according to its fourth-quarter report to investors in 2014. Target also set up a customer response operation and an active social presence via platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter, to regain lost trust (Riley et al., 2014). Target corporation faced profit losses in 2014, estimated at 40 percent in the 4th quarter of 2014 (Harris, 2014). In addition, Target’s profit for the holiday shopping period, in 2013-2014, fell 46 percent as compared to the same quarter the year before, indicating that there was a financial impact of the hack on sales and customer loyalty (Riley et al., 2014).

## 4. Data Collection and Analysis

Data collection and analysis were done iteratively. From the case details for Home Depot and Target, we were able to gather the timeline spanning each hack incident. For example, news reports from known media agencies (e.g. Bloomberg, New York Times, Daily Mail, Time) and Target’s official news releases were a source of information for knowing when the credit card hack at Target became public. On the basis of this information, we first collected all firm-generated posts (primary) on their respective official Facebook pages. Then, for each such primary post, we collected all sub-posts (secondary) i.e., all customer posts as a response to the primary post. Because the primary posts did not contain any customer reactions, we analyzed only the secondary posts (1404 posts for Target and 1035 posts for Home Depot). The analysis was driven by the three key dimensions of the justice theory i.e., distributive justice, procedural justice, and interactional justice. Coding was done by the two authors of this paper. It is important to note that given the context of the cases (i.e., after security breach), we expected to see evidence related to the injustices rather than justices. For example, we expected to look for outcome-related injustices and categorize them as distributive injustice (Colquitt et al 2015). Similarly, perceived problems with the procedures related to rectifying an issue were categorized as procedural injustice and subpar experience for a customer interacting with the firm to sort out their issues regarding the hack were treated as interactional injustice. Consequently, while we base our analysis on the three dimensions, we reconceptualize the dimensions from the contrary perspective of injustice.

## 4.1 Firm Advocacy

Our data analysis was primarily deductive as we were looking for evidence of specific justice dimensions. However, good qualitative research also requires being open to identify other themes or trends that emerge from the data during the analysis (Glaser & Strauss 1967). When a particular Facebook post did not semantically fit any of the above-mentioned codes, we used open and axial coding mechanisms to create a new theme (Strauss & Corbin, 2007). For example, it was reasonable to assume that most customers who will post on Target and Home Depot’s page would have negative reactions toward these firms. Accordingly, the injustice dimensions would be viewed from the customer’s perspective. However, we found that a subset of users took the firm’s side whenever they felt there was “unfair critique” coming from unreasonable customers. In this case, customers had a positive reaction and as such the injustice dimensions are to be viewed from the perspective of the firms. We define this customer reaction as firm advocacy and add it to our list of identified themes. This theme does not fit with any of the injustice themes as it does not relate to the fairness judgments of affected individual customers but about their unfairness judgments of the firm. This tells us that while firms, indeed, must invest in ensuring that they address the negative reactions that ensue such an event, it is also valuable to engage with customers advocating for the firm and leverage those reactions to balance the onslaught of negative reactions.

The first step, data collection, involves collection of textual data from a text-based data source such as a social media platform like Facebook or Twitter or survey tools such as Qualtrics and Google Forms. Once data have been collected, the next step is pre-processing, which employs multiple techniques of text cleansing such as extra whitespace removal, punctuation removal, text stemming, stop word (and, or, the, etc.) removal, and spelling corrections. The involved removal techniques (extra whitespace removal, punctuation removal, and stop word removal) are conducted because the removed components using these techniques do not contain any sentimental value. Techniques such as text stemming are incorporated to reduce redundancy and to reduce a word to its original word. For example, the word “interesting” would be stemmed to the original word “interest” because that would help in understanding the implied sentiment and to reduce redundancy if the word “interest” is used in any other way (e.g., interested, interesting, etc.) in the collected text data.

Figure 1. Sentiment Analysis Process

The third step is, sentiment scoring. To calculate the sentiment score, sentiment words obtained from text are compared to a lexicon or a dictionary to determine the strength of the sentiment. For example, words in the lexicon such as “bad”, “worst”, or “horrible” would be assigned as negative sentiment score of -1 for each occurrence of such words. Words in the lexicon such as “good”, “excellent”, or “awesome” would be assigned a positive sentiment score of 1 for each occurrence of such words. A sentence that includes multiple positive sentiment words is then labeled very positive as its sentiment score. Similarly, a sentence that might contain multiple negative words would be assigned very negative sentiment score. Sentences that do not contain any positive or negative words are labeled as neutral. We used the RSentiment package in R-Studio for our analysis, which uses a structured algorithm based on the above process to assign each word with six types of sentiment scores – very positive, positive, very negative, negative, neutral, and sarcasm. We discuss these scores and the implied meaning in our analysis.

The final step in the process is visualization (Bose et al., 2017), which uses methods to create appropriate visualization for generated sentiment scores. Such visualization methods (bar graphs, trend lines, word clouds, etc.) require the calculation of frequency counts for different sentiment types (e.g., positive, negative, very positive, very negative, neutral, and sarcasm) and then the use of visualization tools (e.g., Tableau) to plot the frequency score of the sentiment to the sentiment label.

It is to be noted that given the context of our study, the expectation for the two firms was to seek sentiments. While the neutral sentiment does not imply any inherent emotional reaction, an analysis of posts with neutral sentiment could be useful to monitor changes for two key reasons. First, neutral sentiment does not imply a state between positive and negative sentiment but a separate state that denotes a lack of sentiment (Koppel & Schler, 2006). Accordingly, monitoring the neutral sentiment is important because it increases the accuracy of the positive and negative sentiments. In addition, if the overall proportion of a neutral sentiment between two time points has increased or decreased, it must be at the expense of decrease or increase of other sentiments, respectively. Accordingly, tracking changes in the neutral sentiment can give us an additional insight into the ebbs and flows of the overall firm-related of the positive or negative sentiments. Sarcasm is inherent to the English language where it used to mean: when people say the opposite of the truth, or the opposite of their true feelings, to be funny or to make a point (Maynard & Greenwood, 2014). As such, there is an issue in sentiment classifiers not considering the sarcasm sentiment that might incorrectly classify a negative sentiment as a positive one. Third, of the two negative sentiments (negative and very negative), very negative was found to be dominant. Figure 2 presents a visual comparison of these findings.

According to these findings, one might interpret that while customers are conservative when they show positive emotions toward a firm, they become liberal when they show negative emotions. This is in line with the literature on consumer marketing that suggests that people manifest negative sentiments more than positive sentiments (Glynn Mangold, Miller, & Brockway, 1999; Hart, Heskett, & Sasser Jr, 1989). In addition, a negative sentiment has a greater impact on the firm than an equivalent positive sentiment (Charlett, Garland, & Marr, 1995). These findings show that it is important for firms to identify and handle negative sentiments quickly to avoid any losses. Our study contributes to this literature by showing that in addition to valence of sentiment, magnitude of the valence could provide additional insight to formulate targeted strategies.

## 6. Discussion

Firms are increasingly investing in social media platforms to directly engage with customers; however, how interactions on social media can bring value to these firms is still under investigated. In this study, we analyzed customer reactions to a credit card hack event on a firm’s social media page. Specifically, we identified themes in customers’ reactions i.e., different category of complaints and feedback related to the credit card hack and mapped it onto the sentiments contents of their posts i.e., their opinion about their experience interacting with the firm in matters related to the hack. We found that in an after-crisis event such as a security hack, the most recurring theme in customer reactions is with regards to procedural justice i.e., customers care more about the procedure and policies that help them navigate through the crisis. Our analysis also pointed to key emergent subthemes – i.e., inaccessibility of the firm’s representatives, firm’s response speed and lack of flexibility in procedures. These findings indicate that after-hack customers immediately wanted to gather as much information to understand the scope of the crisis and actions taken by the firm before making judgments about whether the outcome was favorable (i.e., distributive justice) to them or not. This is also in line with the fairness heuristic theory (Lind, 2001; Lind et al., 2001) which posits that in many situations information about procedure is made known before information about the outcome. For example, the way a court trial is conducted is well known before the jury decides the outcome. As such, it is possible that customers discuss and form opinions about the procedure before the outcome (Van den Bos et al., 1997).

## 6.1 Theoretical contributions

Findings of this study have four key theoretical contributions. First, we apply the justice theory to an actual instance of a service encounter. Most studies that apply justice theory run a post-hoc survey that asks customers questions about their service experience with a firm to analyze the different justice themes (Colquitt & Rodell, 2011; Folger & Konovsky, 1989; Tax et al., 1998). Although these studies help us understand customers’ reactions to a firm’s responses in times of a crisis, reliability and integrity of these findings directly depends on the survey participants’ ability to accurately recall their service experience. Because we extracted customer reactions from the firm’s official Facebook page as the hack incident occurred, the justice categorizations that we developed are more grounded in data from a real service encounter.

Second, as we collected customer reactions, we identified specific customers who were defending the firm from any unfair critique by other customers. This interaction has a profound impact on how topics of concern and customer sentiment toward the firm evolve. Studies based on survey sampling are not geared to furnish this insight because individual responses are not transparent. In comparison, social media interactions are highly transparent and as such provide an opportunity for customers to shape and be shaped by other customers’ views.

Third, we use the justice theory to evaluate injustice rather than justice. Typically, justice theory studies evaluate justice-enhancing factors (Colquitt et al., 2001; Cowherd & Levine, 1992; Eddy et al., 1999). In contrast, we analyzed injustice enhancing factors because they were relevant to the context of our case sites. The two perspectives have subtle differences. For one, justice and injustice should not be treated as binary concepts, that is, existence of justice is not the absence of injustice and vice versa (Sen, 2011). Instead, justice needs to be treated as a continuum between justice and injustice. We use the injustice end of continuum and evaluate factors that have an impact on customers’ perception of injustice. By doing so, we believe that there is scope to find new themes that could offer a nuanced explanation of the phenomenon. For example, we found loyalty break and trust deficit as subthemes for distributive injustice. While distributive justice refers to perceived equity in the firms’ actions after-crisis, distributive injustice as we found referred to loyalty break and trust deficit and not ‘inequity’ in the firms’ actions. It is to be noted that while inequity might be correlated with loyalty break and trust deficit, it is not necessary that it causes them.

Finally, we address the call in recent research to derive insights by combining known theoretical foundations and inherent advantages of analyzing available customer reactions on social media (Culnan et al., 2010; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Kietzmann et al., 2011). We combine thematic coding based on the justice theory and the sentiment analysis to create a synergy that should work well for firms thinking seriously about solidifying their social media presence.

## 6.2 Practical implications

Our findings have some key practical implications. First, the sentiment analysis is generally used to categorize posts to be positive, negative, or a specific variant of both sentiments. While this is valuable for social media managers to evaluate aggregate brand sentiment, it fails to provide specific actionable insights. For example, consider a case where a retail firm is facing a credit card hack and through a sentiment analysis found that there is a strong negative sentiment towards the firm in customer responses. While this may be useful, it does not provide insights on what can be done to reverse the negative sentiment. However, when key topics or themes that customers are discussing are identified and mapped onto sentiments, it is possible to create strategies specifically catered to the themes that are responsible for inducing a negative sentiment. For example, if it was found that accessibility to a customer service agent was a key concern for customers and was the cause for negative sentiment, then the firm can specifically address this concern by either hiring temporary customer service agents or creating a customer service hotline for a specific event at hand.

Second, since the procedural injustice theme was found to be most prevalent in our analysis, firms that face crisis situations need to ensure that they create and adhere to customer centric policies and procedures. Customers associate following procedures as opposed to doing a favor to an individual customer as being fair (Sparks & McColl-Kennedy, 2001). Consequently, in crisis situations, it is beneficial to explain to the customer the set of relevant policies and procedures of the service recovery process. This could mean setting up expectations on time and possible outcomes. This is dependent on whether customers can get in touch with the firm to express their concerns. It is suggested that when customers are given an opportunity to express views about the injustice they faced, it enhances customer satisfaction (Goodwin & Ross, 1990). Similarly, if customers are not given such an opportunity, it may lead to them feeling that they have no control over outcomes (Lind et al., 1990). Accordingly, firms need to create mechanisms – such as social media spaces or phone lines so that customers can get in touch with the firm quickly to express their concerns. Equally important is a timely response from the firm to the customer, which could again imply staffing enough people to handle the volume of customer complaints.

Third, a noticeable aspect of Target and Home Depot cases was that the Home Depot incident happened more than 1 year after the Target incident. Our data analysis did not indicate any actions that might have been taken by Home Depot, leveraging the learnings from the Target incident. We understand from our analysis that Target showed more empathy in their posts, a key learning Home Depot could have leveraged in their social media communications. In addition, Home Depot only generated three posts during the time frame relevant to the incident as compared to seven posts by Target. This was another area where Home Depot could have leveraged Target’s communication strategy during the incident. Firms like Target and Home Depot that fall into the category of retail firms with large physical presence need to engage in vicarious learning and leverage from each other’s strategies, especially for sensitive incidents like credit card hacks. Communication strategies that helped one firm in a critical situation, such as a credit card hack along with consequent successful actions taken in response to it, can be documented as best practices for other firms to follow. This can perhaps reduce the frequency of such incidents and in addition, where such incidents happen, can help firms reduce the impact of such incidents on their business.

## 6.3 Limitations and future research directions

Our findings should be interpreted considering following limitations, which point to specific future research directions. First, we focused on retail firms (Target and Home Depot), both having an official was suitable for understanding customer reactions after a crisis because customers have direct visibility of such firms in the form of their physical store locations, the reactions might differ for online-only retail firms such as Amazon and Alibaba. In addition, beyond general retail firms that we focus on, customer reactions might vary for industry-specific retail firms. For example, firms such as Volkswagen (automobile industry) have been a topic of social media conversation due to the recently known emission scandal where the company lied about the level of emissions from their vehicles and manipulated the vehicle emission tests (Bloomberg, 2015). We speculate that while our findings regarding the categorization of customer posts across justice themes and mapping of those themes to customer sentiments are generalizable to other crisis situations, it might be useful to conduct research that specifically investigates this phenomenon across other contexts and industry sectors. Also, given the subjective nature of qualitative coding it is fair to assume that some posts may be ambiguous, which are open to more than one interpretation. For example, there may be a post that could be argued to represent the theme of either distributive or procedural justice. In such scenarios best judgment was used by each author.

Another key limitation of this research is that we specifically focus on Facebook comments. There are other social media such as Twitter and LinkedIn on which firms have an official presence. In addition, the nature of interactions on these social media is likely different from that on Facebook. For example, conversations on Twitter are limited to 140 characters and are known to be directed by one user to another by use of specific hashtags. LinkedIn is typically perceived as a professional network. Consequently, it is possible that the nature of customer reactions on these platforms might differ as compared to those on Facebook. Future research can focus on understanding specific distinctions across social media to analyze if there is an inherent advantage for firms to communicate information via one platform as compared to another.

Finally, our analysis was driven by the justice theory. While there is an inherent advantage of employing the justice theory as an analytical lens because the context was an after-crisis customer reaction, there are theories that could provide insights from other perspectives. Theories from marketing and management literature such as a resource-based theory of firm or a customer value-based theory of firm (Slater, 1997) help bring perspectives related to how an analysis of social media data can provide insights that bring value to organizations. Recent research related to the creation of virtual environments for engaging customers in new product development (Nambisan, 2002) could also utilize techniques such as the sentiment analysis to understand how customer reactions can be leveraged for the creation of such environments.

Our research can also be further extended by including a study on firm responses to customer reactions. While our study primarily looked at how firms can develop intelligence by systematically analyzing customer reactions during crisis events, it does specifically focus on actions firms could take to mitigate any negative effects of the crisis. Further studies that both analyze firms’ actions post crisis and then implement the learnings of this analysis to monitor and control a real crisis are natural extensions of our study.

## 7. Conclusion

When firms face crisis events like security hacks, it becomes important for firms to develop strategies that balance customers’ needs and organizational resources. We analyze customer reactions on official Facebook pages of two retail firms that faced a security hack to identify topics or themes that customers discussed. We developed a method that combines sentiment analysis and qualitative thematic coding using the justice theory that is positioned to help firms build a social media response strategy. While sentiment analysis computes the valence of customer interactions, the qualitative thematic coding helps uncover the different themes or topics that customers talk about during and after-crisis events. By combining these techniques, we not only analyzed the sentiment of a customer’s post but also explain the reason for the sentiment, that is, identify the topic of concern for which the sentiment is expressed.

Findings of this study suggest that customers associate firms having policies and procedures that help them recover any losses with justice, more than the quality of interaction or eventual outcome of the process. In addition, we discovered that subtle differences on what firms convey and how they interact via their official social media spaces has an impact on the overall sentiment toward the firm. This equips firms with better information to take relevant action than just having information about valence of interaction. It also helps firms prioritize specific customer interactions that are strategically important and consequently better manage the work allocation of their staffing resources.
