# Manufacturer driven strategic coordination as a response to “showrooming”

Shounak Basak<sup>a</sup>, Preetam Basu<sup>b,⁎</sup>, Balram Avittathur<sup>b</sup>, Soumyen Sikdar<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Production, Operations and Decision Sciences Area, Xavier School of Management, Jamshedpur 831001, Jharkhand, India

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Multi-channel retail Supply chain management Showrooming Game theory Contract

## A B S T R A C T

“Showrooming”, a practice where consumers visit a brick-and-mortar store to examine and research a product before buying it online, is being increasingly observed in recent times. This not only adversely afects the physical store but also the manufacturer in the long run. Showrooming leads to reduced sales eforts on the part of the brick-and-mortar retailer which leads to a decline in showcasing of the product to consumers. This afects the overall demand for the product in the market. In this paper, we analyze the efect of wholesale prices set by the manufacturer on the retail prices of the products in a multi-channel environment afected by showrooming. To combat the adyerse impact of showrooming, we further investigate the feasibility of a manufacturer-driven alliance with the brick-and-mortar retailer so that it expends adequate sales efort that leads to higher demand and a dedicated consumer base. We derive a three-parameter contract that can coordinate the channel and arrive at a win-win situation for the manufacturer and the brick-and-mortar retailer. Our analysis shows that the contract is more beneficial for a brick-and-mortar retailer with lower relative market potential. Additionally, we find that the contract brings down the retail price of the product which benefits the end consumers.

## 1. Introduction

Have you ever seen something you wanted in a shop, tried it, checked the price online on your smartphone, found it was cheaper, and walked out and bought the product online? Welcome to the world of “showrooming" — BBC News Online.

Showrooming has become an aspect of consumer behavior today as a direct consequence of advances in technology and the proliferation of the internet. Today, consumers can get information about and purchase a product using their smartphones. This raises the threat of show rooming to a new level for brick-and-mortar (B&M) retailers and brings a new dimension to multichannel retail. B&M stores like BestBuy have inevitably been afected by showrooming [1,2].

B&M retailers play an important role by ofering consumers the touch and feel experience of the product. Additionally, they provide attractive shelf-space that draws consumer attention, which is critical for newly launched products. Therefore, manufacturers need B&M re tailers to showcase their products and capture the attention of potential buyers. This is evident from Samsung opening experience stores in New York, not to sell their products, but to enable consumers to experience the product [3]. These stores encourage visitors to check their emails on Samsung devices, watch shows on flat-screen television sets and make calls on cellphones. Other companies like AT&T, Microsoft, Sony Motorola have also, opened experience stores. Even companies selling their product only through the online channel have resorted to opening experience stores to gain consumer base. Recent examples include Oneplus opening an experience store in Bangalore (India) to enable customers to “touch and feel” the product before purchase [4]. Similar strategies have been employed by the travel gear company “Away” and eyeglasses brand “Warby Parker” [5]. Hence, manufacturers realize the value of showrooms in generating demand for their respective products. In many cases, when the manufacturers have constraints preventing them from opening their outlets to provide the product experience, they depend on the B&M retailers. Samsung, the world's largest consumer electronics maker, and BestBuy, the largest electronic retailer, entered into an alliance to set up Samsung experience mini-stores that feature Samsung products inside BestBuy stores [6]. The alliance between Samsung and BestBuy displays the necessity of the product manufacturers to coordinate with the B&M stores to build a consumer base for their products. BestBuy has tied up with other brands like Microsoft, Sony, LG to allow their products to be sold through a shop-in-shop concept [7]. These shop-in-shops use the employees from the product maker which in a way leads to sharing the cost of maintaining the showroom between the manufacturer and the retailer.

It is evident that showrooming is costly for the B&M retailer.

Product manufacturers require the B&M stores to showcase their products to create a consumer base. Sales assistance provided by the B&M stores works toward efective matching of products with the requirements of the consumer. Manufacturers must act smartly given the changing landscape of consumer behavior to address these conflicting objectives.

This paper is an attempt to explore the changing dynamics of multi channel retail under the impact of showrooming. Though the business press has highlighted the efects of showrooming on the B&M retailers, there is a need to academically explore the efects of showrooming in detail [8]. Accordingly, this paper attempts to investigate the following key issues.

1. What should be the prices charged by the manufacturer and the retailers, and the efort spent by the B&M retailer under showrooming?

2. What should be the manufacturer's strategy given the negative impact of showrooming on the efort level of the B&M retailer?

3. What could be the coordinating mechanism that incentivizes the B& M retailer to contribute with higher efort?

To address the problems mentioned above, we propose a coordination mechanism between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer that can boost the profits of both the entities. We derive a discounted wholesale price contract with efort sharing initiated by the manu facturer that enables the B&M retailer to expend adequate sales efort and is beneficial for the manufacturer as well as the B&M retailer. We find that the alliance is more beneficial for the B&M retailer with lower market potential. Additionally, consumers too benefit from this alliance as it drives the retail prices down.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 gives a brief review of the related literature. Section 3 provides a detailed model including unintegrated decision making and the contract set up. In Section 4 we carry out numerical analysis to provide significant managerial insights. Finally, Section 5 gives concluding remarks and limitations of the model.

## 2. Literature review

Our research is primarily a contribution in the domain of multi channel retail by drawing motivation from two lines of literature, first, the efect of non-price factors like sales efort and second, the free riding behavior exhibited by consumers on the pricing strategies of the diferent stakeholders in a competitive multi-channel environment. Additionally, we also contribute to the supply chain contract literature by deriving a contract that helps the manufacturer incentivize the B&M retailer to expend adequate sales efort to address the negative efects caused by showrooming.

We first review the literature on multi-channel retail with a focus on sales efort to increase demand. The efect of non-price factors like sales efort and retail service has been studied comprehensively in the economics literature. Iyer [9] investigated the efort put in by the manufacturer to coordinate the retailers competing in price as well as non price factors such as the provision of product information or service. Tsay and Agrawal [10] studied firms competing simultaneously in service and price where demand is afected by service level and price. Bernstein and Federgruen [11] analyzed a model with demand distributions dependent on price and retail service and studied infinite horizon models under demand uncertainty. Yan and Pei [12] studied the impact of retail services in a dual channel while considering the manufacturer's strategy to increase the retail services. We refer the readers to Wang, Wallace, Shen, and Choi [13] for a detailed review of the efect of service efort in the supply chain. In our paper, we model the impact of sales efort spent by a physical store to display the product attributes to the consumers which subsequently increases product demand in the market. Nonetheless, the presence of multiple retailers can lead to some of the retailers taking advantage of the efort provided by the other competing retailer to increase their market demand, in efect, exhibiting a free-riding behavior.

Since showrooming is related to the online retailer free-riding on the efort expended by the B&M retailer, we study the literature on freeriding behavior. In one of the earliest studies by Telser [14], the profits of the manufacturer was found to be impacted by free-riding behavior. In one of the subsequent studies, Mathewson and Winter [15] found that in the event of one retailer trying to free- ride on the other retailer, the manufacturer prefers channel management mechanisms like retail price maintenance and territorial restrictions aligning with the interests of the retailer. Rey and Tirole [16] derived that lack of control by the manufacturer can give rise to many of the externalities due to competition between the retailers and free-riding is one such phenomenon. The authors also highlighted that manufacturers can place vertical restraints that can curtail these externalities. Carlton and Chevalier [17] found that the retailers reduced the retail service levels when faced with free-riding phenomenon. Wu, Ray, Geng, and Whinston [18] studied a model where some channels provide information about the product and other channels free-ride on it. Van Baal and Dach [19] empirically studied the free-riding behavior employed by consumers and found that 20% of them engaged in it. Shin [20] concluded that the free-riding behavior of consumers benefitted the retailer that free-rides, but along with that the retailer that puts sales efort also benefits by charging higher prices. Bernstein, Song and Zheng [21] proposed that the manufacturers can use the free-riding behavior of consumers by setting up customer service-oriented direct store, to provide the freeriding consumers with the required information and thus, expanding the market base. Iyer and Kuksov [22] studied the implication of shopping experience in the competitive behavior of the retailers in setting equilibrium prices and advertising strategies. Our paper draws from the above literature and our contribution lies in studying freeriding behavior with a specific focus on showrooming.

Showrooming is a specific type of free-riding phenomenon where customers free-ride on the B&M stores for gaining information on the product and finally buying it online. Showrooming has been highlighted by Zimmerman [2]. Mehra, Kumar, and Raju [23] studied three diferent strategies to ofset showrooming that included price matching, charging customers for showrooming and making product matching harder between retailers. Wu, Wang, and Zhu [24], analyzed the choice of a consumer to purchase from a B&M store or an online store based on the associated cost of travel and the role of price matching between the retailers. Balakrishnan, Sundaresan. and Zhang [25] studied the con; sumer behavior to browse and switch in a multi-channel retail set up. Gao and Su [26] analyzed omnichannel consumers who strategically choose where to gather information and characterized the conditions under which consumers engage in showrooming. Gensler et al. [8] analyzed non-price factors that play a key role in consumers' showrooming decisions such as perceived gains in the quality of the product and waiting time for service. Arora and Sahney [27] studied relative search benefits and relative purchase benefits to determine consumers showrooming behavior. Li, Zhang, and Dan [28] analyzed a supply chain where an ofline showroom provides experience service for an existing online retailer and a new competing online retailer that satisfies consumers' heterogeneous demand. Li, Li, and Sun [29] studied the influence of the showrooming efect on a firm's pricing and service efort in a dual-channel supply chain. Basak, Basu, Avittathur, and Sikdar [30] studied the equilibrium behavior of offline and online retailers considering the interaction of power dynamics, market potential, and showrooming. They concluded that higher levels of showrooming not only impacted the B&M retailer negatively but also the online re tailer. The above stream of literature has studied various aspects of consumer behavior and various strategies adopted by the online and B& M retailers in the context of showrooming. However, the strategic behavior of the manufacturer in this multi-channel environment and its role in devising a coordination mechanism with the B&M retailer to curb the negative impact of showrooming has not been studied. There is an incentive on the part of the manufacturer to strategically plan and ofset the negative efects of showrooming. Therefore, in this paper, we study the feasibility of a coordination mechanism between the manu facturer, and the B&M retailer to combat the negative impact of showrooming and enable the B&M retailer to devote more efort to showcase the products. Table 1 gives a summary of the related research in showrooming and positions our paper with respect to the extant literature. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first attempts to model a contractual agreement between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer to counter the negative impact of showrooming.

Next, we study the literature on supply chain coordination in the presence of sales eforts. In one of the earliest work, Taylor [31] studied a model with mean demand dependent on promotional effort and found a four-parameter contract to coordinate the chain. Krishnan, Kapuscinski, and Butz [32] investigated both centralized and decen tralized supply chains in the presence of retailer promotional efort and found the equilibrium prices and service levels. Yan and Zaric [33] extended the work of Krishnan et al. [32] by studying the contracts with demand promotional eforts. The authors considered the manufacturer's online channel to free-ride on the pre-sales efort of the B&M retailer. Our paper considers a broader scenario under the phenomenon of showrooming where two diferent retailers are competing, and we develop a coordination mechanism that provides win-win benefits to the manufacturer and the B&M retailer with respect to an unintegrated structure.

## 3. Model

In this section, we explain the analytical model and the related assumptions. We analyze the competitive dynamics between a manufacturer and two retailers, one an offline retailer and the other an online retailer. The ofline B&M store provides shelf space and sales efort to display the product so that the consumers can get a touch and feel of the product before purchasing it. The store bears a cost for providing these amenities to the consumer. However, because of showrooming, even after getting relevant information about the product from the ofline store, the consumer may not purchase the product from there but do so online.

We consider the retailers to be strategic in deciding their respective selling prices of the product. Additionally, the amount of efort to be expended to showcase the product is also determined by the B&M retailer. Both the retailers purchase the product to be sold from the manufacturer at a wholesale price w. Subsequently, after adding their respective profit margins to the manufacturer's wholesale price, both the retailers determine the respective retail prices. Let, p and $p _ { e }$ denote the retail prices strategically fixed by the B&M and the online retailer respectively. Additionally, the B&M retailer strategically decides to expend an efort θ. θ is the amount of efort expended to showcase and explain the product features to the consumer that might lead to a sale. Therefore, the efort θ aids in increasing the demand for the retailer. However, as mentioned earlier, all this demand may not be materialized at the B&M store. We assume γ as the proportion of demand that the B&M retailer serves. The rest of the demand is captured by the online retailer. As defined by Basak et al. [30], we consider γ as the “retentive component of the B&M retailer's sales efort”. Thus, $1  \gamma$ is the showrooming impact that denotes the proportion of demand that switches to the online retailer. We represent this showrooming impact by $\xi .$ Therefore, $1 - \gamma = \xi .$ . In light of the above assumptions, the B&M retailer, and the online retailer, respectively face the demand given by:

$$
D _ {r} (p _ {r}, \theta) = a _ {r} - b p _ {r} + c p _ {e} + (1 - \xi) \sqrt {\theta}
$$

$$
D _ {e} (p _ {e}) = a _ {e} - b p _ {e} + c p _ {r} + \xi \sqrt {\theta}
$$

where, b measures the responsiveness of the consumer demand to the price ofered by that channel and c measures the responsiveness of the consumer demand to the price set by the competing channel. Here, c denotes the cross-price elasticity between the competing retailers. $a _ { r }$ and a are the market potential of the B&M, and the online retailer, respectively. This indicates the base demand that each retailer obtains in the absence of any price efects. We assume a linear demand function for the retailer relative to its own as well as competitor's prices. This assumption is prevalent in the operations and marketing literature [32,36,37]. Further, the efort (θ) expended by the B&M retailer drives its demand. However, the increase in potential demand faced by the B& M retailer follows diminishing returns to scale with respect to the efort put in by the B&M retailer. The square root function over efort (θ) models this diminishing return of the sales efort with respect to an increase in demand. This is in line with the extant literature on the impact of sales eforts on potential customer demand [38–41]. The demand faced by both the retailers is catered by the manufacturer. Therefore, the demand faced by the manufacturer is given by:

$$
D _ {m} (w _ {c}) = D _ {r} (p _ {r}, \theta) + D _ {e} (p _ {e}) = (a _ {r} + a _ {e}) - (p _ {r} + p _ {e}) + c (p _ {r} + p _ {e}) + \sqrt {\theta}
$$

The B&M retailer maintains the shelves and pays the salespersons who are involved in explaining the product features to the consumers, as part of the efort expended to exhibit the product. Because of this, the retailer incurs a cost for furnishing the sales efort to display the product. We assume this cost to be directly dependent on the extent of the sales efort by the B&M retailer. The cost can thus, be kθ, where k is the per unit cost of expending the sales efort. In this context, we analyze a series of models to understand the dynamics between the various stakeholders.

This wholesale price w is strategically determined by the manufacturer. The structure of the model is shown in Fig. 1. The profit functions of both the retailers are given by:

$$
\pi_ {r} (p _ {r}, \theta) = (a _ {r} - b p _ {r} + c p _ {e} + (1 - \xi) \sqrt {\theta}) (p _ {r} - w _ {c}) - k \theta
$$

$$
\pi_ {e} (p _ {e}) = (a _ {e} - b p _ {e} + c p _ {r} + \xi \sqrt {\theta}) (p _ {e} - w _ {c})
$$

Assuming the manufacturer incurs a cost s to produce the product; its profit function is given by:

$$

Accordingly, the prices set by the retailers are also dependent on the amount of showrooming. We find that the optimal wholesale price set is monotonically increasing or decreasing with respect to the amount of showrooming (viz. “the retentive component of the B&M retailer” [30]). The following Proposition summarizes it.

Proposition 1. The wholesale price set by the manufacturer is increasing/decreasing with decreasing showrooming (ξ) when the B& M retailer's market potential is greater/lesser than that of the online retailer. The wholesale price does not change with the amount of showrooming when the market potentials of both the retailers are equal.

The manufacturer tries to maximize its profit which in turn depends on the impact of showrooming. Decreasing showrooming leads to a higher “retentive component of the sales efort expended by the B&M retailer" which implies higher demand for the B&M retailer. This induces the B&M retailer to expend more efort. This is valid under the condition that the B&M retailer's market potential is higher, leading to more inherent demand. Witnessing this, the manufacturer increases its wholesale price to acquire a higher proportion of the profit generated. With the market potential of the B&M retailer declining compared to that of the online retailer, the phenomenon is reversed and the wholesale price decreases. $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { A l s o } , \frac { \partial \mathbf { w } _ { \mathrm { c } } ^ { * } } { \partial \boldsymbol { \xi } } } \end{array}$ is proportional to the diference in the market potential of the online and ofline retailers $( a _ { e } \mathrm { ~ - ~ } a _ { r } ) .$ . The wholesale price increases with the decrease in showrooming impact, when the B&M retailer has a greater market share. Conversely, with the increase in showrooming impact, the wholesale price increases when the online seller has a higher market potential. In summary, the manufacturer charges a higher wholesale price when both the showrooming efect and the market potential tend to favor one retailer over the other. We further investigate the impact of the retailers' relative market potential on their respective profits and decision variables.

Proposition 2. When the B&M retailer's market potential is lower than that of the online retailer

i) The B&M retailer's profit increases/decreases with decreasing/in creasing showrooming.

ii) The B&M retailer's optimal level of efort increases/decreases with decreasing/increasing showrooming.

The B&M retailer decreases its profit margin as the extent of showrooming grows, to maximize its profit from the higher proportion of demand that it serves. The B&M retailer reduces its efort as the negative impact of showrooming increases which contributes to lower demand. That stops the B&M retailer from increasing its price any further. It is also worth noting that the trend is not followed distinctly when the online retailer's market potential is lesser than that of the ofline retailer.

Under high levels of showrooming $( \xi \ge 0 . 5 )$ and higher relative market potential retained by the B&M retailer, it intends to increase its profit by charging higher price as demand triggered by the efort expended by it is used up by the online retailer (expressions for s′ and $a ^ { \prime }$ have been provided in the appendix). However, when the level of showrooming decreases $( \xi ~ \le ~ 0 . 5 )$ , the demand triggered by the expended sales efort is retained by the B&M retailer. Therefore, when the production cost is low, which implies lower wholesale price, it charges higher price compared to the online retailer to maximize its profit. But as the production cost increases, that is the wholesale price increases, the retail prices of the online retailer as well as the B&M retailer are pushed up. But the B&M retailer, owing to its high market potential can keep its retail price lower in comparison to the online retailer and makes profit through increased demand. With lower market potential for the B&M retailer along with lower levels of showrooming $( \xi \leq 0 . 5 ) ,$ it turns appropriate for the B&M retailer to charge a higher price in order to compensate for the lower demand. In other scenarios, the online retailer optimally charges a higher retail price than the B&M retailer.

One of the important highlights of the previous analysis lies in the fact that the free-riding phenomenon exhibited by showrooming induces a lower efort to be expended by the B&M retailer which leads to a reduction in showcasing of the product to the consumers. The man ufacturer is also directly afected by this as the product does not get properly showcased to the consumer. Taking this into perspective, we envisage a framework where the manufacturer and the B&M retailer form an integrated channel and analyze the ramifications in detail.

## 3.2. Manufacturer – B&M integrated channe

We now consider an integrated channel comprising the manufacturer and the B&M retailer. It is imperative that the integrated channel mirrors the decision-making sequence in the unintegrated setting. In the unintegrated setting, the manufacturer acts as a Stackelberg leader in determining the wholesale price to charge from both the retailers and the retailers determine the retail prices and the B &M retailer specifically determines the efort level through a simultaneous move game considering the wholesale price charged by the manufacturer. Since in the unintegrated case, the B&M retail price and efort, as well as online retail price, are decided after observing the wholesale price, similar decision sequence is modeled in the integrated case. This integrated framework is similar to the one developed by Cai

[43].

We denote, $p _ { i }$ as the retail price of the integrated channel and $\theta _ { i }$ as the efort spent. Considering this, the profit function of the integrated channel can be written as follows:

## 3.3. Discounted wholesale price contract with efort sharing

We develop a contract between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer that brings mutual benefit for both. We find that as the B&M retailer expends efort to display the product to consumers, it is beneficial for the manufacturer to incentivize the B&M retailer to increase this efort. We first try to apply the existing contracts in our setting to explore their usability and the corresponding benefits to the manufacturer and the B&M retailer. We explore a two-part tarif contract and a cost-sharing contract [36,44,45] in our setting. The two-part tarif comprises a discounted wholesale price charged by the manufacturer in return for a fixed fee paid by the B&M retailer. The cost-sharing contract involves the manufacturer sharing the sales efort cost incurred by the B&M retailer. The detailed derivations and the results of the application of the two-part tarif and the cost-sharing contract in our setting are provided in the appendix (A.6). We find that the two-part tarif contract and the cost-sharing contract are unable to incentivize the B&M retailer adequately and in turn fail to achieve coordination. Thus, the total channel profits are less than that obtained in the integrated channel. Therefore, there is a welfare loss while moving from the integrated channel to these contracts. Hence, we attempt to develop a contract that could alleviate this welfare loss and achieve coordination between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer.

We formulate a contract between the manufacturer, and the B&M retailer that emulates the efects of the integrated channel and provides win-win benefits to both. The contract consists of the manufacturer ofering a discounted wholesale price to the B&M retailer and sharing the cost of efort expended by the retailer in return for a fixed fee. Thus, this is a three-parameter contract that attempts to achieve the benefits of an integrated channel (Fig. 5). The mechanism needs to control the quantity of efort that is expended by the B&M retailer so that it optimizes the total profit earned by the manufacturer and the B&M retailer. This is in line with vertical restraints placed by the manufacturer to control the showrooming phenomenon [16].

In the discounted wholesale price contract with efort-sharing, the manufacturer charges the B&M retailer a discounted wholesale price, denoted by $w _ { d }$ and shares $( 1 ~ - ~ \phi )$ of the cost incurred by the B&M retailer to expend sales efort $( \theta _ { c s } ) _ { ; }$ , in return for a fixed fee, $f _ { r } .$ We analyze the cost-sharing component in conjunction with the discounted wholesale price and the fixed fee. The B&M retailer optimizes its profits to strategize its best response in the form of optimum margin to be charged for the product and optimum efort to be put in. Hence, the manufacturer must design the instruments such that the retail price of the product (sum of retail margin and discounted wholesale price) and the efort spent by the B&M retailer are the same as that in an integrated channel. Thus, the B&M retailer's profit can be represented in the following form:

The online retailer also, determines its profit margin per product and in the process fixes the online retail price. At equilibrium, the competing retailers will determine their respective margins along with the B&M retailer determining the optimum efort to be put in. As the manufacturer acts as a Stackelberg leader (as has been explained earlier), it fixes its best response through the wholesale price and the cost sharing proportion to match the profits of the integrated channel. It does so, in return for a fixed fee from B&M retailer who stands to benefit from the contract. This fixed fee $( f _ { r } )$ is the incentive for the manufacturer to ofer a discounted wholesale price and sharing of efort cost to the B&M retailer. Under the contractual agreement, the B&M retailer earns higher profit due to the discounted wholesale price ofered by the manufacturer and the sharing of the sales efort cost. The contract enables the B&M retailer and the manufacturer to reach a win-win situation by earning more profit than under unintegrated decision-making. The intent of the manufacturer to induce the B&M retailer to showcase its products fails under unintegrated decision making. On the contrary, a contract, as defined above, leads the manufacturer to achieve its goal by incentivizing the B&M retailer to showcase its product more and thus, increase the demand for its product. Higher levels of showrooming intensify the increase in sales efort when the contract is in place. Therefore, the contract is efective in countering the negative impact of showrooming that led to decreasing sales efort by the B&M retailer. The alliance between the B&M retailer and the manufacturer not only help the consumers enjoy lower retail prices but also results in higher sales assistance at the stores. Real-life examples such as BestBuy (B&M retailer) renting out space for Samsung where, the salespersons from Samsung (manufacturer) provide knowl edgeable assistance to the customers, thus, sharing the retail efort [7] justify the collaborative approach of the retailer and the manufacturer in reducing the negative impact of showrooming. ## 5. Conclusion

Showrooming is a trend that is increasingly impacting B&M retailers. Consumers use showrooming to check out product features and experience them in B&M stores, and then finally order them online, often at a cheaper price. Hence, the sales efort expended by a B&M retailer does not convert to revenues leading to lesser efort in the fu ture. Though the B&M retailer seems to be more immediately afected by showrooming, the manufacturer is equally afected as it relies on the sales efort provided by these retailers. As the B&M retailer is challenged by the phenomenon of showrooming, the manufacturer must conceive innovative strategies to counter the threat for itself as well as the B&M retailer. This paper is an attempt to study showrooming and develop coordinating mechanisms between the manufacturer, and the B &M retailer so that a win-win situation can be attained.

This study leads us to significant revelations in the context of multichannel retailing under the phenomenon of showrooming. First, we investigate the influence of showrooming on the pricing behavior of the retailers and the manufacturer, and the amount of effort expended by the B&M retailer. Our study examines the efect of showrooming on the wholesale price set by the manufacturer under unintegrated decision making. We find that with an increase in showrooming the wholesale price decreases when a favorable market share is enjoyed by the B&M retailer compared to that of an online retailer. However, the trend reverses with the relative market share of the online retailer becoming higher.

As mentioned at the beginning of the paper, the manufacturer has a strong incentive to facilitate an increase in the level of efort provided by B&M retailer to display their manufactured items as it not only increases the demand temporarily but also helps in creating a dedicated customer base. Therefore, the manufacturer must strategize such that the B&M retailer increases the sales efort. We find that forming an alliance between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer can addres the negative impact of showrooming to a significant efect. Such a contractual agreement is not uncommon in the retail world, with Samsung and Bestbuy serving as a very good example of such an alliance. In this case, Samsung sets up its mini store within a Bestbuy store. The shop inside BestBuy's shop is managed by employees hired by Samsung. This provides the consumers with well-trained salespersons to help them appropriately select their products. The contract enables the B&M retailer to procure products at lower wholesale prices and the manufacturer shares the cost of promoting the product with the consumers in return for a fixed fee. The manufacturer might share the cost of promotion in monetary terms or might put in sales efort at its own expense. The contract leads to win-win benefits for both the manufacturer, and the B&M retailer. The cooperation between the manufacturer and the B&M retailer helps them to hedge the adverse efects of showrooming. We also find that the contract between the manufacturer, and the B&M retailer is a favorable strategy especially for the B&M retailers with lower market potential. The contract ofers more benefits to the B&M retailer with lower market potential to engage in this alliance. Additionally, we find that the contract brings down the retail prices for the products which is beneficial for the consumers. Based on numerical analysis, we derive a decision matrix for the retailers under the proposed contract.

One of the possible extensions of the model is to endogenize the degree of showrooming that afects the B&M retailer. Additionally, we can also consider online retailer prices to be always lower than that of the B&M retailer. This can be used as the basis for consumer preferences and in turn, would help to model the amount of the showrooming that afects the B&M retailer. An empirical analysis showcasing the pricing mechanism under showrooming for various products will be a fruitful research endeavor.
