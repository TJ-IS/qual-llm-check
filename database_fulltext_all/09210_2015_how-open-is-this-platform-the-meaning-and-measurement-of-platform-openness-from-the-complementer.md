## Abstract

Software platforms’ success largely depends on complementors’ willingness to repeatedly invest their time and effort to the development of platform applications that attract users and increase the platform’s installed base. But how can platform providers encourage desirable behaviours by complementors (i.e., application developers) in the absence of formal roles and hierarchical control structures? Although previous studies of software-based platforms have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed openness as critical instrument at the macro (i.e., platform) level and have provided initial attempts to measure the construct, no research has been dedicated to comprehensively conceptualize and operationalize platform openness at the micro level from the perspective of application developers. To go beyond these preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ndings and to theorize about the nature and effects of platform openness as perceived by application developers, we develop a construct called perceived platform openness (PPO). Drawing on recently advanced scale development methodologies, we conceptualize PPO as a multidimensional construct and empirically validate it with important consequent variables linked to developers’ continuous platform contributions. Empirical evidence from several rounds of qualitative and quantitative steps supports the conceptual validity of the construct and empirical relevance of the scale across different smartphone platform contexts (i.e., Apple iOS and Google Android). Researchers will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the study’s systematic and comprehensive conceptualization of PPO, how it is measured, and how it relates to critical application developer beliefs and attitudes. Platform managers may use our results to target the underlying facets of PPO most likely to contribute to the platform’s long-term goals.

## Introduction

n recent years, software-based platforms such as Apple’s App Store with more than one million registered apps or Facebook’s social networking site supporting almost 10 million apps have emerged as a prevalent organizational Imodel for software development and software-based applications (Tiwana et al., 2010). Regardless of whether the focu is on social networking sites, smartphones, or video game consoles, the essential challenge for platform providers is to continuously maintain and nurture an innovative ecosystem around the platform (Cusumano, 2010a). The importance of sustainable software platform management became particularly obvious in the smartphone sector. Despite smartphone sales’ overall growth of 72% in 2010, the previously dominant market share of Nokia Symbian eroded signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly due to the emergence of a new generation of software platforms like Google’s Android OS or Apple’s iOS (Goasduff and Pettey, 2010). These new platforms’ overwhelming success cannot, however, solely be attributed to the hardware’s superior quality or built-in features, but may also be ascribed to the software platforms’ attractiveness to third-party complementors (hereafter also called application or app developers). Unlike in traditional software development contexts in which companies usually draw on a rather limited and homogeneous set of dedicated developer resources, platform-based ecosystems leverage the expertise and ingenuity of a diverse developer community – with skills and appreciation of user needs that platform providers might not possess – to creatively develop new capabilities (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). Constantly attracting new developers or avoiding that existing developers churn to rival platforms are thus important goals for the platform’s long-term viability and success.

Platform concepts have been discussed in many research disciplines so far. Consolidating <sup>fi</sup>ndings from research in marketing, software-engineering, economics, information systems (IS), and industrial organization, Tiwana et al. (2010) de<sup>fi</sup>ne software-based platforms as the extensible codebase of a software-based system that provides core functionality shared by the modules that interoperate with it and the interfaces through which they interoperate (675). In this context, the platform owner, together with third-party developers of complementary modules (or also called applications) and end-users, form a platform ecosystem (Cusumano and Gawer, 2002). Such an ecosystem is typically characterized by indirect network effects: On the one hand, a platform product’s attractiveness for end-users is strongly correlated with the availability of complementary applications. On the other hand, third-party developers are only willing to produce complementary applications if a platform provides suf<sup>fi</sup>cient incentives to ful<sup>fi</sup>l developers’ needs such as value appropriation or recognition (Rochet and Tirole, 2003). In this respect, platform governance, which is generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned as who makes what decisions about a platform (Benlian and Hess, 2007; Tiwana et al., 2010), plays a particularly important role for platform owners to make deliberate choices about platform access, ownership, and control when appropriately involving and engaging app developers. Tiwana et al. (2010) note that governing platforms requires a delicate balance of control by a platform owner and autonomy among independent developers (676f.) and, hence, call for examining the formal and informal mechanisms implemented by a platform owner to encourage desirable behaviors by module developers (680). In a similar vein, Yof<sup>fi</sup>e and Kwak (2006) suggest that a deep understanding of complementors and their individual attitudes and beliefs is crucial for an effective platform governance.

Against this background, platform openness, as a governance-related concept re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the trade-off between retaining and relinquishing control over a platform, has recently received increasing attention in the ongoing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c debate on software-based platforms and platform-centric ecosystems (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Boudreau, 2012; Wareham et al., 2014). However, while a number of studies have looked at platform openness from a macro (i.e., platform) perspective based on objective evaluation criteria (e.g., West, 2003; Boudreau, 2010), there is as yet neither a consistent conceptualization nor a validated measurement scale for assessing complementors’ subjective perceptions of platform openness that re<sup>fl</sup>ect key governance choices on access to and control mechanisms on a software platform.

Capturing the individual perspectives of third-party developers on platform openness is however crucial for two main reasons. First, prior studies have shown that several aspects of platform openness (e.g., a platform’s application review process or applications’ portability to other platforms) are important evaluation criteria for developers (aside from other factors such as the potential <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of apps or the competitive intensity on the platform), when they think about contributing to and engaging on a platform (Bergvall-Kåreborn et al., 2010; Qiu et al., 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Second, individual developers (e.g., hobbyist developers, freelancers, developers in small start-ups) typically represent the bulk of app developers on mobile platforms (e.g., Hilkert et al., 2010) such that their individual beliefs and attitudes should in<sup>fl</sup>uence third-party complementors’ key decisions (e.g., platform choice) and important platform contribution behaviours (e.g., participation in the developer community or word-of-mouth communication with other developers and end-users).

Given the existing calls for research and the important role of application developers and their perceptions of platform openness for enduring platform success, the objectives of this work are to (1) provide a holistic and rich conceptualization of third-party developers’ perceived platform openness (PPO); (2) develop a useful measurement instrument that can be applied across different platform contexts; and (3) examine PPO’s effects on developers’ loyalty with the platform in a nomological network of IS continuance intentions with data gathered from application developers of mobile platforms that are recognized for their different openness regimes.

This study is expected to contribute to the extant literature on several fronts. First, we enhance the existing literature on platform openness by developing a comprehensive and rich conceptualization of PPO grounded in in-depth qualitative and quantitative investigations. Although prior research on platform management has examined how openness stimulates network effects, the focus in much of that literature has been on the perspective of the platform owner or demand-side users (Gawer and Cusumano, 2008; Eisenmann et al., 2009). Only in some instances studies took the explicit perspectives of supplyside complementors (or developers) with much emphasis being on the investigation of independent software vendors’ performance gains through platform participation (e.g.; Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). In the rare cases in which platform openness has been investigated (e.g., Boudreau, 2010), scholars most often did not provide an explicit de<sup>fi</sup>nition of platform openness and mainly treated it as a uni-dimensional and relatively lean construct examined at the platform (macro) level. As such, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to embrace an approach that develops a comprehensive and rich conceptualization of platform openness from an individual (micro) and subjective perspective of platform complementors. Second, and building on this conceptualization, we develop and validate a PPO measurement instrument in the context of mobile software platforms that demonstrates that PPO can have a substantial and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on complementors’ satisfaction with and continuous contribution behaviours on platforms (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Our results also show that PPO is a nomologically valid, multidimensional conceptualization of developers’ perceptions of platform openness that has strong predictive relevance and high sensitivity in capturing variations in openness across different mobile platform contexts. Third, by developing a comprehensive and deep conceptualization of PPO, we help practitioners, in particular platform providers, better understand the factors that form complementors’ perceptions of platform openness and thus the mechanisms by which these factors can be targeted to contribute to the platform’s longterm goals.

The paper is organized in four sections. The next section presents the theoretical background of this study and derives an initial conceptual framework of PPO. Building upon this framework, the penultimate section covers an extensive construct development process that presents the various steps taken to develop a comprehensive conceptualization and measurement instrument of PPO and how it behaves in a nomological network of IS continuance. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal section, we discuss the implications of the results for research and practice and point out the paper’s limitations, as well as promising areas for future research.

## Conceptual background

## Vertical platform openness: diversity vs control

The concept of openness has been investigated in several research disciplines so far, with diverse research foci. Besides possibly the most prominent example in personality psychology as one factor of the Big Five, openness to experience (e.g., McCrae and John, 1992), openness has been examined in natural sciences (e.g., Bertalanffy, 1950), economics (e.g., Mankiw, 2010), personnel psychology (e.g., Wanberg and Banas, 2000), and in education science (e.g., Hodgkinson-Williams and Gray, 2009). Furthermore, to <sup>fi</sup>nd a balance for the trade-off between openness and control of standards in both software and hardware platform markets has a long tradition in the literature on the strategic management of information technologies (e.g., Teece, 1986; Shapiro and Varian, 1998; Cusumano and Gawer, 2002).

Building on the IS platform management literature, Eisenmann et al. (2009) were the <sup>fi</sup>rst to propose an explicit de<sup>fi</sup>nition of platform openness, suggesting that a software platform should be considered open […] if the contribution, the development, the usage, and the commercialization is not restricted, or if all existing restrictions are reasonable and equally applied to all participants (Eisenmann et al., 2009: 1). On the basis of this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, two distinct ways of opening a platform can be distinguished: On the one hand, opening a platform horizontally means giving up some control by licensing the platform to additional platform providers, interoperating with established rival platforms, or broadening a platform’s sponsorship (Eisenmann et al., 2009). On the other, granting third-party complementors access to the development platform and sales market of complementary applications is regarded as vertically opening a platform (Boudreau, 2010). Of these two forms of platform openness, we will focus on vertical platform openness in this study because conceptualizing platform openness from a developer perspective resonates with our intent of closing knowledge gaps in how third-party complementors perceive openness on an individual level and how it in<sup>fl</sup>uences their willingness to contribute to a software platform.

The problem of <sup>fi</sup>nding the right degree of vertical platform openness arises from a fundamental trade-off known as ‘diversity vs control’ (Boudreau, 2010). When a platform is inherently dependent on a continuous supply of complementary innovations, such as on a stream of new smartphone applications, opening a platform to external developers potentially increases the diversity of complementors and their innovations contributed to the platform (Tiwana, 2014). However, opening a platform vertically and the resultant loss of control could pose problems for a platform provider in at least two respects. First, the coordination of resources becomes more complex, simply because more players and interests are involved (Almirall and Casadesus-Masanell, 2010). Second, by delegating the production of complements to external developers, the platform provider also loses control over the assortment and content of complementary platform features provided to the platform and, as a result, over the integrity and strategic roadmap of the platform (Boudreau, 2012). As an example, third-party developers of a mobile platform might prefer to offer game applications, although the platform provider had initially planned to design the platform for personal productivity applications.

While previous studies on platform openness have primarily focused on regulatory implications (e.g., Economides and Katsamakas, 2006; Evans et al., 2006) and platforms technological architecture (e.g., Anvaari and Jansen, 2010; Schlagwein et al., 2010), research on IS platform management with a special emphasis on vertical platform openness has been scarce. Building on <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding companies’ technology strategy, Cusumano and Gawer (2002) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed rather general factors of vertical openness as part of platform leadership strategies. West (2003) introduced the notion of platform openness as being a continuum rather than a dichotomy of purely closed and purely open platforms. More recently, platform openness was considered an instrument to stimulate external innovation (Boudreau and Lakhani, 2009; Boudreau, 2010) and competition (Economides and Katsamakas, 2006).

Despite the importance of understanding and managing vertical platform openness for the success and sustainability of entire platform ecosystems, only few studies have proposed a conceptualization of platform openness (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Schlagwein et al., 2010; Laffan, 2012). While these studies offered no explicit de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and just global and relatively lean approaches to conceptualize and measure platform openness from a platform (i.e., macro) level of analysis, the IS and software platform literatures still lack both a comprehensive and rich conceptualization of platform openness that captures the individual perspective of platform complementors.

The complementors’ perspective as a basis for conceptualizing PPO Previous studies on vertical platform openness have investigated the interdependencies between platform stakeholders based on the assumption that complementors share common goals and intentions regarding their platform-related activities (e.g., Parker and Van Alstyne, 2008; Boudreau and Lakhani, 2009). Consequently, it has been assumed that any of the platform provider’s openness-related stimuli result in the group of complementors reacting uniformly towards the platform. As such, the complementors’ individual motivations that in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decision making have been completely neglected so far. However, as we argue throughout this article, conceptualizing vertical platform openness from complementors’ individual perspective helps better understand the factors that constitute complementors’ perceptions of platform openness and how these perceptions translate into continuous contribution behaviours on a platform.

Assuming the perspective of complementors as a way to conceptualize platform openness is furthermore endorsed by the following two considerations. First, the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of socalled hard-power instruments (Nye, 1990), such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives or threatening sanctions, is limited in a platform-centric ecosystem in which platform providers’ are highly dependent on complementary contributions due to the lack of hierarchal control structures (Goldbach et al., 2014). Hence, to manage complementors, a paradigm shift has been proposed towards instruments that more heavily rely on soft power instruments emphasizing to persuade complementors to consider shared goals and visions (Yof<sup>fi</sup>e and Kwak, 2006). However, this approach requires a deeper understanding of the participating actors, their goals, and decision processes. Investigating complementors’ individual perceptions of openness on an individual level may thus help gain this understanding.

The second consideration is based on the observation that, owing to the low entry barriers (e.g., the low technical upfront costs to develop apps) in vertically open platforms such as mobile platforms, joining a software platform has also become increasingly possible for less professional complementors (West, 2003). This is in particular re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the bulk of hobbyist developers, freelancers, and developers of small startups populating mobile platforms (Hilkert et al., 2010). Accordingly, the assumption of a homogeneous group of platform complementors sharing the same goals and intentions may not hold true in practice. On the contrary, it is rather likely that complementors’ assessment of whether a speci<sup>fi</sup>c openness criterion (e.g., developer programme fee) is perceived as a constraint or reasonable regulatory mechanism depends on their individual perceptions. Accordingly, we argue that conceptualizing and measuring platform openness on the individual level will allow a <sup>fi</sup>ner grained and richer explanation of platform openness perceptions and their subsequent impact on developers’ attitudes and contribution behaviours on such platforms.

## Conceptualizing, measuring, and validating PPO

This paper strives to contribute to a more advanced and richer understanding of how the concept of platform openness is formed in the perception of complementors. Since existing studies offered no systematic de<sup>fi</sup>nition and just global and relatively lean approaches to conceptualize and measure platform openness, no validated measurement scales of the complementors’ perception of platform openness have been reported so far. This study aims at bridging this research gap.

On the basis of the most recent recommendations on construct measurement and validation procedures in IS research (MacKenzie et al., 2011), we divide our construct development approach into four major process steps (see Figure 1). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we introduce a basic conceptualization of PPO derived from previous research and present a high-level framework encompassing four complementary sub-dimensions of PPO that we use as a conceptual foundation for further investigations. Subsequently, we develop and derive lower-level facets of openness that ground the abstract high-level framework on more tangible developer perceptions. The second step is dedicated to develop and re<sup>fi</sup>ne measurable indicators to operationalize PPO in its dimensions and as a whole construct. In the third step, we gathered data from a random sample of Google Android application developers in order to statistically evaluate and validate our measurement instrument. To cross-validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Step 4, we collected a fresh set of data from Google Android and Apple iOS app delevopers allowing us to compare the results across two different mobile platform settings and to investigate PPO’s role in an extended nomological context of IS continuance.

## Conceptualization of the PPO construct

## Deriving a high-level conceptualization of PPO

Our point of departure is a basic de<sup>fi</sup>nition of PPO that serves as conceptual scaffolding around which we will construct a deep and comprehensive conceptualization in subsequent steps. Following Rossiter’s (2002) recommendations on conceptualizing constructs, we consider the attribute of interest (i.e., degree of openness), the focal object (i.e., software platforms), and the rater entity (i.e., third-party application developers) to de<sup>fi</sup>ne PPO at a high-level of abstraction:

Perceived platform openness (PPO) is a software platform’s degree of openness as perceived by its third-party application developers.

On the basis of this basic de<sup>fi</sup>nition and as a conceptual basis for construct development, we subsequently develop a theoretical framework for PPO’s core dimensions by further specify ing the (1) notion of openness (2) on software platforms (Rossiter, 2002; Bollen, 2011).

As the term ‘degree of openness’ already indicates, our understanding of openness draws on West’s (2003) premise that a technology or platform is neither completely open nor completely closed, but somewhere in between these two extreme points. Such a conceptualization not only allows for a more realistic representation of openness on platforms but also permits to derive more practically relevant recommendations when assessing platform openness (West, 2003). Building upon this premise, we reviewed extant literature on platform openness to derive key underlying concepts. Table 1 summarizes implicitly or explicitly derived conceptualizations of platform openness along with their constituent dimensions, focal objects, level of analysis, and basic study methods.

Our review has uncovered two predominant trends pertaining to past theorizations of platform openness. First, the majority of studies conceptualize platform openness as a relatively lean and uni-dimensional construct (i.e., most often as a continuum between access- and control-related aspects), despite having acknowledged it as a complex construct. Second, all of the previous studies conceptualized platform openness on a macro (i.e., platform) level of analysis with a special emphasis on technical aspects of the platform. On the basis of this literature review, we subscribe to West and O’Mahony’s (2008) high-level conceptualization of platform openness encompassing the two distinct sub-dimensions transparency and accessibility that resonates with our intent of capturing a comprehensive and broad notion of openness. In doing so, we build upon a notion of platform openness that conceives platforms as a participation architecture that extends opportunities to poten tial external developers to contribute to the platform on a regular basis. Although West and O’Mahony (2008) neither provide a more profound conceptualization nor operationalization, the conception of platform openness with its two constituent dimensions serves as a useful starting point for further and deeper conceptual development.

Adopting these two dimensions to our context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne transparency as the extent to which it allows third-party complementors to fully understand how to create and distribute third-party applications on a platform, as well as to comprehend and follow all platform-related governance decisions. In essence, transparency is about complementors understanding of what is happening and why (West and O’Mahony, 2008: 151) and, hence, refers to all aspects that in the broadest sense refer to the platform’s information policies. Accessibility, on the other hand, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which a software platform provides resources that allow developers to contribute to the platform by creating and distributing third-party applications without having to face platform-speci<sup>fi</sup>c restrictions (West and O’Mahony, 2008). Hence, accessibility involves all aspects of how developing and commercializing applications of third-party developers is constrained or supported on the platform. At this point, it should be noted that transparency and accessibility should be considered as two conceptually distinct dimensions of openness, since a software platform could very well be transparent without allowing externals to contribute, or vice versa. However, a platform can only be considered completely open if it is fully transparent and fully accessible. Taken together, transparency and accessibility are considered two distinct but complementary dimensions of platform openness.

Referring to the second part of our basic de<sup>fi</sup>nition of platform openness and consistent with the interdisciplinary idea of IS research, software platforms are simultaneously technical and social artifacts (Tiwana, 2014), and thus, the technical architecture and organizing principles of these platforms jointly determine their evolutionary trajectories (Tiwana et al., 2010: 676). Given that our study aims at developing a developer-centric conceptualization of platform openness and given that previous studies have primarily focused on technical components of platforms (see Table 1), extending the notion of platform openness to the distribution components of platforms (i.e., to their marketplaces) provides an opportunity for a richer and more exhaustive conceptualization of PPO. Indeed, third-party developers do not only interact with the technical platform (i.e., development centre) by developing applications but also with the platform’s marketplace as the major distribution channel for their complementary applications (Schlagwein et al., 2010).

In this regard, we refer to technical platform as all facets of a platform related to the technical development of third-party applications including, for example, the provision of APIs and SDKs as well as all kinds of regulatory processes (e.g., quality and content checks), documentations (e.g., help <sup>fi</sup>les) and communications (blogs or forums in the developer community) that go along with application development. By contrast, the platform’s distribution channel refers to all facets of a platform related to promoting, selling, and distributing applications. Besides the functionalities and user experience features provided on platform marketplaces, the terms and conditions for participating and getting access to end-users are all relevant aspects for developers to take into account when assessing a platform’s overall openness (Claussen et al., 2013).

In terms of perceived openness, the technical platform and the distribution channel should be considered conceptually distinct components of software platforms. Platforms may be relatively open in terms of the technical degrees of freedom and support provided in the development centre while, simultaneously, only speci<sup>fi</sup>c functionality or application genres may be allowed to be distributed via the corresponding marketplace. Conversely, platforms that have established less stringent distribution policies on their marketplaces may still fall short with regard to their technical interoperability and compatibility with other platforms.

By including transparency and accessibility as the two de<sup>fi</sup>ning concepts of platform openness, and the technical platform and the distribution channel as a software platform’s major components, we derive four complementary sub-dimensions that serve as a high-level framework of PPO (see Table 2).

## Developing lower-level facets of PPO

Although the high-level framework of PPO helps delineate the construct’s conceptual realm, it still remains at a rather high level of abstraction and thus does not result in easily identifiable dimensions for which items can then be generated (Hinkin, 1998: 107). Accordingly, an inductive approach was performed to conceptualize concrete PPO facets by means of an exploratory investigation. As such, concrete PPO facets were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, classi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and re<sup>fi</sup>ned in a comprehensive process that included open-ended exploratory interviews, a cardsorting procedure, and focus group discussions with expert judges and target raters (see construct conceptualization steps 1–3 in Table 3 for an overview).

In line with recommendations by Rossiter (2002), our qualitative investigations were conducted with the intention of covering the full extent of the developers’ openness perceptions. As such, we decided to deploy open interviews that should motivate the developers to freely describe their associations with the term ‘platform openness’ and subsequently name concrete facets or restrictions of openness they were aware of. This approach is especially well-suited for emergent phenomena providing the broadest possible contextual information for assessment and understanding (Flick, 2009). The criteria to select our interview partners were led by the idea to reproduce the diversity of all developer types with regard to the organizational background (e.g., salaried developer, entrepreneur, hobby developer, etc.) and the type of applications developed (e.g., games, tools, etc.). The size of our sample was determined by a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned stop criterion: The interview procedure was repeated until three successive rounds of interviews did not reveal any new openness facets, which allowed us to assume information saturation (Rossiter, 2002). In total, 10 application developers that were recruited from the iOS and Android developers user group on Xing. com (a social networking website for professionals) were interviewed face-to-face by the authors. Following standard guidelines on conducting qualitative interviews (e.g., Kvale, 2008), we asked open questions (e.g., What do you associate with openness of a software platform from an app developers’ point-of-view? or What aspects of openness of a software platform do you experience or recognize when you develop apps?) to let developers freely associate and think aloud about aspects of platform openness.

After transcribing the interviews, a two-phased content analysis (i.e., segmentation and encoding) was conducted by two independent coders. Phase 1 involved segmentation of participants’ responses to ensure a robust unit of analysis, which is critical in content analysis (Krippendorff, 2004). The unit of analysis chosen for this study is a semantic chunk, that is, a thematic section of the content. A semantic chunk is commonly chosen to capture an idea or an expression because interpretation of subtle nuance in the content is necessary to identify an idea or an expression (Miles and Huberman, 1994). As such, the participants’ responses were segmented into semantic chunks, each of which pertains to only one idea or topic. Phase 2 involved identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a semantic chunk referring to platform openness as perceived by app developers. A total of 23 distinct semantic chunks – agreed upon by the two coders – were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Agreement between the two coders was measured by Krippendorff’s α, deemed the most rigorous measure of agreement among multiple coders. It was 0.82, exceeding 0.70, the acceptable level. All of these semantic chunks were then consolidated into easily understandable and concrete statements.<sup>1</sup> Finally, these statements were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed with one of the four PPO sub-dimensions of our high-level conceptual framework.

To evaluate this initial classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of lower-level facets of PPO, a Q-Sort procedure was performed (Hinkin, 1998; Benlian et al., 2011). For this purpose, a different group of six application developers from the Xing user group was invited and given a thorough description of the four PPO subdimensions, along with a pile of 3×5-inch index cards that each included one openness facet and a corresponding brief description. The developers were then instructed to assign each of the cards either to one of the four sub-dimensions or to an ‘ambiguous’ category. During the task, the developers were asked to think aloud and explain each of the card assignments. We noticed that all judges felt comfortable with assigning each of the facets to either the technical platform or the distribution channel (hit ratio<sup>2</sup> = 89%) as well as to transparency and accessibility (hit ratio = 84%).

After this Q-Sort task, another seven experts on software platforms, of whom three were application developers with more than 4 years of experience and four were IS researchers with a special expertise in software business were invited to participate in a focus group (Morgan, 1997). The speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals of this procedure were (1) to evaluate whether all of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed facets are actually facets of openness, (2) to identify possible overlaps between the facets, and (3) to check if the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed facets are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently inclusive and constitute the main PPO components. The results of this procedure revealed that two facets, namely the platform provider privileges professional developers and the platform provider sets content restrictions without taking into account developers’ interests were found to tap into ‘fairness’ rather than into platform openness. Hence, these two facets were removed from the initial list of items. Furthermore, four facets were deleted because the members of the focus group jointly agreed that these facets were already suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently covered by other superordinate facets. For example, the possibility to get technical support from other developers was removed because the experts considered this facet to be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently covered by the platform offers features to communicate and exchange with other developers. Beyond these issues, all of the experts agreed that the remaining facets cover the content domain of the four PPO components in a comprehensive and mutually exclusive manner. As a result of these three inductive steps of generating lower-level facets of platform openness, we derived a pool of 17 openness facets providing a <sup>fi</sup>ner-grained conceptualization of PPO (see Table 4).

## Development and re<sup>fi</sup>nement of measures

## Specifying the structure of PPO

Before we could derive concrete indicators corresponding to the key facets of PPO, it was crucial for the conceptualization of PPO to determine its conceptual structure in terms of construct speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Construct speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is about identifying the direction of causality between the indicators and the latent variables used to conceptualize a construct. Re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators are affected by the underlying unobserved construct and, accordingly, ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ect’ the construct (MacCallum and Browne, 1993). In contrast, causal or formative indicators are understood as proximal antecedents and cause the construct (Law and Wong, 1999). Owing to this fundamental difference, a possible misspeci<sup>fi</sup>cation could induce both Type I and Type II errors. In order to avoid the risk of potential misspeci<sup>fi</sup>cation, the decision rules suggested by Jarvis et al. (2003) were utilized in construct speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Regarding the relationship between PPO and its main subdimensions, we argue that developers could perceive each of the four sub-dimensions as open, while the other three subdimensions are considered closed. That is, a covariation between the sub-dimensions is possible but not necessary. Furthermore, a platform will only be perceived completely open, if all four sub-dimensions are considered open. Accordingly, the four sub-dimensions are not interchangeable because dropping or adding sub-dimensions would change the construct’s conceptual idea. According to MacKenzie et al. (2011), each of the four sub-dimensions is thus a necessary but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient dimension of the overall construct. For this reason, it can be assumed that the causality <sup>fl</sup>ows from the sub-dimensions to the construct and that the four subdimensions are consequently considered formative indicators of PPO (Bollen, 2011).

The same argument can be made with regard to the relationship between the four sub-dimensions of PPO and their respective lower-level openness facets. Here, each of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed facets (see Table 4) could potentially be perceived as open, while some or all of the other facets are perceived as closed. Similarly, a covariation between the openness facets can be expected but is not a necessity. Since application developers would again regard openness as the sum of these identi<sup>fi</sup>ed facets rather than a common antecedent to all of these facets, the direction of causality is assumed to <sup>fl</sup>ow from the indicators to the sub-dimensions. As a result, we argue that the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed openness facets are considered causal indicators of the PPO sub-dimensions (Petter et al., 2007; Bollen, 2011). Taken together, the resulting structure of PPO can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a formative <sup>fi</sup>rst-order, formative second-order (Type IV) model (Jarvis et al., 2003).

## Developing and pre-testing indicators of PPO

As we elaborated in the previous section, the theoretical and preliminary empirical investigations allude to the PPO construct having a causal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Hence, the procedure proposed for developing re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators, particularly the domain sampling approach of choosing the most suitable subset of indicators from the universe of theoretically possible indicators (e.g., DeVellis, 2003), is not applicable in this case. Conversely, the idea of causal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is that the selected indicators should preferably cover the construct’s entire scope (Petter et al., 2007). In line with this idea of a causal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, the initial pool of indicators had to be suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciently inclusive to cover the above-described content structure of the PPO construct (Jarvis et al., 2003). Accordingly, a pool of formative indicators was generated for each of the lower-level openness facets identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the conceptualization phase (see operationalization steps 4–6 in Table 3 for an overview).

Given the technical requirements for estimating and evalu ating Type IV second-order models, we needed to estimate multiple indicators and multiple causes (MIMIC) models in order to evaluate our measurement instrument’s external validity. This approach requires the development of a redundant set of re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators for identi<sup>fi</sup>cation purposes (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer, 2001; Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009). Consequently, an additional set of 11 re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators was developed that tapped into the general perceptions of PPO and its four sub-dimensions. For the formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective sets of indicators, conventional guidelines were followed, for example, simple language that is familiar to the target raters (i.e., application developers), preferably short statements, avoidance of ambiguity, and consistency in terms of perspective (Hinkin, 1998). All indicators were formulated so that they could be assessed by means of a 5-point Likert scale.

To assess the formative indicators’ content validity and their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation to one of PPO’s four sub-dimensions, a second Q-Sort procedure was performed. For this purpose, another 17 application developers from the Xing user group was given a thorough description of the four PPO subdimensions along with a set of cards with the 17 indicators on it. The developers were then asked to assign each of the cards to either of the four sub-dimensions. During the task, the developers were asked to think aloud and explain each of the card assignments. As in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of Q-Sorts, all raters felt comfortable with assigning each of the indicators to either of the four sub-dimensions. The hit ratios ranging between 79–100% af<sup>fi</sup>rm the validity of the 17 formative indicators representing the lower-level facets of PPO.

Finally, a pre-test was undertaken with <sup>fi</sup>ve application developers and two researchers by means of cognitive interviews (including extensive probing and think-aloud answers) to ensure that all the items were understood as intended. As a result, several of the initially generated items were reformulated to increase their understandability and eliminate possible misunderstandings. The reiteration of this pre-test procedure with three different application developers and two IS researchers revealed no further problems in terms of face and content validity of both the formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators for PPO. Table 5 shows a list of the 17 resulting formative indicators; the 11 re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators are reported in Table 6.

## Evaluation of PPO in empirical study

## Context of empirical study and data collection procedures

An online survey was prepared to collect data for the statistical assessment of the PPO scale. The re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative PPO indicators were included in the survey instrument, along with a few control variables on the developers’ background and demographics. Furthermore, a 4-item satisfaction scale was included to evaluate PPO’s nomological validity drawing from Bhattacherjee (2001). In order to minimize response-set artefacts, the order of the indicators was randomized.

A personalized invitation to participate in the survey was sent via email to 4978 Android application developers. The application developers were selected by crawling all new Android applications and the respective developer contact information via the Android-Market-API for a period of 3 months. This approach ensured that a representative subsample was selected in terms of application genres, and that only active application developers who could actually evaluate the platform openness were invited. Participation was encouraged by offering a free management report of the results after completion of the study. To further increase the response rate, a follow-up email was sent out to all non-respondents after 2 weeks.

## Evaluating the measurement models of PPO

To evaluate the measurement models of PPO, we undertook three steps. First, we assessed the re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators’ reliability and validity that were necessary for the evaluation of PPO given that we estimated a MIMIC model. Second, we checked for multicollinearity among the 17 formative indicators. Third, based on the results of steps one and two, we conducted the MIMIC analysis.

To establish the re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators’ reliability and validity, we assessed the psychometric properties of the re<sup>fl</sup>ective model of PPO (i.e., including only re<sup>fl</sup>ectively measured constructs of PPO) by examining individual item loadings, internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity (Fornell et al., 1981). The loadings of the measurement items on their respective factors were above the threshold value of 0.70, and all were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (P<0.05). Composite reliabilities and Cronbach’s α of constructs ranged between 0.78 and 0.92, and values for AVEs ranged from 0.63 to 0.77. Thus, all of the constructs met the norms for convergent validity. In addition, all square roots of AVE exceeded interconstruct correlations, providing strong evidence of discriminant validity.<sup>3</sup>

Owing to the formative speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of PPO and the underlying multiple regression approach, excessive correlations between the causal indicator variables could pose a problem. Hence, the formative indicators’ variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs) were evaluated (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer, 2001). We found that multicollinearity did not prove to be a problem, since the maximum VIF was 2.11, which is far below the conservative cut-off value of 3.33.

A widely accepted approach to assess the external validity of a formative measurement instrument as a set is to estimate a multiple indicators and multiple causes (MIMIC) model (Jöreskog and Goldberger, 1975; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer, 2001). The nature of a MIMIC model is that a latent variable is directly caused by its causal indicators and is simultaneously re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a set of redundant re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators (Bollen, 2011). Given the content validity of the formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators, a MIMIC model’s acceptable model <sup>fi</sup>t provides evidence of the instrument’s external validity.

The LISREL software (Jöreskog and Sörbom, 2006) was utilized for the analysis of the MIMIC structural equation models (SEM), since the underlying estimation approach allows for evaluating a SEM’s overall <sup>fi</sup>t and is considered highly accurate in terms of parameter estimations. The observed variables’ correlation matrices were used as input to derive standardized solutions (Jöreskog and Sörbom, 2006). Consistent with established recommendations on the evaluation of LISREL estimation results, a number of <sup>fi</sup>t indices were assessed to evaluate the overall model <sup>fi</sup>t (Byrne, 1998). According to the proposed second-order model structure, and in line with previous causal second-order model testing (Barki et al., 2007; Shin and Kim, 2011; Ackermann et al., 2012), the evaluation was performed in two subsequent steps: First, the isolated MIMIC models’ <sup>fi</sup>t was evaluated in respect of each of the four PPO sub-dimensions (see Figure 2a–2d). Thereafter, PPO was assessed as an aggregate second-order construct.

As depicted in Table 7, the estimation of the isolated MIMIC measurement models for the four PPO sub-dimensions resulted in good <sup>fi</sup>t statistics. All openness facets could be validated as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant formative indicators of their respective sub-dimension except for two. The cost of required technical equipment just had a weak impact on TechAccess, while the cost of selling applications in the platform’s marketplace did not prove signi<sup>fi</sup>cant as a formative indicator of DistAccess. However, following Cenfetelli and Bassellier’s (2009) recommendation on handling formative indicators’ non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant weights, we decided to keep these indicators because of conceptual considerations. In our context, it can be argued that Android application developers (i.e., the group of raters in this <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical study) are unlikely to perceive cost of required technical equipment and cost of selling applications as restrictions, because these costs are comparatively lower for the Android platform than for other mobile platform contexts. Evaluations of PPO in other platform contexts may thus show that these two facets play a more critical role (in our crossvalidation study reported in §3.4, these two facets were indeed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicators in the iOS platform context).

For the evaluation of PPO as an aggregate construct, a model was estimated with PPO (identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by its re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators) explained by its four sub-dimensions and their facets (see Figure 3). Evaluating the structural model and nomological validity of PPO

The nomological validity of the PPO construct was assessed by linking PPO to the developers’ overall satisfaction with their platform experience. The theoretical rationale for the relationship between perceived openness and satisfaction stems from well-established <sup>fi</sup>ndings in social psychology. On the basis of self-determination theory by Deci and Ryan (1985), it was found that circumstances that limit task autonomy in a possibly controlling manner undermine feelings of self-determination. Hence, in the context of IS usage, it is argued that limited task autonomy reduces the perception of self-determination and, vice versa, an open environment that provides the perception of unrestricted participation possibilities increases the self-determination and satisfaction with the IS (McKeen et al., 1994). Transferring these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the given context of software-based platforms that can be viewed as ISs used by developers to produce and distribute complementary applications, we argue that complementors’ platform openness perceptions are positively related to their overall satisfaction with the platform. Accordingly, the relationship between PPO and overall satisfaction was tested to assess PPO’s behaviour in its nomological network.

With χ<sup>2</sup> = 768.04, d.f. = 323, AGFI = 0.76, RMSEA = 0.068, NFI = 0.9, and CFI = 0.94, the model’s overall <sup>fi</sup>t was slightly better than the <sup>fi</sup>t of the MIMIC model for PPO. Furthermore, as reported in Figure 4, all path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients within the aggregated PPO construct differed only slightly from the PPO MIMIC model’s coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, supporting the robustness of our speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Regarding the highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between PPO and satisfaction (β = 0.8, P<0.001), and the ratio of explained variance of satisfaction $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 2 )$ , the hypothesized relationship between the developers’ perception of openness and their overall satisfaction with the platform was strongly con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Hence, we conclude that our measure of PPO demonstrates adequate nomological validity.

## Cross-validation of PPO across mobile platform contexts

We conducted a follow-up empirical study across two different mobile platform settings to address three objectives. First, the applicability of PPO should be cross-validated to demon strate that PPO is sensitive enough to capture differences in PPO across platforms that are known for their different openness regimes. Second, we aimed at validating PPO’s behaviour in a larger nomological network of IS continuance, adding perceived usefulness and developers’ behavioural intentions to continue contributing to a platform to our research model. Third, to check for robustness across different SEM techniques, we applied PLS-based SEM in addition to covariance-based techniques which is recommended for newly developed measurement instruments (Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009).

PPO in an extended nomological network of IS continuance To examine PPO’s effect behaviour in a larger nomological network, we added perceived usefulness and developers’ intention to keep contributing to their mobile platform to our research model. This was motivated by Bhattacherjee’s (2001) post-acceptance model of IS continuance that argues that users’ continuous usage behaviour is primarily affected by their satisfaction with and perceived usefulness of the IS. Both factors are in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by performance perceptions of important IS variables.

Transferring this theoretical logic to our context, developers’ intentions to continuously contribute to a platform can also be hypothesized to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their satisfaction with and perceived usefulness of the platform. More importantly for our cross-validation purposes, we argue that PPO should positively affect developers’ satisfaction with and perceived usefulness of the platform and in doing so, should increase developers’ continuance intentions.

SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al., 2005) was used to test the relationships of our extended nomological network for PPO. The choice of a PLS-based approach was motivated by its appropriateness for explorative investigations and its ability to estimate complex models that include many indicators despite medium sample sizes (Chin, 1998). Furthermore, Cenfetelli and Bassellier (2009) recommend to cross-validate newly developed measurement instruments using covariance-based as well as component-based SEM approaches.

Estimates of standard errors for testing the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients using t-tests were obtained based on a bootstrapping resampling procedure (Chin, 1998). The results in Figure 5 indicate that the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between PPO and both satisfaction and perceived usefulness are highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive for both mobile platform contexts. In addition, the results show that PPO contributes to affecting developers’ continuance intentions via an indirect mechanism through satisfaction and perceived usefulness and thus conforms to the post-acceptance model of IS continuance (Bhattacherjee, 2001).

The predictive relevance of PPO was also tested by means of the nonparametric Stone-Geisser test that shows how well the data collected empirically can be reconstructed with the help of the model and the PLS parameters. The Stone–Geisser-Q<sup>2</sup> value for PPO was well above the threshold level of zero for both the Android $( Q ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 1 9 )$ and iOS $( Q ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 7 0 )$ subsamples, thus validating PPO’s predictive relevance. In sum, the hypothesized behaviour of PPO in its extended nomological network was supported in our cross-validation study indicating strong nomological and predictive validity for PPO.<sup>5</sup>

## Known-groups comparison

An important step in assessing a scale’s validity is to evaluate whether the developed indicators are accurate representations of the underlying construct (MacKenzie et al., 2011). A method to test this for a set of newly developed indicators is the known-groups comparison technique, which suggests that if two identi<sup>fi</sup>able groups are for a certain reason expected to differ in their judgment of the construct of interest, the mean measurement results of these groups should also differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Since with iOS and Android, we are investigating two platforms whose approaches to opening their platforms to external developers are widely acknowledged to differ in the sense that the Android platform is perceived as more open than the iOS platform (Cusumano, 2010b), PPO should be able to adequately capture these differences in openness perceptions. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we would expect Android developers to exhibit higher levels of PPO than iOS developers.

## Discussion

This paper advances a conceptualization of software platform openness that sheds light on how platform openness may be construed from an individual, developer-centric perspective and how it is vital in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing continuous developer contributions on platforms. The study also empirically develops a measurement instrument that encompasses a comprehensive yet succinct suite of openness facets forming perceptions of accessibility and transparency on both the technical (i.e., the app development centre) and distribution (i.e., the app marketplace or app store) side of a platform, which has been derived via a combination of deductive and inductive techniques.

## Key <sup>fi</sup>ndings and insights

There are several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study. First, our developer-centric and multidimensional conceptualization of PPO appears to be a valid and relatively robust representation of developers’ appreciation of platform openness. Coupled with strong discriminant validity among the four dimensions forming PPO, we can conclude that our conceptualization of PPO not only comprises distinguishable facets of openness. PPO is also collectively more exhaustive than previous conceptualizations in that it covers both the technical and the business realm of software platforms which has been overlooked so far in past studies.

Second, the direct and indirect effects of PPO on important downstream outcome variables such as satisfaction, perceived usefulness, and continuous contribution intentions seem to be strong and robust across different mobile software platforms as all of the nomological validity tests are corroborated by our empirical evidence. As such, the construct ‘PPO’ is not only a sound representation of platform openness in itself, but is equally informative in explaining developers’ attitudes and behavioural intentions towards the platform. Moreover, results from the Stone–Geisser test and known-groups comparison attest to the predictive relevance and sensitivity of PPO in predicting these important outcome variables across different mobile platform contexts.

## Theoretical contributions

From a theoretical viewpoint, this study contributes to a comprehensive and rich conceptualization of software platform openness from a developer-centric perspective, the lack of which has constrained our conceptions of openness to rather technical and unidimensional aspects. A prevailing proposition in the strategic platform literature that has gone unchallenged in the realm of software platforms is the delineation between the development and distribution parts of platforms in theorizing PPO. The conventional focus on technical components of platforms has led to the misconception that there are no other relevant areas for third-party developers that affect their perceptions of platform openness. The advent and success of app marketplaces such as Apple’s App Store or Google’s Play Store through which developers’ apps are made public and accessible to potential users, however, has made such a distinction prominent. By theoriz ing PPO as openness facets that emanate from a complementary blend of technical and business components, this paper provides a more holistic account of how developers form their perceptions about a platform’s openness and acknowledges that their perceptions are not only shaped by technical platform features but also by the degrees of freedom provided on the corresponding marketplaces of such platforms.

Although scholars have provided conceptualizations of platform openness from an aggregated developer perspective (e.g., Ceccagnoli et al., 2012), there is paucity of studies that delve into developers’ individual perceptions of platform openness. Consequently, via a combination of deductive and inductive techniques, we derived a delineation between a platform’s development and distribution components as well as between a platform’s accessibility and transparency as distinct but complementary elements forming platform openness. Further, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed lower-order openness facets that, when combined, constitute a prescriptive model detailing developmental actions for manipulating the openness of platforms. By subjecting the conceptualization of PPO and its effects to subsequent empirical testing across different plat form contexts, we not only verify the robustness and practicality of our conceptualization of PPO, but also ascertain the causal chain linking PPO to developers’ platform-related beliefs, attitudes, and behavioural intentions.

Our study expands on extant literature in several ways. First and foremost, this work contributes to research on software platform governance. The conceptualization of PPO proposed here complements and advances prior work in this domain that is largely at the macro (i.e., platform or <sup>fi</sup>rm) level and primarily focused on the platform provider und demand-side user perspective. Although platform openness has long been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as key structural instrument to govern the relationship between the platform and its key stakeholders, this work is – to the best of our knowledge – the <sup>fi</sup>rst to shed light on a comprehensive conceptualization of platform openness from the individual perspective of supply-side users (i.e., complementors). We conceptualize PPO as a multidimensional, second-order construct formed by four factors that collectively contribute to the explanation of important downstream variables (i.e., satisfaction and perceived usefulness) that may help predict developers’ continued platform contributions. Knowing the constituting dimensions of application developers’ PPO thus advances our understanding of their motivations to continuously contribute to a platform and thus provides a theoretical perspective to platform governance. More broadly, PPO serves as unique conceptual lens linking platform owners’ governance choices to complementors’ platform openness perceptions and subsequent attitudes and behaviours. This is an important result given that harnessing outside expertise and ingenuity has been found to be a crucial element of platform innovation, and ultimately of platform success.

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings constitute a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical development because this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst of its kind to theoretically differentiate and empirically establish a platform’s development and distribution components as distinct contributors to a platform’s openness as perceived by thirdparty developers. Past studies tended to neglect the importance of marketplaces as being a vital driver of platform openness (e.g., Boudreau, 2010; Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). As such, our study paves the way for an extension of previous platform governance research by advancing a more comprehensive and richer conceptualization of platform openness.

Third, the delineation of PPO into <sup>fi</sup>nely grained openness facets, through a blend of deductive and inductive techniques, opens up the black box of PPO. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identify 17 openness facets for software platforms that, when considered together, contribute to a developer’s overall PPO. While previous studies have used only lean and unidimensional conceptualizations (and operationalizations) of platform openness, there are several independent and distinct openness facets that are uniquely elicited from our interviews and cardsorting techniques. The empirical validation of these openness facets substantiates the prominence of each of these elements in affecting developers’ overall perceptions of platform openness and also veri<sup>fi</sup>es the conceptualization of PPO as a multidimensional construct.

On a more general note, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study – although constrained to the context of mobile software platforms – hint at the possibility of differentiating between IT- and business-oriented components for a wider spectrum of software platforms when conceptualizing platform openness. There are two justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for our position. First, given the growing importance of the Internet as digital infrastructure for new software services (e.g., software-as-a-service or locationbased services), software providers increasingly open up their various development platforms (e.g., for stationary PC, mobile, video game console, or social networking platforms) as well as their corresponding distribution channels to thirdparty developers to leverage their external expertise (Tilson et al., 2010). Second, as demonstrated by our empirical evidence across different mobile platforms, all of the 17 openness facets do not cater to speci<sup>fi</sup>c mobile contexts. As such, they should be transferable to contexts other than mobile software platforms such as video gaming platforms or social networks.

## Practical implications

In ecosystem environments in which persuading third-party developers to contribute to a platform on a regular basis is a cornerstone for sustainable success, a clear conception of developers’ perceptions of platform openness is an invaluable tool for platform providers. However, platform providers are oftentimes challenged by the lack of actionable openness standards for designing their platforms (e.g., Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). To combat this, our proposed conceptualization of PPO may perform two vital functions: (1) it derives a taxonomy of generic openness facets along four complementary but distinct dimensions (i.e., TechTrans, TechAccess, DistTrans, DistAccess), and (2) it validates the practical value of these facets across different mobile platform contexts. In this sense, our study drew upon different openness regimes of mobile platforms to derive and validate a rich set of openness facets that can be leveraged by platform providers to adjust and enhance openness-related design features of their platforms. Our model can, therefore, act as an analytical toolkit for platform providers to (1) pinpoint missing or inadequately addressed openness facets on platforms, and (2) decide whether the inclusion or enhancement of these facets can overcome openness inadequacies given the nature of the overall openness regime chosen by the platform provider. Accordingly, the diverse set of openness facets uncovered in our study may be used as key design parameters to consider when enhancing the platform’s technical architecture and marketplace.

Our investigation further indicates that the business side (i.e., the marketplace) of a platform cannot be divorced from the technical side (i.e., development centre) when addressing platform openness as perceived by third-party developers. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that the openness factors related to the distribution channel of platforms can be regarded as being equally predictive of PPO as evidenced by their almost equivalent path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. As such, coupled with strong discriminant validity among all higher-order openness factors, we can conclude that the openness dimensions related to a platform’s distribution channel not only comprise distinguishable elements that have to be taken into account when targeting developers’ appreciation of platform openness. They are also equally informative in explaining developers’ overall perceptions of platform openness and important downstream outcome variables.

## Limitations and further research

This section highlights several limitations to our study that provide avenues for future research. First, our results’ external validity is limited to the context of mobile platforms. Although our multidimensional conceptualization of PPO has been derived via a combination of deductive and inductive techniques in the context of smartphone platforms, we believe that all of the derived openness facets are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently generic and abstract to be transferable to other platform contexts. None theless, an opportunity for future research may be to investigate whether and how these openness facets are applicable to other platform ecosystems such as social networking sites or video gaming platforms.

Second, the focus of this study was on the conceptualization, measurement, and predictive validity of PPO from a developer perspective. As such, we explicitly neglected other factors affecting developer perceptions and behaviours (e.g., application pricing, platform competitiveness) and did not consider other perspectives from users or platform owners. An opportunity for future empirical research may thus be to examine how PPO compares to these other factors in driving developer attitudes and actual behaviours and how developer perceptions are congruent or incongruent with other perceptions (e.g., Benlian, 2013; Benlian, 2014).

Third, despite the rigorous methods we used to identify and validate the most salient facets forming PPO, we acknowledge that this set of facets might not have been exhaustive.<sup>6</sup> For example, although the scope of functionalities supported on a platform has been recognized as openness facet, altering the way in which components function on the technical platform has not been explicitly mentioned in our interviews. Furthermore, distribution channel substitutability (i.e., the <sup>fl</sup>exibility to distribute apps via different channels) has also not been explicitly mentioned by developers. Although we surmise that these aspects were of less salient importance to developers or conceptually captured by other facets (e.g., technical interoperability or scope of functionalities accessible on a platform), future research should explore how PPO can be complemented or extended by other distinct openness facets.

Fourth, since our conceptualization of PPO was derived and tested using cross-sectional data, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide only a snapshot in time. Since developers’ perceptions of platform openness are subject to change, longitudinal studies can be useful for enhancing our understanding of the dynamics of PPO over time. Future research that pays special attention to changes over longer periods might shed additional light on the evolution of openness perceptions and subsequent effects on developers’ contribution behaviours on software platforms.

Overall, using the concept and measure developed in this study, future research should build and test theories about PPO. Some of the important questions that need to be answered include: (1) How do different platform governance con<sup>fi</sup>gurations (e.g., central vs decentral allocation of decision rights) affect developers’ PPO? (2) What are the effects of PPO on positive (e.g., acceptance, stickiness, creativity) and negative (e.g., complaints, resistance, switching) developer reactions? (3) Under what conditions (e.g., open vs closed source environments, formal vs informal control mechanisms on platforms) does PPO become more or less critical to developer attitudes and decisions? (4) How can platform providers design governance-related mechanisms on their technical and distribution platforms to favourably in<sup>fl</sup>uence developers PPO? Future research answering these questions would not only help develop and test unique and novel theories about a comprehensive cause-and-effect chain from platform owners governance choices via key platform openness perceptions to complementors’ actual platform decisions. It would also help platform owners manage their developer base more effectively to enhance platform competitiveness.

## Conclusion

While scholars and practitioners have recognized the relevance of providing a clear notion of openness for the design and governance of software platforms, past studies have failed to come up with a rich and developer-centric conceptualization either due to skewed emphasis on technical aspects of platforms or due to the lack of research on the individual level of analysis. This study is hence novel in that it closes the gap of a comprehensive understanding of platform openness by advancing a middle-range theory of PPO that gives equal prominence to both technical and business components of platforms. Moreover, through a comprehensive scale development process, we conceptualized, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and validated a measurement instrument that may support a wide range of studies as researchers attempt to understand openness-related issues in platform ecosystems. Although individual researchers may focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c openness-related issues arising in the platform context, and may need to develop or re<sup>fi</sup>ne measures for their own specialized scales, the existence of a validated baseline measure should be a major advantage. It provides a good starting point that will speed up and enhance the quality of measurement for many studies. Further, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst of its kind to shed light on the role of PPO in encouraging desirable behaviours by application developers on mobile platforms. As such, we believe that this research provides a basis for platform managers to enhance their platform governance con<sup>fi</sup>gurations and commu nication policies, and offers academics a foundation for future efforts to re<sup>fi</sup>ne our understanding of complementors’ beliefs about and contributions to software platforms.
