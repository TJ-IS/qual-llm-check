Abstract: The market for security software has witnessed an unprecedented growth in recent years. A closer examination of this market reveals certain idiosyncrasies that are not observed in a traditional market. For example, it is a highly competitive market with over 80 vendors. Yet the market coverage is relatively low. Prior research has not attempted to explain what makes this market so different. In this paper, we develop an economic model to find possible answers to this question. Our model uses existing classification of different types of attacks and models their resulting network effects. We find that the negative network effect from indirect attacks, which is further enhanced by value-based targeted attacks, provides a possible explanation for the unique structure of this market. Overall, our results highlight the unique nature of the security software market, furnish rigorous arguments for several counterintuitive observations in the real world, and provide managerial insights for vendors on market competition.

Key word s and phrases: market structure, mass attacks, negative network effect, network effect, oligopoly, pricing, security software, strategic hacker, targeted attacks.

In a society that d epend s on computer-based systems and network s for almost everything, security of computerized systems is critical for individuals and organizations alike. Because of the severe need for protecting valuable information assets in today’s networked world, the industry of security software, along with those of hardware and services, has grown rapidly. According to Gartner [15, 16], the worldwide security software market has expanded substantially in recent years—from \$6.4 billion in 2004 to \$16.5 billion in 2010. Based on Gartner’s annual market share analysis reports, Figure 1 depicts how the worldwide market has grown in the past few years. As can be seen from this figure, this market has witnessed a double-digit growth rate every year between 2005 and 2010, with 2009 being the lone exception because of the recent economic slowdown. Understanding the nature of this rapidly expanding market, along with its evolution and trends, is of importance to vendors as well as consumers.

In general, security software can be classified into two main categories: (1) offthe-shelf third-party standalone tools, such as antivirus, antispyware, Web filter, antispamware, and antiphishing, for different operating systems, and (2) system components, such as encryption software and firewalls that are often bundled with different operating systems. In this paper, we examine only the first category, which dominates the security software market and has several major players, including Symantec, Trend Micro, McAfee, Kaspersky, and dozens of other companies. A recent survey [38], for example, has found that there are 81 antivirus vendors (with 257 antivirus products) in North America and 87 vendors worldwide (with 367 products).

In the typical off-the-shelf software market, users usually enjoy a higher network benefit from a larger market share, which is often referred to as the positive network effect [25, 26]. This positive network effect primarily arises from users’ need for compatibility—the need to share files and information, to edit and critique documents created by others, and most importantly, to work in a collaborative setting. It is well known that positive network effects can lead to a near-monopoly market condition: if a vendor’s market share is large enough to exceed a critical mass, other competitors will lose opportunities to enter the market [39]. Studies on markets for off-the-shelf application software, such as spreadsheets and word processors, empirically validate this near-monopolistic structure [5].

The market for third-party security software, however, is markedly different. As mentioned above, this market is characterized by many vendors, with no single dominant player. Symantec, which once led this market with more than 50 percent of the industry sales, currently holds less than 20 percent of the market share [16]. In fact, the total market share of the top 5 antivirus software vendors is below 45 percent. Many of the current antivirus products did not exist in the market even a couple of years ago. Giarratana and Fosfuri [18] found that between 1989 and 1998, at least 270 vendors entered this market, with a very high percentage not surviving beyond the first two years [17]. Despite the presence of a large number of vendors, the price for this type of software has remained relatively high. For example, an annual subscription to Kaspersky Internet Security 2012 can cost as much as \$80. At the same time, a two-year subscription can cost up to \$160, well above the price of a perpetual license of Microsoft Word 2010, a product that enjoys substantial monopoly power.

Furthermore, in spite of so many choices in the marketplace, a significant percentage of individual computers are still lacking in basic protection [20, 30]. A recent study on online banking finds that as many as 47 percent of household finance managers (those who manage their household finances using online banking) do not use any antivirus software at all [10]. These observations naturally lead to several questions:

•	 Why has the market coverage been so low despite the market being competitive?

•	 What explains such high prices in a competitive market?

•	 What makes this market so attractive to new entrants?

The objective of this research is to develop a quantitative model to find possible answers to these questions and obtain useful insights about the market.

It is difficult to explain the competitiveness observed in this market under the assumption of positive network effects. In reality, positive network effects are unlikely to be significant for most security software. Security software is simply used to prevent security exploitations, and there is hardly any benefit from the compatibility of user data.<sup>1</sup> Therefore, we start with a setting without positive network effects. We consider two types of attacks—mass and targeted. When only mass attacks are considered, there are two benefits to adopting security software: (1) a direct benefit representing the value of thwarting direct security attacks by hackers and (2) an indirect benefit arising from the prevention of indirect attacks or infections from other users in the network. In the case of an indirect attack, a system is not a direct target, but could become an eventual target from the security exploitation of another system. Examples of indirect attacks abound, as evidenced by the prevalence of Internet worms [4] and the wide presence of botnet agents that can launch large-scale attacks with the ability to convert ordinary nodes into malicious agents [19, 37].

A recent report finds that most of the well-known botnets involve several million bots or compromised computers—for example, the Bredolab and Conficker botnets are supposed to have about 30 million and 13 million infected machines, respectively [19]. Each month, hundreds of thousands of new machines are being added to some botnet, and the total annual loss from this type of indirect attacks is currently estimated at a whopping \$10 billion [19]. In our model, the user’s indirect benefit eventually leads to a type of negative network effect—the larger the total market coverage of security software, the less is the indirect benefit because the chance of getting infected from others reduces. This is analogous to the case of vaccines for infectious diseases—a consumer’s incentive to obtain a flu shot decreases when more people around him or her are already vaccinated [32].

We find that in the presence of mass attacks, there is a unique symmetric equilibrium in the oligopoly market: as the negative network effect caused by indirect attacks increases, the equilibrium price rises but the market coverage shrinks. Thus, we find evidence that this negative network effect can explain many of the unique characteristics of the security software market, such as the prevalence of higher prices and lower coverage, which otherwise appear counterintuitive. We further find that, notwithstanding the shrinking market coverage, the industry can make higher profits by charging consumers handsomely for indirect benefits that are often substantial in a deficiently covered market. We argue that this higher profitability, in turn, means that an oligopoly market is particularly attractive to entrants when compared to a market without such an effect,<sup>2</sup> a result consistent with the empirical observation by Giarratana and Fosfuri [18] that the security software market has historically attracted a very large number of vendors. We also carefully study the effect of a new entry into the oligopoly market and find that the new entrant is likely to adopt a more aggressive strategy here than in the traditional market; as explained later, this result, too, is reflective of the realities of the security software market. Overall, we find that when this negative network effect is included in the economic analysis, it leads to results that coincide with the unique nature of the security software market, with important implications for vendors.

When targeted attacks are incorporated into the economic model, they affect the market in two ways. First, when hackers specifically target unprotected, vulnerable computers, the benefit to a user increases with the market coverage, resulting in a positive network effect. Second, targeting of high-valuation users supplements the negative network effect from indirect attacks. We find that it is actually the combined strength of these network effects that determines the market structure in the end. When the combined network effect is negative in nature, the results from our model coincide with real-life observations, suggesting that the negative network effect likely dominates the security software market.

Toward the end of the paper, we explain what would happen if hackers strategically adjust their activity levels based on their costs and benefits. We argue that strategic hackers lower their activity level as more users adopt security software, resulting in a different type of negative network effect. We find that even in the presence of this new network effect, the basic insights remain applicable, although this new effect by itself cannot fully explain the unique structure of the market.

## Literature Review

The behavior of a mark et for a technol og y prod uct in the presence of positive network effects has been extensively studied [12, 25, 26]. This stream of research recognizes the existence of an additional utility, which depends on the vendor’s market size, in addition to the utility derived directly from the product. However, the market for security software is quite different because a user’s valuation of a security product often diminishes with an increasing market size. Png and Wang [34], for example, identify negative network externalities arising from strategic hacker behavior to be a possible explanation for users’ inertia in taking security precautions. We also consider this issue. In addition, we explicitly model other forms of network effects that may arise from different types of attacks and hacker behavior.

We examine two main types of attacks on information systems—mass and targeted attacks [34, 35]. Mass attacks involve both direct and indirect attacks. The rate of indirect attacks increases with the number of breached nodes, which in turn increases with the number of unprotected nodes. As a result, the larger the number of protected nodes in a network, the lower is the value of security software to a consumer, implying that indirect attacks lead to a negative network effect in the security software market. August and Tunca [2, 3] and Lahiri [31] also recognize the existence of this type of network effect. There is, however, a fundamental difference between their work and ours. Their context is that of an application software: A large network of unpatched users reduces the value a consumer gets from the application. In our case, however, the context is the security software market where the effect is exactly the opposite— here, a large number of vulnerable nodes threaten consumers’ security and increases their willingness to pay for security solutions.

While modeling targeted attacks, we consider the fact that such attacks can be of different varieties [35]. These attacks can be value based, as hackers can preselect high-valuation users as their targets. We find that such targeting augments the negative network effect from indirect attacks. On the other hand, when hackers target vulnerable computers, it leads to a positive network effect, which pulls the overall network effect in the opposite direction. Finally, we consider that hackers may act strategically and adjust their effort level based on the size of the user base of security software [8, 34]. Such hacker behavior leads to a new form of negative network effect, with very different implications for the competitive equilibrium. Galbreth and Shor [14] explore another type of strategic behavior by hackers who specifically target popular systems or platforms, resulting in product-level or “localized” network effects. Such effects seem to have little relevance in our context and are therefore not considered in this work. Nonetheless, we contribute to the literature by tracing different types of network effects to their root causes—different types of attacks and hacker behavior—and by analyzing the impacts of these network effects on the market structure.

Our research is also related to the literature on the economics of information systems security. This stream has grown substantially in recent years. Gordon and Loeb [20] consider the investment decision for information system security and develop an economic model that weighs the cost of security against the expected loss from attacks. In a subsequent empirical work, Gordon and Loeb [21] find that this kind of economic analysis is widely used in practice for security investment decisions. More recently, however, Cavusoglu et al. [7] find that a game-theoretic approach actually leads to a more effective security investment decision when compared to such decision-theoretic approaches, whereas Herath and Herath [22] propose a real-options model for the same purpose. Anderson and Moore [1] and Varian [43] look at the provision of security from the perspective of underlying incentives, legal liability, and network externalities.

Kumar et al. [29] and Sun et al. [40] provide economic tools necessary for assessing security risks and countermeasures. Giarratana [17] undertakes an empirical analysis of the number of existing vendors in the security software industry, and Giarratana and Fosfuri [18] evaluate the post-entry strategy for start-ups in this industry. These last two studies are of particular interest to us because they reveal the extreme level of fragmentation existing in this market. However, they do not provide an economic explanation of what leads to such fragmentation or to what types of attacks and network effects it may be attributed. These are the issues that we focus on, along with those involving a relatively lower market coverage and an unexpectedly higher price. We provide an extensive analysis of the competitive equilibrium under different types of attacks and hacker behavior. We augment prior research by explaining the implications of different types of network effects for vendors in this market.

## Hacker Behavior and Different Types of Attacks

Based on prior l iterature, we assume that hackers engage in two types of attacks: mass attacks and targeted attacks [33, 34]. These types of attacks can be explained by the choice and chance models of hacker behavior [35]. Mass attacks essentially follow a path of chance—here, hackers create and distribute exploits with the objective of breaching the security of as many computers as possible, and they do not choose specific targets. Targeted attacks follow a path of choice, where hackers primarily preselect certain targets. Finally, as argued by Png and Wang [34], hackers may also incur a cost for developing and propagating malicious codes, and they may adjust their effort level in order to maximize their net benefit. When this happens, hackers overall activity level declines as more people adopt security software.<sup>3</sup> Initially, we disregard this strategic behavior and assume an exogenous level of hacker activity (henceforth denoted l); later, we relax this assumption. Appendix A provides a table of mathematical notations to support the reader’s understanding of the details of our modeling approach.

A consumer’s (or user’s) decision to protect his or her computers from malicious attacks—mass or targeted—depends on the benefit derived from thwarting these attacks, which in turn depends on the overall level of hacker activity. Further, the amount of benefit from thwarting an attack varies from one user to another. In order to capture this, users are indexed by a parameter u that indicates their relative expected benefit if an attack is thwarted; we assume that u is uniformly distributed over the interval [0, 1]. The absolute expected benefit to user u from thwarting an attack can then be expressed as Lu, where L is a constant; Lu can also be viewed as a proxy for the potential loss to user u from an attack [20].

## Mass Attacks

Mass attacks make use of generic exploits that are capable of breaching the security of a large variety of users [11, 35]. These attacks consist of (1) direct attacks and (2) indirect attacks. Accordingly, there are two types of benefits to be derived from adopting security software—direct and indirect. To model the direct benefit, we assume that the rate of direct attacks is the same as $\lambda ,$ the exogenous level of hacker activity. Therefore, by adopting security software, user u has a direct mitigation benefit rate of $\lambda \theta L u .$ where q is the probability that an attack is successful in breaching the security.

Next, we consider the indirect benefit. Let X be the fraction of users who have adopted security software, so $( 1 - X )$ is the fraction of unprotected users. Since an indirect attack ultimately originates from exploitations of intermediate vulnerable nodes through a direct attack, we assume the rate of indirect attacks to be $g \lambda ( 1 - X )$ where $g$ is a model parameter representing the strength of the negative network effect from indirect attacks—the higher $g$ is, the larger is the potential indirect benefit. An alternative motivation for this assumption is as follows.<sup>4</sup> For a given attack, let Z(t) be the size of the user base breached by this attack at time t, and let $\mu ( t )$ be the instantaneous rate of propagation of the attack per breached node. Because mass attacks follow a path of chance, the number of computers directly breached by this attack, $Z ( 0 )$ , should be proportional to $( 1 - X )$ and can be written as $Z ( 0 ) = \gamma ( 1 - X )$ for some constant $\gamma > 0$ . Now, the change in the breached user base over a small time interval, $\Delta t ,$ , can be written as $Z ( t + \Delta t ) - Z ( t ) = Z ( t ) \mu ( t ) \Delta t$ . Taking limit as $\Delta t \to 0$ leads to ${ d z ( t ) } / { d t } = Z ( t ) \mu ( t )$ , which results in

$$
Z (t) = Z (0) e ^ {\int_ {0} ^ {t} \mu (t) d t} = \gamma (1 - X) e ^ {\int_ {0} ^ {t} \mu (t) d t}.
$$

Since $Z ( t )$ is proportional to $( 1 - X )$ , so should be the number of indirect attacks originating from a single direct attack. Consequently, when the number of direct attacks per unit time is $\lambda ,$ the overall rate of indirect attacks must be $g \lambda ( 1 - X )$ , where $g$ is the ensemble average of $( \gamma e ^ { \int _ { 0 } ^ { t } \mu ( t ) d t } )$ . For a sparsely connected network, m(t) would be relatively smaller, leading to a smaller $g ;$ conversely, g would be higher for a densely connected network.

A user who adopts security software avoids security breaches due to these indirect attacks from the compromised users and, therefore, derives an indirect benefit of $g \lambda \theta ( 1 - X )$ , where, as before, q is the probability that an attack is successful in breaching the security. The total benefit (per unit time) to user u from adopting the software, in a market with coverage X, can then be written as

$$
B _ {u} = \lambda \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X)).\tag{1}
$$

## Targeted Attacks

In addition to mass attacks, a networked environment may also experience targeted attacks, which can be of two types: (1) vulnerability-based attacks and (2) value-based attacks. Hackers may target unprotected and vulnerable computers because these are easily breached [8]. Hackers are often able to identify such vulnerable computers by running reconnaissance probes themselves or by visiting illegitimate online marketplaces where vulnerability information is sold by other hackers [35]. Targeting can also be based on how valuable a target is to hackers—the greater the value of the target, the higher is the loss inflicted by a successful attack.

We first consider vulnerability-based targeting. Similar to mass attacks, vulnerabilitybased targeting also makes use of generic exploits [11, 35]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that the rates of both of these types of attacks depend on the level of hacker activity toward generic exploits. In addition, when hackers target vulnerable systems, the attack rate experienced by these systems should increase with a higher level of market coverage, as their chances of getting targeted increase. To capture this dependence, we model the rate of vulnerability-based attacks as experienced by unprotected users as $h _ { 1 } \hat { \lambda } X ,$ , where $\hat { \lambda }$ is the rate of direct attacks. Furthermore, by definition, a targeted attack is one-to-one, whereas a mass attack is one-to-many [34]. Since a one-to-one attack is aimed at a particular victim, such an attack is an unlikely source for additional indirect attacks [33, 34, 35]. As a result, the victim of a targeted attack should not create additional risks of indirect attacks to other users, and there is no user-level externality [34]. Therefore, the rate of indirect attacks in this case is simply $g { \hat { \lambda } } ( 1 - X )$ , implying that when both mass and vulnerability-based attacks are present, the total benefit rate for user u can be written as

$$
B _ {u} = \hat {\lambda} \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X) + h _ {1} X).\tag{2}
$$

Note that when the market is fully covered, indirect attacks disappear, and the total rate of attacks using generic exploits becomes $\hat { \lambda } ( 1 + h _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } )$ . This rate is now the effective level of hacker activity, so we set $\begin{array} { r } { \lambda = \hat { \lambda } ( 1 + h _ { \ i } ) } \end{array}$ . Consequently, Equation (2) reduces to

$$
B _ {u} = \left(\frac {\lambda}{1 + h _ {1}}\right) \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X) + h _ {1} X).\tag{3}
$$

We now turn our attention to highly customized exploits targeted toward highvaluation users. By nature, hacker activities involved in creating such customized exploits are quite different from those related to generic exploits and therefore constitute an entirely separate group of activities beyond the ones we have considered thus far [11]. In order to represent a user’s benefit in thwarting value-based targeted attacks, we assume that the rate of such attacks is proportional to the user’s valuation—for user u, this rate is therefore denoted by $h _ { 2 } \hat { \lambda } u$ . We can then express the benefit from thwarting these attacks as $\hat { \lambda } \theta L h , u ^ { 2 } ;$ ; note that this benefit does not depend on the market coverage, $X ,$ and hence, there is no network effect from these attacks, per se. When this new benefit is included, Equation (3) changes to

$$
B _ {u} = \left(\frac {\lambda}{1 + h _ {1}}\right) \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X) + h _ {1} X + h _ {2} u).\tag{4}
$$

## Strategic Hackers

For ease of exposition, when considering strategic behavior by hackers, we consider only mass attacks; extending this analysis to include targeted attacks is conceptually straightforward. In developing a model of hackers’ strategic behavior, we closely follow the approach used in Png and Wang [34]—strategic hackers choose their effort level, denoted $a \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , based on their own costs and benefits; the resulting rate of direct attacks is simply $\lambda a .$ . Hackers derive their benefit from compromising the security of systems and inflicting as much damage as possible. Therefore, when a fraction X of the market is covered, hackers can only get the satisfaction of breaching the security of some of the remaining $( 1 - X )$ fraction. Since the expected damage inflicted by an attack to user u is $\theta L u ,$ the total expected damage from breaching the unprotected $( 1 - X )$ fraction can be found as

$$
\int_ {0} ^ {1 - X} \theta L u d u = \frac {\theta L (1 - X) ^ {2}}{2}.
$$

The normalized benefit to hackers must be proportional to this expected potential damage, as well as to their normalized effort level, a, and can thus be written as $a ( 1 - X ) ^ { 2 }$ . We assume that hackers’ normalized cost is convex in a and model it as $( ( r - 1 ) / ( r + 1 ) ) a ^ { ( r + 1 ) / ( r - 1 ) }$ , where $r > 1$ represents the level of convexity of the cost function. The net utility to hackers can then be written as

$$
a (1 - X) ^ {2} - \frac {r - 1}{r + 1} a ^ {\frac {r + 1}{r - 1}},
$$

which can be maximized to obtain the optimal effort level as $a ^ { * } = ( 1 - X ) ^ { r - 1 } .$ . The rate of direct attacks therefore becomes $\lambda a ^ { * } = \lambda ( 1 - X ) ^ { r - 1 }$ , resulting in the following total benefit rate to user u:

$$
B _ {u} = \lambda \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X)) (1 - X) ^ {r - 1}.\tag{5}
$$

It is easy to verify that when r approaches one, our earlier discussion of mass attacks becomes a special case of this analysis.

## Market Structure Under Mass Attacks

In this section, we anal yz e the structure of an ol ig opol y mark et in the presence of mass attacks and examine whether it is consistent with that of the security software market. Security software products are usually licensed as a subscription; let P denote this subscription price per unit time. A user adopts a security software if the total benefit from the software is larger than its subscription price: $B _ { \scriptscriptstyle u } \geq P$ , where $B _ { \phantom { } _ { u } }$ is given by Equation (1). For the marginal user u who is indifferent between adopting and not adopting, this benefit is equal to the price:<sup>5</sup>

$$
\lambda \theta L u (1 + g (1 - X)) - P = 0.
$$

As shown in Figure 2, any user to the right of this user adopts the software, whereas anyone to the left does not. Therefore, $u = 1 - X$ . Substituting this and letting $p = P / ( \lambda \theta L )$ , we get

$$
p = \big (1 + g (1 - X) \big) (1 - X).\tag{6}
$$

Figure 2. Consumers Choose to Adopt (or Not Adopt) Based on Their Relative Benefit

Equation (6) represents the normalized price associated with a total market coverage of X.

In order to study the market competition in the security software industry, we employ the concept of fulfilled expectations Cournot equilibrium [25]. We use the Cournot competition to model the relatively long-term decisions by vendors, using the market size as their decision variables. Cournot competition fits this context much better than Bertrand competition. A large majority of subscriptions to security software happens through preloading contracts with computer manufacturers. All leading security software vendors engage in such long-term contractual agreements with them. Some of these contracts are exclusive, while others are not. For example, Dell has a nonexclusive contract with Symantec, McAfee, and Trend Micro to preload their antivirus software on all the computers Dell sell to consumers, whereas Hewlett-Packard currently has an exclusive contract with Symantec. Consumers get a free trial period for the preloaded software, but must purchase the subscription if they wish to continue to use it beyond the trial period. Engaging in this kind of long-term preloading contracts means that a vendor plans how many subscriptions it intends to sell, since the contractual fee the vendor is willing to pay for preloading is contingent on the expected sales. As shown by Kreps and Scheinkman [28], when vendors plan for a certain quantity in the first stage, a Bertrand-like price competition still leads to a Cournot equilibrium, even if the marginal production cost is zero.

It is also instructive to see why the alternative, Bertrand competition, does not work in this setting. First, a pure Bertrand would drive the price down to zero—not something that is observed in practice. Second, a differentiated Bertrand—the circular city [36] or the linear city [24] model—would require a complete coverage of the market for a real competition to set in. Assuming a complete coverage, however, would be contrary to the real-world observation that the market is far from being completely covered.

## Equilibrium Coverage and Price

Suppose that, in equilibrium, there are n identical vendors in the market with $x _ { i }$ representing vendor $i \gamma _ { \mathrm { s } }$ market size. Therefore, $\begin{array} { r } { X = \Sigma _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } x _ { i } } \end{array}$ . Let $X _ { - i } = X - x _ { i } .$ . For vendor $i ,$ the revenue maximization problem can then be formulated using Equation (6):

$$
\operatorname{Max} _ {x _ {i}} R _ {i} = \Big (1 + g \big (1 - X _ {- i} - x _ {i} \big) \Big) \big (1 - X _ {- i} - x _ {i} \big) x _ {i}.\tag{7}
$$

We can solve Equation (7) to obtain the following result:

Proposition 1 (Market Size and Price Under Mass Attacks): When only mass attacks are considered, in the oligopoly market with n identical vendors, the equilibrium market size and price for each vendor are given by

$$
x ^ {*} = \frac {(2 g + 1) (1 + n) - \sqrt {4 g (g + 1) + (1 + n) ^ {2}}}{2 g n (2 + n)}\tag{8}
$$

and

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {4 g (g + 1) - (1 + n) + (2 g + 1) \sqrt {4 g (g + 1) + (1 + n) ^ {2}}}{2 g (2 + n) ^ {2}}.\tag{9}
$$

The proofs of this proposition and all other results in this paper are available in Appendix B . The optimal profit per vendor can be written as $\boldsymbol { R } ^ { * } = \boldsymbol { p } ^ { * } \boldsymbol { x } ^ { * }$ . It can be shown that the results in Proposition 1 converge to those in a traditional oligopoly market:

$$
\lim _ {g \rightarrow 0} x ^ {*} = \frac {1}{n + 1}, \lim _ {g \rightarrow 0} p ^ {*} = \frac {1}{n + 1}, \text { and } \lim _ {g \rightarrow 0} R ^ {*} = \frac {1}{(n + 1) ^ {2}}.
$$

We observed in the introduction that the security software market has a higher price and a lower coverage. We now show that this observation is indeed consistent with the oligopoly model described above:

Theorem 1 (Impact of Negative Network Effect on Coverage and Price): The oligopoly market described above has a smaller market coverage and higher price, when compared to an oligopoly market without network effects; mathematically, $f o r g > 0 , \partial x ^ { * } / \partial g < 0 a n d \partial p ^ { * } / \partial g > 0 .$

The result in Theorem 1 that the market coverage shrinks with $g$ is interesting. Note that a user’s willingness to pay increases with $g$ (see Equation (1)). Because consumers are willing to pay more, one might expect a vendor to be more interested in serving them, thereby resulting in an expansion in the coverage. However, we find that, for a vendor in this market, a higher willingness to pay does not really entail an expansionary strategy. Recall from Equation (6) that $p$ increases quadratically with $( 1 - X )$ and this increase is more rapid at a higher $g .$ Therefore, when $g$ is high, a slight decrease in the coverage can lead to a large increase in the price. This means that vendors can significantly increase their pricing power by slightly reducing the market coverage. This is why, under the negative network effect, the market coverage is low—a reality in the security software market. In other words, Theorem 1 shows that incorporating a negative network effect into the economic analysis leads to realistic results. Without considering this network effect, it is difficult to explain practical observations, such as the existence of hundreds of antivirus products—with several of them commanding a price similar to what a near-monopolist such as Microsoft does for a product such as Word—and an unusually low market coverage despite the presence of multiple vendors.

An implication is that with the world getting more connected and indirect threats increasing, it has become possible for security software vendors to maintain high prices and make substantial profits while at the same time leaving a significant portion of the market uncovered. Their profitability, however, has attracted other firms to enter the market, an important issue that we discuss next.

## Level of Competition

Since the security software market continues to attract new entrants—despite the presence of several major players—we now investigate whether including the negative network effect into the economic analysis leads to results consistent with this market characteristic. As mentioned above, the industry becomes more profitable with g, but at the same time the profit per vendor should decrease with competition. Stated formally:

Lemma 1: The revenue for a vendor in the oligopoly market above has the following characteristics: for $g > { \cal O } ,$ $\partial { \cal R } ^ { * } / \partial g > 0$ and $\partial R ^ { * } / \partial n < 0 .$

We now derive the number of vendors in this market in a long-run equilibrium. In order to incorporate the cost of operating in the security software market, we assume that the marginal cost to the vendor for an additional subscription is zero. This is a reasonable assumption because once the software is developed and updating facilities are established, the marginal cost of supporting an additional subscription in terms of production, distribution, and updating is negligible. Therefore, we consider only the fixed cost incurred by vendors to develop, market, and maintain their software. We use k to denote the normalized fixed cost.<sup>6</sup> Clearly, a vendor’s participation constraint requires it to obtain a revenue no less than this fixed cost. Therefore, if $n ^ { * }$ denotes the number of vendors participating in the market in equilibrium, then $R ^ { * } ( n ^ { * } ) \geq k$ and $R ^ { * } ( n ^ { * } + 1 ) < k$ . Since $R ^ { * }$ is a decreasing function of $n ,$ this implies that $n ^ { * } = [ \tilde { n } ]$ , where $\tilde { n }$ solves $R ^ { * } ( \tilde { n } ) = k$ . We now show that for a given development cost $k ,$ , in equilibrium, the number of vendors in the oligopoly market with the negative network effect can be no less than what it would be in the absence of any network effect. Stated more formally:

Figure 4. Number of Vendors as a Function of Negative Network Effect

Theorem 2 (Impact of Negative Network Effect on Level of Competition): The negative network effect from indirect attacks leads to a more competitive oligopoly in the long run; mathematically, $n ^ { * } \geq n _ { o } ^ { * } ,$ where $n _ { o } ^ { * } = l i m _ { g \to \ d 0 } n ^ { * }$

We should mention that even though we are considering the number of vendors in the market as an indicator for the level of competition, prior literature has proposed other metrics for this purpose, such as the Herfindahl index and concentration ratio [41]. Even when measured against these criteria, we find that the negative network effect translates to a higher level of competition.

Theorem 2 indicates that the competitiveness of the security software market can be linked to the presence of the negative network effect. The intuition is clear: For any given market configuration, if $g$ is high, the market coverage is low, but, as mentioned earlier, the loss in revenue from this decreased coverage is amply compensated by the much higher price. The implication is that just as positive network effects can enhance the profit of a vendor in the case of an application software, modest levels of the negative network effect can enhance the industry profit in the case of security software. This enhanced profitability, in turn, can entice new players to enter the market.

In order to illustrate the result in Theorem $2$ more clearly, we plot in Figure 4 how the number of vendors, $n ^ { * }$ , changes with $g$ . Two observations can be made from this plot. First, as expected, $n ^ { * }$ increases when the fixed cost, $k ,$ decreases. Second, $n ^ { * }$ is a step-wise increasing function of $g ,$ clearly indicating that the long-run equilibrium competition level increases with the negative network effect.

Interestingly, we again find that when the negative network effect from indirect attacks is included in the analysis, it becomes much easier to find an explanation as to why this market has remained lucrative to new entrants. With more and more people getting interconnected, chances of indirect attacks have been on the rise. In the short run, this has worked against the existing vendors’ incentives to expand coverage and reduce price. However, a lower coverage and higher price together have also meant opportunities for new entrants.

Although Theorem 2 and Figure 4 are about the long-run equilibrium of the market, their implication must also be noted in terms of how the market is likely to behave during the transitory period—the initial years during which the industry matures. During this transitory phase, vendors typically make decisions with incomplete information about their own costs, the level of competition, and consumers’ reservation prices. A decision maker must carefully consider the chance of success and profitability before entering the market [13, 42]. If it is believed that the market would be able to support a larger number of vendors, a potential entrant would anticipate a greater chance of succeeding. This, in turn, would attract more vendors during the early transitory period—a result consistent with the empirical observation by Giarratana and Fosfuri [18] that the security software market has historically attracted a very large number of vendors, although many of them did not survive beyond the initial years.

## New Entry

We now turn our attention to what happens to the market price when there is a new entry to the market. In 2006, when Microsoft entered the security software market, there was a large outcry about Microsoft practicing “predatory” pricing to drive out competition [9, 27]. Of course, it is well understood, both in theory and practice, that a new entry is supposed to drive prices down in a traditional market [13, 25]. The question we seek to address here is whether the existence of the negative network effect makes the new entrant more aggressive and encourages it to reduce the price more drastically when compared to a traditional market.

In order to analyze this in a rigorous manner, we calculate the relative price reduction when a new entrant enters a market with n players, as

$$
\begin{array}{c} \Delta p (n) = \frac {p ^ {*} (n) - p ^ {*} (n + 1)}{p ^ {*} (n)} \\ = 1 - \frac {(2 + n) ^ {2} \left(4 g (g + 1) - (2 + n) + (2 g + 1) \sqrt {4 g (g + 1) + (2 + n) ^ {2}}\right)}{(3 + n) ^ {2} \left(4 g (g + 1) - (1 + n) + (2 g + 1) \sqrt {4 g (g + 1) + (1 + n) ^ {2}}\right)}. \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

Proposition 2 (Impact of Negative Network Effect on New Entry): The negative network effect makes an entrant price more aggressively: the relative price reduction resulting from a new entry, Dp(n), is an increasing function of $g : \partial ( \Delta p ( n ) ) / \partial g > 0 .$

![](/api/attachments/2QX376MH/fulltext/images/1fb1d8d6c91e776c5d62e113c7e0e629beb4ddd9dc8c2735c19796f2fdb71d37.jpg)  
Figure 5. Relative Price Reduction as a Function of Negative Network Effect

It should also be noted that $\Delta p ( n )$ is bounded:

$$
\frac {1}{2 + n} \leq \Delta p (n) \leq \frac {5 + 2 n}{(3 + n) ^ {2}}.
$$

Proposition 2 tells us that the negative network effect induces a higher reduction in price with a new entry when compared to the traditional market (where $g = 0 )$ . If this is not taken into consideration, the entrant’s pricing policy may indeed seem “predatory,” as perceived by Eckelberry [9]. Furthermore, the higher the network effect, the larger is this price reduction. In order to see this more clearly, we plot $\Delta p ( n )$ as a function of g in Figure 5. This figure illustrates that the impact of the negative network effect on the relative price reduction is significant. For example, for $n = 2 , \Delta p ( n ) = 2 5$ percent when $g = 0$ , but it increases to about 35 percent when $g = 5$

Given what we already know about this market, the intuition behind Proposition 2 can be explained as follows. An entry always results in an expansion of the market coverage. In a traditional market, this expansion, of course, translates to a lower price. In a market with the negative network effect, the expanded coverage exerts an additional downward pressure on the willingness to pay of consumers, causing a more severe price reduction. A clear implication of this result for a potential entrant to this market is that it must be willing to accept a lower price than what one would expect when entering a traditional market.

## Market Structure with Strategic Hacker Behavior

Al thoug h our mod el provid es possibl e expl anations for the unique structure of the security software market, we would like to examine whether these insights are applicable in broader settings as well. To that end, we now explore the competitive equilibrium under strategic hacker behavior. For expositional simplicity, we consider only mass attacks here.

When hackers are strategic, $B _ { \phantom { } u }$ is given by Equation (5). Once again, considering the marginal user, indifferent between adopting and not adopting, and setting $u = 1 - X ,$ we find the equilibrium price as

$$
P = \lambda \theta L (1 + g (1 - X)) (1 - X) ^ {r}.
$$

A careful examination reveals that the parameter r captures a new form of negative network effect—a higher r also means a lower willingness to pay. Rearranging terms, and letting $p = P / ( \lambda \theta L )$ , we get the normalized demand function:

$$
p = \big (1 + g (1 - X) \big) (1 - X) ^ {r}.\tag{12}
$$

In an oligopoly with n vendors, the revenue maximization problem for vendor i then becomes

$$
\operatorname{Max} _ {x _ {i}} R _ {i} = \Big (1 + g \big (1 - X _ {- i} - x _ {i} \big) \Big) \big (1 - X _ {- i} - x _ {i} \big) ^ {r} x _ {i}.\tag{13}
$$

Solving Equation (13), the following result is obtained:

Proposition 3 (Market Size and Price with Strategic Hacker Behavior): In the oligopoly market with n identical vendors, where hackers behave strategically, the equilibrium market size and price for each vendor are given by

$$
x ^ {*} = \frac {g (r + 2 n + 1) + (r + n) - \sqrt {(g + n + r (g + 1)) ^ {2} - 4 g n}}{2 g n (r + n + 1)}\tag{14}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l} p ^ {*} = \left(\frac {g (r + 1) - (r + n) + \sqrt {(g + n + r (g + 1)) ^ {2} - 4 g n}}{2 g (r + n + 1)}\right) ^ {r} \\ \times \left(\frac {g (r + 1) + (r + n + 2) + \sqrt {(g + n + r (g + 1)) ^ {2} - 4 g n}}{2 (r + n + 1)}\right). \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

Note that, when r → 1 $r \to 1$ , Proposition 3 converges to Proposition 1.

Theorem 4 (Market Structure with Strategic Hacker Behavior): (a) Even in the presence of strategic hackers, results in Theorems 1 and 2 hold—a higher g leads to a lower market coverage and higher price, as well as a more competitive market in the long run. (b) A higher r leads to a lower market coverage and lower price, and eventually to a less competitive market.

The first part of Theorem 4 makes it clear that our earlier insights regarding the effect of mass attacks remain valid in a wider context, further strengthening our intuition that the negative network effect due to indirect benefits can indeed explain the market structure for security software. The second part provides us with an additional insight: Not all negative network effects are the same. Their effects depend on how they manifest themselves in a specific market. Although the negative network effect caused by strategic hacker behavior furnishes some support for the low market coverage commonly observed, it does not lend any support to other market characteristics, namely, its fragmented nature.

In order to understand why this new negative network effect manifests itself differently, we first note that the willingness to pay in this case declines rapidly with $r ,$ and it becomes harder for vendors to maintain high price levels. In fact, along with the market coverage, the equilibrium price also declines with $r ,$ that is, $\partial p ^ { * } / \partial r < 0$ Viewed differently, unlike the previous case, it is no longer possible to compensate for the lower market coverage by hiking the price. The end result is that the industry profit is also decreasing in r, implying that the market becomes incapable of sustaining as many players as it can when r approaches one.

## Conclusion

A security software is a tool that ind ivid ual s and org aniz ations d epl oy to prevent security exploitations of computerized systems. Over the past decade, the market for this type of software has seen a tremendous growth, both from the supply as well as the demand side. Unlike the typical software market, where the supply side is dominated by only a handful of providers, the market for security software is highly competitive with several major players. In this paper, we search for possible explanations as to why the security software market behaves differently from the other ones.

![](/api/attachments/2QX376MH/fulltext/images/033bd5210cc1293fd1e8fa71549d5ec6d32841b54612386082098173e5cc9d73.jpg)  
Figure 8. Classification of Attacks and Resulting Network Effects

Positive network effects enjoyed by other software are usually not observed for security software; this is because the compatibility issue of application data across users is not a big concern. On the contrary, there is a negative network effect in the security software market—as the coverage declines, the chance of indirect attacks from unprotected computers increases. This network effect (represented by g), which has been the major focus of our study, is able to support the basic economic intuition we seek about this market. However, in order to paint a more comprehensive picture of how different types of security threats affect this market, we have analyzed how they manifest themselves as different network effects, influencing the overall market structure; Figure 8 summarizes this analysis.

We argue that the effect of value-based targeted attacks (represented by $h _ { 2 } )$ is that of enhancing the negative network effect above. But vulnerability-based targeting moves the market in a contrary direction through a positive network effect (represented by $h _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } )$ . The picture of the market is finally completed by considering hackers’ strategic behavior, which leads to a different kind of negative network effect (represented by r). This picture fills an important void in the literature by linking different types of attacks and hacker behavior to the network effects they induce.

Table 1 summarizes how these network effects influence the market structure. It is clear from this table that $g _ { \overline { { } } }$ —along with its supplement $h _ { 2 } -$ —drives the market in a direction that is consistent with practical observations. The impact of $h _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } ,$ , however, is exactly the opposite. Recall that when both mass and targeted attacks are present, the resulting market structure depends on the combined parameter $G = ( g - h _ { _ { 1 } } + h _ { _ { 2 } } ) / ( 1 + h _ { _ { 1 } } )$ If G is positive, the negative network effect dominates, and the market structure derived from our model resembles practical situations. Finally, the negative network effect from strategic hacker behavior, $r ,$ reduces the market coverage, which is consistent with practice, but its effects on the price and fragmentation are not so.

In conclusion, we have found reasonable analytical support for the possibility of certain network effects explaining the idiosyncratic nature of the security software market. Our results suggest that, in reality, the dominant effects seem to arise from indirect and value-based attacks. This is an important observation, as it has cardinal implications for vendors of security software. First, when the combined effect of these two attacks is dominant, it is optimal to use a strategy that keeps the total market coverage somewhat low. By adopting such a strategy, vendors can indeed increase prices substantially. This perhaps explains why, despite severe competitive pressure, a two-year subscription of antivirus software made by Trend Micro and Kaspersky cost as much as popular desktop applications from vendors with near-monopoly power. Second, acknowledging the role of their combined effect also makes it possible to explain why the industry is so profitable and attractive to new entrants. Without this role, it is difficult to explain why there are over 80 vendors in this market, which is far more than what one finds in any other. Third, the combined effect also requires new entrants to be more aggressive, when compared to a traditional market—another result that explains some real-life observations.

Although not intended to do so, our study nonetheless provides a few policy implications. For a product with zero marginal cost, the social welfare monotonically increases with the market coverage. However, achieving a complete coverage is impossible without directly subsidizing users. As a second best, a central planner may try to increase the coverage as much as possible; one way to achieve that would be to reduce the negative network effect induced by indirect attacks, which is further enhanced by value-based targeting. Therefore, a central planner should direct its resources toward aggressively pursuing and prosecuting perpetrators of such attacks. Furthermore, it can incentivize network operators to actively monitor and take remedial steps against them.

As is the case with most quantitative models, we have made a few assumptions as well, and our results should be interpreted accordingly. Some of these assumptions, such as the uniformly distributed consumer valuation or the zero marginal cost, are easily relaxed—although at the expense of analytical tractability—without changing the qualitative nature of our findings. The effects of a few others are not as clear. For example, the propagation dynamics of an attack used in this study was adapted from the epidemiology literature [23], which makes certain simplifying assumptions for analytical convenience. Future research should examine the validity of those assumptions and the applicability of this approach in the context of computer attacks, which are largely empirical propositions.

Another important limitation of this work is the omission of product-level or “localized” network effects that can result from hackers strategically targeting certain popular platforms or products [14]. Should that happen, the network effects experienced by users of different security products could be different and may not depend on the total coverage alone. Therefore, incorporating such hacker behavior into our framework will lead to a model with both market- and product-level network effects. Although product-level effects are important in the context of application software products—the very context that Galbreth and Shor [14] consider—their importance in the context of security software is not so clear; there is no evidence that hackers target different security software platforms differently. More specifically, in our extensive research, we were unable to locate a virus or worm that specifically targeted a particular antivirus product.

There are several other directions in which our results can be extended. For example, we have assumed that the level of the negative network effect from indirect attacks, g, is the same for all consumers; it would be interesting to analyze a situation in which g is allowed to vary across users. Further, nonlinear pricing through volume licensing is common in many software markets. One could investigate how nonlinear pricing might help security software vendors leverage certain network effects. In addition, our model of a symmetric competitive equilibrium can be extended to asymmetric settings, including those in which vendors compete on quality. We are examining these issues in our ongoing efforts to understand this market more completely.

\begin{array}{c} \frac {\partial p ^ {*}}{\partial r} = \frac {p ^ {*}}{y} \left(\left(r + \frac {g y}{1 + g y}\right) \frac {\partial y}{\partial r} + y \ln y\right) \\ <   \frac {p ^ {*}}{y} \left(\left(r + \frac {g y}{1 + g y}\right) \frac {\partial y}{\partial r} - \frac {y (1 - y) (1 1 - 7 y + 2 y ^ {2})}{6}\right) \end{array}\tag{B4}
$$

because from power series expansion of lny, we can show that

$$
y \ln y <   - \frac {y (1 - y) (1 1 - 7 y + 2 y ^ {2})}{6}.
$$

We can also show that $y _ { 1 } \leq y \leq y _ { 2 }$ , where

$$
y _ {1} = \frac {g + r (g + n) + r ^ {2}}{(n + r) ^ {2} + g (r + n + 1)}
$$

and

$$
y _ {2} = \frac {g n (1 + r) + r (r + n + 1)}{r + (n + r) ^ {2} + g n (r + n + 1)}.
$$

Now $( r + ( g y / ( 1 + g y ) ) ) ( \partial y / \partial r )$ is a decreasing function of $y$ and achieves its maximum at $y = y _ { 1 } . \mathrm { A t }$ the same time, $y ( 1 - y ) ( 1 1 - 7 y + 2 y ^ { 2 } ) / 6$ is a concave function of $y _ { : }$ , and its minimum happens at one of the two limits, $y _ { 1 }$ or $y _ { 2 } .$ We can also show, after some algebra, that

$$
\left(r + \frac {g y}{1 + g y}\right) \frac {\partial y}{\partial r} \Bigg | _ {y _ {1}} - \frac {y _ {1} (1 - y _ {1}) (1 1 - 7 y _ {1} + 2 y _ {1} ^ {2})}{6} <   0
$$

and

$$
\left. \left(r + \frac {g y}{1 + g y}\right) \frac {\partial y}{\partial r} \right| _ {y _ {1}} - \frac {y _ {2} (1 - y _ {2}) (1 1 - 7 y _ {2} + 2 y _ {2} ^ {2})}{6} <   0.
$$

Therefore, from Equation (B4), we conclude that $\partial p ^ { * } / \partial r < 0$ , that is, a higher r lead to a lower price.

Now, since $\partial x ^ { * } / \partial r < 0$ and $\partial p ^ { * } / \partial r < 0$ , it is clear that

$$
\frac {\partial R ^ {*}}{\partial r} = p ^ {*} \frac {\partial x ^ {*}}{\partial r} + x ^ {*} \frac {\partial p ^ {*}}{\partial r} <   0.
$$

Further, since $\partial R ^ { * } / \partial n < 0$ as well, the equilibrium number of vendors must decrease with r.
