## a b s t r a c t

The rising popularity of open source software (OSS) calls for a better understanding of the drivers of its adoption and diffusion. In this research, we propose an integrated framework that simultaneously investigates a broad range of social and economic factors on the diffusion dynamics of OSS using an Agent Based Computational Economics (ACE) approach. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that interoperability costs, variability of OSS support costs, and duration of PS upgrade cycle are major determinants of OSS diffusion. Furthermore, there are interaction effects between network topology, network density and interoperability costs, which strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the diffusion dynamics of OSS. The proposed model can be used as a building block to further investigate complex competitive dynamics in software markets.

## 1. Introduction

OSS is any piece of software whose source code is made publicly available under terms that follow the “Open Source De<sup>fi</sup>nition” [36: pp 171–188]. Generally, such software is freely available online. However, in some cases, vendors such as Red Hat and Ubuntu charge a fee for providing support and complementary services. There is increased interest in the phenomenon of open innovation in general and open source software (OSS) in particular. A growing number of organizations are either looking to move completely to open source systems or making their existing systems compatible with OSS [57]. However, there is a limited understanding of some of the more unique aspects of OSS and their impact on its adoption and diffusion at the enterprise level [27].

In contrast to proprietary software (PS), OSS is generally offered at zero or very low license costs and upgraded frequently [10]. There is also a perception of required level of technical expertise for effective use of the software which contributes to considerable uncertainty over total support costs [12,20,22,25]. From an organizational perspective, these characteristics make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ascertain the total cost of ownership (TCO). Furthermore, organizations have to evaluate additional factors when making technology adoption decisions such as decisions of partner <sup>fi</sup>rms and associated hardware/software upgrades. Prior studies address only a limited range of these economic and social factors simultaneously in one model, which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to provide an adequate assessment of the critical drivers of OSS diffusion at the enterprise level. This is also evidenced by a recent call for more research to identify “strategic variables other than price [to] better understand the drivers of adoption” and diffusion of OSS [27, p. 1083].

Therefore, in this research, we develop an integrated framework to simultaneously investigate the effect of both social and economic factors on the diffusion dynamics of OSS using an Agent-Based Computational Economics (ACE) approach [46]. ACE enables us to investigate complex economic processes by modeling heterogeneous agents and studying their repeated interactions over time. It is considered to be a suitable tool for modeling diffusion processes [16]. We apply this framework to illustrate how key variables, such as license, support and interoperability costs, frequency of upgrades and interactions with partner firms, individually and collectively affect the diffusion dynamics of OSS at the enterprise level. Our model investigates OSS diffusion in a more parsimonious way. It reveals interesting main and interaction effects that can help vendors identify effective competitive strategies that do not rely entirely on price [27].

The desktop operating system (OS) market is used as an exemplar in this study because it is an important market which is experiencing ongoing competition between a dominant PS (Windows) and the best known OSS (Linux). Furthermore, there has been a call in previous research to develop a better understanding of critical variables affecting software adoption speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the context of Windows and Linux [27].

Our results demonstrate that interoperability costs, variability of OSS support costs and frequency of PS upgrades are major determinants of OSS diffusion. There are interaction effects between network topology, network density and interoperability costs, which strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the diffusion dynamics of OSS. The results imply that vendors should incorporate these factors in their competitive strategies to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS diffusion. The proposed framework can also be used as a building block to further investigate complex competitive dynamics in software markets.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: the next section provides a brief review of the relevant literature diffusion of OSS. This is followed by the details of key model variables, the proposed model, the methodology, description of results and discussion. Finally, conclusions and ideas for future research are discussed along with the limitations of this research.

## 2. Literature review

Since Raymond's discussion of the process of open and collaborative development of software [38], OSS has been studied from various perspectives [23]. Researchers have investigated the OSS development process [32,41], OSS development teams and factors that motivate programmers to contribute to OSS projects [5,43,59], evolution of OSS as against evolution of proprietary software [17,60], quality and release management of OSS [30,37,45,61], use of OSS in product development and critical systems [35,40], adoption and diffusion of OSS [2,3,6,9,20,34] and types of pricing and licensing options available for developing OSS [20,34,47]. However, since the present research focuses on diffusion of OSS, we review key papers in this area.

Diffusion of innovations is a very broad area of investigation [39]. Fichman [13] and Westarp and Wendt [56] provide detailed reviews. The body of research within this area can be whittled down to the investigation of “three basic research questions: RQ1: What determines the rate, pattern, and extent of diffusion of an innovation across a population of potential adopters? RQ2: What determines the general propensity of an organization to adopt and assimilate innovations over time? RQ3: What determines the propensity of an organization to adopt and assimilate a particular innovation?” [13: pp. 106–107]. We address both RQ1 and RQ3: the speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation is OSS with <sup>fi</sup>rms forming the population of potential adopters. Unlike some of the previous diffusion models that investigate either the direct impact of neighbors or the impact of social structures on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to adopt a given innovation [56], we model both types of effects.

Prior studies have developed empirical, analytical and simulation models to investigate the factors that affect OSS adoption and diffusion. Bonaccorsi and Rossi [3] simulated a network of N <sup>fi</sup>rms (agents), whose software adoption decision was based on the perceived intrinsic value of OSS, the network externality and coordination factors (based on other member-<sup>fi</sup>rms in the network). The study concluded that OSS diffusion depended on the initial distribution of intrinsic values assigned to the technology by the agents. Dalle and Jullien [9] proposed that any <sup>fi</sup>rm would choose OSS over PS if its local and global bene<sup>fi</sup>ts outweighed its idiosyncratic preferences. Both the local and global bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were considered to be a function of the number of participants in a <sup>fi</sup>rm's network (including <sup>fi</sup>rms using the same or different standards). Mustonen [34] showed through mathematical modeling that PS and OSS could co-exist provided the <sup>fi</sup>rm selling PS carefully evaluates its pricing strategies. Kim et al. [20] studied two types of consumer <sup>fi</sup>rms (high/ low-type based on internal technical capability) and three types of pricing schemes for OSS (commercial, dual licensing, and support) under different market conditions (monopoly and duopoly). Using mathematical modeling, they demonstrated various feasible pricing strategies in monopoly and duopoly markets for PS and OSS vendors.

These studies have contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to our understanding of adoption and diffusion of OSS by identifying several important factors. However, the effect of some of these factors has not been investigated in detail and additional factors need to be studied in terms of their impact on adoption and diffusion of OSS.

For example, although prior research does note the effect of network topology on diffusion, it is not clear whether the effect remains the same over the course of the simulation. Similarly, although empirical studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the uncertainty over OSS support costs, there is no investigation of the impact of this uncertainty over the process of OSS diffusion. In terms of investigation of new factors, the timing and frequency of software upgrades have not been investigated in the context of OSS diffusion. Prior research has shown that it can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the technology adoption decision of organizations [28,29]. There is greater uncertainty regarding the timing of new releases in the case of PS than OSS because the upgrades for the former are vendor-driven and for the latter they are demand-driven [10]. Furthermore, with a PS upgrade, the vendor eventually withdraws support for older versions, thus, in many cases, coercing the customer to either upgrade to the latest version or support the software on its own. A case in point is that of Microsoft which intends to release major upgrades of its desktop OS every four years [19] and tends to withdraw support for older versions [4]. On the other hand, Ubuntu releases major upgrades of its desktop OS more frequently and does not withdraw support for earlier versions. Consequently, any discussion of OSS diffusion must also investigate the policy of vendor upgrades.

On the basis of this review, a set of six critical, yet under-researched factors was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: network topology, network density, OSS support issues, interoperability issues, frequency of PS upgrades and present mass of OSS adopters. The following section provides a detailed review of these variables and how we intend to incorporate them in our model. In order to better understand the effect of these strategic variables, we keep vendor's pricing strategy as <sup>fi</sup>xed in the model. The insights generated from our research will allow software vendors to devise competitive strategies in addition to pricing, such as manipulating interoperability costs and upgrade cycles. The proposed model can also be extended to study competitive dynamics in software markets in future studies.

## 3. Key model variables

## 3.1. Network topology and network density

Prior research has shown that network topology does have an impact on purchase decisions made by consumers regarding available software [56]. Delre et al. [10] studied the effect of social factors and word-of-mouth processes on the consumer decision-making process. In their model, the adoption decision was affected by “external marketing effort” and social pressures imposed on and by the consumers in their neighborhoods. Harkola and Greve [15] compared the effect of cohesion and structural equivalence on the diffusion of technology innovations in an empirical study. They concluded that the effect of structural characteristics on diffusion varied based on the different densities of the network. Similarly, Choi et al. [8] investigated the impact of network structure on the dynamics of innovation diffusion using simulation modeling. Their study revealed that different network topologies affected the process of diffusion differently. While there is some understanding of the effect of network topology and density on the process of diffusion, insights from previous models are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient in the context of OSS diffusion. For instance, it is not clear how other strategic factors, such as interoperability costs, can interact with the effect of network characteristics on the diffusion of innovations. Our integrated approach enables us to reveal such potential interactive effects which can create a richer understanding of the overall effect of network structure on the diffusion of software.

We examine OSS diffusion under three types of network topologies based on the previous literature: random, clustered and small-world. Network density is modeled as the number of immediate neighbors – higher number of immediate neighbors on average represents a denser network. The varying network topologies and densities re<sup>fl</sup>ect different types of real world networks. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms working in the same R&D consortia cooperate multilaterally and are highly clustered. In other words, a <sup>fi</sup>rm's neighbor's neighbor is likely to be its neighbor as well (clustered network). On the other hand, in a network with very low clustering, a <sup>fi</sup>rm's neighbors may not be each other's neighbors (random network). For instance, professional service <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., consulting and accounting companies) deal with customers who may not do business with each other. In small-world networks, both kinds of <sup>fi</sup>rms exist, i.e., some neighbors are well connected and some are not as well connected with each other. There are many real world examples, such as fashion markets [10], and OSS development communities [42], of small-world networks. Watts and Strogatz's algorithm [53] was employed to simulate the three network topologies.

In our network of <sup>fi</sup>rms, two <sup>fi</sup>rms have a link between them if they conduct transactions with each other. These transactions could represent an exchange of documents, reports, data, or any other kind of electronic information between neighboring <sup>fi</sup>rms. For example, a hospital may exchange documents with insurance companies and pharmacies. Firms conduct a certain number of transactions on each link. Therefore, the links are undirected. If the neighboring <sup>fi</sup>rms are using incompatible software, they will both incur interoperability costs per transaction. These interoperability costs will in part affect the decision of each <sup>fi</sup>rm in upgrading its existing software or switching to the alternative software.

## 3.2. OSS support costs

The impact of support costs on diffusion has been recognized in the previous literature. However, this impact needs to be investigated further in the context of OSS since practitioner reports suggest that there is considerable uncertainty regarding the magnitude of OSS support costs [22]. Unlike PS users who receive guaranteed support from vendors,<sup>1</sup> OSS users can choose to receive support from vendors (e.g., Red Hat) or support the software on their own. Since users have heterogeneous technical capabilities, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ascertain overall costs to support non-vendor-backed software such as OSS or even a vendor-backed OSS that may not be compatible with other software. In addition, lack of complementary software and services, heterogeneous degrees of software integration and customization, and less systematic version management [17], contribute to higher uncertainty of OSS support costs. Firms with programmers that participate in OSS development could have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower support costs when compared to other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, not only do we consider the possibility of uncertainty regarding OSS support costs, we weigh it with a <sup>fi</sup>rm's technical capability in managing OSS. We keep PS support costs to be the same among all adopters and assume that OSS adopters face heterogeneous support costs drawn from a normal distribution. Three normal distributions with different variances are used to model different levels of support variability incurred by OSS adopters.

## 3.3. Interoperability costs

Previous literature has indicated that interoperability issues play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in standard adoption [7,18]. In this paper we assume that neighboring <sup>fi</sup>rms using different systems incur overhead costs per transaction. These costs are aggregated over a volume of transactions to compute interoperability costs incurred by each <sup>fi</sup>rm with each one of its neighbors. Switching platforms (e.g. operating systems) can have several implications on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's existing portfolio of applications [14]. In the current desktop OS market, Microsoft has a clear dominance. Thus, even advocates of Linux concede that the pool of compatible applications for Linux is smaller than it is for Windows. Although this difference may diminish over time, right now <sup>fi</sup>rms can expect to incur some interoperability costs to communicate with partners using a different platform. These interoperability costs could be additional effort (labor hours) or software required to ensure interoperability of applications.

3.4. Frequency of PS upgrades and the threat of withdrawal of support

To the best of our knowledge, availability and timing of technology upgrades in the context of diffusion of OSS have not been studied. Research suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms “jump ship” when making major technology upgrade decisions [28]. If <sup>fi</sup>rms neither upgrade nor switch to a different software, they anticipate that soon the support for the existing version will be withdrawn [4]. Similarly, the timing of upgrades from the consumer's perspective can affect the diffusion dynamics as well. Mukherji et al. state that “<sup>fi</sup>rms would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a long term ‘plan’ for investment in IT upgrades” [33: pp. 1685]. Furthermore, it is important that long-term costs are taken into account during upgrade decisions since choice of an operating system is more like a platform decision that affects hardware, existing application portfolio, staf<sup>fi</sup>ng/ training issues etc. [14]. The duration of this “long term” may vary for <sup>fi</sup>rms depending on their size, industry and risk pro<sup>fi</sup>le with respect to the adoption of new technology. Hence, <sup>fi</sup>rms may face different annual upgrade costs which must be factored into the decision-making process.

To model these differences related to upgrades, we assume that the support cost for a PS customer increases if its version is two or more versions older than the vendor's current version. There is no such increase in support costs for OSS customers using older versions of OSS. We also assume that PS upgrade cycle is the same as or longer than OSS upgrade cycle.

## 3.5. Initial proportion of OSS adopters

Literature on innovation diffusion suggests that the mass of existing adopters can affect the non-adopters and vice versa [26]. The concept of “critical mass can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the minimum amount of some resource … needed before another condition or product explodes into existence” [11: pp 235]. This implies that the size of the existing base of adopters of an innovation can strategically impact the future diffusion process. In the desktop OS market, Microsoft has by far been the most dominant player, becoming the de facto standard. Based on prior studies, we consider two different proportions for the initial number of OSS adopters in a population of consumers (10% in [57] and 30% in [9]).

## 4. Methodology

This paper uses simulation modeling to investigate the diffusion dynamics of OSS. This is a suitable methodology because in experimental contexts where signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of empirical research is lacking, simulations can illuminate or eliminate avenues for future research. Simulations have been used in the past in the investigation of adoption and diffusion of OSS, standards and other phenomena [10,34,56]. Furthermore, literature also suggests that agent-based modeling, which we intend to use, may be most suited for modeling diffusion compared to other methodologies such as statistical/analytical models and qualitative studies [16]. This is because agent-based simulations facilitate the development of more sophisticated models where behavior can be studied over time and the investigation is not limited by considerations of mathematical tractability. Such models allow joint investigation of a combination of social and economic factors as well which is an otherwise challenging task with the traditional research methodologies [46]. Hence, agent-based models facilitate crossing boundaries (economic vs. social modeling) in diffusion research. We review the concept of agent-based modeling and propose our simulation model in the following subsections.

## 4.1. Agent-based modeling and agent-based computational economics

Agent-based computational economics (ACE) is a relatively new, growing area of economics [46]. Using agent-based modeling and simulation as a tool, ACE models complex economic processes as evolving interactions among autonomous agents [52]. Depending on the nature of the system being modeled, there can be many types of agents (cells, species, individuals, <sup>fi</sup>rms, nations etc) and each type of agent can behave differently. These agents could also act independently, collaboratively or competitively. By specifying individual attributes and how they interact, ACE can compute aggregate behavior resulting from repeated interactions [51]. ACE studies can be replicated by knowing agents' behaviors, <sup>fi</sup>tness function, the simulation environment and the overall performance-measuring objectives of the environment.

We use ACE to model the OSS market in which consumer <sup>fi</sup>rms, proprietary software vendors, open source support-providing <sup>fi</sup>rms (all acting as agents), work towards their individual goals (pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization and/or sustainability in the market) while taking different actions (adopting different standards, pricing strategies etc.). The population of consumer <sup>fi</sup>rms is heterogeneous across different attributes such as choice of software, size of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, costs incurred over licenses, support, training etc. Each <sup>fi</sup>rm has to choose between upgrading its existing software and switching to the alternative software based on an objective function, which measures the average expected annual cost savings over a planning horizon. Firms can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the behavior of other <sup>fi</sup>rms to different degrees. Any meaningful behavior exhibited by the system arises from the collective behavior of the group of these agents.

## 4.2. Model and parameters

In our diffusion model, agents are <sup>fi</sup>rms where each <sup>fi</sup>rm is using a (randomly assigned) proprietary or open source desktop OS (for example, Microsoft Windows and Red Hat Linux). As is the case for desktop OS, we assume that the proprietary software dominates the network at the beginning of the simulation. Each <sup>fi</sup>rm periodically evaluates its technology (hardware/software) depending on its planning horizon. This notion of a planning horizon is based on Mukherji et al.'s statement that “in the case of frequent upgrades, it is important for <sup>fi</sup>rms to decide the frequency at which its technology must be replaced” [33: pp. 1685]. Therefore, in our model, <sup>fi</sup>rms decide to upgrade the existing software or switch to the alternative software at the beginning of their planning horizon. A <sup>fi</sup>rm's planning horizon is closely tied to the technology vendor's release cycle. As an example, consider a <sup>fi</sup>rm that has a Windows OS deployed on its desktops. If this <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses to upgrade its existing desktop OS, it will only choose to do so after a newer version has been released. In other words, the desktop OS upgrade frequency of the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot be shorter than Microsoft's Windows release upgrade frequency. Therefore, we model the length of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's planning horizon (PH) to be greater than or equal to the upgrade cycle (UC) of its respective vendor. The longer the PH, the more reluctant a <sup>fi</sup>rm is to consider software changes due to reasons such as organizational inertia, risk aversion or lower innovativeness. To simulate that behavior we chose a range of values of PH for the <sup>fi</sup>rms and these values were distributed across the entire population in proportions similar to a S-shaped curve (i.e., 20% <sup>fi</sup>rms have a PH=UC, 30% have a PH=UC+1, 30% have a PH=UC+2, and 20% have a PH=UC+3). Each <sup>fi</sup>rm decides whether to upgrade its existing software or switch to the alternative software, at time t based on the following decision function:

$$
\left[ \frac {C _ {t + 1} ^ {U} - C _ {t + 1} ^ {S}}{C _ {t + 1} ^ {U}} \right] \geq [ (1 - \alpha) \times (1 - n) ]\tag{1}
$$

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} T R U E \Rightarrow S w i t c h t o a d i f f e r e n t s o f t w a r e \\ F A L S E \Rightarrow U p g r a d e t h e e x i s t i n g s o f t w a r e \end{array} \right.
$$

where

$C _ { t + 1 } ^ { U }$ represents annual costs if the <sup>fi</sup>rm decides to upgrade its software

$C _ { t + 1 } ^ { S }$ represents annual costs if the <sup>fi</sup>rm decides to switch to the other software

α represents the degree centrality of a <sup>fi</sup>rm

n represents the proportion of a <sup>fi</sup>rms' neighbors who use the proposed new software.

The left-hand side (LHS) of Eq. (1) represents expected annual cost savings or expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t from switching to the alternative software as against upgrading the existing software. These costs include license, setup, training, support and interoperability costs. However, with new innovations such as OS, these costs could be uncertain [17,20]. Hence consistent with economics and innovation theory, depending on thei risk preferences and how they process uncertain information [6], <sup>fi</sup>rm could expect the value of the left-hand side to be higher than a threshold to switch to a different software. The right-hand-side (RHS) of Eq. (1) represents a threshold unique to each <sup>fi</sup>rm. For example, if the left hand-side for a <sup>fi</sup>rm A is evaluated to X then a <sup>fi</sup>rm such as Wal-Mart and a <sup>fi</sup>rm such as one of Wal-Mart's suppliers will value these saving differently and hence require LHS values to be greater than different thresholds (RHS values). Thresholds could represent risk preferences [6] and/or social in<sup>fl</sup>uence [10], and are well-accepted in diffusion research Furthermore, prior research also suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms anticipate the decision of their neighbors when making their own technology decisions [54] and they may not place an equal level of emphasis on the decision of their neighbors. The RHS of our decision function combines economic and social perspectives to incorporate this multi level heterogeneity by stating that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's valuation of its savings will be determined by a combination of factors: its level of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (or centrality, captured by α) and the decision of its neighbors (captured by n). A highly in<sup>fl</sup>uential/central <sup>fi</sup>rm such as Wal-Mart will not worry too much about the decision of its neighbors (who have low centrality) when it makes technology adoption decisions. Therefore, for a <sup>fi</sup>rm suc as Wal-Mart (1−α) will become a low weight attached to the decision of its neighbors (1 n). On the other hand, for one of Wal-Mart' suppliers, with low-centrality, the decision of its (few) neighbors might be more important so a low α will result in a higher weight (1 α) attached to the decision of the neighbors. The added interesting element is that the software choice of neighbors also affects the LHS of the decision function. If more neighbors are using the other software, then holding centrality (α) constant, a <sup>fi</sup>rm is more likely to be pulled towards the choice of its neighbors when interoperability costs are high Interoperability costs reduce expected cost savings and make a <sup>fi</sup>rm likely to conform to its neighbors. However, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is not bound to conform. As mentioned earlier, a highly central <sup>fi</sup>rm will attach low weight to the decision of its neighbors, which would mean a lower threshold or lower RHS which in turn would mean that even a small proportion of cost savings will encourage the <sup>fi</sup>rm to switch to the alternative even if all its neighbors continue to use a different software Furthermore, in our simulations, the most central <sup>fi</sup>rm makes an adoption decision prior to its neighbors because it is less prone to social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in making its own adoption decision. However, once it has made an adoption decision, it in<sup>fl</sup>uences all its neighbors who make subsequent adoption decisions. For the same LHS and n, a more centra <sup>fi</sup>rm (higher α or lower 1−α) is more likely to switch. <sup>2</sup>

Going back to the simulation process, since <sup>fi</sup>rms upgrade to keep up with the latest technology/features and/or to maximize the utility they can derive from the existing software [36], we assume that if they do not switch, they will necessarily upgrade to the latest available version of their existing software. Firms upgrade under the assumption that upgrades offer quality advantages and that if <sup>fi</sup>rms do not <sup>fi</sup>nd the other software viable, they will avail the quality improvements offered by the upgrades. The heterogeneity amongst <sup>fi</sup>rms in their eagerness to upgrade is modeled by the varying planning horizons.

A 3×3×3×3×2×2 study was designed in order to study six main variables. This study uses three different parameters each for the following: network topology (random, small world, clustered), network density (low, medium, high), OSS support costs (support costs slightly higher than PS on average with low variability; support costs slightly higher than PS on average with very high variability; support costs much higher than PS with very low variability), and interoperability costs (low, medium and high). Two different parameters are used for the length of the PS vendor's upgrade cycle (short and long) and the initial proportion of OSS <sup>fi</sup>rms (low and high). Fifty samples were drawn from respective distributions for each of the random variables, and the results were averaged. The simulation was run for 100 time periods, for each of the 324 parameter combinations. Fig. 1 illustrates the <sup>fl</sup>ow diagram for our model. The key parameter values used in the simulation are summarized in Table 1. Wherever possible, we used numbers obtained from the practitioner literature. The model was developed using NetLogo [58]. Simulations were extremely computationally intensive and were run on a Linux-based cluster with over 200 CPU cluster blade servers, Intel Xeon CPUs and gigabit Ethernet interconnections with 2TBs of dedicated NAS.

## 5. Simulation results and analysis

Two criteria were de<sup>fi</sup>ned for diffusion: a) if the number of OSS adopters doubled [9], b) if the number of OSS adopters increased by 50% during the course of the simulation. All results remained the same under both conditions which indicates the robustness of the model. Due to space limitations, only actual numbers based on the <sup>fi</sup>rst criterion of diffusion will be reported. Diffusion predominantly occurred with high initial proportion of OSS adopters and did not occur under very high OSS support costs. Out of 324 cases, diffusion occurred in 72 cases. Logistic regression was performed to test which of the six factors increased the likelihood of diffusion of OSS. The analysis revealed initial proportion of OSS adopters to be the most statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in increasing the likelihood of diffusion of OSS, followed by interoperability costs, network density, high variability in support costs and network topology. The model correctly predicted 86% of the diffusion cases and 96% of the non-diffusion cases.

## 5.1. Main effects

The analysis revealed that high interoperability costs favored the dominant standard by locking-in its users. Low interoperability costs, on the other hand, shifted the emphasis to other costs, network topology and network density thus reducing the possibility of lock-in (Fig. 2). This leads to the <sup>fi</sup>rst result:

## Result 1. When PS is the dominant software in the market, increasing interoperability costs reduces the diffusion of OSS.

This is consistent with what we are seeing in the desktop OS market: interoperability issues are a major reason why many organizations are reluctant in adopting Linux or any other open source desktop OS. The important implication is that despite being open source, software vendors could manipulate interoperability issues between platforms to generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant switching costs and affect the process of diffusion. For example, despite the prevalence of open standards, there is evidence of such behavior exhibited by vendors in the market for network devices [7].

Similarly, shorter duration of the PS upgrade cycle tended to favor the diffusion of OSS (Fig. 3). This happened because with frequent PS upgrades <sup>fi</sup>rms using PS faced the possibility of incurring higher onetime costs (such as setup and training costs), averaged per year, more frequently than OSS users. Therefore, ceteris paribus <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to avoid the frequent and high one-time costs with PS. For example, adoption of Microsoft Vista required signi<sup>fi</sup>cant one-time investment particularly in newer hardware to get the system to function at an acceptable level. As a result, some <sup>fi</sup>rms chose not to upgrade or moved towards OSS solutions. In addition, PS adopters are likely to consider OSS options more often if they have to frequently upgrade their software. Hence, the second result:

## Result 2. Shorter PS upgrade cycles favor the diffusion of OSS.

The practical implication is that the timing and frequency of upgrades can be manipulated by the vendors to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the process of software diffusion. Results 1 and 2 are somewhat intuitive and corroborate what we are seeing in reality. However, these results are valuable nonetheless as they establish face validity of the model [21] and allow us to expound more interesting results with greater con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the validity of the model. This is an established practice in simulation based studies where some intuitive results or results that corroborate theory or reality are used to build con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the model (see for example, [48,50,51]).

Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant diffusion of OSS occurred in the presence of high variability in support costs (N(60,60) in Table 1). This is not a readily intuitive result. Under high variability some <sup>fi</sup>rms incurred very low OSS support costs, thus their intrinsic value of OSS was large enough to motivate them to switch. When a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient mass of early OSS adopters was acquired, this further drove the OSS diffusion. It is worthwhile to note that if the proportion of OSS adopters was high (30%) to start with, then ceteris paribus, the critical mass was attained much faster than when the initial proportion was 10%. The low variability or very high magnitude of support costs (N(60,15), N(250,50)) was not able to make OSS seem more attractive than PS because most of the <sup>fi</sup>rms on average had very high support costs. Again, this result corroborates what is happening in reality. Despite having an established base of locked-in customers in the desktop market, Microsoft is slowly losing some of its market share to Linux. On the one hand, there are <sup>fi</sup>rms (at the high end of this distribution) which feel that (either due to their lack of technical capability or lack of available compatible pool of applications) the support costs for OSS are too high. On the other hand, in addition to the low upfront costs, there are some <sup>fi</sup>rms which are facing very low OSS support costs (due to their technical capability or involvement in the open source online communities, or the way they are implementing these systems, <sup>fl</sup>exibility in customizing and independent bug <sup>fi</sup>xing and lock-in avoidance). These <sup>fi</sup>rms are <sup>fi</sup>nding it more attractive to adopt OSS than PS. Their adoption in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences other <sup>fi</sup>rms (Fig. 3). Hence we state our third result (Fig. 4):

## Result 3. High variability in support costs favors the diffusion of OSS.

One practical implication is that PS vendors could consider increasing support cost variability by subsidizing support or giving the option of self-support to the more technically capable <sup>fi</sup>rms. This would make adoption or continued use of PS more attractive. The simulations also revealed interaction effects between network topology, network density and interoperability costs that truly highlight the dynamics of diffusion. ## 5.2. Interaction effects

Simulations indicate that the effect of varying network density is strongly linked to the strength of interoperability costs. When interoperability costs are high, less dense networks (small neighborhoods) encourage the diffusion of OSS the most (Fig. 5a), followed by denser networks (medium and large sized neighborhoods). As explained earlier in the context of the decision function, each <sup>fi</sup>rm simultaneously in<sup>fl</sup>uences the decision of its neighboring <sup>fi</sup>rms and gets in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their decisions. For example, in high density networks, <sup>fi</sup>rms on average have more neighbors. This means that simultaneously, the decision of a <sup>fi</sup>rm a) in<sup>fl</sup>uences many <sup>fi</sup>rms (its neighbors) and b) can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by many <sup>fi</sup>rms. The level of this in<sup>fl</sup>uence is affected by the strength of interoperability costs. If most of the neighbors of a <sup>fi</sup>rm have adopted a particular platform, they will likely in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>rm in question to adopt the same software in the case of high interoperability costs. In a denser network, where <sup>fi</sup>rms on average have more neighbors, such in<sup>fl</sup>uence will increase. However, with low interoperability costs high density networks encourage the diffusion of OSS (Fig. 5b). This is because with low interoperability costs <sup>fi</sup>rms are less concerned about the software being used by their neighbors and in a high density network one <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to switch can in<sup>fl</sup>uence many <sup>fi</sup>rms and spread the ‘news’ quickly. Results 4a and 4b demonstrate that the effect of interoperability costs on the speed of diffusion of OSS varies with the density of the network (two-way interaction). Results 5a and 5b demonstrate that the effect of network topology on speed of OSS diffusion is closely tied to the effects of network density and interoperability costs (three-way interaction). An important practical implication of these results is that software vendors should try to capture information regarding the network of target consumers because the effect of strategic variables such as interoperability issues is tied to the network structure. In other words, vendors can use the structure of the network to their advantage by manipulating interoperability issues between competing platforms/software.

These results automatically raise another question: given that network topology makes a difference, which network topology might favor the diffusion of OSS? Fig. 6b shows that, when interoperability costs are low, regardless of the network density, rate of diffusion is fastest in a random network. However, in Fig. 6a, when interoperability costs are high in low density networks, different topologies exhibit fastest rate of diffusion over time. Rate of diffusion is initially fastest in clustered networks, then in random networks and eventually in small world networks. Fig. 6a, also shows that, when interoperability costs are high, in high density networks, rate of diffusion is considerably slower overall, but fastest in clustered networks followed by small world and random networks. These are very interesting results which have not been captured in earlier studies and warrant an explanation. Let us <sup>fi</sup>rst consider the differences between the three network topologies. In a random network topology, the neighbors of a <sup>fi</sup>rm may not be well connected with each other whereas in a clustered network, the neighbors of one <sup>fi</sup>rm are very likely to be neighbors of each other as well. This means that the number of potential new adopters in<sup>fl</sup>uenced in every time period is higher in a random network than in a clustered network. This can be understood by considering a PS cluster where every agent is connected to every other agent. When the <sup>fi</sup>rst PS user switches to OSS, all of its neighbors are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the decision. Thus, no new node will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced later on. On the other hand, in a random network, new nodes are more likely to be affected over time as <sup>fi</sup>rms second tier neighbors (i.e., neighbors' neighbor) are less likely to overlap with <sup>fi</sup>rms' immediate neighbors. This has the potential of triggering OSS diffusion in different parts of a random network. Furthermore, this potential would be present regardless of the density of the network. However, whether OSS diffusion is actually triggered or the extent to which it is triggered is also dependent on the strength of interoperability costs (as evidenced by the <sup>fi</sup>rst and the last four results). In an initially PS dominated network with low interoperability costs, <sup>fi</sup>rms evaluate OSS and PS on other cost factors, which are more favorable for OSS, with only limited additional pressure from their PS-neighbors. This, coupled with the potential of random connections to trigger diffusion across the network, results in rapid diffusion of OSS (Fig. 6b).

Result 6. In a PS dominated network, in the absence of or under low interoperability costs, random network has the fastest OSS diffusion regardless of the density of the network.

However, as interoperability costs increase, initially if there is any diffusion in a PS dominated network, it occurs within the cliques of the clustered networks. Despite their low overall numbers in the network, some of the OSS <sup>fi</sup>rms may be in majority in cliques across the network and they will drive the diffusion in the early part of the simulation. That cannot happen in a random network where the same number of OSS adopters are widely dispersed throughout the network and cannot enforce any ‘social pressures’ in the absence of cliques. Therefore, initially adoption is slowly driven within clusters in the presence of high interoperability costs. However, as the number of OSS adopters reaches a critical mass, the random networks use their ‘random’ connections to drive diffusion globally throughout the network.

Result 7. In a PS dominated network, in the presence of relatively high interoperability costs, initial diffusion is driven by the local connections or cliques and later on the global or random connections drive the diffusion process.

The point where this changeover takes place is decided by the density of the network. Notice for instance that in the bottom three curves of Fig. 6a, the rate of diffusion is the fastest in the clustered network throughout the course of the simulation. In this case OSS is never able to attain a critical mass of adopters from where diffusion can then really kick off. In a dense network with high interoperability costs, the OSS adopter may require a much longer period to attain that critical mass than in a less dense network (top three curves of Fig. 6a).

From a practical perspective this implies that under high interoperability costs a) OSS vendors need to focus more on groupbased adoption of OSS to gain critical mass, b) it will take longer to achieve critical mass in denser networks, c) once critical mass is achieved, group-based adoption may no longer be as valuable as before and small world networks will result in the fastest diffusion of OSS (Fig. 7).

## 6. Discussion

The objectives of this paper were to a) devise a framework for simultaneous investigation of social and economic factors affecting OSS diffusion, b) explore the effect of network topology, network density, uncertainty regarding OSS support costs, interoperability issues, the frequency of PS upgrades, and initial proportion of OSS adopters on the diffusion dynamics of OSS. An agent-based computational economics approach was applied in pursuit of these objectives by modeling the market forces affecting software diffusion based on both academic and practitioner literature. The research has important implications for software vendors or third party software providers. The model is essentially an investigation of the effect of controllable and uncontrollable factors. The controllable factors include interoperability issues, support costs, upgrade cycles/costs etc. whereas the uncontrollable factors include network topology, network density etc. It may not be possible for software vendors to change the type of network topology or network density they are targeting. However, software vendors can consider capturing data regarding their target network. Once such data is captured, it can be determined whether the network resembles a random network (i.e. low cliquishness), clustered network (i.e. high cliquishness) or small world (i.e. elements of both low and high cliquishness [53]). Then, knowledge regarding network topology and network density could allow software vendors to better understand the importance of and use of the controllable factors, such as interoperability costs and timing/frequency of upgrades, in affecting the process of software diffusion. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings present a more accurate understanding of the effect of network structure because prior studies did not take a holistic view of key factors that affect adoption decisions and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly interact with the effect of network structure [8]. The issue of capturing data regarding the network structure has been addressed in past research [55]. Also, with growing interest towards software as a service or on-demand computing and cloud computing service providers could be in a better position to capture data regarding their network of clients.

Our results are based on parameter values that have been justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Table 1. However, it is important to note that the simulation framework is general and could be applied in other contexts to stimulate discussion in those areas. This notion of using simulation results to “stimulate discussion” on a particular topic is a well established practice [48,50,51]. Nonetheless, it is worthwhile to revisit key assumptions made in the study and brie<sup>fl</sup>y address their effects on the results.

## 6.1. Assumptions and results

First we had assumed zero license/upgrade costs for OSS In reality, there might be some upgrade costs for OSS but they would still be very low compared to PS upgrade costs [57] and would not have any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on our results. Second, we assumed that support costs for PS adopters would go up if their existing version is two or more versions older than the latest release from the PS vendor. Although it is known that PS users do not have guaranteed support once support is withdrawn for older version of the software by the PS vendor, we re-ran our experiments by removing this assumption and holding the support cost constant for PS adopters. We found that relaxing the assumption did not change our results. This result further establishes the robustness of our model and the resulting insights. Third, we assumed that OSS upgrades are more frequently available than PS upgrades and that upgrades mean more costs than bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Although, in our exemplar of Microsoft's Windows and Ubuntu's Linux releases it was evident that Linux releases are more frequent, we used two different PS upgrade cycles in our model – short and long. While result 2 showed that speed of diffusion was faster when PS upgrade cycles were shorter, there was no qualitative difference in the rest of the results as a result of the different PS upgrade cycles. As for the costs associated with upgrades, we do take the qualitative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from an upgrade into account by assuming that in light of the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts offered by an upgrade, <sup>fi</sup>rms will either choose to switch or upgrade. Result 2 will no longer hold if upgrades indeed bring signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more quantitative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than cost (i.e., positive cost saving from upgrade in the left-hand-side of the decision function). However such a case is rare in reality, Fourth we assumed that <sup>fi</sup>rms will either switch or upgrade their software. In practice, however, it is possible that firms may neither switch nor upgrade and continue to support the software on their own. However, other than slowing down the eventual diffusion of software, this assumption should have no qualitative impact on the insights generated through the results. Fifth, we assumed that when <sup>fi</sup>rms conduct transactions with each other, they incur interoperability costs per transaction. In reality, <sup>fi</sup>rms may choose to handle interoperability issues differently. We chose to use some numbers to model varying strengths of interoperability costs to accommodate those differences in reality. However, the point remains that some interoperability costs will be incurred. Again, changing the actual numbers of the interoperability costs will not affect the insights from our model. Finally, we assumed that PS dominates the software market at the start of the simulation This is a realistic assumption in the desktop OS and other relatively new OSS markets. Results 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7 are dependent on this assumption because high interoperability costs will favor the dominant software at the start of the simulation. However, the dominance of one software or another will not affect the interaction effect between network topology, network density and interoperability costs.

In summation it can be seen that barring two assumptions, all assumptions made in the model can be relaxed or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed without affecting the results and insights generated from the model. Recently, Lazer and Friedman [21] proposed four key criteria for assessing simulation based studies: a) face validity i.e. the behavior of the model should closely follow reality, b) robustness, i.e. the results should hold in the face of trivial changes to the model c) replicable, i.e. other researchers should be able to completely replicate the results of this study and, d) “non-obvious non-trivial results” i.e. the model should allow the researchers to make new and insightful observations. This research satis<sup>fi</sup>es all four criteria. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two criteria are met by modeling the behavior of the individual agents (instead of aggregate behavior in traditional simulations) and choosing speci<sup>fi</sup>c simulation parameters on academic research as well as practitioner reports. In addition, some of the results produced by the model are intuitive (Results 1 & 2). The paper provides suf<sup>fi</sup>cient detail about the simulation process and parameters for any other researcher to replicate the study (third criterion). Finally, the results stated earlier, particularly those regarding the interaction effects are indeed ‘nontrivial’ results that have not been highlighted in previous OSS research (fourth criterion).

## 6.2. Contribution to research

This paper has made the following contributions to the growing body of research on OSS. It has provided a framework that can be used to study the diffusion processes of competing software. The framework concurrently includes multiple under-researched variables and could serve as a building block for diffusion dynamics under different pricing schemes. It was applied to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally study the diffusion processes of OSS and PS where the former is characterized by low license costs, high variability in support costs, various OSS technical capabilities and no threat of withdrawal of support. Although the desktop OS was used as an exemplar, we believe that our framework is general enough to be applied to the investigation of other software as well. For example, in the server OS market, the initial number of OSS adopters, setup costs, support costs and training costs will be higher than those in the desktop OS market, but the model will still be applicable. Similarly, in the open source ERP market, the strength of interoperability issues may be higher than those described in our model in the context of the desktop OS, however the results should still hold.

Furthermore, our study has demonstrated that the diffusion of software is dependent on strategic factors other than price, such as interoperability costs, network topology and density. This result has been demonstrated while incorporating signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heterogeneity among adopters and considering factors such as the threat of withdrawal of support by the PS vendor and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of centrality of neighbors on adoption decisions. We also illustrated that variability in OSS support costs hastens the diffusion of OSS, given other factors. Last but not least, it has incorporated the use of social networking concepts such as degree centrality to study diffusion of OSS and standards. Compared to earlier papers, our model provides a richer depiction of the critical variables and their interactions with each other.

## 6.3. Contribution to practice

In order to illustrate the effects of key variables on the diffusion of OSS we deliberately chose a PS vendor that did not react to the changes in the market. Earlier models that investigated various pricing strategies for a PS vendor in competition with an OSS vendor did so without realizing the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of other variables such as interoperability costs, duration of PS upgrade cycle, threat of withdrawal of support, network topology and network density on the diffusion of OSS. As a consequence we have offered a model that captures the interesting effects of these variables and can now be used as a building block to better investigate the impact of various pricing schemes on the PS and OSS software market. Furthermore, by modeling the effects of these key variables we have shown that PS and OSS vendors need to focus on strategic variables other than price to compete against each other. This helps sensitize vendors to the importance of non-price base of competition. The simulation results revealed that the PS vendor is only threatened by OSS if interoperability costs are low and there is high variability in OSS support costs. With high variability in OSS support costs, the OSS vendor can offer very low support costs to some <sup>fi</sup>rms and build a critical mass that drives diffusion across the network. Our result also suggests that the applicability of the group-based adoption strategies depends on the existing interoperability cost and network density. From a PS vendor's perspective the upgrade policy could be revised by changing a) the frequency of upgrades, b) the additional cost of upgrades and c) the timing of withdrawal of support for earlier versions, all of which can potentially impact the diffusion of its software. The model proposed in this paper could serve as a building block for vendors to develop their own market simulation models.

## 7. Conclusions, limitations and future research

This paper proposes a model to better understand the impact of six key, yet under-researched factors on the diffusion dynamics of OSS. We believe that our model builds on prior research and helps to create a richer theoretical foundation for studying OSS diffusion. Such modeling helps to structure the debate and open up the <sup>fi</sup>eld for additional research [24]. The model presented in this paper could serve as a foundation on which different competitive actions by PS vendors can be superimposed. For example, different pricing policies for withdrawal of support can be examined. The results also indicated that apart from license costs, there are other key variables which the PS vendor can manipulate to prevent the diffusion of OSS. For example, the PS vendor could in<sup>fl</sup>uence interoperability and upgrade costs, change the timing of withdrawal of support to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision of existing and potential adopters. Hence, our model can be considered to be similar in purpose to the agent-based models that have been used in other domains such as supply chain management to create building blocks for studying dynamics [44]. The model also demonstrated the importance of knowing the network structure of consumers. Future research could evaluate which <sup>fi</sup>rms within these network structures can trigger diffusion more or affect diffusion more than others. Those <sup>fi</sup>rms can then be targeted by software vendors and third-party software providers. This research assumed that the unit of adoption is an organization. However, it is possible to generalize the agent behavior to other units of adoption, such as a department or project or individual. Of course, model parameters would have to be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Such a generalization would facilitate hybrid (OSS and PS) adoption in organizations. Future research could also include competitive dynamics with the model presented in this paper to build a Complex Adaptive System [31].

There are some aspects of this study that limit its scope and applicability. First, some of the probabilistic variables in the simulation were modeled using uniform/normal distributions. While these are reasonable choices in the absence of other information, one approach would be to use multiple distributions to condition the results. Second, the decision function of the individual <sup>fi</sup>rms is based on net cost savings. These cost savings are used as a proxy for measuring bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We believe this is a reasonable approach since bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are typically dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to quantify. However, some domainspeci<sup>fi</sup>c bene<sup>fi</sup>t modeling may be possible. Again, we view this as an extension that can be superimposed on the basic model proposed in this paper. Third, diffusion dynamics highlighted by the results regarding interaction effects may not always be visible if one or more parameters dominate the diffusion process. For example, at one extreme, if interoperability costs are extremely high, diffusion will not occur and interaction effects will not be visible. Similarly, at the other extreme if OSS license costs are zero and interoperability costs are close to zero then diffusion dynamics can be driven by these two parameters and the effect of network topology or density is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Fourth, the network structure remains static throughout the course of a simulation. However, from a practical perspective, the business relationships between <sup>fi</sup>rms evolve over time. A <sup>fi</sup>rm that is forced into adoption of an innovation by its neighbor may not necessarily continue its business ties with that neighbor. Finally, though this paper has focused on OSS diffusion, we believe that the model is fairly general and is applicable to studying the dynamics of software diffusion in a variety of contexts. Examining the generalizability of this model is another area of future research.

This would allow social in<sup>fl</sup>uence for upgrades as well as switching. However, switching would be prone to a greater degree of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Hence, <sup>fi</sup>rms would have different cost structures for upgrades as well as switching, both of which are a function of α and n.
