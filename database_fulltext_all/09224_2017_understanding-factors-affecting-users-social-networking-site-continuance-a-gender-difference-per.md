## A B S T R A C T

Social networking sites (SNSs) have attracted more and more people to interact on line. Because of their popularity, <sup>fi</sup>rms and organizations are now marketing their business on SNS pages. It is essential for both SNS providers and <sup>fi</sup>rms to retain their current members. Consequently, use continuity of SNSs has gained the attention of both practitioners and researchers. However, few studies have systematically examined gender differences in such a context. To address this gap, we have developed an advanced framework to explain and analyze gender differences in users’ SNS continuance decisions. We propose an SNS continuance model by integrating SNS-oriented constructs (perceived privacy risk, perceived enjoyment, perceived reputation, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation) into the established ECM-ISC model and introduce gender as a key moderator. Our research results indicate that all the added SNS-oriented constructs in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ SNS continuance directly and indirectly. Furthermore, the impact of each factor on SNS continuance varies by gender. Each gender bases SNS continuance decisions on a different set of factors and/or different weights of the same factors. This study provides evidence that gender effects should be considered in understanding the continued usage of SNSs. It also provides an opportunity to develop a deeper understanding of gender differences in SNS continuance and <sup>fi</sup>lls the research gap regarding this. The theoretical and practical implications of these results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) research has paid a great deal of attention to people’s initial adoption of information technology (IT) [1,2] and continuance use of IT [3]. However, studies on continuance of social networking site (SNS) usage are limited. Our review highlighted only few studies. For example, Wang, Xu, and Chan [4] used computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and the technology acceptance model (TAM) to examine Facebook continuance, while another study by Yin, Cheng, and Zhu [5] explored SNS continuance through the lens of the expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation model in IT (ECM-IT). Today, many <sup>fi</sup>rms and organizations have moved their businesses online and engaged their customers on SNSs. SNSs have created new opportunities for <sup>fi</sup>rms and organizations to gain business values through both internal and external interactions with their stakeholders. Retaining existing subscribers is critical for <sup>fi</sup>rms and organizations to continue gaining pro<sup>fi</sup>t values. For example, in online businesses, individual users’ continuance has contributed to the success of online service businesses [6] and online banking [3]. Given this evidence, studying individual users’ SNS continuance is critical for SNS <sup>fi</sup>rms to retain membership and for businesses to gain pro<sup>fi</sup>table values. The success of SNSs depends largely on users’ continuance usage. However, in the context of SNS, research on people’s adoption, post-adoption, and continued usage remains scarce, and few studies have attempted to examine the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ SNS continuance. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question is: what are the primary antecedents of continued usage of SNSs?

A recent review by Trauth [7] highlights that many studies where gender has been used falling into two broad categories including: one is concerned with gender and the IS workforce, focusing typically on the underrepresentation of women in the IS professions; and the other focuses on the adoption and use of IT, typically searching for gender differences. Gender must be considered in order to achieve a fuller understanding of technology about its adoption, use, and impacts [8–10]. However, gender research in IS discipline lacks suf<sup>fi</sup>cient theorization, as stated “a considerable amount of the gender research that is being published isn’t suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently theorized” (Trauth [7], p. 278). If gender continues to be inadequately studied and theorized, the gender implications of potential transformation from new information and communication technologies will continue being ignored [10]. Thus, it is essential to research into gender issue in the adoption and use of IT in order to provide further insights into the design and development of IT. In IS research, some studies have investigated gender differences in the use of IT. For example, Gefen and Straub [11] found gender differences in the perception of email use when they integrated gender as a moderator into the TAM to explore the role of gender in the IT diffusion model. Venkatesh and Morris [12] posited that women are more strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by subjective norms and perceived ease of use and men are more strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their perceptions of usefulness in their decisions to use a new technology. However, in the context of IT continuance, no known work has yet examined the role of gender in people’s decision-making. A review of the current IS continuance literature of the eighth basket of IS journals (see Appendix A) reveals two underrepresentation areas: (1) IS continuance in the context of SNS and (2) the role of gender in the IT continuance literature. Research on the role of gender in the context of SNS continuance has been neglected. This lead to our second research question: does gender play a moderating role?

Our goal is to <sup>fi</sup>ll in the research gaps by developing a model of SNS continuance and to examine the role of gender in SNS users’ decisions. It is among the very <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to focus on and compare men’s and women’s decisions to use such a timely technology as SNSs. New opportunities and challenges continue to arise as technologies evolve; awareness of gender differences in SNS continuance has valuable implications for SNS <sup>fi</sup>rms to successfully retain their current members. Therefore, determinants of continued use for each target market and gender deserve sustained research. We seek to inform both academicians who study the use of SNSs for business purposes and practitioners who seek to grow SNS usage. Thus, this paper contributes to the IS continuance literature by extending gender differences into SNS continuance. It also informs SNS <sup>fi</sup>rms how to better retain their current members by gender awareness in SNS continuance decision-making. This study has two objectives:

1. to identify the factors affecting SNS continuance, and

2. to investigate gender differences regarding users’ SNS continuance

The paper proceeds as follows. First, we provide literature on our theoretical foundations, which leads to our research model and justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of hypotheses. Second, the chosen research methodology, data analysis, and results are presented. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the research and its practical implications.

## 2. Theoretical background and research model

Our research uses two main theoretical foundations: the expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation model and IS continuance (ECM-ISC) and social role theory (SRT). ECM-ISC is used to develop our conceptual model, while SRT serves as the theoretical frame to posit that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of each construct on users’ decisionmaking about continuing to use SNSs varies by gender. In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the literature on ECM-ISC, and then explain why we chose perceived privacy risk, perceived enjoyment, perceived reputation, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as the key antecedents affecting SNS continuance intention from several theoretical underpinnings. Lastly, we explain why SRT is selected as the theoretical lens to study gender differences in this paper. This leads to our research model of SNS continuance.

## 2.1. IS continuance and SNS

IS continuance research examines the factors that contribute to system usage over time. IT continuance involves investigating the long-term factors (i.e., post-adoption) that contribute to the recurring use of the system [13,14], and is thus integral to the success of a system [3]. Bhattacherjee [3] developed the ECM-ISC by borrowing the expectation–con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT) from the consumer behavior literature and empirically examining factors affecting continuance intention of IS directly and indirectly. The ECT is a cognitive theory that explains consumer satisfaction as a function of expectations and expectancy discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation/con<sup>fi</sup>rmation, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers’ repurchase intention [15]. ECT is widely used in marketing discipline to study consumer repurchase behaviors and continuance service use [16,17], as satisfaction is a key factor that builds and retains customer loyalty for a long-term success [18]. In the IS discipline, users’ continuance decision is similar to consumers’ purchase decision, which could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by user satisfaction with previous experience of IS usage [3]. Furthermore, Bhattacherjee proposes user satisfaction could be determined by con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation and expectation of the IS that is captured by Davis et al.’s [19] perceived usefulness. ECM-ISC could be brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarized as follows: (1) user satisfaction is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by users’ con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation and perceived usefulness; (2) user satisfaction and perceived usefulness determine their continued intention of using an IS; and (3) users’ perceived usefulness is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation from previous IS usage. The ECM-ISC focuses on users’ post-adoption expectations and requires empirical validations when studying IT continuance across different technology contexts to determine its generalizability (Hong et al. [13]). To date, it has been extensively tested in a number of IS continuance research and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed as a strong theoretical underpinning of IS continuance in the context of relatively new technologies [5,20].

Moreover, new features of technology should be incorporated into the original continuance model when studying IT continuance in new technology contexts. In the context of SNS, some notable initial IT continuance studies incorporate various constructs derived from previous literature into IT continuance research. For example, Wang, Xu, and Chan [4] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of general and Facebook-speci<sup>fi</sup>c computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy on Facebook users’ continuance intention based on an extended TAM. In a more recent study, Yin, Cheng, and Zhu [5] incorporated both individual motivations (namely, perceived enjoyment and perceived privacy risk) and nonindividual motivation (namely, structural embeddedness) into ECM-ISC to study users’ SNS continuance usage intention in the Chinese context. The proposed models in these studies have good predictions of users’ SNS-related technology continuance intention; however, they do not account for such primary individual difference factors as reputation and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as important factors in users’ decision-making about SNS usage [21,22]. Each of these factors is critical in understanding users’ SNS continuance decisions. Furthermore, previous continuance research also fails to examine gender differences in studying SNS continuance and usage. In this study, we try to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap by investigating gender differences in SNS continuance decision-making, which is our key contribution. Thus, we propose a comprehensive model of SNS continuance by incorporating more SNS-oriented constructs from the previous literature into ECM-ISC and introducing gender as a key moderator. The original ECM-ISC is shown in Fig. 1. We will explain why these constructs are integrated into our proposed model below.

## 2.2. SNS continuance and specific constructs

## 2.2.1. Integrating perceived privacy risk into the ECM-ISC

Security is usually a major concern that in<sup>fl</sup>uences online use behavior. According to a national survey conducted by the Consumer Reports National Research Center in 2010, >40% of SNS users share their private information online, opening themselves to various online hazards such as scams and identify theft. Privacy risk of information refers to “an individual’s interests and abilities in controlling the handling of data about themselves [23,24].” In IS research, such privacy risk has been shown to in<sup>fl</sup>uence people’s psychological perception and intention to use an IT [25]. A current study of information privacy risk focuses on explaining and predicting theoretical contribution and lacks an understanding of design and action contributions [23]. In the SNS context, privacy risk has been shown to be the primary topic of many studies of SNS users [26,27]. Satisfaction, an individual psychological perception of SNS usage, together with continuance intention will be affected by an individual’s perception of privacy risk. Therefore, we add perceived privacy risk as a predictor of users’ satisfaction with SNS usage and their SNS continuance intention.

## 2.2.2. Integrating perceived enjoyment into the ECM-ISC

Perceived enjoyment is “the extent to which the activity of using the computer is perceived to be enjoyable in its own right, apart from any consequences that may be anticipated [28].” It is an individual difference construct spawned from intrinsic motivation and has been shown to be an important factor that affects an individual’s decisions about IT adoption and usage. For example, Venkatesh [29] detected that as one’s experience with a technology increases, perceived enjoyment becomes more of a player in one’s intention to use a technology, <sup>fi</sup>ndings that could be considered indicative of IT continuance research. Perceived enjoyment has been shown to be an important factor that affects people’s intention to use a hedonic system [30]. The use of SNS is often seen as an enjoyable way to connect with friends and manage networks; thus, perceived enjoyment is considered a direct, vital construct to research SNS continuance intention.

## 2.2.3. Integrating perceived reputation into the ECM-ISC

Reputation in an online context is “an individual user’s recognition as a valuable member among the peers of the virtual community [31,p. 4].” We adopt an individual’s perspective to investigate the reputation-conferring and reputation-enhancing aspects of SNSs. It is reported that 71% of SNS users aged 18–29 years have changed the privacy settings on their pro<sup>fi</sup>les to limit what they share with others online [32], implying that SNS users are aware of and concerned about their online reputations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the SNS environment, a user’s reputation is not built overnight; a user spends time crafting and tweaking his or her status updates, personal information, and photos so that other users perceive him or her as reputable. Reputation is seen as an important role affecting individual use behavior on SNSs; however, its in<sup>fl</sup>uences on SNS continuance have not been examined yet. Thus, we add perceived reputation as a predictor of an individual’s SNS continuance intention.

## 2.2.4. Integrating community identification into the ECM-ISC

Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is from social identity theory. It refers to an individual’s perception of belonging and being involved on SNSs. In online communities, an individual has a notion of membership and forms his or her relationships with others in that community [33]. SNSs provide web-based services empowering individuals to construct a public or semipublic pro<sup>fi</sup>le and interact with other users in various SNS communities [34]. User identi<sup>fi</sup>cation would help them to manage their relationships with their SNS friends [35] and to manage their social network of weak ties [36] on such SNS communities as group pages and brand pages, enabling users to feel a sense of connection to that SNS. SNS users establish a strong sense of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation by their social interactions with other users. Communities of like-minded individuals use SNSs; therefore, community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing an individual’s continued engagement on SNSs [37,38]. We add community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as a predictor of an individual’s SNS continuance intention.

## 2.3. Social role theory

SRT is seeded in the belief that behavioral differences between men and women originate from the social roles humans play in daily life [39]. Social roles are the key causes of gender differences in people’s behaviors within an environment. Such social roles encompass those at a cultural and societal level and “follow from the typical characteristics commonly held by women versus men [40,p.126].” For example, Eagly, Wood, and Diekman [41] stated “in contemporary American society and in most other societies, women have less power, status, and resources” (p.126). It is posited that men and women play different social roles, which may affect their social behaviors because of different societal and cultural expectations [42,43]. Eagly and Wood [43] delineated gender differences in social behaviors into distinguished dimensions and proposed that women are expected to be communal (friendly, unsel<sup>fi</sup>sh, and concerned about others) and men are expected to be agentic (independent, masterful, and competent). It is posited that gender differences in their behaviors could be explained through these extreme dimensions [42]. Women tend to exhibit facilitative and friendly behaviors, which can be termed communal, indicating that their behaviors tend to focus on social- and people-oriented factors. In contrast, men favor a pattern of assertive and independent behaviors, which indicates that their behaviors tend to focus on technology-oriented factors.

SNSs are “web-based services that allow individuals to (1) construct a public or semi-public pro<sup>fi</sup>le within a bounded system, (2) articulate a list of other users with whom they share a connection, and (3) view and traverse their list of connections and those made by others within the system [34].” SNS, viewed as a sociotechnical system, enhances people’s social experience and social roles through their social interactions with others in virtual communities. Given the salience of users’ social roles in SNS, it is more suitable to explain gender differences from sociology and social psychology perspectives rather than typical biological and psychological perspectives of gender essentialism. SRT assumes that men and women play different social roles, shaping their different social experiences [41]. It provides indeed assumptions to study gender differences in the use of SNS from sociology and social psychology resources, supporting that SRT could be viewed as another strand of gender essentialism [7]. One example from the recent IS literature is the work of Chai, Das, and Rao [44]. On the

basis of the assumption that men and women show different social behaviors because of their different societal and cultural expectations, women bloggers’ knowledge sharing behavior is more in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by trust, reciprocity, and social ties than men bloggers. It is worth to note that this study include gender as a variable, indicating that gender differences observed in bloggers’ knowledge sharing behavior is assumed to be applied to both men and women as a group. This suggests that SRT focuses on betweengroup differences rather than within-group differences in explaining the role of gender in the context of IT use. On the basis of the above discussion, SRT is selected as a theoretical lens to explain gender differences in this study. From SRT perspective, all women will be considered the same as a group regarding their social communities in the context of SNS environment. Thus, in this study, gender will be measured as a variable in terms of men and women.

Following this logic, we assume that social roles in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world largely transfer to the online world and affect users’ online use behaviors. Our study uses SRT to explore gender differences in continued usage of SNSs and to examine whether gender in<sup>fl</sup>uences how people make their decisions to continue using SNSs. We assume that social roles in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world do transfer to the online world. In particular, we propose that SNS is an exemplary context in which SRT should apply, because people regularly interact with one another in both of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online contexts. The agentic nature of men and the communal nature of women lead us to believe that men and women will perceive technology- and people-oriented factors differently and therefore will behave differently in SNS continuance decisions. This seems to suggest that gender will have different effects on SNS continuance. In addition, as SNSs involve a solitary person logging in and spending time on the sites, we deem this SNS environment to be individually driven; thus, we explore how the relationships between the studied constructs and intention to continue using SNSs vary within genders. Therefore, we integrate gender as a moderator into our proposed model of SNS continuance. We believe that the effects of added factors on SNS continuance will vary across genders because of the nature of gender social roles on SNSs.

## 2.4. SNS continuance model with a gender perspective

Overall, we propose an SNS continuance model by integrating perceived privacy risk, perceived enjoyment, perceived reputation, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation into the ECM-ISC, and use gender as a moderator, as noted in Fig. 2. Our aim is to investigate the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of those added SNS constructs on SNS continuance intention and the moderating effects of gender. Thus, we will not test the hypotheses pertaining to the ECM-ISC, shown in compound lines.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Hypotheses on added constructs in SNS continuance

Privacy risk of information refers to “an individual’s interests and abilities in controlling the handling of data about themselves [23,24].” Some previous studies have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences of privacy risk on people’s attitudes, such as the willingness to conduct online transactions [25] and to share personal information [45]. The study of system users’ risk concerns throughout the cycle of usage is important because consumers may not understand the hazards and risks of usage until after they have gained experience with a system. In a rush to adopt a popular SNS, many users do not read the terms of usage, nor are many adopters interested in or savvy enough to appreciate any potential hazard and resultant personal losses from the usage. As a credence service whose security is uncertain even after usage, users’ risk concerns can easily change over time. Privacy risk has persistently been a major concern in the context of SNS because of its openness nature and popularity. As such, continued usage of SNSs should not be assumed. ## 3.2. Gender difference hypotheses

On the basis of socialized assumption that men favor individualistic and task-oriented environments, men are more attentive to such stimuli as goals and objects [43]. People use SNSs for a variety of purposes including developing/maintaining relationships, group interaction, collaboration, and commercial activities [35,51,52]. These online activities are accomplished with well-designed features and functions provided by SNSs. In other words, SNS technology features empower people to achieve their purposes of SNS usage, which determines the level of user satisfaction with SNS usage. As such, satisfaction will have stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uences on men’s SNS continuance decision, because it depends on SNS technology features more than social features. SNS will be used more as a task-oriented environment for men users to achieve goals in relations to online activities. We expect that satisfaction with SNS usage will be more salient for men when making decisions about SNS continuance.

Hypothesis 6. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of satisfaction on SNS continuance intention will be stronger for men than for women. Drawn on SRT, men are believed to be more task oriented than women [39]. We believe men will view an SNS in a more taskoriented manner as opposed to a socially oriented manner and women will view the same SNS with a more socially oriented manner. Perceived usefulness addresses how “capable [a system is] of being used advantageously” and how effective one believes “using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance [1,p. 320].” Such a de<sup>fi</sup>nition associates perceived usefulness with task completion and task orientation, which are said to be “more important for men than for women on an ongoing basis.”[12] As such, perceived usefulness would in<sup>fl</sup>uence people’s decisions as a more task-oriented factor than as a social-oriented task factor. Usefulness will be salient for men to master the SNS environment in completing individual tasks rather than for women to manage their social relationships with others in the same SNS because of their different social roles [41,53]. Women would ignore such personal outcomes because of their communal nature and social role of “other-oriented” in the environment [41], weakening the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of usefulness. Consequently, we believe that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived usefulness on continuance decisions will be different for men and for women.

Hypothesis 7. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived usefulness on satisfaction will be stronger for men than for women.

Hypothesis 8. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived usefulness on SNS continuance intention will be stronger for men than for women.Concerns for risk and resultant personal losses in<sup>fl</sup>uence online service adoption. Studying online users’ purchasing behavior, users’ perceived risk has shown to be more relevant to women than to men [54]. Previous research has shown that men and women differ in their perceptions of risk and their risktaking behavior [55,56]. Women are believed to have consistently higher levels of concerns about risk, and men will be more willing to take risks [57]. By conducting a national study of mutual fund investors, Dwyer, Gilkeson, and List [55] reported that women exhibit less risk-taking behavior than men do in their mutual fund investment decisions. Women have been found to be more concerned with loss of privacy and perceive higher levels of severity of the negative outcomes of losing privacy [54]. These previous studies indicate gender differences in risk perceptions and risk-taking behavior, suggesting that the negative outcomes (including psychological perceptions and behavior intentions) of such risk perceptions would be stronger for women than for men. Similar gender differences are likely to occur in users’ decision about SNS continuance.In understanding gender differences in the role of privacy risk as a determination of users’ decision about SNS continuance, it is necessary to <sup>fi</sup>rst understand the primary causes of people’s privacy risk in the context of SNS. The popularity of SNSs in people’s daily life has empowered users to manage their social relationships with their SNS friends and other others using the same SNS [34,35]. As a connection strategy of maintaining and developing relationship in SNSs, users disclose a variety of information including their personal information through presenting themselves on personal pro<sup>fi</sup>les and communicating with others in various online SNSs [48,50,58]. It suggests that social interactive nature of SNSs raises users’ privacy risk, as personal information could be easily collected and used without users’ knowledge and permission. Gender differences are likely to occur in responding to privacy risk and its in<sup>fl</sup>uences based on the assumptions of SRT. SRT posits that female gender role “favors the acquisition of superior interpersonal skills,” where women inherently are more communal [41]. This suggests that women users will focus on SNSs’ congenial social interactions with other users, resulting in disclosing more information and a higher potential risk of privacy risk than men users. As such, women perceive more privacy risks and are more concerned about privacy in online environments [59,60]. Women have been shown to engage in privacy risk protection behaviors more often than men have when using SNSs and to be more aware of privacy risk in such online environments in general [59]. Thus, we expect that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of privacy risk will be relatively stronger in women’s satisfaction with SNS usage and their SNS continuance decisions compared with that of men.

Hypothesis 9. The negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived privacy risk on satisfaction will be stronger for women than for men.

Hypothesis 10. The negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived privacy risk on SNS continuance intention will be stronger for women than for men.People using SNSs as hedonic system in maintaining and developing social relationships online could obtain enjoyment. Users’ social interaction plays an important role for people to experience and gain enjoyment or playfulness on SNSs. SRT suggests that gender differences would occur in this process because of different gender social roles on the basis of the assumption that women tend to focus on social- and peopleoriented factors as well as tend to focus on technology-oriented factors. A recent example is the work of Zhang, Lee, Cheung, and Chen [61], studying the role in users’ decisions about switching blogging platforms. It posits that “additional variances could be explained by other important factors, which may include. . perceived enjoyment” (p. 545). Perceived enjoyment is an individual difference construct spawned from intrinsic motivation, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the performance of an activity for no apparent reinforcement other than the process of performing the activity per se [28,p. 1112].” Intrinsic motivation is believed to be more of a focus for women because of their predominant process orientation rather than task orientation from a social role perspective [39,62]. Therefore, we project that perceived enjoyment will be stronger for women than for men in relation to one’s continuance intention to use SNSs.

Hypothesis 11. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived enjoyment on SNS continuance intention will be stronger for women than for men.SNS users have control over their pro<sup>fi</sup>les, allowing them to craft and self-portray their online reputations. This activity of reputation management [63] remains understudied in the IS discipline; however, other disciplines suggest that gender differences do exist. For example, women are more likely to use privacy settings, compose more status updates, and delete friends on social media [64]. In relation to SRT, which claims that the female gender role is associated with less power and a lowered social status [40], we posit that women are more likely to engage in reputation management to improve their self-branded image and status than men are. SNS users’ online reputations are shaped among their online social networks. The communal nature of women suggested by SRT indicates that women will focus more on social interactions with others to improve their reputations on SNS than men will. As such, in an effort to manage their reputations, women are more likely to continue using SNS more frequently. Therefore, we assert that women will be more concerned than men will that SNS continue to provide them with the ability to champion their online reputations and thus will value the reputation-conferring aspects of SNS more than men will during continuance decisions.

Hypothesis 12. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived reputation on SNS continuance intention will be stronger for women than for men.SNS users will be more committed to the community when they interact with other community members. Women SNS users will feel more committed to an SNS community because of their communal nature and interest in interpersonal social interactions. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is believed to be relative to a socially oriented manner rather than to a taskoriented manner, as it focuses on building users’ commitment to SNSs by interacting with and connecting to others. According to SRT, women will have more of a sense of community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation because they are interested in social interactions and personality of concern for others [43]. This leads to our justi<sup>fi</sup>cation that community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is more likely to be a focus for women, which will in<sup>fl</sup>uence their SNS continuance intention more strongly.

## 4.1. Sample and data collection procedure

## 5.3. Structural model testing and results

PLS was used to analyze the structural model and test the proposed hypotheses. Fig. 3 shows the results of structural model test. Results suggest that H1, H3, and H5 are supported, while H2 and H4 are not supported. These results indicate that perceived privacy risk negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences users’ satisfaction with SNS usage. Perceived enjoyment and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation are both positively associated with users’ SNS continuance intention. This may indicate that the relationship between privacy risk and SNS continuance intention is fully mediated by user’s satisfaction with SNS usage. Furthermore, we found the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived reputation on users’ SNS continuance intention. However, in contrast with our hypothesis, the relationship is negative, indicating that people are worried their SNS pro<sup>fi</sup>les and usage could harm their reputation and thus social status. Moreover, consistent with the assumptions of ECM-ISC, users’ con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of SNS usage is positively related to their perceived usefulness and satisfaction, which in<sup>fl</sup>uence their SNS continuance intention positively.

## 6.1. Theoretical contribution

This study makes some important contributions to IS research. First, this study integrates different SNS-oriented constructs into the ECM-ISC to study users’ continued use of SNS. The proposed extended model of SNS continuance very well predicts users’ decision-making about SNS continuance. The research results provide support for the importance of the added SNS-oriented constructs (perceived privacy risk, perceived enjoyment, perceived reputation, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation) in users’ SNS continuance decisions. Thus, this foundational contribution paves the way for additional usage of speci<sup>fi</sup>cally tailored ECM-ISC models for new research contexts. Our study contributes to the technology usage and continuance literature by developing an SNS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c continuance model. It provides good practice into how a theory or model could be generalized into new contexts. It indicates that new technology features could be brought into users’ decisions in their adoption and continuance decisions as long as technology evolves. IS researchers need to tailor these features into researching IS adoption and usage. Further research may use this as a foundational model to study continuance decision-making in other newly emerged IT.

Second, this study underscores the differences in how men and women perceive the importance of the various constructs when making decisions about SNS continuance. It explores and explains why and how gender differences exist in users’ SNS continuance. By highlighting such differences, this study demonstrates the value of devoting speci<sup>fi</sup>c research attention to the needs of the gender role in IS usage. Although previous studies have focused on SNS continuance with the assumption that men and women base their decisions on similar criteria [4,5,84], this study extends the existing literature by systematically comparing gender differences in SNS continuance and con<sup>fi</sup>rms gender variance in determinants of IT continuance decisions. The research results indicate that genders base their SNS continuance decisions on different factors and different weights of the same factors across SNS contexts. It <sup>fi</sup>lls the research gap about gender and SNSs by focusing on and examining gender differences in users’ SNS continuance decisions. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings become valuable and useful for researchers to understand the role of gender in SNS users’ continuance decisions. As such, this study contributes to advancing gender differences research by extending it to IT continuance and an SNS context. Our study may encourage researchers to study gender differences in IS usage.

Third, this study uses SRT to study gender differences in SNS continuance and to show that SRT could serve an appropriate theory to explain gender differences in SNS usage. This theoretically grounded study provides some support for how gender and IS research may be theorized and how gender differences may be explained theoretically in the use of IT. Previous studies have used various theories to study gender in IS research, such as individual difference theory and IT [85]. Successfully bringing SRT into IS continuance research, this paper examines gender differences in IS continuance and usage from a new theoretical perspective. This leads us to believe that theories that may be used to theorize the phenomenon of gender differences may vary by differences in research domains. Future research may further incorporate SRT into other IS usage research to substantiate and extend our results as well as bring in new theoretical perspectives in studying the role of gender.

Fourth, H12 is not totally supported, although signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gender differences do exist in the relationship of perceived reputation and users’ SNS continuance intention. In contrast with our hypothesis, reputation negatively affects SNS continuance intention for both men and women. This indicates women base continuance on reputation-conferring aspects of SNSs more than men do. The effect was negative, however, rather than positive, suggesting that women were worried their SNS pro<sup>fi</sup>les and usage could harm their reputations and social statuses. As a result of men’s higher social status, such <sup>fi</sup>ndings could stem from SRT’s assumption that women are more careful when engaging in activities to craft and manage their reputations. SNS reputation management tools are important decision criteria for women and must be implemented carefully, warranting interests into future research.

## 6.2. Practical contribution

Given the limited studies on SNS continuance and the importance of users’ SNS continuance in retaining <sup>fi</sup>rm memberships, this study has some important implications for practice. Users’ continuance usage is critical for SNSs to sustain success, as users have many choices of SNSs. By obtaining an enhanced understanding of users’ SNS continuance decisions, SNS <sup>fi</sup>rms will be able to retain their current members by designing and implementing more effective technological features and providing better services. For example, given that perceived privacy risk negatively affects users’ continuance intention of SNSs, we know that users are very cautious about security issues of SNS usage. Users want personal information they post on SNSs to be con<sup>fi</sup>dential and protected. Protecting users’ privacy information is necessary to promote continued use of SNSs. SNSs may provide better customer service by developing more effective technological functions and setting up relative rules and policies to protect users’ privacy. Furthermore, given that perceived enjoyment and satisfaction are highly associated with users’ SNS continuance intention, SNS <sup>fi</sup>rms may provide better service by improving the level of playfulness of their website and users’ satisfaction.

Second, businesses (e.g., social commerce) are following consumers to social media channels, seeking to use SNSs to further engage their customers online. This research suggests businesses could better engage their customers by advocating for the SNS platform to provide reliable protection of customers’ personally identifying information. By obtaining insights about individuals’ continued usage of SNSs, businesses may interact with their customers and develop their marketing strategies more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. For example, businesses may increase their customers’ commitment to their websites by frequently interacting with their customers and posting marketing information on SNSs. SNSs also could be used as platforms for customers to interact with each other and acquire information and knowledge about products, brands, and <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Third, our gender difference <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide important information to purveyors of SNSs and businesses engaged on SNSs. The gender differences reported here can aid designers of SNSs in developing more user-friendly social networking services. For example, given that perceived reputation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on women users’ SNS continuance intention, SNS providers may provide reputation management tools for women to better control their SNS pro<sup>fi</sup>les and usage.

Fourth, the gender difference results found examining SNSs might extend to other social media sites, for example, special interest groups, whereby participants manage a pro<sup>fi</sup>le, such as hobbyist or technical and medical support. At present, many <sup>fi</sup>rms use SNSs for viral marketing, word-of-mouth promotions, and crowdsourcing. Such social strategies allow companies to manage public and customer relationships and to develop, test, and promote new products and online services. Similarly, organizations are creating their own branded SNSs or establishing their brands on many other SNS platforms. In these contexts, it is important to recognize that gender-based roles and gender differences affect usage of SNSs. Practitioner development of new SNSs and fan pages on existing SNSs should consider these gender differences and tailor content to each gender.

## 6.3. Limitations and suggestions for future research

This study has some limitations that may create interesting opportunities for future research. First, this study used Facebook as the sole investigation context. Facebook’s platform-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors may affect the study of SNS continuance. Future research that uses a cross-platform survey would be useful to validate the proposed SNS continuance model and generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other contexts. Second, this study uses college students as the research sample. Although college students could present the largest portion of SNS users, future research may generalize our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other user populations of SNSs in other environments (e.g., the workplace). Third, this study explores a limited set of antecedents of SNS continuance. Future research may enrich the research model by incorporating more antecedents based on the relevant literature and theoretical perspective. For example, future research that examines SNS continuance in the workplace may integrate constructs such as organizational factors. Fourth, SNSs are in a global environment. It would be interesting to look at cultural differences of SNS usage in future studies.

## 7. Conclusion

Overall, our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms the important role of gender in the extended ECM-ISC model, which is tailored to the context of SNS continuance decision-making. The research results show that the added factors (perceived privacy risk, perceived enjoyment, perceived reputation, and community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation) and gender have complex in<sup>fl</sup>uences on users’ SNS continuance decisions. The research results show that all the added factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ SNS continuance and that gender moderates these effects. Gender has a predictive moderating power in users’ SNS continuance, offering important contributions to both theory and practice.
