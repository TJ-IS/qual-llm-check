# Stakeholder management in IOS projects: analysis of an attempt to implement an electronic patient file

Albert Boonstra<sup>1</sup>, David Boddy<sup>2</sup> and Sheena Bell<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Faculty of Economics and Business, University of Groningen, Groningen, The Netherlands; <sup>2</sup>Department of Management, University of Glasgow, Glasgow, U.K.

Correspondence: Albert Boonstra, Faculty of Economics and Business, University of Groningen, Post Box 800, 9700 AV Groningen, The Netherlands. Tel: + 31 50 363 7289: E-mail: albert.boonstra@rug.n

## Abstract

Implementing an inter-organizational system (IOS) requires significant organizational as well as technical changes. These will affect stakeholders (upon whom promoters depend) with varying degrees of power and with varying degrees of interest in the system. Identifying stakeholders and understanding the sources of their attitudes will enable promoters to meet their expectations more fully, and hence encourage acceptance. We examine these issues through a theoretically based study of an attempt to introduce an Electronic Patient File system in The Netherlands. All saw that the system would benefit patients, yet powerful players resisted its implementation, fearing it would affect their interests. This paper develops and tests a model of stakeholder management, showing that those with high interest in the system lacked the power to implement it, while those with low interest had the power to block it. These negative attitudes were shaped by concerns not about the system itself, but about the likely effects on working routines, power, culture and finance. This paper implies that those promoting an IOS (in any sector) should, from the outset of a project, identify their power sources and seek to reconcile stakeholder interests.

## Introduction

Health care agencies routinely collect administrative and clinical information about their patients, traditionally storing it in paper files. Most use computer systems to support their procedures, but since these typically operate independently they cannot automatically exchange patient information. So, while much information about a patient’s medical history and current condition is stored electronically, it is unlikely that health workers will have online access to it.

Medical staff agree that patients would benefit if those treating them had access to a comprehensive electronic record of their medical history – variously called an Electronic Healthcare Record (Grimson, 2001), Computerized Patient Record (Raghupathi and Tan, 2002) or, as in this case, an Electronic Patient File (EPF). The benefits include savings from faster referrals between doctors, fewer delays in ordering tests and getting results, fewer errors in reporting and fewer redundant tests. Online access would support hospital treatment and enable pharmacies (retail outlets supplying medicines etc. to patients) and general practitioners (GPs – doctors providing care to patients in the community) to update their files when patients leave hospital. Yet, progress in implementing such systems in health care has been slow (Andersen, 1998; Burt and Hing, 2005).

Computer-based systems that enable information to flow automatically between organizations are becoming common, and a Delphi study by Daniel and White (2005) found a strong expectation among the participants that organizations will be characterized by a greater number of external linkages.

Obtaining the full potential of an inter-organizational system (IOS) depends on realizing that it is not just a technical project, but also an organizational one. To have any chance of success, the common technical features of an IOS need to become embedded in the diverse social contexts of distinct units, with unique histories, circumstances and degrees of autonomy. As Williams (1997) pointed out, an effective IOS requires high levels of rational control and/or high levels of conscious consent and cooperation – all of which are likely to be problematic between autonomous organizations. The organizational changes affect numerous stakeholders, who may choose not to cooperate. Promoters need to understand stakeholder attitudes and develop a strategy for managing them.

Implementing an IOS is likely to be particularly challenging in health care, as a feature of the sector is that responsibility for delivering health care to a patient is typically shared between many autonomous organizations. This paper aims to analyse the implementation issues that promoters face through an empirical study of a project to introduce an EPF within a (fragmented) regional health care system in The Netherlands. Management at a large hospital proposed a system which, using web technology, would enable GPs and pharmacies to share medical data with the hospital. Authorized users could access patient data from any location, and the system would conduct automated checks for drug and allergy interactions, record clinical notes and prescriptions, schedule appointments and distribute laboratory data. The hospital’s Information Systems (IS) department would manage the system, costs would be shared and a representative council would provide control.

At the time of writing no progress had been made in implementing the system, and the paper examines the reasons for this failure. It contributes to our understanding by developing and testing a theoretically grounded approach to managing stakeholders in any IOS project. The next section draws on the organizational literature on IS to develop our model, which we then use to analyse and interpret the empirical data. This analysis suggests theoretical and practical implications for those implementing IOS projects in any sector.

## Theoretical perspectives

The purpose of our model (shown in Figure 1) is to explain the success or failure of IOS projects, so that the first variable identifies the outcomes. There are many ways of assessing these: Cooper and Zmud (1990) suggest that implementation is only complete when it is integrated into the routines of the organization and used in the intended way. More recently, and in a health care context, May (2006) proposed a normalization model. This evaluates the extent to which a system has become embedded into routine clinical practice, and is taken for granted as part of normal professional work.

That potentially valuable systems fail to achieve the expected outcomes suggest a need for a theory to explain why this happens, and to suggest ways of improving the success rate. The challenge facing those implementing an IOS is that it is likely to have radical effects on the external relations between each of the participating organizations and at the same time require internal changes in each. Members of autonomous organizations need to accept the IOS if it is to become ‘embedded’ or ‘normalized’ in the respective units.

Lucas (1975); Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) and Sauer (1993) have developed models of, and explanations for, the problems faced when implementing IS in general. Lyytinen and Hirschheim’s (1987) paper is especially relevant to the case of IOS, as they defined failure as ‘the inability of an IS to meet a specific stakeholder group’s expectations’ (p. 263). As such they brought the (then) recently developed stakeholder concept (Mitroff, 1983; Freeman, 1984) into the IS field. Mitroff (1983) defined stakeholders as ‘all those parties who either affect or who are affected by an organisation’s actions, behaviours and policies’. The definition implies that those implementing a system need to ensure that it meets stakeholder expectations if they are to gain, or retain, their support.

Pouloudi and Whitley (1997), Pouloudi (1999), Pan (2005) and Mantzana et al. (2006) have used the stakeholder concept to study why systems fail. Pouloudi (1999), for example, showed how an inability to meet the expectations of the medical profession limited the use of a health information network, even though other professionals were willing to participate. Meeting stakeholder expectations will be especially challenging when implementing an IOS since the independent players are likely to have different objectives and expectations. For example, Barrett (1999) and Fearon and Philip (2005) related the low adoption of electronic trading systems in the insurance industry to the different cultural assumptions and expectations of the relevant stakeholders.

If stakeholders develop a negative attitude to a system, promoters need to analyse both the interests that shape those attitudes, and to assess the power of the stakeholder to affect the outcome. A useful tool for this is Eden and Ackermann’s (1998) ‘power-interest grid’, which distinguishes stakeholders according to their interest in a project, and their power to affect it. Such a tool helps to ‘determine which players’ interests and power bases must be taken into account to address the problemy (and)yprovides some information on how to convince stakeholders to change their views’ (Bryson, 2004, p. 31).

Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) observed that stakeholder attitudes to a system develop from their beliefs about whether it will serve their interests. Such attitudes may relate to the system itself and/or to its wider context (Fitzgerald and Russo, 2005): the significance of the latter being stressed by Czarniawska (2004), who argued that it was impossible to understand human intentions by ignoring the settings in which they develop.

Pettigrew (1987) showed the significance of analysing the context within which stakeholders operate, a context that has outer, inner and historical dimensions. The outer refers to the social, political and other features of the wider environment of a health care organization. The inner context refers to features of the organization itself (such as its structure or culture) within which an IOS project evolves, while the historical dimension reflects the view that past events influence how people interpret the present, and the decisions they make. This contextual perspective leads Pettigrew to propose that change is an iterative, multi-level process, with outcomes shaped by the interests and commitments of individuals and groups as they interact with each other in response to their interpretations of the changing context.

Such contexts have many dimensions: stakeholders may take a positive view of some, and a negative view of others. Cummings and Doh (2000) therefore advocate ‘context-specific’ stakeholder mapping to identify the threats (or benefits) seen by each group of stakeholders, and their likely reactions. These reactions reflect an interpretive process (Walsham, 1993): an IOS is not an objective phenomenon, since a promoter (also a stakeholder) has consciously created it to reflect their responsibilities and interests. Others will evaluate the proposal from their perspective, and perhaps attach a different meaning to it: do they recognize the problem? is the proposal feasible? how will it affect their interests?

Such context-specific stakeholder analysis can draw on cumulative IS research to identify the aspects of a system and its context that are likely to shape interpretations. Davis (1993) developed the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which proposes that acceptance depends on perceptions about a system’s usefulness and ease of use. Others have looked beyond the immediate features to other aspects of the inner or outer contexts. One line of research has examined the links between a new system and established routines (Marakas and Hornik, 1996) or business processes (Currie and Brown, 1997; Mellin, 2002). Markus (1983) and Knights and Murray (1994) focus on the interaction between a system and the distribution of power, while others focus on culture (Cooper, 1994; Boonstra et al., 2004) or finance (Boonstra et al., 2004). This leads us (in the present case) to examine how stakeholder attitudes (positive or negative) arose from the perceived usefulness of the system itself, or from its likely effects on the contextual factors of working practices, power, culture or finance.

We also need to analyse the relative power of the stakeholders in an IOS, and the sources of that power. Hales (2001) identified four organizational power sources:

\- Coercive – the authority to give instructions;

\- Reward – the authority to reward desirable behaviour;

\- Administrative expertise – the authority to create policies or rules; and

\- Technical expertise – the authority to access expertise and information.

Using any of these sources to influence others’ action depends on the target recognizing and valuing the source (such as technical expertise).

The issue in the present research is whether those promoting the EPF have the power to influence others to accept it, since they are unlikely to have direct formal authority over stakeholders in other organizations. While coercion by a powerful trading partner can encourage acceptance (Morris et al., 2003), those who lack that power over potential partners in an IOS need to use other means to convince them of its merits.

The final variable in our model is the implementation process – the actions, reactions and interactions of the stakeholders as they seek to move their organizations from the present to a future state. The challenge arises from Mitroff’s observation that stakeholders do not generally share the same definition of an organization’s problems, and hence do not share the same solutions. This implies that ‘approaches to organizational problem solving, which pre-suppose prior consensus or agreement among partiesybreak down. Instead a method is needed that builds off a starting point of disagreementy (Mitroff, 1983, p. 5).

Pettigrew’s emphasis on interaction implies that stakeholder attitudes towards a proposal can change in response to the actions of others. Understanding stakeholder attitudes and interests, and the implementation process, enables promoters to manage a project in a way that increases their chances of a successful outcome.

This proposes that the outcomes of an IOS will depend on the interaction of stakeholders and aspects of the inner and outer contexts, and on the processes which those promoting the system create to implement it. To test the model we use the EPF case, structuring the analysis around these questions:

\- Who were the active stakeholders in the initial stages of the EPF project?

\- What contextual factors shaped their attitudes to the proposal?

\- What contextual factors shaped their interest in the system, and their power to affect it?

\- How did the implementation process affect the outcomes?

\- What theoretical and practical conclusions follow from our analysis?

## Method

We used a qualitative, single case study method, as this reflects the nature of the issues under discussion: the research questions were exploratory (Yin, 1999; Dube´ and Pare´, 2003), intended to identify the attitudes, interests and power of stakeholders towards the proposed system. The issues were unclear and had to be studied in their natural setting to understand the nature and complexity of the proposal and its context (Benbasat et al., 1987). The unit of analysis of the research is the EPF system and its possible acceptance by intended users and other stakeholders. We also took an interpretive approach, assuming that stakeholders would view the system and its context subjectively in the light of their interests.

To ensure construct validity we used several sources of evidence – interviews, written reports, letters, observations, minutes from committee meetings and media coverage. We sought internal validity by looking for patterns, dominant themes and explanations in the material and tried to avoid bias by asking respondents to comment on our interpretation. We sought external validity by interviewing several people from each stakeholder group.

We negotiated initial access in 2004 through the manager of the IS department in the hospital proposing the EPF. We then interviewed him and the hospital’s general manager. These initial respondents identified other stakeholders who would be affected by the system: we then asked those who were directly involved in this stage of the project to participate in the study. This was therefore an iterative process, which gradually broadened the range of stakeholders whose views were recorded. Almost all agreed, and we interviewed them by telephone or at their place of work between August and November 2004. Respondents included hospital managers, IS staff and physicians and, beyond the hospital, GPs, pharmacists and EPF vendors (see Table 1).

We assured interviewees that discussions were confidential. Interviews typically started with open-ended questions about the proposed system, followed by more specific questions about their involvement with, understanding of, and attitudes towards, aspects of the system, such as the likely impact on working processes, relative power, culture and finance. Most interviews lasted 30– 90 min, with notes being taken to prepare an account of the meeting. Respondents checked these accounts for accuracy and sometimes suggested changes.

The interviews contained three sets of questions. The first set was directed at identifying the most relevant stakeholders at the time of the study, and the second at assessing their interests and power in relation to the system. The third set was directed at identifying their attitudes to the system.

We analysed responses by allocating them to one of the categories that theory suggested would affect their attitudes to the EPF proposal – the system and its likely usefulness, and the likely effects on working processes, power, culture and finance. We discussed this categorization extensively within the research team and with some initial respondents (M1, ISM2 and GP2), which led to some redefinition of the categories. ## Results

## Who were the active stakeholders in the initial stages of the EPF project?

Responses to the first set of questions showed that the groups in bold in Table 2 were most important at that stage of the project. They were aware of the proposed system, and could see that it would affect their interests.

Patients, insurers, nursing homes, government agencies, system vendors and consultancies are potentially relevant stakeholders, although many would only be indirectly affected. At the time of the study they were either unaware of the system, had not developed a coherent representation about the arrangements, or were not yet involved in discussions about it.

Attitudes to the EPF and working practices Table 4 shows that the parties generally agree that an EPF would improve patient care, and enable smoother administration.

However, GPs especially comment that changing to an EPF may be complicated, and are especially concerned about the starting point. If the system reflects the current working practices of one speciality, the others may see this as detrimental to them. Pharmacists and GPs feel that the hospital would force them to use the hospital system, which would make it more powerful.

Attitudes to the EPF and power A more integrated chain of health care providers will require orchestration and leadership. It is clear from Table 5 that the hospital’s management and its IT department favour a hospitalled proposal, while GPs and pharmacies oppose this. Hospital managers believe that they are best able to fill that role: they are the biggest player and the one most able to put resources into the system. Other parties suspect the hospital of using this to dominate the whole chain.

More broadly, there are also hints in these comments of a broader GP concern, that insurers or government agencies could use the system as a means of control. It would enable others to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of treatments and providers, leading to tighter control and less professional autonomy.

Attitudes to the EPF and culture Few respondents commented on this aspect, but their views contrast sharply. Table 6 shows that the GPs fear a move from a fragmented to a more integrated culture, while hospital management sees many potential benefits in this.

Attitudes to the EPF and finance Table 7 shows equally contrasting views on the financial implications of EPF.

The promoters believe the benefits will outweigh the costs, and that the benefits will be shared fairly: the critics are unsure, believing their costs will rise without bringing any clear financial benefits.

However, the hospital has little power to force others to cooperate. Of the sources of power identified by Hales (2001), the hospital has no coercive or administrative power over the independent pharmacies and GPs. It has no sanctions over them and no authority to create policies or rules governing their behaviour. It has potential reward power, and the power of technical expertise in IS – especially in relation to the GPs. However, this is only relevant if the other parties recognize and accept that power – which in this case the pharmacies and GPs were not prepared to do.

GPs run their medical practice as independent businesses and have complete autonomy in their working practices, including how they deal with a patient. All said that EPF would improve administration and patient care but were unwilling to support the system, which the hospital was proposing.

Most pharmacies use advanced computer systems, connected in regional networks, to record the drugs prescribed to patients. However, these systems are not connected with GPs’ or hospitals’ systems, nor are the various regional pharmacy systems linked. This limits the ability to exchange electronic data with GPs, health workers and hospitals. The chairman of a group of pharmacies said:

Pharmacies have invested heavily in joint IT systems and networks which enable them to trace their customers’ drug use, even if they buy them at different pharmacies. These systems are very advanced and work quite well. A big improvement could be achieved if GPs and hospitals would open up their systems and transfer their data to our systems. We believe that we are the appropriate party to manage and operate an EPF for all health care providers. We posses the best infrastructure and the systems are there.

This quotation illustrates that pharmacies feel that they are in a strong position to lead this idea. They use modern IS and have an effective lobbying body – and were unwilling to support the hospital’s proposal.

A hospital director commented:

There are so many parties with their own specific view of this problem. We need a project champion with the vision and the power to bring the parties together and to force them to invest and cooperate. But there is no such person who can force others to contribute or participate. All participants are autonomous and cannot be forced. Everyone sees the benefits, but no-one wants to spend the time and money.

The stakeholders evidently had different attitudes to the EPF. Although the hospital was keen to introduce the system, the GPs and pharmacies opposed it. During the research period the promoters made no progress towards the expected outcomes.

## How did the interaction of stakeholders during implementation affect the outcomes?

The positions of the stakeholders shown in Figure 2 represent their relationship at one point in time. The essence of managing stakeholders is to change aspects of a system, or of its context, to encourage stakeholders to take a more positive attitude towards it. Those advocating a new system need to ensure that enough influential stakeholders support the project because they believe it will serve their interests.

The hospital managers developed the system in isolation from the other stakeholders and then presented it to them, stressing the benefits to patient care. They relied on rational arguments and statements of benefit – making no attempt to understand the interests of the GPs and pharmacies upon whose cooperation the system depended. Presenting rational arguments for EPF seems a reasonable step – but was clearly insufficient when powerful players opposed it, and so blocked implementation. The outcome was that the system was not embedded in the respective organizations, nor did it become a normal feature of working practice.

Innovation depends not only on rational arguments, but also on promoters developing political will and expertise to overcome resistance (Boddy, 2002). IOS projects inevitably affect people differentially, so promoters need to be able to manage these interests as they pursue local as well as organizational goals. So, promoters of the EPF could follow Strategy 1 (arrow 1 in Figure 3) of strengthening the power of the hospital. They could do this by mobilizing other parties, such as patients, their representatives, insurers and governments who would have an interest in an EPF. These stakeholders could then actively enter the field and try to persuade GPs and pharmacies to join. If some start to cooperate and make use of the IOS, laggards will be isolated and the public, insurers or government may be able to force them to join. Hales’ (2001) model of the sources of power suggests other possible strategies: the hospital could use their resource and expertise power to make the proposal more attractive – for example, by offering to meet more of the GPs’ financial costs, or sharing their technical expertise with the GPs and pharmacies.

Alternatively, they could follow Strategy 2 (arrow 2 in Figure 3) – of increasing the stakeholders’ interest in EPF. They could do so by actively dealing with the threats that these parties perceived by, for example, compensating them financially or inviting them to participate in designing the system to fit current working practices. This may help to balance more evenly the costs and benefits experienced by the main players. A final possibility would be to use aspects of both strategies, perhaps at different stages of design and implementation.

## Conclusions

Table 8 summarizes the results, and indicates the implications of the analysis.

This paper has shown how a hospital, which proposed an EPF to share information with GPs and pharmacies, failed to secure their support, even though all agreed that the system would improve care and administrative efficiency. We showed that while the hospital had a high interest in the EPF, it had no power to oblige the others to take part. Conversely, the GPs and pharmacies had little interest in the proposed system, but had the power not to take part. The promoter of an IOS cannot assume the support of other players, even though it promises both private and public benefits.

We examined theoretically and empirically grounded dimensions of the system and its context, and found that stakeholders agreed on some dimensions, and disagreed on others. There was general agreement on the value of an EPF system, but marked differences on the dimensions of working practices, power, culture and finance. On each of these, the GPs and pharmacies believed that the system would damage their interests, and that this loss was greater than the benefits. They therefore used the power of their autonomous position to decline to cooperate, preventing implementation. Their opposition was grounded in factors which previous IS research has shown to influence attitudes towards an IS project.

We also showed that the hospital promoting the project had two strategic options for moving the project forward, based on the power–interest matrix. They could have sought to increase their power to enforce compliance, by seeking support from other less directly involved stakeholders – such as government, insurers or representatives of patients. They could also have used their resource and expertise power to make the proposal more attractive – for example by offering to meet more of the GPs financial costs, or sharing their technical expertise with the GPs and pharmacies. Alternatively, they could have redesigned the proposal to meet some of the problems identified by pharmacies and GPs, and so increase their interest in the system. By identifying the sources of their attitudes, the research shows the dimensions of the project that the hospital could try to change, though some obstacles will be more deeply rooted than others.

## Implications for theory

This paper confirms the value of taking account of stakeholder expectations and interest in designing an IS (Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987), especially in an IOS where the promoter cannot coerce players to join. It also shows the value of the power–interest grid (Eden and Ackermann, 1998) to compare the position of stakeholders, and to explain why an apparently beneficial system is not implemented. It confirms previous IS research that shows that attitudes to an IS do not relate just to the system itself but to how players interpret its effects on aspects of the context, such as working practices, power, culture and finance. These factors affect attitudes to the system, and if those who oppose it have the power not to join, implementation will fail.

The research also casts light on the implementation process. Boddy (2002) identified four perspectives on implementation – project management, emergent, participative and political. This research indicates that the hospital concentrated on the first, in the sense that they designed the system and expected other players to join. Recognizing the emergent nature of the project, encouraging participation to identify stakeholder interests, and making fuller use of available sources of power may have encouraged acceptance. A theory of IS implementation that takes a one dimensional, linear approach to implementation will be less successful than one which takes account of identifiable stakeholder interests.

## Implications for practitioners

This study identifies four lessons for practitioners. First, it shows that those promoting an IOS project should attend to potential stakeholders from the outset. Having identified the most significant stakeholders, they can then consider how they are likely to view the proposal (Fowler and Gilfillan, 2003).
