## 1. Introduction

## A B S T R A C T

The objective of this research is to critically explore four interrelated issues at the country level by evaluating the values of e-commerce (EC) and information technology (IT) both separately and simultaneously; examining the possibilities of complementarity and substitutability phenomena; analyzing the impacts of substitution and complement relations among ordinary capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital on the business values of EC and IT; and revisiting the productivity paradox by using the stochastic frontier approaches, which incorporate the CES production functions. The empirical results imply that it is important to assess the values of EC and IT simultaneously rather than separately.

\- 2015 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

A careful review of the literature on production and operations research/economics (POR/E) and information systems (IS) clearly indicates that the business values of information technology (IT) and e-commerce (EC) have been assessed separately rather than jointly. This mode of research inquiry undermines the positive and/or negative interactions between IT and EC and thus leads to an under- or over-estimation of the real values of IT and EC. Zhu and Kraemer [48] and Zhu [49] sought to confirm the positive interaction (called complementarity) of IT infrastructure and EC capability at the firm level by using a classical regression approach.

It is conceivable that the presence of EC may strengthen the value of IT (thus giving rise to the phenomenon of complementarity) or weaken it (thus resulting in the phenomenon of substitutability), and vice versa, which provides a good explanation for the disappearance or existence of the so-called productivity paradox. In other words, it can be argued that when EC and IT are present simultaneously, they do not necessarily become more powerful in creating greater value than by themselves alone; hence, the notions of both complementarity and substitutability may prevail. Note that being powerful is measured by productive (technical) efficiency. For instance, if EC creates larger (smaller) productive efficiency than IT, then EC is more (less) powerful than IT, and vice versa.

Moreover, according to Chen and Lin [10], the functional form of frontier productions (e.g., the constant elasticity of substitution known as CES, CD, BC, and BT) and the substitution and complement of inputs are important sources of productive (in)efficiency on the technological side. Dewan and Min [15], Lin and Shao [28], and Chen and Lin [10] agreed that the substitution and complement relations of production factors (e.g., ordinary capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital) are a very complex matter; therefore, their impacts on IT and EC values must not be ignored. Thus, four interrelated research problems must be resolved to more accurately and properly assess the values of EC and IT: (i) evaluating the business values of IT and EC simultaneously rather than separately; (ii) examining the possibilities of complementarity and substitutability phenomena; (iii) deploying an appropriate production frontier function that enables us to analyze the impacts of inputs substitution and complement relations among ordinary capital (i.e., non-IT capital), ordinary labor, and IT capital upon the values of IT and EC; and (iv) revisiting the existence of the productivity paradox with the notion that the values of IT and EC must be evaluated jointly rather than independently.

Thus, the objective of this paper is to investigate four problems of critical importance both simultaneously and jointly: the assessment of IT and EC values, the presence of the complementarity and substitutability phenomena, the impacts of the complement and substitution of inputs, and a revisit of the productivity paradox. To accomplish this goal, we propose to apply the time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) approaches that are derived from the theory of production and their built-in performance metric, known as productive (technical) efficiency, by incorporating the two-factor [2,24,28,45] and three-factor (Chen and Lin [10]) constant elasticity of substitution (known as CES) production frontiers (see Appendix 1 for details). The stochastic production frontiers specified by the Cobb–Douglas (CD) production function, the Box–Cox (BC) transformation function, and the Box–Tidwell (BT) transformation function, as applied by Lin and Shao [28] and Lin [29], are not capable of resolving the four issues jointly. Although CD, BC, and BT can be deployed to investigate research issues (i), (ii), and (iv), they, unlike CES, are not equipped with the so-called substitution parameter (Appendix 1) that enables us to resolve research issue (iii) with the other three issues.

EC and IT are also essential parts of production and operations (PO) and information systems (IS); accordingly, the values of EC and IT are critically relative to both firm and country performance. The present study is expected to provide new insights into EC and IT values and will consequently lead to better decisions on IT and EC investments in production processes.

Though this research is devoted to the impacts of IT and EC on country performance at the country level of analysis, it actually informs managers of PO and IS and benefits firms. Numerous authors (e.g., [10,16,29,32,37,38,41,44]) have argued in favor of research at the country level. The reasons cited include, but are not limited to, the following (Lin [29]): (i) because too much attention has been paid to large U.S. firms, further research in comparative studies at the country level is called for; (ii) IT and EC value research at the firm level is abundant, but the knowledge accumulation of IT and EC values at the country level is poor; (iii) firm-level results require validation to determine whether they are applicable across national boundaries, i.e., external validity of firm-level conclusions on IT and EC values is required; (iv) a key dimension of external validity is international generalization, and a country-level study on IT and EC values is an international generalization; (v) the scarcity of IT and EC data at the firm level makes it impossible to undertake a research inquiry such as this study intends to do; and (vi) cross-country studies look for both similarities and differences in PO/IS practices [37] and can be used to discover emergent universality and shape competitive advantages when managers of IS/PO face increasing globalization. This study responds to the call for country-level research.

The remainder of this research is organized as follows. Section 2 undertakes a literature review. Section 3 describes the theory of production, research methodology, and performance measures; and Section 4 details the stochastic CES production frontier models. Section 5 defines the variables involved and describes the country-level panel data used. Section 6 reports and discusses the empirical results obtained and derives major findings. Finally, Section 7 concludes the paper with some remarks.

## 2. Literature review

Because of the rapidly increasing IT investment, the business value of IT has been the focus of many studies at the firm level and several studies at the country level over the past two decades (see Table 1 in Lin [29]; the list includes, e.g., [3,6,7,9,16,26– 29,31,32,35,36,39–41,44]). Some of these studies have reported a positive impact of IT on the firm’s or country’s performance, which suggests that the productivity paradox has disappeared. Contradictory conclusions, however, have also been reached in a number of studies (e.g., [10,26,28,29]), which claim that the productivity paradox remains existent.

In contrast, recent years have witnessed the increasing popularity of EC activities; therefore, the business value of EC investments has become a great concern. EC is an unfolding phenomenon [49] defined as business activities (e.g., e-links with supplies, online connections with customers, dissemination of product information) that are constructed over the Internet. A list of studies examining the benefits of EC as the primary subject includes [4,8,11,14,17,18,20,25,34,42,43,46,48,49]. In particular, Zhu [49] has reviewed fourteen papers relevant to EC value, which were published over the past ten or so years.

Virtually, these studies are characterized by a common feature, that is, they have assessed IT and EC value separately and independently, rather than jointly and simultaneously. [49] is an exception but differs from this study in its theory, research approach, analytical method, performance measure, data used, and, accordingly, conclusion. Zhu’s study is based on the resourcebased theory; applies the classical OLS regression approach; uses four performance measures (i.e., the ratio of sales to employees, the ratio of the cost of goods sold to employees, return on assets, and inventory turnover) to serve as dependent variables; relies on the collective analytical method (i.e., based simply on the statistical significance and signs of coefficient estimates) to analyze the OLS estimates that were obtained from a set of cross-sectional data from 114 firms in the U.S. retail industry; and concludes that a positive interaction effect (i.e., the phenomenon of complementarity, not substitutability) exists between IT and EC. No comparisons of individual firms are permitted by the OLS regression approach of Zhu [49]. The estimates of APE s, as presented in this study, make comparisons of individual countries possible. In the absence of similar estimates for individual firms, Zhu [49] is unable to conduct a comparison of the performance of individual firms.

In contrast, the present work is based on the theory of production; applies the time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) approaches underlying this theory; uses productive (technical) efficiency derived from the SPF approaches as the performance measure (i.e., rather than from outside the research approaches); analyzes empirical estimates using both collective and individual analytical methods; and uses a panel dataset at the country level. An important conclusion is that the joint presence of EC and IT may create the phenomena of not only complementarity but also substitutability; therefore, the so-called productivity paradox may exist in a country, regardless of whether it is a developed or developing country. Thus, Zhu’s [49] conclusion is only partially validated by findings from country-level data.

[10,26,29,32] observed that IT value research at the firm level is abundant but that, in contrast, the knowledge accumulation of IT value at the country level is poor and little work has yet been done to validate firm-level results to determine whether they are applicable across national boundaries.

One way to validate findings at the firm level is to compare the impacts of EC and IT across different countries. This requires the external validity of firm-level conclusions on EC and IT values to be determined (Lin [29]; Prasad and Babbar [37]). Moreover, Rosenzweig [38] emphasized that a key dimension of external validity is international generalization and that a country-level study on EC and IT values is also an international generalization. In effect, the importance of EC/IT research at the country level has been recognized by, for example, Prasad and Babbar [37], who argued that cross-country studies search for both similarities and differences in PO/IS practices and can be used to discover emergent universality. In addition, on a global scale, these similarities and differences can be used to shape competitive advantages, as stated above.

The present study attempts to simultaneously resolve the aforementioned four research problems. It is an international generalization in an effort to validate firm-level results and determine whether they are applicable across national boundaries. This research must employ both two- and three-factor CES frontier production models to resolve the four research issues simultaneously.

## 3. The theory of production, research method, and performance measures

Because of their importance and relevance to this study, this section repeats the theory, research method, and performance measure as described by Lin and Shao [27] and Lin [29].

## 3.1. The theory of production

This research is based on the theory of production that is characterized by three notable components: (i) a production process in which a production unit (e.g., firm, industry, or country) utilizes different resources (e.g., inputs such as capital, labor, IT stock) to produce tangible goods or intangible services called actual or observed outputs; (ii) a production frontier that defines the relationship between outputs and inputs and specifies the maximum (ideal or desired) output level realizable from a given combination of inputs and assumes that the maximum output and the actual output are connected by a production function denoted as $Y _ { i t } \le f ( \mathbf { X } _ { i t } ; \mathbf { \mathbf { \{ \beta \} } } )$ , where $Y _ { i t }$ is the actual output produced by production unit i (country i in this study) at time $t , \mathbf { X } _ { i t }$ is a vector of inputs, b is a vector of unknown coefficients, and $f ( \mathbf { { X } } _ { i t } ; \mathbf { { \{ \beta } }  )$ represents the maximum output that, in reality, must be greater than or equal to the actual output $Y _ { i t } ,$ due to technological constraints; and (iii) productive (or technical) inefficiency, which is the difference between the maximum output and the actual output and is denoted by

$$
u _ {i t} = f (\mathbf {X} _ {i t}; \boldsymbol {\beta}) - Y _ {i t}
$$

or

$$
Y _ {i t} = f (\boldsymbol {X} _ {i t}; \boldsymbol {\beta}) - u _ {i t}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n \text { and } t = 1, \dots , m\tag{1}
$$

where $u _ { i t }$ is called productive (or technical) inefficiency and is random, must be non-negative and is thus assumed to be one-side normally distributed according to $\left| N ( 0 , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) \right|$ (cf. [10,23,27,29]) Thus, a production unit operates either on the frontier (if it is technically or productively efficient, i.e., $u _ { i t } \equiv \equiv 0 )$ or below the frontier (if it is technically or productively inefficient, i.e., $u _ { i t } > 0 )$

Based on production theory, we can derive the research approaches that are implied by the theory and performance measure (called productive or technical efficiency) that follows immediately from the research approaches.

## 3.2. The time-varying stochastic production frontier approach and productive efficiency: a one-equation model

Eq. (1) can be generalized by adding the traditional random error $( \nu _ { i t } )$ to its right-hand side. This addition results in the traditional time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) model of one equation given by

$$
Y _ {i t} = f \left(\boldsymbol {X} _ {i t}; \boldsymbol {\beta}\right) - u _ {i t} + v _ {i t}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n \text { and } t = 1, \dots , m\tag{2}
$$

where for this study, $\mathbf { X } _ { i t } \equiv \left( K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } \right)$ without IT investment and $\mathbf { X } _ { i t } \equiv \equivq ( K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } , I _ { i t } )$ in the presence of IT investment, with $K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } ,$ , and $I _ { i t }$ denoting ordinary (non-IT) capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital, respectively; and where $\nu _ { i t }$ is assumed to be distributed according to $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ . Alternatively, Eq. (2) represents the timevarying SPF approach under the normal-half normal specification.

We can now transform productive inefficiency $\left( u _ { i t } \right)$ into productive efficiency $( P E _ { i t } )$ defined as (cf. [10,23,27–29])

$$
P E _ {i t} = e ^ {- u _ {i t}} = \exp (- u _ {i t})\tag{3}
$$

for country i at time t. Because u $\mathsf { \Pi } _ { \mathrm { t } } \in [ 0 , \infty ] , P E _ { i t } \in [ 0 , 1 ] ,$ , which means that when $u _ { i t } = 0$ (no inefficiency), $P E _ { i t } = 1$ (the highest efficiency) and when u tends to infinite (the highest inefficiency), $P E _ { i t }$ tends to zero (no efficiency). Because of this one-to-one correspondence property and because the higher the $u _ { i t }$ is, the lower the $P E _ { i t }$ will be, and vice versa, $P E _ { i t }$ is considered to be a better measure of IT and EC values for a number of prominent reasons, as observed by Lin and Shao [27]. Accordingly, $P E _ { i t }$ is used to gauge the business values of IT and EC investments. More importantly, because the timevarying SPF approach is the companion of the theory of production and the productive efficiency measure is the companion of the time-varying SPF approach, there is no need to search for other performance measures (e.g., revenue, costs of goods sold, return on assets, consumer surplus, Tobin’s $q )$ from outside the research models.

Furthermore, to rank and compare the performance of IT and EC for the individual countries considered, we employ the average productive efficiency of country i (APE ):

$$
A P E _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {t} P E _ {i t}}{m}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n\tag{4}
$$

Finally, overall performance can be determined by the overal productive efficiency (APE), which is equal to

$$
A P E = \frac {\sum_ {i} \sum_ {t} P E _ {i t}}{m n}\tag{5}
$$

## 3.3. The generalized time-varying stochastic production frontier approach: a two-equation model

The productive inefficiency $\left( u _ { i t } \right)$ in the one-equation model (2) may be affected by various controllable factors. To account for this effect or identify the sources of productive inefficiency, the generalized two-equation time-varying SPF approach (proposed by Chen and Lin [10]) is required:

$$
Y _ {i t} = f (\mathbf {X} _ {i t}; \boldsymbol {\beta}) - u _ {i t} + v _ {i t}\tag{6}
$$

$$
u _ {i t} = \mathrm{g} (\mathbf {Z} _ {i t}; \boldsymbol {\alpha}) + \omega_ {i t}, \quad i = 1, \dots , n \text { and } t = 1, \dots , m\tag{7}
$$

where time-varying stochastic productive inefficiency $\left( u _ { i t } \right)$ is constituted of two components: the deterministic component, ${ \bf g } ( { \bf Z } _ { i t } ; { \bf \underline { { \sigma } } } { \bf \alpha } )$ , subject to (determined by) the influence of $\mathbf { Z } _ { i t } ,$ , and the one-side distributed random component, $\omega _ { i t } , \mathbf { Z } _ { i t }$ is a vector that represents a broad set of country-specific characteristics or firm- or industry-specific factors and macroeconomic factors that are common to all countries (or firms or industries) considered, observable and/or unobservable, and cause or explain the differences in productive (in)efficiencies across countries (or firms or industries) (Chen and Lin [10]). The vector may include the time variable (t) to serve as the proxy of general economic conditions or technological progress, EC, and IT (if IT is not treated as a production factor). Again, a is a vector of unknown coefficients. The two-equation model constituted by Eqs. (6) and (7) is usually referred to as the generalized time-varying stochastic frontier production model with a stochastic and dynamic inefficiency, and it represents a significant departure from model (2).

In the first Eq. (6) of the two-equation model, just like in the one-equation model (2), the observed (actual) output $( Y _ { \mathrm { i t } } )$ is again decomposed into three components: the maximum (ideal, desired) output represented by f(), the random inefficiency $u _ { i t } ,$ and the traditional random shock $\nu _ { i t } .$ The half-normal distributed random inefficiency (u ) is effectively equal to the difference between the maximum output and the observed output, i.e., $u _ { i t } = f ( \cdot ) - Y _ { i t } ,$ which must be nonnegative. Therefore, there are two ways to change $u _ { i t }$ (Chen and Lin [10]). One way is technological and is determined by $f ( \cdot ) ,$ , which in turn is determined by its functional form and the inputs entering f(). As such, the determinants of $u _ { i t } \mathrm { i n }$ the technological aspect are functional form (e.g., CES), the inputs entering $f ( \cdot ) ,$ , and the substitution and complement of inputs, thus implying that inputs substitution and complement are important factors that affect productive efficiency and, hence, the values of IT and EC. The other way to shift $u _ { i t }$ is to identify the factors that influence the observed output (Y ) (such as the national characteristics used in Chen and Lin [10] and Lin and Chiang [26]; and firm characteristics, e.g., concentration and dynamism, as mentioned by Melville et al. [33]). Stated alternatively or equivalently, an increase (a decrease) in $Y _ { i t }$ will reduce (increase) $u _ { i t } .$

These two ways represent two major sources of productive (in)efficiency (Chen and Lin [10]). On the technological side, the functional form of $\mathbf { \dot { \rho } } f ( \mathbf { X } _ { i t } ; \mathbf { \vec { \mathbf { \rho } } } )$ must be specified. As mentioned above, this study requires the application of the CES frontier production and the inputs to be ordinary (non-IT) capital $( K _ { i t } ) ,$ , ordinary labor $( L _ { i t } )$ , and IT capital $\left( I _ { i t } \right)$ . On the other side of the coin lies the factors that can change $Y _ { i t } .$ EC is one of the national characteristics that plays such a role on this side. IT is another characteristic that plays the same role as EC. In other words, similar to EC, IT is treated as an actual output-affecting variable. Therefore, the two major sources of productive (in)efficiency also imply that there are two ways to treat IT in the production system. One way (Case 1) is to treat IT as an observed output-influencing factor, along with EC. In this way, the two-equation-two-factor model is required, with $\mathbf { X } _ { i t } = \left( K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } \right)$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf Z _ { i t } = \big ( I _ { i t } , E _ { i t } \big ) . } \end{array}$ . The other way (Case 2) is to treat IT as a production factor, along with $K _ { i t }$ and $L _ { i t } ,$ that is, as a maximum (desired or unobserved) output-affecting factor. Thus, the second way calls for the two-equation-three-factor model wherein $\mathbf { X } _ { i t } = \left( K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } , I _ { i t } \right)$ and ${ \bf Z } _ { i t } = \left( E _ { i t } \right)$

Chen and Lin [10] established these two cases well. However, the empirical evidence obtained by Chen and Lin [10] suggests that these two ways have led to virtually the same conclusions<sup>3</sup> with, of course, different magnitudes of numerical estimates. As noted in Section 6.6, the total neglect of the impacts of input substitution and complement among $K _ { i t } , L _ { i t } ,$ and $I _ { i t }$ holds the key.

## 5. Variables and data

## 5.1. Variables

According to our CES time-varying SPF models, we require the following variables: (i) capital; (ii) labor; (iii) IT investment or computer capital, which consists of computer software, computer hardware, and other office equipment; (iv) IS (information systems) staffing spending, i.e., expenses for information systems staffing<sup>4</sup>; and (v) e-commerce investments $\left( E _ { i t } \right) . ^ { 5 }$ The production factors can then be calculated as follows: (vi) ordinary or non-IT capital $\left( K _ { i t } \right)$ and ordinary or non-IT labor $\left( { L _ { i t } } \right)$ are obtained by subtracting IT investment (or computer capital) and IS staffing spending from capital and labor, respectively; and (vii) IT capital $\left( I _ { i t } \right)$ is defined as IT stock equal to computer capital plus 3 multiplied by IS staffing spending [7,15]. The multiplier of three that is used to calculate $I _ { i t }$ is the assumed service life of the asset created by IS labor, which is commonly used in the literature. A number of studies (e.g., Lin and Shao [27,28]; and this study) have conducted sensitivity analyses by allowing the multiplier to shift from 1 to 7 and, in doing so, have reached the same conclusion.

The dependent variable is represented by the value added by input factors; therefore, it is measured by the gross domestic product (GDP) of a country.

## 6.2. The likelihood ratio test

It is useful to perform the likelihood ratio (LR) test (Coelli [12]; Coelli et al. [13]; Godfrey [19]; Greene 21); Johnson and Wichern [22]; Kmenta [24]) to evaluate model parameter magnitude differences. In particular, the two-step NML estimation method used in this research supplies the log-likelihood values to facilitate such a test. Here, we are interested in testing the hypothesis constituted by the null hypothesis H (i.e., model parameter magnitude differences do not exist) against the alternative hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathbf { a } }$ (i.e., model parameter magnitude differences do exist). This is particularly meaningful because we want to determine whether differences in the parameters associated with any two research models differ significantly from zero. Consequently, $\mathrm { i f } \mathrm { H } _ { \mathbf { a } }$ is not rejected, it would suggest that a comparison of the two models is appropriate.

The test statistic ([12,13,21]) is described by

$$
L R = - 2 \left[ \log \left(L _ {o}\right) - \log \left(L _ {a}\right) \right]\tag{14}
$$

where $\log ( L _ { 0 } )$ and log $\left( L _ { \mathrm { a } } \right)$ are, respectively, the log-likelihood values under $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { a } } .$ Godfrey [19] and Greene [21] defined $\log ( L _ { \mathbf { o } } )$ and $\log ( L _ { \mathrm { a } } )$ as log-likelihood values with and without restrictions, respectively, which indicates that $\log ( L _ { \mathbf { 0 } } )$ cannot be larger than log(L ). Furthermore, the test statistic LR is assumed to be asymptotically distributed according to the chi-square random variable, with degrees of freedom (d) equal to the number of restrictions imposed. In Coelli [12] and Coelli et al. [13], d is set equal to 1. We feel that assuming d = 1 is an arbitrary practice because setting $d = 1$ , regardless of the number of restrictions involved may reach a misleading test conclusion. Here, d is set equal to the number of restrictions involved (Greene [21]).

The LR test conclusions are reported in Table 2. We can readily observe from Table 2 that the conclusions for all pairs of research models at the 1% and 5% levels are $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathbf { a } } ,$ which means that $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathbf { a } }$ is not rejected and suggests that the model parameter magnitudes differ from model to model. Consequently, comparisons of Model 5 with Model 1, Model 6 with Model 4, etc., are appropriate.

## 6.3. Methods of analysis

Following Lin [29], we analyze the empirical results by employing two obviously distinct methods:

One method analyzes the business values of IT and EC collectively. The other method ranks and compares the contributions of IT and EC to productive efficiencies individually. The first method is a commonly practiced skill and is used when attempting to explore the values of IT and EC based on the signs and statistical significance of the coefficients of the IT and EC variables and $R ^ { 2 } .$ . The second method represents a significant departure from traditional analytical practice and is permitted by the stochastically timevarying frontier approaches; it uses the average productive efficiency (APE ) of an individual country i to undertake individual country comparisons across different models and countries.

Thus, the application of the time-varying SPF approaches makes it wholly possible to conduct a highly comprehensive individual analysis and comparison of the business values of IT and EC, as measured by productive efficiency across individual countries and different models. The performance measure is a companion of the time-varying SPF models; therefore, it is absolutely unnecessary to look for measures outside the models. These are some of the major advantages associated with using the time-varying SPF approaches.

6.4. Analyzing and assessing the values of EC and IT separately and jointly: Case 1

Recall that in Case 1, IT, similar to EC, is considered to be an observed (actual) output-affecting variable. We begin by observing Table 3, which shows the estimates of Models 1–4 for this case. Collectively, we immediately notice a number of points of interest from Table 3, which are stated as follows.

First, both IT and EC exerted positive effects on the actual output, as judged from the significantly positive estimates of the coefficients of IT and EC variables. Moreover, the presence of IT and EC, separately or jointly, increased productive (technical) efficiency; the APEs are .8580 and .9415, respectively, when IT and EC appear separately and are .8725 when IT and EC are present jointly, compared with the .7798 obtained in the absence of IT and EC investments. Positive signs and the increase in productive efficiency clearly imply that the productivity paradox does not exist in the sample of countries (developed and developing) under study. These results and implications are consistent with conventional wisdom (e.g., [7,16,27]).

Second, the joint presence of IT and EC has led to an efficiency (.8725) that is larger than the efficiency (.8580) when IT appears alone but is smaller than the efficiency (.9415) when EC stands alone. In other words, the presence of EC strengthens the value of IT, which suggests that EC is complementary for IT; however, the presence of IT weakens the value of EC, which indicates that IT is substitutable for EC. This phenomenon of substitutability between IT and EC based on the CES production frontier contradicts the conclusions of Zhu and Kraemer [48] and Zhu [49].

Third, the output shares of capital and labor in the absence of IT and EC were 78.42% and 21.58%, respectively; however, the presence (alone or joint) of IT and EC increased the share of capital or decreased the share of labor by a range of 8.7% to 11.3%. Overall, the output attributed to capital is approximately 7–8 times that attributed to labor.

Fourth, when both IT and EC were absent, the returns-to-scale parameter was 1.0575; an outcome greater than 1 implies an increasing returns-to-scale. On the contrary, the presence of IT and/or EC shifted the value-creating transformation process from increasing to decreasing returns-to-scale, especially when IT appeared alone (with the returns-to-scale parameter being equal to .6736, the smallest among the four models associated with Case 1). This empirical evidence clearly implies that the constant or increasing returns-to-scale assumption that is often made in POR/E and IS research with the presence of IT and/or EC (e.g., [15]) is untenable.

Finally, for all four models in Case 1, ordinary capital and ordinary labor were shown to be complementary. In particular, estimates of the substitution parameter strongly indicate that IT and EC investments enhanced the complementary relationship between ordinary capital and ordinary labor, especially when IT appeared alone (with a substitution parameter estimate of 11.0786, the largest in absolute value among the four models associated with Case 1). This evidence implies that IT and/or EC can be an instrument with which to change the relationships of substitution and compliment between ordinary capital and ordinary labor, thereby shifting the observed output via the g() function and, consequently, changing productive efficiency and the country’s performance.

6.5. Analyzing and assessing the values of EC and IT separately and jointly: Case 2

Recall that in Case 2, IT is considered to be a production factor along with ordinary capital $( K _ { i t } )$ and ordinary labor $( L _ { i t } ) .$ Thus, Models 5 and 6 are our key; we must base the collective analysis on Table 4 and the individual analysis on the last two columns of Table 5.

Collectively, both IT and EC have positive effects on productive efficiency from the CES frontier. Again, this evidence implies the absence of the productivity paradox. However, the APE was not enhanced by the joint presence of IT and EC because the APE decreased from .8759 with IT alone to .8713 with IT and EC appearing jointly. Stated alternatively, this outcome implies that the joint appearance of IT and EC led to the phenomenon of substitutability, which is again inconsistent with the firm-level evidence of [48,49].

In the absence of EC, the output share going to capital (.4344) is the highest, followed by the share of IT capital (.3283); the share of labor (.2773) is the smallest. When EC appears jointly with IT, the share of capital increases by 17.41%, and the shares of labor and IT capital decrease by 5.96% and 15.45%, respectively.

When EC is absent, we find the returns-to-scale (1.0270) increasing. However, the joint appearance of EC and IT caused a shift from increasing returns-to-scale to decreasing returns-toscale, with an estimate of the returns-to-scale parameter equal to .9243 (which is less than one). Again, this evidence implies that the constant returns-to-scale assumption that is frequently made in applied research (e.g., Dewan and Min [15]) is untenable.

Regardless of whether EC appears (Model 6) or not (Model 5), both capital and labor and capital and IT capital are complementary; however, labor and IT capital are substitutable. More importantly, we observed that the existence of EC along with IT has strengthened the complementary relationships between capital and labor and between capital and IT capital as well as the substitutable relationship between labor and IT capital. Thus, in Cases 1 and 2, (ordinary) capital and (ordinary) labor are complementary. However, the estimates of the substitution parameter in Case 2 clearly indicate that capital, labor, and IT capital are not pairwise substitutable, thus supporting the finding of Chen and Lin [10] and implying that the conclusion of Dewan and Min [15], i.e., that these three inputs are pairwise substitutable, is not empirically confirmed.

Individually, a comparison of Model 5 with Model 1 suggests that IT has a positive impact on the productive efficiencies of 17 of the 18 countries (i.e., CN, FR, GM, IL, JP, UK, US, CH, KR, SG, AS, BG, DM, NL, NW, SP, and SL); SD is the only exception. IL appears to be the biggest winner, with a 42.25% increase in productive efficiency led by IT. In the case of SD, a 13.54% decrease in its productive efficiency was caused by the country’s IT spending. The most important implication is that the productivity paradox does not exist in those 17 countries (developed and developing) but does obviously exist in SD.

Similar to Case 1, Case 2 shows that the group of G7 countries outperforms the group of other countries. This empirical evidence is stronger when based on Model 5—in which EC is absent—than when based on Model 6—in which EC is present (cf. the last two columns of Table 5).

To determine whether either the complementarity or substitutability phenomenon or both exist, we once more compare the results of Model 6 (in the joint presence of IT and EC) with those of Model 5 (in the presence of IT alone). The presence of EC in Model 6 strengthens the productive efficiencies of 7 countries, namely, FR, AS, BG, DM, NL, SD, and SL, and thus indicates the phenomenon of complementarity. However, it reduces the productive efficiencies of 11 nations, namely, CN, GM, IL, JP, UK, US, CH, KR, SG, NW, and SP, and thus indicates the phenomenon of substitutability. It is interesting to note that the substitutability phenomenon occurs in most of the developed countries (e.g., CN, GM, IL, JP, UK, and US) included in the sample. Thus, the firm-level evidence of Zhu and Kraemer [48] and Zhu [49], via international generalization, has failed the external validity test once again.

Therefore, we are able to conclude that assessing the business values of IT and EC separately may lead to a serious under- or over-estimation of the effects of IT and EC on a country’s performance.

6.6. The impacts of inputs substitution and complement on productive efficiency

As argued in Section 6.4, K and L are complementary based on Models 1–4 in which I does not enter the desired or maximum output function f() and K and L remain complementary, L and I are substitutable, and K and I are complementary, based on Models 5 and 6. Additionally, as mentioned above, the substitution and complement of capital, labor, and IT capital are very complex matters (Lin and Shao [28]; Chen and Lin [10]) and are expected to have a substantial impact on the values of IT and EC in both operations and production processes. To examine their impact on the values of IT and EC and, hence, on country performance, we design and answer three questions that are highly relevant to the problem at hand.

Question 1: Do input substitution and complement strengthen (S) or weaken (W) the value of IT in the absence of EC (i.e., IT is assessed separately from EC) when IT is treated as an input (Case 2) along with capital and labor compared with when IT is not used?

Question 2: Do input substitution and complement strengthen (S) or weaken (W) the value of IT in the absence of EC (i.e., IT is assessed separately from EC) when IT is treated as an input (Case 2) compared with when IT is treated as an actual output-influencing variable (Case 1)?

Question 3: Do input substitution and complement strengthen (S) or weaken (W) the values of IT and EC in the joint presence of IT and EC (i.e., IT and EC are assessed jointly) when IT is treated as an input (Case 2) compared with when IT is considered to be an actual output-influencing variable (Case 1)?

The results of columns (a), (b), and (c) presented in Table 12 are required to answer Questions 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Clearly, the answer to Question 1 is S, with the exception of SD (Sweden). CH (China) took first place with a 142.69 percentage point increase compared with last place, which was achieved by SD with a 13.54% decrease. The empirical evidence strongly implies that the productivity paradox hypothesis, under the impact of input substitution and complement, is rejected for a country, regardless of whether it is a developed or developing nation, with SD being the only country wherein the productivity paradox hypothesis is not rejected, regardless of whether IT is treated as an input (Case 2) or as an actual output-influencing factor (Case 1).

The answer to Question 2 is S for 13 countries and W for 5 countries. US ranks first with an increase of 10.91%, and SD again ranks last with a decrease of 4.01%. The empirical results suggest that the conclusions reached by the model (Model 5) in which IT is treated as an input differ from those reached by the model (Model 2) in which IT is treated as an actual output-influencing factor under the impact of input substitution and complement.

Finally, the answer to Question 3 is mixed: S for 10 countries and W for 11 counties. AU (Australia) took first place, with a 3.4% improvement over its productive performance, whereas last place went to JP (Japan), with a decrease of 3.18% in average productive efficiency over the period in question. These results imply that Australia would benefit from substitution and complement relationships among K, L, and I because IT is treated as a production factor when IT and EC are assessed jointly. In contrast, Japan would be better off if IT were treated as an actual output-influencing factor, rather than an input, when assessing IT and EC jointly. In other words, the answer to Question 3 suggests that a positive percentage increase supports the treatment of IT as input and that a negative percentage decrease supports the treatment of IT as an observed output-affecting variable in the presence of EC, that is, when IT and EC are valued jointly. The examples in our sample for the former consist of 10 nations (i.e., CN, FR, IL, US, AU, CH, SG, TW, DM, and NW), whereas the countries in our sample exemplifying the latter condition consist of 11 economies (i.e., GM, JP, UK, HK, KR, AS, BG, NL, SP, SD, and SL).

Thus, the answer to Question 3 provides an important procedure for determining how best to treat IT in one of two ways: as a production factor or an actual output-affecting factor. If IT is considered a production factor, it must enter into the f() production function in the first equation. If IT is treated as an actual output-affecting factor, it must appear in the g() efficiency function in the second equation. Nonetheless, Models 4 and 6 are the tools necessary for determining how to treat IT in the operations and production process and, hence, how much should be invested in IT by operations and production management.

In summary, our empirical evidence strongly suggests that input substitution and complement have ripple effects on the values of a country’s IT and EC, regardless of whether it is a developed or developing nation. Such effects exerted by possible substitution and complement relations among the three inputs (K, L, and, I) cannot be ignored when the objective is to heighten a country’s performance, as measured by its productive efficiency, and when major concerns arise with regard to obtaining accurate assessments of the values of EC and IT for making prudent EC and IT investment decisions.

## 6.7. Practical consequences

This study has some obvious practical consequences. These include, but are not limited to, the following: (i) the constant returns-to-scale assumption, which is frequently made in applied research in POR/E or IS, is untenable. (ii) The productivity paradox of IT may exist in a country, regardless of whether it is a developed or developing nation. (iii) Ordinary capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital are not pairwise substitutable, as observed in previous research. (iv) Ordinary capital and ordinary labor are found to be complementary—as are ordinary capital and IT capital—though ordinary labor and IT capital are substitutable; the joint appearance of EC and IT does not have the power to change these relationships. (v) The choice between treating IT as a desired (expected) output-affecting factor and treating IT as an actual (observed) output-influencing factor is a critical decision; as such, Models 4 and 6 are the instruments required to determine how best to treat IT in the production or value-generating process. (vi) The group of G7 countries outperforms the group of other countries, especially when IT is present. (vii) The firm-level finding of the complementarity phenomenon without the substitutability phenomenon has failed the external validity test via international generalization. (viii) The ripple impacts of inputs complement and substitution on the business values of EC and IT are enormous and, hence, are not negligible. (ix) It is important to evaluate the values of EC and IT simultaneously rather than separately by considering complement and substitution relations among non-IT capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital.

## 7. Concluding remarks

This research was devoted to evaluating the business values of information technology (IT) and e-commerce (EC) separately and jointly under the impact of input substitution and complement. This study also considered the controversial issues of the productivity paradox and the phenomena of complementarity and substitutability.

To achieve the objective, this study is based on the theory of production and the time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) approaches underlying the theory, uses the so-called productive (technical) efficiency to measure the values of IT and EC, and employs the constant elasticity of substitution (known as CES) frontier production function within the framework of oneand two-equation models. Ultimately, we have developed six research models. The theory is well established, the SPF methodology is novel, and the performance metric is a companion of the methodology—which means that there is no need to look for any performance measures outside the research models.

The empirical evidence strongly suggests that assessing the values of EC and IT separately rather than simultaneously would lead to over- or under-stating the true values of EC and IT, that the impacts of inputs substitution and complement on the business values of EC and IT are substantial, that the productivity paradox may exist in a country regardless of whether it is developed or developing, and that not only the complementarity phenomenon but also the substitutability phenomenon associated with the values of EC and IT may occur in a nation (developed or developing).

Nonetheless, we have arrived at a number of important findings, most of which appear to conflict with both firm- and country-level evidence from previous research. These contradictory conclusions explain the critical importance of functional forms of production frontiers and the substitution and complement relations among inputs (e.g., ordinary capital, ordinary labor, and IT capital) in the value-creating and value-assessing process.

## Appendix 1. Specifications of the two-factor and three-factor CES stochastic frontier productions

First, in the two-factor case, we simply follow in the footsteps of Arrow et al. [2], Kmenta [24], and Lin and Shao [28]. The three twofactor SPF models proposed by Lin and Shao [28] are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Model   I   :   } & \ln Y _ {i t} \\ & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + \nu_ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Model   II   :   } & \ln Y _ {i t} \\ & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln I _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Model   III   :   } & \ln Y _ {i t} \\ & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln I _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln L _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ is the observed output, $K _ { i t }$ is the traditional (non-IT) capital, $I _ { i t }$ is the IT capital, $L _ { i t }$ is the traditional labor, and $\nu _ { i t }$ and $u _ { i t }$ are, respectively, the traditional random error and the random productive inefficiency, distributed according to $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ and $| N ( 0 , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) |$ (see, e.g., Lovell [30]). Here, input vector $\mathbf { X } _ { i t }$ is equal to $( K _ { i t } , \ L _ { i t } ) , \ ( K _ { i t } , \ I _ { i t } ) , \ ( L _ { i t } , \ I _ { i t } )$ in Model I, Model II, and Model III, respectively. Model I is the CES stochastic frontier production model when ordinary capital $\left( K _ { i t } \right)$ and ordinary labor $\left( L _ { i t } \right)$ are employed; Model II is obtained from Model I when IT capital $I _ { i t }$ is used to substitute for $L _ { i t } ;$ and Model III follows from Model I when $K _ { i t }$ is substituted by $I _ { i t } .$ Model I uses no IT capital $\left( I _ { i t } \right)$ but traditional capital $\left( K _ { i t } \right)$ and labor $( L _ { i t } )$ in the production process and is frequently seen in the literature.

These three models are based on the two-factor CES production function originally proposed by Arrow et al. [2] to allow for observed variation in the degree of substitutability (or complementarity) between $K _ { i t }$ and L , which can be described as: $Y _ { i t } = \gamma [ \hat { \delta K _ { i t } ^ { - \rho } } + ( 1 - \delta ) L _ { i t } ^ { - \rho } ] ^ { ( - \alpha / \rho ) } e ^ { ( \nu _ { i t } - u _ { i t } ) } , \gamma > 0 , 1 > \delta > 0 , \alpha > 0 ,$ $\rho \ge - 1$ , where $\gamma$ is called the efficiency parameter, d and $1 - \delta$ are the distribution parameters for traditional capital and labor, respectively, a is the returns-to-scale parameter, and r is the substitution parameter. The nonlinear CES function corresponds to the following stochastic production efficiency frontier approximation given by (cf. Kmenta [24]):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln Y _ {i t} = \ln \gamma + \alpha \delta \ln K _ {i t} + \alpha (1 - \delta) \ln L _ {i t} - (1 / 2) \rho \alpha \delta (1 \\ - \delta) (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

which is the same as the following unrestricted version with nonlinear restrictions under exact identification:

$$
\ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} + \nu_ {i t} - u _ {i t}.\tag{A1}
$$

Thus, the parameters of the restricted approximation correspond to the coefficients of the unrestricted model as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \gamma = \text { anti } \ln \beta_ {0} = \exp (\beta_ {0}), \delta = \frac {\beta_ {1}}{\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}}, 1 - \delta \\ = \frac {\beta_ {2}}{\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}}, \alpha = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}, \end{array}\tag{A2}
$$

and

$$
\rho = \frac {\left[ - 2 \beta_ {3} \left(\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}\right) \right]}{\beta_ {1} \beta_ {2}}.\tag{A3}
$$

Relation (A3), coupled with Models I–III, is particularly relative to the input substitution/complement issue, which in turn relates to the IT value (measured by productive efficiency) and the paradox of productivity; that is, it measures the possibilities of the substitution or complement between $K _ { i t }$ and $L _ { i t }$ based on Model I, between $K _ { i t }$ and $I _ { i t }$ based on Model II, and between $L _ { i t }$ and $I _ { i t }$ when Model III is used. However, to facilitate a meaningful comparison between the two-factor (K, L) and three-factor (K, L, I) results and to avoid potential bias and inconsistency arising from the two-factor models when there are three input variables available, we consider Model I only and ignore Models II and III.

Next, we show the CES model for the three-factor case. The three-factor CES counterpart of the two-factor CES function has been proposed in Chen and Lin [10] and is given by

$$
\begin{array}{c} Y _ {i t} = \gamma (\delta_ {1} K _ {i t} ^ {- \rho} + \delta_ {2} L _ {i t} ^ {- \rho} + (1 - \delta_ {1} - \delta_ {2}) I _ {i t} ^ {- \rho}) ^ {- \frac {\alpha}{\rho}} e ^ {\nu_ {i t} - u _ {i t}}, \\ \gamma > 0, 1 > \delta_ {1}, \delta_ {2} > 0, \alpha > 0, \rho_ {i} \geq - 1, \\ i = 1, \ldots , N \text { and } t = 1, \ldots , M, \end{array}\tag{A4}
$$

where $\delta _ { 1 } , \delta _ { 2 } ,$ , and $1 - \delta - \delta _ { 2 }$ are the distribution parameters for capital, labor and IT capital, respectively, and the definitions of other parameters in the nonlinear CES function (A4) remain the same as given above. Using Taylor’s series expansion, we obtain the stochastic approximation to (A4) given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln Y _ {i t} = \ln \gamma + a \delta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + a \delta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} + a (1 - \delta_ {1} - \delta_ {2}) \ln I _ {i t} \\ \qquad - \left(\frac {1}{2}\right) \rho \alpha \delta_ {1} \delta_ {2} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} - \left(\frac {1}{2}\right) \rho \alpha \delta_ {2} (1 - \delta_ {1} \\ \qquad - \delta_ {2}) (\ln L _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} - \left(\frac {1}{2}\right) \rho \alpha \delta_ {1} (1 - \delta_ {1} \\ \qquad - \delta_ {2}) (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{A5}
$$

As in the two-factor CES case, the right-hand side of Eq. (A5) is constituted of two parts: one part corresponds to the Cobb– Douglas production function (represented by the first four terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (A5)), and the other part is a correction (or an adjustment) factor (represented by the 5th, 6th, and 7th terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (A5)) due to the departure of p from zero such that as p tends to 0, the adjustment factor would disappear and the CES function would approach the three-factor Cobb–Douglas function.

The estimation of the restricted Eq. (A5) corresponds to that of the following unrestricted model with nonlinear restrictions under exact identification:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \ln I _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln L _ {i t}) ^ {2} \\ + \beta_ {5} (\ln L _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + \beta_ {6} (\ln K _ {i t} - \ln I _ {i t}) ^ {2} + \nu_ {i t} - u _ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{A6}
$$

and, as a result, the coefficients of the restricted Eq. (A5) relate to those of the unrestricted version (A6) as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \gamma = \text {anti} \ln \beta_ {0} = \exp (\beta_ {0}), \quad \delta_ {1} = \frac {\beta_ {1}}{(\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3})}, \quad \delta_ {2} \\ = \frac {\beta_ {2}}{(\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3})}, \quad 1 - \delta_ {1} - \delta_ {2} = \frac {\beta_ {3}}{(\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3})}, \quad \alpha \\ = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3} \end{array}\tag{A7}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \rho_ {1} = \frac {[ - 2 \beta_ {4} (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3}) ]}{\beta_ {1} \beta_ {2}}, & \rho_ {2} \\ = \frac {[ - 2 \beta_ {5} (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3}) ]}{\beta_ {2} \beta_ {3}}, & \rho_ {3} = \frac {[ - 2 \beta_ {6} (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3}) ]}{\beta_ {1} \beta_ {3}} \end{array}\tag{A8}
$$

where $\rho _ { 1 }$ can be used to measure (estimate) the substitutability (or complementarity) between ordinary capital and labor when IT capital is held constant, $\rho _ { 2 }$ between labor and IT capital when ordinary capital is held constant, and $\rho _ { 3 }$ between ordinary capital and IT capital when labor is held constant.

Lin and Shao [28] created a table to summarize some general and limiting cases of correspondence between the substitution parameter (r in the two-factor CES model) and the elasticity of substitution (s, Allen [1]). We consider this table to be useful and expand it to include the substitution parameters $\rho _ { i } , i = 1 , 2 , 3$ , for the three-factor model. The expanded table is designated as Table 1. Lin and Shao [28] provided a detailed discussion in relation to Table 1, which is not repeated here to save space.

In sum, methodologically, the three-factor model (A5) or its unrestricted form (A6) is the counterpart of the two-factor model. In addition to the one-equation-two-factor CES stochastic production efficiency frontier models (Models I–III), we have now shown the one-equation-three-factor CES stochastic production efficiency frontier model. Incorporating the two single equation models into the two-equation framework of Eqs. (6) and (7), we have a two-equation-two-factor model and a two-equationthree-factor model.
