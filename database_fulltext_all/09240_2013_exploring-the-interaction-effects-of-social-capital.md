## A B S T R A C T

Most social capital studies either treat the three dimensions of social capital as parallel or hypothesize that causal relationships exist among them. We attempt to further explore the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital. In the context of information system development (ISD) projects, we propose direct and interaction effects of different dimensions of social capital on process and product performance. A survey of system developers indicates that all three proposed interaction effects on process performance are significant but with different patterns. Only one (structural and relational) interaction effect is found to have an effect on product performance. Academic and practica implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The importance of users has long been emphasized in the ISD literature [e.g., 18,36,52]. The inclusion of users can effectively improve acceptance [36] and reduce the negative impacts of uncertainty [39,71]. A lack of user support may increase variation in the development process and, therefore, may lower project performance [104,105]. Although having users in the ISD project is critical, the effect is heavily contingent on the interaction between users and developers [106]. Empirical studies have highlighted the need to have formal mechanisms for users to interact with developers [36], the importance of developers maintaining a strong relationship with users [44], and the benefits of developers having sufficient business knowledge to interact with users efficiently and effectively [e.g., 91]. The above concepts represent the different dimensions of social capital between users and developers, which should be considered in one complete model. However, the effects of different dimensions of social capital have been examined individually in the past. Furthermore, related studies focused only on the direct effects of these factors on the project process or outcome variables. Because managers may take different actions simultaneously, there is a need to consider the joint effects of these factors to clarify their contributions to project performance.

In recent decades, social capital has been shown to have an impact on actors’ performance, collaborative behaviors, and intellectual capital creation (for a comprehensive review, please see Payne et al. [76]). The importance of social capital in projects has been highlighted [e.g., 21,25,69]. Nahapiet and Ghoshal [68] classified social capital into three dimensions—structural, relational, and cognitive—and proposed the effect of each dimension on the outcome variables. This classification has since been adopted by many studies. While some studies have followed the original parallel concept and examined the individual impacts on various outcomes [5,20,108], other research has attempted to extend our understanding by proposing potential causal relationships among these three dimensions of social capital [95,99, 100,107]. The proposed causal relationships indicate that one type of social capital may be affected by the level of another type of social capital. For example, structural social capital can foster cognitive and relational social capital, while cognitive social capital can promote relational social capital.

In examining the proposed causal relationships among three dimensions of social capital, many past studies used crosssectional data [51,100]. However, such data are insufficient for demonstrating causal relations; rather, longitudinal data are required to identify precise antecedent-consequence relationships, particularly when reversed relationships are also plausible. For example, structural social capital is frequently hypothesized to have an effect on the formation of cognitive social capital and relational capital. Researchers argue that frequent interaction allows social actors to build a shared understanding and trust. Although empirical data have demonstrated a certain level of correlation between these dimensions of social capital, the structural-to-cognitive and structural-to-relational relationships may be questionable. It is reasonable to argue that having a shared code or understanding allows members to interact with each other in an efficient way. Moreover, members are more willing to interact with each other when mutual trust exists [49,57,61,79,80]. In short, cross-sectional data are insufficient for showing this type of ‘‘either way’’ causal relationship. The strong correlations among these three dimensions of social capital can only indicate that all three are interrelated.

Despite their limitation in explaining the ‘‘either way’’ causal relationship, cross-sectional data are not completely useless. In fact, cross-sectional data are useful in answering different research questions, e.g., about the interaction effect between different dimensions of social capital. In the information system development area, past studies have shown that each dimension of social capital has a positive effect on knowledge sharing and clan control [20,47]. Knowing the interaction effect of two or more types of social capital allows us to clarify a number of questions. For example, will better outcomes be achieved when two different dimensions of social capital are high? Can the desired outcomes still be achieved when one dimension of social capital is missing? Therefore, following Tiwana’s [93] argument, we adopted the widely accepted complementarity and substitution concepts from the field of economics to clarify the way in which paired dimensions of social capital interact with each other. To be clear, ‘‘complementary relationship’’ refers to the limited effect generated by one type of social capital when another is absent. ‘‘Substitutive relationship’’ refers to the strong effect of one dimension of social capital on an outcome variable even when the other dimension of social capital is not present.

We view developers as major actors in ISD and attempt to explore how their social capital with users contributes to project performance. We also treat these three dimensions of developers’ social capital with users as parallel components and examine how the different dimensions of social capital directly and interactively affect project outcomes. Particularly, we investigate the following questions: (1) Do paired dimensions of social capital complement or substitute for each other? (2) Can the complementary and substitution effects between paired dimensions of social capital be observed consistently when different project outcomes are employed as dependent variables? By answering the above questions and exploring the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital, this study aims to contribute to the research streams of both social capital and project management. Particularly, we attempt to bring new insight into the social capital area by showing that different dimensions of social capital may function as complements to or substitutes for each other. It is important to note that we do not argue for the suppressing effect of interaction in this study. Instead, we simply attempt to demonstrate the possibility of considering the interaction effect when cross-sectional data are collected. Meanwhile, for project managers, we also illustrate the consequence of exercising different actions simultaneously.

In the following sections, we first review the project performance literature and recent social capital studies in the field of IS. We then present the constructs of developers’ social capital with users and propose hypotheses regarding their impacts on project outcomes. In the third section, we introduce our research design and data-collection method. The fourth section presents an analysis of our results and further discussion. In the final section, conclusions are drawn, and the implications of the study are provided.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Social capital theory

Social capital is defined as ‘‘the aggregate of the actual or potential resources which are linked to possession of a durable network of more or less institutionalized relationships of mutual acquaintance or recognition’’ [15]. Social capital depicts the interaction structures of a social network and presents the structures as facilitators of certain actions undertaken by actors within the social network [22]. In addition to studies in sociology, political science, and economics, organizational research has shown that social capital influences various individual and organizational level variables. Social capital is shown to promote positive outcomes such as career success, compensation, job searching, team effectiveness, resource exchange, and product innovation. Researchers have also demonstrated that social capital can suppress unwanted negative phenomena such as problematic behaviors among youths, turnover rates, and organizational dissolution rates [2]. In summary, social capital has attracted attention from various social science disciplines.

Based on the content of the interaction, Nahapiet and Ghoshal [68] classified social capital into three dimensions: structural, relational, and cognitive. The structural dimension of social capital represents the configuration of the physical ties that connect actors. Those physical ties among members make access to resources possible. The relational dimension of social capital refers to the emotional and affective status within the social network. Relational elements facilitate the exchange of resources between actors. The cognitive dimension of social capital refers to shared representations which allow actors to effectively access resources [68]. This classification has been adopted by several studies to understand the impact of social capital on resource exchange and combination [95], knowledge contribution [108], quantity and quality of knowledge sharing [20], exploitative and exploratory learning [5], knowledge sharing [99,100], instant messenger usage [51],actioncontinuanceintention[107],andteam-basedclancontrol [47]. Detailed information about these studies is provided in Table 1.

The studies listed in Table 1 can be classified into two streams. The first stream aligns with the original concept proposed by Nahapiet and Ghoshal [68] and treats three dimensions of social capital as parallel elements. Studies in this research stream found that each type of social capital has an effect on the quantity of knowledge sharing, quality of knowledge sharing, helpfulness of knowledge contributions, volume of knowledge contributions, explorative learning, exploitative learning, and instant message usage. In general, the structural dimension has a positive effect on the dependent variables, with the exception of the negative impact of extra-industry managerial ties on exploitative learning [5]. Variables related to the relational dimension are found to have generally positive effects, although specific variables may generate diverse effects on different dependent variables. For example, commitment increases instant message usage [51] but reduces the helpfulness of contributions [108]. In one study, reciprocity was found to increase the quantity of knowledge sharing [20], while in another study, reciprocity was found to reduce the volume of contributions [108]. Finally, variables related to the cognitive dimension were found to have a positive effect on the dependent variables, with the exception of the negative effect from shared vision on the quantity of knowledge sharing, as found by Chiu et al. [20]. In team-based studies, all three dimensions of social capital were also found to have positive impacts on team-based clan control [47], and the relational dimension was shown to have the capacity to enhance knowledge integration [94].

In the second research stream, initiated by Tsai and Ghoshal [95], it is argued that certain causal relationships may be found. As a consequence, three links among the three dimensions of social capital are highlighted [e.g., 95,99,100]. Particularly, the structural dimension is argued to be the antecedent of the relational and cognitive dimensions, while the cognitive dimension leads to the relational dimension. For example, Tsai and Ghoshal [95], adopting a social network analysis approach to measure interaction ties to the structural dimension, found only support for structural-to-relational and cognitive-to-relational links. Furthermore, the cognitive dimension was found to have no effect on resource exchange and combination. Wang and Chiang [107] also adopted a social network analysis approach to capture interactions among members. The authors found a significant relationship between social interaction (structural) and shared vision (cognitive) and between shared vision (cognitive) and trust (relational); however, the authors failed to find support for the link between the structural and relational dimensions.

However, cognitive social capital may conversely lead to structural social capital. It is reasonable to assume that the shared cognition of the roles, responsibilities, and acceptable interaction patterns in a community enables members to know both with whom to interact and how to interact with that target when needed. For example, in the study of team shared understanding, researchers showed that a shared understanding of teamwork and task work improves the teamwork process as measured by coordination, cooperation, and communication [61]. Shared understanding promotes effective communication among members, reduces coordination costs, and facilitates cooperation among members. Marks et al. [57] also indicated that although early interaction facilitates shared understanding, shared understanding later also contributes to an enhanced interaction process (e.g., communication). In one early and critical team mental model paper, Klimoski and Mohammed [48] also illustrated a correlated, rather than a causal, relationship between shared understanding and teamwork processes. In summary, although structural social capital leads to the cognitive dimension in the early stage, the cognitive dimension, conversely, may also have an impact on the structural dimension in the later stage.

Similar arguments for the causal relationship from the relational dimension to the structural dimension can also be found in the literature. For example, trust is the basis for interpersonal interaction, with people being more willing to engage in exchanges and cooperative activities when trust levels are high [49,79,80]. Trust is central in relationship building [1,67]. In the IS area, trust also has been shown to have an effect on relationship and partnership building [34]. Knowledge management studies indicate that trust is the basis for knowledge-related interaction, such as exchange, sharing, and integration [23]. In addition, trust is a critical antecedent of cooperative problem solving and constructive dialog [86]. As indicated by social identity theory, social identification is associated with cooperation and altruism [96,97].

Finally, with respect to the causal relationship between the relational and cognitive dimensions, although a cognitive-torelational relationship has been proposed, the direction is not reversible. For example, social identity theory indicates that individuals tend to identify themselves with those with similar intrinsic or extrinsic features. Moreover, people tend to view themselves as being similar to those within the same group after viewing themselves as a member of this group [53]. Furthermore, a cohesive group generates similar attitudes [16]. Transactive memory literature also suggests that individuals in close relationships tend to develop a shared understanding about who the knowledge experts are [109]. Other empirical studies have also indicated that friendship leads to similar perceptions among friends toward distributive and interactional justice [98].

However, even though cross-sectional data may be insufficient for showing either-way causal relationship, cross-sectional data can still be used to understand the joint effect of the different dimensions of social capital. Different from a causal effect in which the temporal sequential order should be clearly defined, a joint effect shows only the status of both variables together and how their combination affects outcome variables. We suspect that two dimensions of social capital may jointly generate effects on outcome variables. It is interesting to understand the level of outcome variables under certain conditions of two dimensions of social capital. For example, can the project still achieve high performance when one dimension of social capital is low and another dimension remains high? Can the project achieve a higher performance when both dimensions of social capital are high?

Recently, the joint effect of two independent variables has been studied broadly. For example, in the project control area, Tiwana [92] indicated that different control mechanisms may be substitutable for or complementary with each other. Analogizing this concept to our study, we also suspect that if two dimensions of social capital complement each other, project performance may be higher when both dimensions are present. In contrast, when two dimensions of social capital are substitutable, an absence of either one does not harm the project performance significantly.

## 2.2. Information system development project performance

There are many different views of what the concept of ISD project performance really means and how it should be measured. Previous empirical studies divide project outcomes into two types, product and process, when exploring factors that contribute to ISD performance [71,103]. Because the goal of an ISD project is to implement an information system that supports business operations, the quality of the final deliverable outcome represents the effectiveness of the project teamwork. Product performance refers to the success of the system developed, which describes the performance of the system actually delivered to users [70,104]. Researchers frequently use system quality to represent product quality. In general, system quality can be viewed as the capacity of the system to maintain its level of performance when it is used under specified conditions. For example, a high-quality system should be available when needed (reliability) and able to react to users’ requests within a reasonable time (response time). From the users’ perspective, the interface should be intuitive, and the learning cost should be low (ease of use) [10]. In addition, system quality also represents the system’s ability to provide outputs that meet the users’ needs and to allow itself to be modified or revised [12].

At the same time, an ISD project is a series of processes, which include initialization, planning and control, execution, and termination. The performance of the project team is also determined by the extent to which those processes can be accomplished with efficiency. Process performance describes how well the software development process has been undertaken [70] (for example, the extent to which the project is delivered on schedule and within budget [87,103]). A considerable body of the project management literature has measured the efficiency of project team performance by using two indicators: ‘‘the team’s adherence to schedules’’ and ‘‘the team’s adherence to budgets’’ [38]. Therefore, in light of the extant literature, both process performance and product performance are considered in this study because while the final delivered system may be of a high quality, the development process might be inefficient, and vice versa.

One of the various factors that contributes to low product and process performance is the extent to which developers are able to manage user issues effectively, a factor that has been highlighted by a number of researchers [7,18,39,42,63,84]. Specific require ments can be better identified when users engage in the development process. As a result, the developed systems are better able to respond to users’ needs. Furthermore, many projects are unable to adhere to predefined schedules or budgets because development teams fail to identify the real user requirements in the early stages, a phenomenon caused in large part by a lack of user support. The costs of revising an inadequate initial design in the later stages are much higher (40 to 100 times) than revisions in the early stages [101], and the direct consequences include a delayed schedule and/or overrun budgets.

Although developers are major actors in the ISD process, achieving high performance is difficult when input from users is absent. Project performance is also determined by the extent to which developers have opportunities to interact with users (structural social capital), know how to interact with users effectively (cognitive social capital), and are able to maintain a strong relationship with users (relational social capital). In this study, we treat the above dimensions of social capital as developers’ social capital with users and highlight its importance to project outcomes. In the following section, we introduce each dimension of social capital and build its respective links with project outcomes.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Developers’ social capital with users

User issues in ISD have drawn significant attention in the last decade because ISD requires various types of expertise and insight from different stakeholders such as developers and users [3,18,36,56,73,89]. In the ISD context, collaboration with users is critical during the project [102] and is expected to increase system quality and user satisfaction. However, a lack of an appropriate structure for interaction, insufficient common knowledge, and poor interpersonal relationships may lead to conflicts and communication gaps, which in turn reduce the benefits of having users involved in the project [60,88]. Because the above issues are covered by social capital, we need to understand how project performance is affected by developers’ social capital with users.

## 3.2. Structural dimension

The structural dimension of social capital usually reflects the interaction pattern of one social actor with others in the social network [68]. The structural dimension of social capital depicts the properties of the structure of a social network and the relationships between actors [31]. In this study, the developers’ structural dimension of social capital refers to the institutionalized formal mechanisms that allow developers to interact with users. An ISD project is a process in which ‘‘user and developer [. . .] are cooperatively involved to the extent that the activities of each facilitate the attainment of the ends of the others’’ [89]. To enable users to contribute to the project, it is important that certain mechanisms are in place. Based on the user participation concept, the formal interaction mechanism is adopted in this study to represent the interaction structure between users and developers. The formal interaction mechanism aims to capture the connection between users and developers facilitated through the formal mechanism. For example, users may be invited to engage in project planning meetings at the requirements determination stage or in the review meeting.

By viewing users as a critical intellectual resource, their fullest potential as contributors to the project can be reached if the organization provides structural procedures to utilize the resource [13]. The structural dimension of social capital provides a context for developers to interact with users so as to access required knowledge resources. Users are able to contribute to the project work if they are formally invited to engage in different development stages. Particularly, at the planning stage, users may express their expectations about the goal and schedule, while at the design stage, users may be able to help determine system requirements through their formal engagement [36]. At the development stage, by joining the review meeting, users can contribute to ensuring system quality and performance control. Finally, users may assist in performing system tests at the testing stage. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1. Structural social capital is positively associated with project performance.

H1a. Structural social capital is positively associated with process performance.

H1b. Structural social capital is positively associated with product performance.

## 3.3. Relational dimension

The relational dimension of social capital refers to emotional and affective bonds between social actors and others in a social network. Key concepts include trust, norms, obligations, and identification. Theorists argued that the relational dimension of social capital influences behaviors by increasing the willingness to share information, reducing communication barriers, and viewing the success of others as one’s own success [68]. In this study, the developers’ relational dimension of social capital with users is viewed as the emotional and affective bonds between developers and users, including mutual trust, respect, reciprocity, and relationship closeness. In the IS area, the relational dimension of social capital has been shown to be important for knowledge integration [94], knowledge sharing [99], and conflict resolution [44].

During the ISD process, the integration of various stakeholders knowledge into the project requires trust and respect [11]. ISD is a process in which developers develop a final system, based on user requirements, to support business operations. This process requires developers to combine their own technical knowledge of the business needs of users to create a high-quality design. The knowledge management literature has indicated the importance of strong relationships to enhance the willingness of project participants to exchange and combine their domain knowledge during the ISD process [68,90]. When strong and trusting relationships exist between developers and users, the costs of communicating, coordinating, and combining each other’s knowledge will decrease. In turn, these strong relationships promote the effectiveness of user-IS cooperation [26,37,81], the direct consequence of which is more effective planning and development of new applications [83]. Empirical studies have also indicated that this type of user–developer partnership helps to ease conflicts and improve interaction quality, important antecedents of project performance [44].

Furthermore, relationships are the basis for fostering solidarity. For example, when problems occur, a high level of trust allows users and developers to possess a more open attitude, which allows them to solve the problem jointly. Believing that neither party would take advantage of the other if an opportunity was to arise, users and developers are better able and willing to adapt their goals to those of the other [11,37]. Moreover, a strong relationship allows both parties to ignore minor issues, such as slight deviations from the predefined plan, and leads to commitment, which is the basis for resource dedication [11,14]. Finally, because successful system development requires not only developers but also users to be fully committed to the development work, users are more willing to commit time to system development when a strong relationship exists. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2. Relational social capital is positively associated with project performance.

H2a. Relational social capital is positively associated with process performance.

H2b. Relational social capital is positively associated with product performance.

## 3.4. Cognitive dimension

The cognitive dimension of social capital theory describes the extent to which members in the social network are able to communicate with each other through the use of shared language and codes [68]. In this study, because we focus on developers only, the developers’ cognitive dimension of social capital is operationalized as the extent to which developers have adequate business knowledge. When developers possess a certain level of business knowledge, they are able to use users’ language to communicate and exchange information [68].

Recent project management literature has also indicated that involving users in the so-called ‘‘knowledge co-production process’’ can lead to improved efficiency in the creation process [40]. Because co-production demands intensive communication between users and developers, the effectiveness of their communication is determined by the extent to which a shared understanding exists [65]. However, the diversity in functional backgrounds between users and developers makes the generation of a common understanding on various issues more difficult [91]. For example, users might become frustrated when developers fail to understand their business language and, consequently, are unable to transfer their needs into the system design. Without a shared understanding, information exchange is costly and inefficient [11,66].

Furthermore, good communication can reduce conflicts and enhance joint problem solving. Mutual adjustment is possible if both parties have the same understanding of both the final goal and the procedures that are necessary to achieve the goal. A common understanding of the main structure of the target system enables IS developers to modify the system design to fulfill users’ specific requirements; at the same time, users are able to abandon their more irrational requirements to avoid possible conflicts [11].

Finally, the importance of developers having adequate business knowledge has been emphasized widely in the literature [e.g., 55] because such knowledge contributes to both process and product performance. In terms of product performance, adequate business knowledge enables developers to communicate effectively with the users and thus understand their specific requirements. A better understanding of user requirements in the early stages will result in the development of a higher quality system. With respect to process performance, extra costs and schedule delays can be avoided if the project team is able to ascertain the precise requirements of the users in the early stages. In addition, a lack of adequate knowledge leads to risks [29,70], increases uncertainty [43], and inhibits the learning process [78], all of which impede the project team from achieving the predefined goal. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3. Cognitive social capital is positively associated with project performance.

H3a. Cognitive social capital is positively associated with process performance.

H3b. Cognitive social capital is positively associated with product performance.

## 3.5. Interaction effects

In addition to the direct effects of the three dimensions of social capital, we hypothesize about the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital. Particularly, we operationalize the structural dimension as the formal interactions between users and developers during the system development process. At the same time, the relational dimension reflects the extent to which users and developers trust and respect each other. The structural dimension is expected to benefit the project by increasing the level of interaction between users and developers. By participating in formal project meetings, users are able to contribute to business knowledge and to validate the quality of the developed system. Formal engagement of users in the development process increases the likelihood of developers to develop a system tailored to the specific needs of the users. Although the probability of the developed system meeting users’ specific needs is higher when users have the opportunity to engage in the development process, the effect can be better when these two parties have a harmonious relationship. The interaction process is smoother and better information exchanges can be observed when a prior relationship exists. A strong relationship allows both parties to resolve possible conflicts; therefore, risks can be reduced, and performance can be enhanced [44]. Therefore, the structural and relational dimensions can function as complements to each other. In this context, a positive coefficient is expected.

Prior studies have indicated that user participation in different development stages can benefit project performance. At the same time, some studies have argued that user participation in the development process may impair project performance and may lead to possible obstacles [82], such as communication gaps and communication lapses [28]. Therefore, simply having users in the development process may not always be sufficient. For example, because of their diversified backgrounds in information systems and organizational operations, users and developers sometimes may not easily reach a consensus; consequently, low quality interactions between the two parties occur [50]. User participation in the development process may also lead to tension between the two parties, reduce commitment, and increase intolerance. As noted by Bettencourt et al. [11], project progress will be hampered if users are unable to ignore minor issues and focus exclusively on the main function. Failure to resolve conflicts impedes the performance of the project team, which ultimately results in diminished project performance. As such, conflicts can be eased by a strong relationship between users and developers [e.g., 44]. Thus, having users in formal meetings may not benefit the project outcome unless developers maintain a strong relationship with the users. Therefore, the structural and relational dimensions complement each other when process performance is the target. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H4. The interaction between the structural and relational dimensions of social capital (SSC\*RSC) has a positive impact on project performance.

H4a. SSC\*RSC has a positive impact on process performance.

H4b. SSC\*RSC has a positive impact on product performance.

With respect to the interaction between the structural and cognitive dimensions, a complementary effect is expected. Empirical studies have indicated that the successful integration of diverse knowledge during the ISD project is a critical factor in project success [9,72]. Although formal mechanisms can increase the interaction between users and developers, we predict that better outcomes can be obtained when developers have adequate business domain knowledge. As suggested in user participation research, user involvement in the project, in terms of identifying actual requirements, validating the quality of the developed system, and even performing hands-on activities [6], is essential to prevent the developed system from deviating from actual requirements. However, the developed system may still fail to meet the users’ needs because of the developers’ inability to incorporate the user-provided information into the system design. In other words, inadequate knowledge integration occurs when developers do not have the required business knowledge to fully grasp or digest the information provided by the users [64,74]. Thus, having users on the development team may not necessarily bring the expected benefits when developers are not familiar with business operating procedures [91]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H5. The interaction between the structural and cognitive dimensions of social capital (SSC\*CSC) positively affects project performance.

H5a. SSC\*CSC has a positive impact on process performance.

H5b. SSC\*CSC has a positive impact on product performance.

In this study, we argue that the relational and cognitive social dimensions of social capital are mutually complementary. Based on the hypothesized main effect, we assume that project performance can still be high when either dimension is available. The cognitive dimension is viewed as the extent to which developers understand the business operational procedures. The cognitive dimension serves as a protocol that allows both parties to communicate with each other effectively, the result of which should be direct benefits to the project outcome [91]. High performance can be achieved if developers have a strong relational connection with users and are able to communicate with them by using the appropriate business language. However, trust and respect are needed to ensure that users and developers can effectively integrate individual-level knowledge at the project level [94]. Therefore, high performance is expected when the levels of both dimensions of social capital are high.

Effective system development relies on the extent to which developers can capture users’ needs and transform them into an actual system. First, although a strong relationship enhances the likelihood of developers and users being willing to exchange and combine their domain knowledge during the ISD process [32,58,94], an effective outcome can not be achieved when a shared understanding is absent. Second, although a shared understanding reduces the cost of communication, coordination and knowledge combination and, in turn, facilitates the effectiveness of the user-IS knowledge combination [59], the absence of trust reduces the effectiveness of this process. Without trust, developers cannot have confidence in the received information and tend to spend extra time and cost to verify the information received from users. Therefore, an effective outcome still cannot be expected because the potential barriers to communication can not be fully minimized. Thus, this study suggests that both dimensions of social capital are important and that better effects can be achieved when both of them are high. Therefore, a positive coefficient between interaction term and project outcome is expected. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H6. The interaction between the relational and cognitive dimensions of social capital (RSC\*CSC) positively affects project performance.

H6a. RSC\*CSC has a positive impact on process performance.

H6b. RSC\*CSC has a positive impact on product performance.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Sampling

Our research model is shown in Fig. 1. Based on our research purpose, individuals who are familiar with an IS project process and the outcome characteristics of an IS project were preferred in this study. For this reason, senior developers with experience being involved in ISD project teams were targeted. As shown in Table 2, our respondents include senior members, managers, and specialists within the project. Almost 80% of them have more than five years of working experience in the information system development area. The target sample was IS practitioners in the Top 1000 companies in Taiwan [24]. The survey consisted of questions about the most recently completed projects in which the practitioners participated.

Given that social capital with users lies in the domain of team members and users, the key respondents of this study were team members rather than other stakeholders. A survey package was sent to the head of the MIS department in each company. He/she was asked to pass the survey questionnaire on to the most senior member in their department who had participated in the most recently completed ISD project. Senior members were chosen because they would be more familiar with the interaction status among members and the final performance of the target project. Senior members were asked to rate the team’s overall interaction with users, the level of domain knowledge, and the relationship with users. Survey packages were sent out through regular mail in early March 2011, and a follow-up letter was then sent to those who had failed to return the survey after 1 month. A total of 253 responses were received 2 months after the initial letter was sent. Thirteen questionnaires containing numerous missing values were deleted, yielding a final response rate of 24%. Hence, our data are representative of the developers’ social capital with users and the final performance of 240 project teams. The characteristics of our respondents are detailed in Table 2.

## 4.2. Constructs and measurement

Product performance refers to the level of success of the system developed and the extent to which it meets user requirements [11,39]. A total of four items adopted from Wallace and Keil [103], Hartwick and Barki [35], and Patnayakuni et al. [74] were used to capture the extent to which the developed system was able to meet users’ needs as well as the level of user satisfaction.

Process performance refers to the success of the development process itself [103]. To assess process performance, five items drawn from existing scales [33,38,45] were used in the questionnaires. These items assessed the subjects’ perceptions of project performance in terms of schedule, budget, and predefined tasks. These items also measured the degree to which the project achieved its intended scope, the tasks were completed efficiently, the project goals were met, and the expected amount of work was completed.

Structural social capital refers to the institutionalized formal mechanisms that allow developers to interact with users. A total of five items adopted from Nidumolu [70] and Bettencourt et al. [11] were used to capture the extent to which users were invited to participate in the project meetings at different stages, were informed about the project progress and problems, formally approved the work undertaken by the project team, and met the developers on a routine basis.

Relational social capital refers to the level of mutual trust, respect, reciprocity, and relationship closeness between users and developers during the ISD project [46,94]. A total of four items adopted from Tiwana and McLean [94] were thought to reflect the mutual respect, mutual trust, friendship, and reciprocity among users and developers.

Cognitive social capital refers to the developers’ knowledge of the business domain. A total of four items adopted from Barki et al. [8] and Tesch et al. [91] were used to measure developers’ knowledge about organizational operations, administrative experience and skills, and expertise in the application area of the developed system as well as the functioning of developers’ user departments.

The definitions and sources of measurement of the variables used are all summarized in Table 3.

## 4.3. Control variables

In this study, we controlled the effects of two important project characteristics to eliminate any possible confounding effect. These two characteristics reflect the scale of the projects. They were controlled because high performance is difficult to achieve for large-scale projects in general. First, large-scale systems often require more personnel to conduct the development work. Because the difficulty in effective communication and coordination increases as the number of people involved rises [85], team size was used as a control to capture any effects of variations on project performance. Second, projects of a long duration are also often large in scale. As a consequence, various issues such as employee turnover and planning difficulties often undermine the performance of such projects. Therefore, the effect of project scope on project performance was also considered to really understand the effect of the proposed independent variables [62]. Each of these two variables was measured with a single item to obtain the size of the team and the duration of the entire project.

## 4.4. Validity and reliability

For all variables included in the model, a five-point Likert scale with anchors ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’ was adopted to represent whether each individual perceived the item to be true during his/her most recently completed project. All survey items were adapted from published literature in English. Because we conducted the survey in Taiwan, a Chinese-based society, all items were first translated into Chinese. In addition, a Chinese-English back translation was made to evaluate the quality of the translation. Because no significant differences between the original and translated meaning were found, we considered the translation to be adequate. Furthermore, the instrument was pretested by two professors with experience in ISD research, three doctoral students, and six industry professionals. The comments and feedback from these experts led to several minor modifications. Therefore, we consider the content validity to be ensured in this study.

In this study, partial least squares (PLS) analysis was used to test item reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity [41]. Individual item reliability can be examined by observing the factor loading of each item. As shown in Table 3, all factor loadings are significant using t-statistics, with most loadings being higher than the recommended 0.7, except for 1 indicator in structural social capital [41]. This item remained in the ensuing analysis because it was not lower than the minimum cut-off value (0.5). The itemtotal correlations (ITC) are also higher than the recommended cutoff value of 0.3. Therefore, individual item reliability is ensured in this study.

Convergent validity should also be examined because more than two indicators were used to measure each construct. We used the composite reliability (CR) of constructs and average variance extracted (AVE) by constructs to evaluate convergent validity [27]. Convergent validity is ensured because the composite reliability of all constructs is higher than 0.7, and the AVE values are all greater than 0.5. Finally, as shown in Tables 3 and 4, the correlations between pairs of constructs are below 0.6, and the square root of the AVE is higher than each corresponding correlation coefficient [19]. These properties indicate that the construct measures are sufficiently distinct from each other (Table 5).

## 4.5. Common method bias

Common method bias should be examined before performing hypothesis testing when both independent and dependent variables are collected simultaneously from the same respondent. Two tests were conducted to examine common bias in our data. First, we performed Harman’s single factor test with all indicators used in this study. A total of five factors were extracted, and the first factor explains 35% of the variance. Second, in PLS, a common method variable was created and linked to all constructs indicators. We then calculated each indicator’s variance substantively explained by the construct and by the method [50,75,77]. The results show that the average substantively explained variance of the indicators is 0.637, while the average method-based variance is 0.011. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance is approximately 55:1, which is higher than the result shown in the study by Liang et al. [50]. In addition, most method factor loadings are not significant; only 5 of 22 were actually significant. Based on the above tests, we are satisfied that common method bias is not likely to be a serious problem for this study.

## 4.6. Non-response bias

To detect the potential bias resulting from sampling, a comparison of the early and late respondents was conducted for all variables (with the late respondents being assumed to be similar to non-respondents) [4]. The results show no significant differences between these two groups in all constructs (t-value ranges from 0.01 to 1.28). Therefore, we believe that the credibility of the following analysis is not undermined by non-response bias. In addition, we also compared the sample structure of our respondents with a previous study whose target sample was also ISD teams in Taiwan [e.g., 54]. Because no significant difference was found, the representativeness of our sample is ensured.

Differing from our expectation, a negative interaction effect on product performance was found between the structural and relational dimensions of social capital. This result, as shown in Fig. 3, implies that the structural and relational dimensions of social capital are substitutable when product performance is the dependent variable. Aligning with the established positive main effect, the structural dimension has a positive impact on product performance under varying levels of the relational dimension. The best quality can be achieved when both dimensions of social capital are strong. In addition, the quality of the developed system is noticeably lower when both structural and relational dimensions are absent. However, when the relational dimension is absent, system quality can be higher when the structural dimension is high. This finding implies that (1) users can contribute to the system design process through the formal participation mechanism even though they may not maintain a strong relationship with developers and that (2) when developers have a harmonious relationship with users but lack a formal participation mechanism, users can still contribute their business knowledge through informal channels. When either dimension of social capital is available, a system that better fits user needs can be developed. This result highlights the importance of maintaining a strong relationship with users when there is no formal mechanism to allow users and developers to interact with each other.

The interaction effects on process performance between paired dimensions of social capital are shown in Fig. 4a, b, and c. First, a positive relationship was found between SSC\*RSC and process performance. As depicted in Fig. 4a, in terms of process performance, these two dimensions of social capital complement each other. Process performance reflects the extent to which developers accomplish the predefined goal efficiently (within budget and on schedule). An ISD project is relatively inefficient when either the structural or relational dimensions of social capital are low or when both dimensions are low. Although the project is likely to be more successful when users and developers maintain a strong relationship. better effects can be obtained if formal mechanisms that allow users to engage exist.

The interaction between the structural and cognitive dimensions of social capital positively affects project performance. This finding indicates that the cognitive dimension tends to affect process performance more when the structural dimension is high, as shown in Fig. 4b. More interestingly, when the cognitive dimension is low, a low structural dimension of social capital may lead to better process performance. In contrast, process performance is higher when both dimensions of social capital are strong. This finding indicates that when a formal participation mechanism is available, project teams who have considerable business knowledge perform significantly better than those who do not have such knowledge. In contrast, for those project teams with little business knowledge, the structural dimension of social capital does not benefit (and may even harm) the process performance.

Contrary to our expectation, the interaction of the relational and cognitive dimensions of social capital was found to have a negative effect on process performance. As indicated in Fig. 4c, these two dimensions of social capital are substitutable rather than complementary. First, although the highest level of process performance can be achieved when developers possess both strong cognitive and strong relational dimensions of social capital, performance is higher if the cognitive dimension is higher all the time. Regardless of the level of the cognitive dimension, process performance is high when the relational dimension is high. Furthermore, a high cognitive dimension may result in the achievement of high process performance irrespective of the level of the relational dimension. One plausible explanation for this result is that process performance refers to the extent to which the project team is capable of overcoming barriers to achieve their predefined goal within budget and on schedule. Having both adequate business knowledge and a strong relationship with users allows the team to grasp the specific user requirements in the early stages and to avoid the potential for redoing work at a later stage. Although having both dimensions of social capital with users allows the team to achieve the predefined goal efficiently, having only one can still ensure that an acceptable performance level is achieved.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Social capital theory has been widely adopted in IS and other areas over the past few decades [e.g., 15,20,22,63,68,99,107]. Past social capital theory-based studies either treated the three dimensions of social capital as parallel or argued that causal relationships exist between the different dimensions of social capital using cross-sectional data. However, the ability of crosssectional data to show causal relations is limited since the directions of the found causal relationship are indeterminable. For example, one may argue that the relational dimension is the predictor (and that the cognitive dimension is the criterion), while another may argue that the cognitive dimension is an antecedent (and that the relational dimension is the consequence). Thus, cross-sectional data are insufficient for examining the ‘‘either-way’’ causal relationships between the different dimensions of social capital. Drawing on this issue, we proposed a possible utilization of cross-sectional data, free from the above limitation, in understanding the relationship between paired dimensions of social capital. Particularly, the purpose of this study was to understand the direct effect of each individual dimension and the interaction effects of paired dimensions of social capital.

We hypothesized that the structural, relational, and cognitive dimensions of social capital individually have a positive impact on both ISD process performance and product performance. In addition, the interaction of the three paired dimensions of social capital (SSC\*RSC, SSC\*CSC, and ${ \mathsf { R S C } } ^ { * } { \mathsf { C S C } } )$ affects process and product performance. Survey data collected from 240 IS professionals show that (1) all direct effects affect both project outcomes, (2) all three interaction effects have a significant impact on process performance $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and (3) only SSC\*RSC affects product performance $( p < 0 . 1 )$ . However, the effects of SSC\*RSC and RSC\*CSC on performance are different from our expectations. There are several implications for practitioners and academia arising from this study.

## 6.1. Limitations and suggestions for future studies

Although this study contributes to the social capital and ISD project management literature by exploring the direct effects of three dimensions of social capital and the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital, it is not without limitations. First, we collected data exclusively from developers. Although our study focused on developers’ social capital with users, social capital is related to both parties; thus, future studies are encouraged to capture users’ perceptions as well. Second, while we focused exclusively on project outcomes, several past studies emphasized the impact of social capital on other process variables, e.g., knowledge sharing, knowledge integration, and resource exchange. Future researchers may wish to examine the direct and interaction effects of different dimensions of social capital on those process variables. Third, we collected data from only one member in each team. Because developers’ social capital with users may vary among team members, future studies may further verify our results by obtaining the opinions of all team members. In addition, developers evaluated the level of product performance in this study. It is reasonable to suspect that different path coefficients and significance levels may be found if users are asked to evaluate system quality. Moreover, different results may be obtained if non-participant users are asked to evaluate the final product quality, as indicated by Markus and Mao [59]. Therefore, future studies may consider obtaining opinions from the user side as well, including both participants and non-participants. Despite the above limitations, the results of this study have academic and practical implications.

## 6.2. Implications for academia

First, as opposed to the majority of (or possibly all) previous social capital studies, this study aimed to elucidate the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital. Most past studies focused on elucidating the causal relationships among the three dimensions of social capital by drawing upon cross-sectional data. However, the explanatory power of cross-sectional data is limited under certain conditions, for example, when the structuralto-cognitive relationship is proposed but the reverse relationship is also possible. To take full advantage of cross-sectional data and to avoid possible drawbacks, we proposed another approach to understand the relationships among different dimensions of social capital. We successfully demonstrated the interaction effects between paired dimensions of social capital in an ISD project context. We advance this research stream by showing how different dimensions of social capital individually and jointly affect product outcomes. Our results suggest that future studies should take the interaction effect into consideration when constructing research models.

Second, among the three proposed interaction effects, several interesting results are noteworthy. All three interaction effects are found to be significant when process performance is the dependent variable. Furthermore, SSC\*RSC and SSC\*CSC have positive impacts while CSC\*RSC has a negative impact. However, not all interaction terms affect product quality; the effects of RSC\*CSC and ${ \sf S S C ^ { * } C S C }$ are found to be insignificant. These results imply that the dependent variable should be selected carefully when attempting to include interaction effects because the dependent variable may play a role in the testing of intended effects. At least in our setting, interaction effects are more significant when process performance is the study target.

Third, for those three interaction terms, their impacts vary on different performance measures: (a) two interaction terms (SSC\*CSC and CSC\*RSC) are found to affect both project outcomes in the same direction but at different significance levels, and (b) the interaction between the structural and relational dimensions of social capital (SSC\*RSC) is found to have significant effects on both project outcomes but in different directions.

For (a), while SSC\*CSC is positively correlated with process and product performance, only the correlation with process performance is significant. A positive correlation indicates that these two dimensions of social capital complement each other. Although each social capital dimension alone can promote process performance, better outcomes can be obtained when both are high. In contrast, while the interaction between cognitive and relational social capital (RSC\*CSC) is negatively correlated with both outcome variables, only the correlation with process performance is significant. As shown in Fig. 4c, these two dimensions of social capital are mutually substitutable for process performance. This finding implies that maintaining social capital is sufficient to achieve process performance, particularly when maintaining both dimensions of social capital is costly. However, the interactions between cognitive social capital and the other two dimensions of social capital have no effect on product performance. This finding implies that it is not necessary to consider the interaction of these two dimensions of social capital when product quality is the focus. Instead, each social capital dimension should be maintained equally.

For (b), the interaction between structural and relational social capital (SSC\*RSC) has a positive impact on process performance but a negative impact on product performance. This result is interesting and unexpected. In terms of process performance, these two dimensions of social capital complement each other. Although having one dimension of social capital allows the team to perform more efficiently, performance can be improved dramatically if both the structural and relational dimensions of social capital are present. However, with respect to product performance, the two dimensions of social capital are mutually substitutable. Although the quality of the developed system is high when both dimensions of social capital are present, the absence of one does not seriously harm system quality.

## 6.3. Implications for practitioners

The direct effects of social capital on project outcomes imply that the project team should not only pay careful attention to maintaining social capital with users, but also set priorities when limited resources are available. In addition, the priorities may change as the goals change. In terms of product performance, among the three dimensions of social capital, the relational dimension is found to have the strongest effect, followed by the structural dimension and the cognitive dimension. We can conclude that although developing strong social capital with users is important per se, maintaining a strong relationship is particularly critical if achieving higher product quality is the main goal. A strong relationship drives users to commit to the project and allows developers to obtain the specific requirements by interacting with users on an informal basis. In practical terms, managers should employ project members who have strong links with users to ensure that the developed system meets the users’ needs. In contrast, when process performance is the target, the cognitive dimension of social capital is found to have the highest coefficient, followed by the structural and relational dimensions. This finding indicates that understanding the business operating procedures allows developers to better grasp the user’s specific requirements. Knowing the political structure of the whole business allows developers to obtain resources and removes potential barriers during system development. As an outcome, the project is better able to achieve its goal within the predefined budget and on schedule. Therefore, we recommend that, when process performance is the highest priority, managers should select project team members with strong business knowledge. Alternatively, managers should provide sufficient training when members do not have the required knowledge.

The interaction effects between the paired dimensions of social capital also have some interesting implications. Although the direct effect indicates that maintaining a strong cognitive dimension of social capital is most critical when the goal is to increase process performance, the benefits of having a strong cognitive dimension of social capital are more significant when users are included in the formal development process (structural dimension of social capital) because these two dimensions of social capital complement each other. However, when considering the relational and cognitive dimensions of social capital at the same time, it is critical to maintain at least one dimension at a high level when having both is impossible or not cost-effective.

In contrast, when product performance is the objective, based on the strength of the direct effect, it is critical to maintain a strong relationship with users. When a strong relationship is absent, managers should attempt to involve users in formal meetings to determine the requirements because the structural and relational dimensions of social capital are mutually substitutable.
