# Efects of peers and network position on user participation in a firm-hosted software community: the moderating role of network centrality

Xiaolei Wang<sup>a</sup>, Terence T. Ow<sup>b</sup>, Luning Liu<sup>a</sup>, Yuqiang Feng<sup>a</sup> and Yuan Liang<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management, College of Business Administration, Marquette University, Milwaukee, United States; <sup>c</sup>Human Resources Department, China National Oil and Gas Exploration and Development Company Ltd, Beijing, China

## ABSTRACT

Customers have become co-creators of value in software development and improvement. The intensifying integration of social networks and online communities requires an understanding of social influence on user participation. Drawing upon social capital theory, we studied the influence of peers and network position on user participation in a firm-hosted software community. Based on an analysis of longitudinal data on the knowledge seeking and knowledge contribution behaviours of 2,192 users, we found that an individual’s quantity of friends’ participation is positively related to his or her participation. The source credibility from expert friends’ participation negatively afects user participation. We especially found that network centrality which depicts an individual’s relative position in the network significantly moderates the efects of peers. Interestingly, closeness centrality and betweenness centrality exert diferent moderating efects on the peer efects. Closeness centrality strengthens the influence of friends’ participation on knowledge seeking but has no efect on knowledge contribution. Betweenness centrality does not afect the efects of friends’ participation. The efects of source credibility from expert friends are weaker for users with lower closeness centrality and higher betweenness centrality. This study yields a better understanding of social influence on user participation and provides insights for software community managers.

## 1. Introduction

Over the last decade, customers have become vital sources and co-creators of business value (Vargo & Lusch, 2016). The software industry is a typical area in which customers have emerged as a kind of involved actor in contemporary software development ecosystems. Firms are increasingly inviting customers into their software development and innovation activities (e.g., Microsoft and SAP) because customer participation is a crucial success factor of agile software development practices (Cui & Wu, 2016; Misra et al., 2009). In software-focused online communities, customers discuss product features, software experience, and suggestions about software functions with each other, which can provide rapid feedback to the development team. Their participation has become a main driver of software innovation (Saldanha et al., 2017; Von Krogh et al., 2012), software development quality and success (Abelein & Paech, 2015; Bano et al., 2017), and user satisfaction (Subramanyam et al., 2010).

The high degree of customer participation in software development is attributed to the connectivity and interactivity of Web 2.0-based virtual communities (Kroenke & Boyle, 2015). On one hand, virtual communities provide users an eficient and efective environment for seeking solutions, sharing knowledge, and making contributions (De Almeida et al., 2014; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). Users with common interests are connected virtually, share knowledge and collaborate with each other through reading or posting to discussion topics (Khansa et al., 2015). Therefore, other users (i.e., peers) are important collaborators and potential channels through whom a user may access knowledge (K. Zhao et al., 2018). On the other hand, connections among individuals create structural links in the social network irrespective of physical distance. Users’ positions within the social structure provide them opportunities such as access to valuable resources and peer support (Sykes et al., 2009). The wide connectivity with peers and the large-scale of social networks are typical functionalities of virtual communities that facilitate customers’ collaboration in software development.

However, alongside mature technical support, managers of software communities still face challenges of inadequate user participation for several reasons. First, user participation is mostly voluntary (Colazo, 2014). The varying degrees of their participation range from very low to intensely high (Malthouse et al., 2013). Second, users’ knowledge is a public good (Goes et al., 2016). Users expect to get some benefits to compensate them for their eforts and time. Third, users are embedded in the social networks through frequent social interactions in the online community. They are exposed to positive or negative influences from peers and network on their participative behaviours (Aral & Walker, 2012). Therefore, managers are eager to know the factors afecting user participation, and then adopt efective measures to facilitate user participation. Specifically, they need to answer the following questions: How are users motivated to exchange knowledge in firm-hosted software communities? Will the behaviours of their peers afect users participation? What is the structure of networks in software communities, and how can it afect users participation?

To answer these questions requires an understanding of the prior work of researchers. Although many researchers have studied the antecedents of user participation, some research opportunities remain. First, prior researchers mainly focused on the individuallevel motivations of users such as desire to build their reputations (Chen et al., 2018; Horng, 2016). They paid limited attention to the influence of peer-level and network-level factors. Considering the complex relationship networks in a virtual community, an individual’s participative behaviour should not only be afected by his or her own motivation but also by peers’ participative behaviours and his or her position in the network (K. Zhao et al., 2018).

Second, few researchers have explored the moderating efect of network position. Network position denotes an actor’s relative power in the group (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Central positions imply users can control or gain more resources and information that may change their self-evaluations and perceptions of other users’ participative behaviours (S. H. Lee et al., 2010). Existing studies have explored the direct influence of network position on an individual’s performance (Horng, 2016; Lu & Yang, 2011). However, some findings difered about the efect of network position on user participation (Rotolo & Messeni Petruzzelli, 2013). There may be a boundary condition such that in certain network positions, users are more sensitive to peer influence than in others. The possible moderating efect of network position should be studied further.

Third, network centrality is the typical indicator that describes an actor’s relative position in a network. Closeness centrality and betweenness centrality are two important measures to describe network position. Both are centrality measures. However, they depict diferent strategic locations of users in the social network (S. Lee et al., 2010). Closeness centrality is based on the distance of an actor from all others in a network and captures the potential for gaining access to information independently. Betweenness centrality describes a network position that controls the flow of information between two clusters of users because the actor is on the shortest path between them (Li et al., 2013). The diferences in their moderating efects on peer influence have not been studied extensively.

This research focuses on users’ participation behaviours in firm-hosted online software communities as they have emerged as a new actor and contributor in contemporary software development ecosystems (Vargo & Lusch, 2016). Lack of user participation is one of the major concerns of software firms and may lead to software development failure because it causes problems in gathering software requirements and feedback (Hoda et al., 2011). Understanding influence factors of user participation will help firms to improve the participation of their customers in software development. Furthermore, users’ contributions to software development relies on interactions with peers through social networks in online communities (K. Zhao et al., 2018). Understanding the influences of peers and network that exist in the software communities will help firms to motivate more customers’ interactions and participation. Therefore, this paper explores how friends and network position can afect user participation by conducting a longitudinal study of 2,192 members in a Chinese firm-hosted software community. We found the antecedent roles of friends’ participation especially expert friends’ participation. More importantly, we identified the moderating roles of network centralities. Our findings will contribute to the literature on user participation and provide insights to software firms.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. User Participation in the Firm-hosted Online Software Communities

Software development is a knowledge-intensive and complex activity, which requires collaboration of multiple actors including the focal firm, developers and end users such as customers (Rashid et al., 2019). The participation of end users provides a huge source of software-related knowledge (Seyf et al., 2015). User participation can also improve the quality and innovation of software products (Subramanyam et al., 2010) by providing precise user requirements (Lynch & Gregor, 2004). More and more firms are trying to appeal to a community of users with a common interest in the software development and usage (Burger-Helmchen & Cohendet, 2011). The firm-hosted online software community is a firm-sponsored, computer-mediated platform whose main purposes are interactions between the firm and end users surrounding the software (Guo et al., 2017).

User participation is defined as “the behaviors and activities that users or their representatives perform in the systems development process” (p.302, He & King, 2008). Users participate in relevant knowledge exchange activities in online communities, which provides a vital source of employee learning (Huang & Zhang, 2016) and organisational performance (Wilkin et al., 2013).

Given the importance of user participation, scholars are trying to understand the motivations of user participation in online communities. They have different research focus for diferent communities. In open source software communities, they focused on users’ project contribution (C. Zhang et al., 2013). In online professional communities, their focus was users’ discussions on certain areas of expertise (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). In online question-andanswer (Q&A) communities, scholars mainly discussed individual incentives of users’ discussions on diverse topics (Khansa et al., 2015). Compared with those communities, the firm-hosted online software community has its critical characteristics. First, users discuss with each other around software-related topics such as features, bugs, upgrade package and functional suggestion. Users’ mutual help is a form of online customer support service from the firm to address software-related issues. Second, the community is initiated and managed by a firm for its software products and services (H. Liu et al., 2014). Users who receive formal recognition of the firm are more influential to other regular users.

We referred to the literature of user participation in virtual communities but are not limited to the software communities because some individual motivations are common in diferent contexts. We then proposed our hypotheses in the context of the firmhosted software community.

## 2.2. The Influence Factors of User Participation

Users’ participation roles in knowledge exchange generally include knowledge seeking by posting questions and knowledge contribution by posting answers (Forsgren et al., 2018; Khansa et al., 2015). They are diferent in terms of the direction of flow of information and might be influenced by the antecedents differently. We provided the antecedents of the two participative behaviours from research found in the most recent ten-year studies in Table 1. The influence factors include intrinsic and extrinsic factors.

Intrinsic motivations stem from the psychological benefits that users expect to gain from the participation itself (Gerow et al., 2013). Self-eficacy, commitment and sense of belonging have been identified as common intrinsic influence factors of knowledge seeking and knowledge contribution behaviours (Chen & Hung, 2010; Nambisan & Baron, 2010; Park & Cho, 2012; Park et al., 2014). Individual intrinsic motivations have been studied extensively in the firmhosted online communities. Hence, our research did not focus on intrinsic motivations.

Extrinsic motivations come from external sources. It is the anticipated or practical external benefits that motivate users to participate (Chen et al., 2018). We classified existing antecedents into individual-level, peer-level and network-level factors, as shown in Table 1. Individual-level factors focus on the benefits that users expect from participation. Prior researchers have focused on informational, functional, and social benefits. First, seeking information is a basic individual pursuit in an online environment (Bock et al., 2006). Knowledge growth, knowledge quality, learning and informational benefits have been found to positively afect knowledge seeking behaviours (Lai et al., 2014; Sivertstøl, 2018; K. Zhao et al., 2018). Similarly, expertise enhancement is also a motivation of customer contributions (Nambisan & Baron, 2010; Sun et al., 2014).

Second, community functions satisfy users requirements for security, usability, interactivity and hedonism. Therefore, these functions and their pursuit shape users’ knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours (Choraria, 2012; Hu et al., 2016; Sivertstøl, 2018; Sun et al., 2014; Zha et al., 2015).

Third, a virtual community provides a platform for social interactions. For user contributions, reciprocity, reputation, and popularity are main motivations that derive from social interactions. The norm of reciprocity prompts users to contribute knowledge with others to reward others’ help (Chan & Li, 2010; Chen et al., 2018). Reputation and popularity are recognised as two contributors of user status (Yang et al., 2016). Popularity is generally measured by the quantity of followers such as the number of fans (De Vries et al., 2012), comments (Yang & Li, 2016) or profile views (W. Yan et al., 2018). Reputation represents others recognition of users’ past performance in the form of approval, status and respect. Users expect to enhance their status through contribution (Chen et al., 2018; Horng, 2016; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Compared with contribution behaviours, some scholars consider status could exert little to no influence on knowledge seeking (Kang et al., 2014; Park et al., 2014). However, Chen and Hung (2010) suggest status enhancement both positively influence knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours (Chen & Hung, 2010).

Peer-level factors and network-level factors have not been explored as much as individual-level factors. Peer-level factors were derived from peers (e.g., peers behaviour and characteristics). Peer members’ postings, recommendations, and feedback were found to relate positively to user contributions (Cheung et al., 2015; L. Liu et al., 2016). In addition, peers’ characteristics such as peers’ tenure, benevolence and structural position in the network could also influence focal users’ knowledge contributions (Nevo et al., 2012; K. Zhao et al., 2018). Similarly, scholars also suggest peer participation, peer tenure and peer expertise could afect users’ knowledge seeking behaviours (Nevo et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2018).

As for network-level factors, researchers have taken individuals’ positions into consideration. For example, Wasko and Faraj (2005) and K. Zhao et al. (2018) studied structural capital which was measured by degree centrality (i.e., the number of direct ties with other users) and found it could positively influence users’ knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours (Wasko & Faraj, 2005; K. Zhao et al., 2018). Similarly, network ties were found to positively influence both knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours (Nevo et al., 2012; Zhang & Venkatesh, 2017). In addition, the strength of relationships (e.g., network density, network closeness) has the potential to predict user contributions (Kang et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2016).

The boundary conditions of relationships between antecedents and user participation have also been studied. Most studies mainly focused on the moderators of overall participation or user contributions. Among studies of overall participation, higher product involvement, community identification, community rewards, self-consciousness were found to be significant moderators of user participation (Nambisan &

Baron, 2007; Wang et al., 2015). In studies of knowledge contribution, some scholars found company identification, reputation, types of users (seller/ buyer) are significant moderators of relationships between extrinsic antecedents (e.g., reward, reciprocity, peer members’ postings) and customer contribution (Cheung et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2016). For knowledge seeking behaviour, user level and tenure were found to moderate the efects of user habits on users’ knowledge seeking behaviours (Khansa et al., 2015).

Several research opportunities are presented in the literature on user participation. First, scholars have mainly focused on individual-level factors and have mostly neglected the efects of peers and network position. Second, few studies have explored the moderating role of network position. Network position reflects an actor’s relative power in the group (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). If users perceive they have higher control of, or more access to, information and resources (i.e., users occupy more central positions), they may difer from peripheral users in their perceptions of other users’ participative behaviours (Lee et al., 2010). To address the gaps, we studied the influence of peers and the moderating efect of network position on user participation in the software community.

## 2.3. The Embeddedness of Social Capital

In firm-hosted software communities, firms make great eforts to involve customers in their processes of software development, innovation, improvement and customer support. Their typical tactic to motivate users to behave as expected is networking (Liao et al., 2017). Establishing social networks and building customer-to-customer interactive relationships can motivate users to discuss with peers about their perceptions and usage experience of products. The influence of peers (especially those who are experts) and network positions on user participation should be further explored. In this study, we used social capital theory to explain such social influence on user participation.

Social capital is defined as resources embedded in a social structure that are accessed and/or mobilised in purposeful action (Zheng et al., 2014). Social embeddedness is the precursor of social capital (Granovetter, 1985; Rai et al., 2009). We used the concept of embeddedness instead of capital because we focused mainly not only on actual resources that users already have but also on potential influence from interpersonal relationships. Social embeddedness is multidimensional, comprising relational and structural dimensions (Granovetter, 1992; Moran, 2005). Relational embeddedness is defined as “personal relationships people have developed with each other through a history of interactions” (Nahapiet &

Ghoshal, 1998). Structural embeddedness refers to “the impersonal configuration of linkages between people or units” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998) such as network ties and network position (Bolino et al., 2002). The two key facets of embeddedness are expected to influence the perceptions and participative behaviours of users.

## 2.3.1. Relational Embeddedness and the Efects of Peers

Relational embeddedness emphasises the role of direct ties between users and the relational outcomes of interactions that can change their behaviours and fulfil their motives (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005). Observations of peers’ participative behaviours have the potential to change the likelihood that a user will engage in a behaviour because their participation provides users with informational cues about reality (e.g., software-related information, group norms in software communities) (Aral & Walker, 2012).

In firm-hosted software communities, peers’ participation in software development-related discussions can motivate more knowledge seeking behaviours of users. Peers’ comments on the developed software features or software usage experience in the community provide related knowledge about software quality (Qiu et al., 2015). The acquisition of helpful software knowledge could motivate even more knowledge seeking behaviours because users rely on peers to address technical questions (Lai et al., 2014; K. Zhao et al., 2018). In addition, a high level of peer participation reflects that active participation in software development activities is a kind of social norm or a shared behaviour of expectations in the software community (Hsu & Lin, 2008). Users may ask more questions about software features or problems to conform to the norm of knowledge exchange in the software community. Therefore, we hypothesise:

Hypothesis 1a. The quantity of friends’ participation is positively associated with a user’s knowledge seeking behaviours in a firm-hosted software community.

More postings about software features by their peers can facilitate individuals to contribute more software knowledge (Cheung et al., 2015; K. Zhao et al., 2018). This peer influence may be from the norm of reciprocity. Peers’ postings give users opportunities to learn software knowledge (Corkindale et al., 2018). The potential informational benefits may motivate users to contribute more software knowledge to return the favour (Chen et al., 2018) or ensure ongoing supportive knowledge exchange with peers (Chen & Hung, 2010).

Hypothesis 1b. The quantity of friends’ participation is positively associated with a user’s knowledge contribution behaviours in a firm-hosted software community.

The quality aspects of peer participation could also influence users’ behaviours. Source credibility is a vital way to identify the quality of postings. It refers to the extent to which a piece of information is perceived to be authentic and credible (G. Wang et al., 2011). Source credibility often relates to expert users’ high expertise. The active users who get formal recognition of their productrelated expertise are referred to as “expert” users or “elite” users or “power” users (Kordzadeh et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2016).

In software communities, expert users usually have more software-related experience and knowledge than regular users. They are often regarded as valuable contributors in software development and improvement. They garner close attention because of their professional generated content. A regular user’s participation may be influenced by the participation of expert friends who receive formal recognition of the firm. When regular users regard themselves as inferior to the average performance of others in the network, they are more likely to exhibit social loafing behaviour (lurking) (Schnake, 1991). Compared with expert users, regular users are less likely to participate in software development activities because they feel they lack enough software expertise (Wasko & Faraj, 2000). Without adequate levels of self-eficacy in software expertise, they might consider they are not competent to raise software problems (Chen & Hung, 2010). We hypothesise:

Hypothesis 2a. The source credibility from expert friends’ participation is negatively associated with a user’s knowledge seeking behaviours in a firm-hosted software community.

The lack of self-eficacy in software expertise from expert friends’ participation can also decrease users’ enthusiasm in software knowledge contribution (Chen & Hung, 2010). Regular users would consider their contributions invisible in the software community with too many expert members (Liden et al., 2004). They may show relatively passive behavioural pattern and contribute fewer answers to software-related questions. Therefore, we contend that users’ contributions are likely to be negatively influenced by expert users’ participation in the software community.

Hypothesis 2b. The source credibility from expert friends’ participation is negatively associated with a user’s knowledge contribution behaviours in a firmhosted software community.

## 2.3.2. Structural Embeddedness and the Moderating Role of Network Centrality

Structural embeddedness indicates that the connections between individuals, or the structural capital created through social interactions, are important predictors of collective action in a network (Putnam, 1995). In a social network, centrality is a typical indicator of structural capital that describes an actor’s relative position and power in the group (Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

Network centrality includes three dimensions: degree, betweenness, and closeness (Freeman, 1978). In this study, we chose betweenness centrality and closeness centrality to denote network centrality for the following reasons. First, the two centrality measures capture not only direct relationships but also indirect relationships in the network (Perry-Smith & Shalley, 2003). Second, unlike measuring only the quantity of ties in degree centrality, betweenness centrality and closeness centrality capture the configuration of ties (i.e., strategic location) (Lee et al., 2010). Betweenness centrality describes the network position that controls the resources and informational flow between pairs of other users (Li et al., 2013). The actors with high betweenness centrality play a bridge role in connecting two clusters of users and facilitate informational exchanges between them. Closeness centrality captures the potential for gaining information independently of all other members of the network (Freeman, 1978). The actors with high closeness centrality could eficiently attain information from others.

Network centrality may not directly afect user participation in software communities because software development requires specialised software knowledge. Higher degree of knowledge specialisation may lead users to develop a narrow knowledge base that can weaken the resource advantage of being in the central position (Rotolo & Messeni Petruzzelli, 2013). Therefore, instead of studying the direct efect, we focused on the moderating efect of network centrality.

The Moderating Role of Network Centrality. In software communities, increased level of available software knowledge from high closeness centrality can give users a perception that knowledge seeking is under control (Lai et al., 2014). The informational benefits from central positions help to improve users understanding and knowledge about the software products (Sivertstøl, 2018). Therefore, central users tend to be more influenced by peers’ participation and ask more questions to seek help from peers than peripheral users.

The increased accessible software knowledge from high closeness centrality can also motivate users to contribute more software knowledge in the community (Nambisan & Baron, 2010). Users who perceive each other as close are more likely to understand that active participation in software-related discussions is a group norm and an expected behaviour by the firm in the community (Kang et al., 2018). To conform to the group norm and the norm of reciprocity, users may contribute more software knowledge. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3a. The efect of quantity of friends’ participation on users’ knowledge seeking behaviours is stronger for higher-closeness-centrality users than for lower-closeness-centrality users.

Hypothesis 3b. The efect of quantity of friends’ participation on users’ knowledge contribution behaviours is stronger for higher-closeness-centrality users than for lower-closeness-centrality users.

Central users with high betweenness centrality have the advantage of receiving diverse valuable software knowledge from other users in the software community (Burt, 2010). Being exposed to a high volume of helpful software information, they may have the desire to maintain such important positions in the informational exchange (Mehra et al., 2001) and pay more attention to the ideas of other users (Lee et al., 2010). They tend to take the responsibility of knowledge exchange by actively collecting peers’ software knowledge and sharing the knowledge to others. We hypothesise:

Hypothesis 4a. The efect of quantity of friends’ participation on users’ knowledge seeking behaviours is stronger for higher-betweenness-centrality users than for lower-betweenness-centrality users.

Hypothesis 4b. The efect of quantity of friends’ participation on users’ knowledge contribution behaviours is stronger for higher-betweenness-centrality users than for lower-betweenness-centrality users.

The social loafing efect caused by informational social influence from expert friends may vary according to the focal user’s level of network centrality. Users with higher closeness centrality are closer to others in the software community. It is easier for them to identify the relatively high-level software expertise and superior performance of expert friends. More participation from expert friends increases both the quantity and quality of accessible software knowledge and resources (e.g., software experience, solutions to bugs, self-developed software packages) that can help to solve users’ software problems. The potential informational help from expert friends may motivate users to ask more software-related questions in the community (Lai et al., 2014; K. Zhao et al., 2018). As a user’s closeness centrality heightens, his or her own visibility in the community also increases. The increased identifiability may increase self-eficacy of users and reduce their social loafing (Liden et al., 2004) that may be caused by the participation of expert friends.

Central users with high closeness centrality communicate frequently with other users in the software community. They can discuss software features, usage experience and tips, software upgrade method, and suggestions about software functions with each other. In this process, close interactions with others can deepen users trust in regular friends (McEvily et al., 2003). They may consider the information from regular users also credible and important. In this case, the efect of the participation of expert friends could be dificult to observe.

Hypothesis 5a. The efect of source credibility from expert friends on users’ knowledge seeking behaviours is weaker for higher-closeness-centrality users than for lower-closeness-centrality users.

Hypothesis 5b. The efect of source credibility from expert friends on users’ knowledge contribution behaviours is weaker for higher-closeness-centrality users than for lower-closeness-centrality users.

In software communities, high betweenness centrality implies high informal power in passing software knowledge between pairs of users. Higherpowered users are less likely to loaf because they realise that their technical ability or specialised software expertise is necessary to some users (Klehe & Anderson, 2007). They will generate a sense of selfeficacy and responsibility to assist other members to learn more software knowledge. Therefore, they may exhibit more participative behaviours such as actively identifying and raising technical problems about software, and answering software-related questions (Chen & Hung, 2010; Shiue et al., 2010). Conversely, users with lower betweenness centrality lack related software expertise. They may believe software development and improvement is more likely to rely on the participation of expert users and prefer to be lurkers.

Hypothesis 6a. The efect of source credibility from expert friends on users’ knowledge seeking behaviours is weaker for higher-betweenness-centrality users than for lower-betweenness-centrality users.

Hypothesis 6b. The efect of source credibility from expert friends on users’ knowledge contribution behaviours is weaker for higher-betweenness-centrality users than for lower-betweenness-centrality users.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Data Collection

## 3.1.1. First-round Data Collection

We collected data from Xiaomi’s online MIUI community (http://www.miui.com/) to test our research model. Xiaomi is a top-five mobile manufacturer in China and MIUI is one of its software products. Customers in the MIUI community discuss their experience with the software product on such topics as product bugs and their perceptions of the service. They can also make suggestions on software innovation and software improvement in the community. Moreover, in this community, there is a special expert user group that is selected from regular users. Zealous followers with high status and much experience can apply to join the expert user group. Managers of the firm select expert users from regular users based on regular users’ participative performance. Periodically, managers evaluate expert users and remove those who do not finish the tasks allocated to them. One important task for these users is to provide customer support to other users and make suggestions about product features. In return, the firm will ofer them the opportunity to experience new functions of its software products and more reading or replying privileges in the community than regular users. Managers also give expert users a special label as their “inner testing group”, which implies that they regard this group as their functiontesting employees. These expert users are part-time employees and empowered by Xiaomi. They actively contribute software-related content to the community. Hence, they become a huge provider of knowledge contribution. Therefore, expert users’ behaviours are likely to influence regular users because of their special roles.

![](/api/attachments/XCN9SAD3/fulltext/images/85870bcbbaba0d3cff956e8992a7a16fb1028e1d5ec623535c3752fa5de0e9f8.jpg)  
Figure 1. Conceptual Model.

In addition to dynamic post-reply relationships among users, the online community facilitates stable interactions among users by allowing them to establish friend relationships with other interested users through a friend request-confirmation mechanism. In this manner, users can easily read all the postings of their friends from the personal community homepages of their friends. Through friend relations, users are embedded in a friendship-based social network with other users, including expert users. Therefore, the MIUI community is an appropriate research target to investigate the influence of peers and network position on users’ participative behaviour.

Since expert users are only a small percentage of users in the MIUI community, we chose only regular users for our sample (hereafter called focal users). We developed a web crawler in the Python language to collect data. We randomly chose as samples 6,861 regular users who had at least one expert friend and then collected the personal profile data of these regular users (i.e., reputation, online time), their list of friends and their friends’ participation data on January 1 2018. We excluded from the study those users with fewer than five friends. This exclusion was to avoid any extreme value efect from peripheral users who had little impact on the network. In the robustness check, we also tested the sample with at least one friend for each user.

## 3.1.2. Subsequent Data Collection

After 12 months of getting independent variables, we further collected on January 6 2019, the participation data of users as dependent variables. We used the number of questions and answers posted by users in the subsequent year to incorporate a time lag from independent variables to dependent variables. In this way, we may remedy the endogeneity issues because independent variables from the previous point in time could not be determined by dependent variables in the lagging time period. Some additional users were removed from the user sample because we did not get their subsequent data. We ultimately retained the data of 2,192 regular users, and their 35,439 friendship relationships. We also utilised an 18-month longitudinal data set that we collected on July 9 2019, to conduct robustness checks to show the consistency of our results.

## 3.2. Variable Measures

## 3.2.1. Dependent Variables

In the MIUI community, users can either initiate a discussion post by asking questions about product usage or reply to an existing post by answering others questions. Therefore, users’ participative behaviours include knowledge seeking and knowledge contribution behaviours. As mentioned before, we used 12- month lagged participation data to remedy the endogeneity. We measured knowledge seeking behaviours as the number of questions (Questions , i = 1, 2, . . ., n denotes a specific focal user), and measured knowledge contribution behaviours by the total number of answers (Answers ). We counted the number of questions and answers that were posted one year subsequent to the data of the independent variables. In our sample, all focal users are regular users, but their friends can be regular or expert members. In an online social networking context, users’ activities, including the number of questions and answers, have been reported to display heavy-tail distribution (Devineni et al., 2017; Q. ## 3.2.3. Moderators

Closeness centrality (Close ) measures how close a node is to other nodes in the network. The measure of closeness centrality is related to the notion of geodesic distance and farness. In graph theory, the geodesic distance between two nodes is the number of edges in a shortest path connecting them (Agneessens, Borgatti & Everett, 2017). Farness is defined as the sum of the geodesic distance from one node to all other nodes (Bavelas, 1950; Rodriguez et al., 2015). Closeness centrality is defined as the normalised form of the reciprocal of the farness. As shown in the following computation formula, $d ( i , j )$ is the geodesic distance between user i and user j. $\textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 , j \neq i } ^ { n } d ( i , j )$ represents the sum of the geodesic distance from user i to all other users, which is the farness of node i in the network. In the community we studied, user j is one of the focal user $i \boldsymbol { s }$ friends, and n denotes the number of members in the network. The closeness centrality is then equal to the normalised form of the inverse value of the farness (i.e., $\textstyle { \overline { { \sum _ { j = 1 , j \neq i } ^ { n } d ( i , j ) } } }$ divided by the maximum possible value $\scriptstyle { \frac { 1 } { n - 1 } } \displaystyle { \big ) }$

$$
\text { Close } (i) = \frac {n - 1}{\sum_ {j = 1 , j \neq i} ^ {n} d (i , j)}
$$

Betweenness centrality (Between ) represents the degree to which an actor controls resources and information passed from one actor to another (Freeman, 1978). It is calculated based on shortest paths. In the unweighted graph, at least one shortest path exists between two nodes in such a way as to minimise the number of edges that the path passes through. The betweenness centrality of a node i is the number of shortest paths that pass through this node (Freeman, 1977). As in the formula shown below, $\sigma _ { j k }$ denotes the number of shortest paths from user j to user k, and $\sigma _ { j k }$ (i) denotes the number of shortest paths from j to k via i.

$$
B e t w e e n (i) = \sum_ {i \neq j \neq k} ^ {n} \frac {\sigma_ {j k} (i)}{\sigma_ {j k}}
$$

We used Brandes’ algorithm (2001) to count the shortest paths between two nodes in order to calculate closeness centrality and betweenness centrality. It has been proven to be an eficient algorithm to compute centralities in large networks (Brandes, 2001).

The network of this research is based on the data of the friends of sample users. We had 2,192 sample users and obtained lists of the friends of each user. Finally, we obtained 13,322 friends in addition to the sample users and the 35,439 pairwise relationships among them. Using the involved users as nodes and the relationships as edges, we depicted the network as shown in Figure 4 (including 15,514 nodes and 35,439 edges). Because the friends’ relations are bidirectional relationships (e.g., user A is user B’s friend and vice versa), the established graph was undirected. Based on this graph, we further computed the closeness centrality and betweenness centrality values of each user. Only those values of sample users were used in this study.

## 3.2.4. Control Variables

The MIUI community tracks each member’s cumulative online time. It reflects the duration of users’ participation. In community research, prior studies have shown positive (Wasko & Faraj, 2005), negative (Khansa et al., 2015) or insignificant (Ma & Agarwal, 2007) relationships between online time and user participation.

In addition, the MIUI community uses a reputation system to reward users’ participative behaviours. It takes into account the quality of the content of their posts (i.e., whether community managers classify their posts as elite posts) and peer recognition (i.e., responses and evaluations from other users). A higher reputation score implies a user has a rich participative experience, a high level of expertise, and respect from others. Some studies have shown that reputations can increase users’ intention to contribute information in online communities (Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Yang et al., 2016).

## 3.4. Endogeneity Issues

Two-way causal relationships may exist between the independent variable(s) and the dependent variable and have the potential to result in an endogenous problem (biased coeficient estimates and standard errors) (Wooldridge, 2015). In such cases, the endogenous independent variable would correlate with the error term in the regression model. In our context, reputation could be a main source of simultaneity. There is a feedback loop between participation and reputation. Reputation has been commonly recognised as the external incentive for user participation (Yang et al., 2016). However, user participation could also have the opposite efect on reputation. A reputation system is an efective tool to evaluate users’ behaviour in a community (Remondino & Boella, 2010). More participation can yield a higher reputation score. Therefore, reputation is an endogenous variable. Moreover, network centralities could also be endogenous because more participative users could receive more friend requests and occupy more central positions in the network (Bowler et al., 2009). Therefore, the centralities could also be determined by user participation.

To remedy possible endogeneity, we used 12- month longitudinal data of user participation. This is because the reputation and peer participation from a previous point in time could not be determined by the focal user’s participation during the lag period. We also used 18-month longitudinal data of user participation in the robustness check and got similar results.

There is also an endogeneity issue associated with peer influence and friendship formation. The simultaneity between peer influence and tie formation make it a dificult challenge to diferentiate the peer influence and homophily. Under the peer influence mechanism, an individual influences or causes outcomes in his or her friends (Aral et al., 2009). Under the homophily mechanism, it is possible that users could not influence each other at all and that the correlated outcome patterns among users comes from intrinsic similarities among them (Bapna & Umyarov, 2015). The two efects are confounded with each other.

Based on the existing studies about social network, we finally adopted two methods to separate homophily from peer influence. First, community membership might to some extent capture the unobservables that determine network structure. Controlling for community membership may help to reduce concerns about suspected unobserved homophily (Godinho De Matos et al., 2014; Shalizi & Thomas, 2011). User tenure and rank are two representative measures of membership (Khansa et al., 2015). On the one hand, we added user tenure $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the length of time a member has been in the community) as the control variable. On the other hand, because the reputation score of the focal user was highly correlated with a user’s level (a user can move to a higher rank if his or her reputation score exceeds the predefined threshold of that rank), taking reputation as a control variable could also help control for unobserved homophily. Second, using longitudinal data is helpful to separate homophily from peer influence (Noel & Nyhan, 2011). Longitudinal data of participation allows us to observe the behaviours of focal users after formation of the network. In the longitudinal study, we used network structure and peer participation in the previous time point and the focal user’s participation during the subsequent time period. We chose the users who still had the same friend relations as before in the subsequent time point as our final sample. Thus, the previous ties could only exert the peer influence during the lag period. This approach could also help to separate homophily from peer influence.

## 4.2. Robustness Check

To avoid the efects of extreme values, we chose only users with more than five friends to establish a social network. Nevertheless, we examined whether our results were robust when we used diferent numbers of friends. We conducted the same regression models on three other social networks (i.e., number of friends ≥1; ≥3; ≥7). The significance of the coeficients was unchanged, denoting that our models are robust. Tables (4,5,6) reveal the results of the robustness checks in diferent social networks.

In addition, we examined the efects with dependent variables of diferent longitudinal lengths. We tested our models by using alternative data of dependent variables that were 18 months after the time stamp of the independent variables. The results (as shown in Table 7) are consistent with our main model.

We measured the peer-level participation by using the total number of questions and answers. However, some scholars consider the number of answers a better measure because it captures the contribution of peers, which is more helpful and influential to focal users (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Therefore, in the robustness check, we measured the quantity of friends’ participation as the average number of answers posted by friends. We computed source credibility as the proportion of expert friends’ answers in the total number of answers from both regular and expert friends. The robustness check with this alternative measurement method of peer-level participation showed consistent results with the main analysis (as shown in Table 8), confirming that our results are robust.

## 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes three key theoretical contributions. First, this paper extends our knowledge of the influence of peers especially those expert peers on user participation in firm-hosted software communities. Previous studies mainly studied individual motivations of user participation and paid limited attention to the influence of peers’ behaviours. In addition, we still know very little about the influence of expert users’ behaviours on regular users’ behaviours from existing literature. In firm-hosted software communities, expert users are regarded as active users, opinion leaders and sources of valuable software knowledge (Grewal et al., 2000; Yuan et al., 2016). This aligns with the implication of Liden et al. (2004) that a higher proportion of expert friends’ participation can make users feel inferior to the performance of average users and consequently exhibit social loafing behaviour.

Second, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to empirically examine the moderating efect of network centrality in user participation. Prior studies have explored the direct influence of network position on an individual’s behaviour (Horng, 2016; Lu & Yang, 2011). Their inconsistent results (i.e., positive or negative influence of network position) suggest users may be more sensitive to certain network positions (Rotolo & Messeni Petruzzelli, 2013). However, the moderating efect of network centrality has not been empirically studied. We filled this gap in this study.

For the influence of friends’ participation, we found that closeness centrality significantly moderated the efect of friends’ participation quantity on knowledge seeking behaviours but did not exert any moderating influence on knowledge contribution behaviours. In addition, betweenness centrality did not exhibit a significant efect on the relationships between friends’ participation and either knowledge seeking or knowledge contribution behaviours. The nonsignificant moderating efects of closeness centrality and betweenness centrality generally implies that no matter which position a user occupies in the network, he or she is always subject to a peer efect. This could be explained by the dual influence of network centrality. Users with high network centrality may pay more attention to peers’ behaviours to conform to the group norms (Kang et al., 2018), or pay less attention to peers’ behaviours because there may be little demand for them to pursue a higher status through interactions with peers (Venkataramani et al., 2010). The potential opposite efects of network centrality may result in a non-significant moderating efect.

For the influence of expert friends, the negative efect of source credibility from expert friends increases with higher closeness centrality, but decreases with higher betweenness centrality. Lowcloseness-centrality users who lack informational resources are motivated to interact with other users to attain the required resources (Bowler et al., 2009). Because of their long distance from other users, they may spend more time searching for the information, which could increase their perceived benefits of information (Seçkin, 2010). Also, they are more likely to participate in return. With higher closeness centrality, many regular users tend to lurk after their informational needs are satisfied (Khansa et al., 2015). In addition, high-betweenness-centrality users who have the potential to control the flow of information may contribute more knowledge to show their intelligence in introducing ideas to others (Carboni & Ehrlich, 2013; Nistor et al., 2014). They can also feel that their own participation could receive more attention from others than would be given to peripheral users (Phang et al., 2013). In such a case, knowledgeable regular users may not consider themselves inferior to their expert friends and would not exhibit social loafing behaviour. This finding supports the view that “with great power comes great responsibility.”

Third, this paper suggests closeness centrality and betweenness centrality exert contrary moderating efects on the influence of expert friends. Such diference between the efects of the two network centrality measures has not been explored before. There are several possible reasons for the contrary results.

From the perspective of independence, closeness centrality can be interpreted as a user’s independence from other users’ control of resources, while betweenness centrality is a measure of others’ dependence on a user’s control of resources and information (Brandes et al., 2016). When interdependence between an individual and others is high, users tend to arouse a sense of empathy (Hoon & Tan, 2008). This shared empathy would lead to a greater concern for overall performance and increase group members’ sense of responsibility towards contributing their eforts. Users with lower closeness centrality depend more on informational help from other users. Users with higher betweenness centrality are relied upon by other users because of their roles in information exchange. Therefore, those users tend to participate more out of high interdependence with others.

From the perspective of similarity comparison, similarity with other users will increase users’ positive feelings and motivate them to participate more (L. Zhao et al., 2012). In the MIUI community, the use of diferent membership labels explicitly distinguishes expert users from regular users. Moreover, the access threshold for experts is relatively high. When users are closer to others, they will be more aware of the characteristics of his or her group (Okoli & Oh, 2007). They will recognise their friends’ contributions and the dissimilarity between their expert friends and themselves. In this situation, regular users become less salient in the group (Wong, 2008). When users perceive that their eforts cannot help them decrease the dissimilarity between them and their expert friends, they will not compare upward (i.e., compare themselves with expert friends) but compare downward (i.e., compare themselves with other regular friends) to gain a sense of confidence. Conversely, increased power and knowledge from high betweenness centrality could make users consider themselves similar to the expert users. Thus, these regular users who occupy central positions in the network tend to contribute more to the community like their expert friends.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

Our results identified the peer-level antecedents and network-level moderators that can provide some insights for managers in the software community who seek to improve the level of user participation. User participation is critical for software companies because it is an important way of understanding customers’ requirements accurately (Bano & Zowghi, 2015), improving software quality, and increasing user satisfaction (Subramanyam et al., 2010). Many companies have established forums and online communities to involve customers in their product development and innovation processes (e.g., Dell’s Ideastorm) (Bayus, 2013).

Understanding the motivations and fundamental causes of user participation helps software companies to design mechanisms to engage more users. Our findings provide some possible pointers for managers. First, users behave diferently in terms of frequency of contributions in the online community. In general, managers can categorise users into regular and expert users (who are also called opinion leaders, highly respected users) (Füller et al., 2007; Tang & Yang, 2006). Expert users are active participants who have adequate relevant domain knowledge. They participate in knowledge-exchange activities more out of enjoyment of the activity itself than because of a desired outcome (Füller et al., 2007). Regular users are mainly need-driven participants. Because they are neither experienced nor mature customers, they are more easily influenced by peers. Managers should identify these diferent user groups. In addition to the active expert users, they should also pay attention to the motivations of regular users and the influence of expert users on them.

Second, we suggest that participative behaviours of peers have the potential to change regular user perceptions and participatory behaviours. We found overall participation of friends positively influences users participation. Therefore, showing the statistical data of users’ friends more saliently seems to be useful to motivate users’ participation. In addition, the proportion of expert friends’ participation negatively afects the participation of regular users. To reduce the social loafing behaviour of regular users, it is better to emphasise the contribution behaviours of regular users and prevent them from feeling inferior to expert users.

Moreover, we show the peer efects will change with a user’s position in the network. This indicates managers should consider network positions when implementing incentive mechanisms. For example, the ability to control resources and information (i.e., higher betweenness centrality) can reduce the social loafing behaviours caused by expert friends’ participation. Therefore, empowering the regular users and emphasising their important roles in the community could be beneficial in motivating more participation. When regular users are far from informational resources (i.e., lower closeness centrality), they tend to participate more to satisfy their informational needs. Managers can show these peripheral users the importance and benefits of acquiring necessary information and increase their need for information. In summary, our findings suggest that managers in the firm-hosted software communities should pay attention to the influence of peers and network positions when motivating the participative enthusiasm of regular users.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single firm-hosted software community. Although the community we studied has similarities with other firm-hosted software communities, it also has unique features. The managers in this community attach great importance to expert users. They select them from the self-recommended regular users with high expertise and enthusiasm. They give them a special label and empower them to propose softwarerelated problems and solutions. Other software communities could have diferent mechanisms to label expert users. For example, Oracle Corporation’s online forum rates each user based on evaluation points from other users. The top-ranked users are regarded as experts (Rafiei & Kardan, 2015). Some algorithms such as PageRank, ExpertiseRank, HITS can also be used to compute expertise levels of users in online communities (Jurczyk & Agichtein, 2007; J. Zhang et al., 2007). Future research should look at how these ranked or computer-assigned users with high expertise in other firm-hosted online communities afect regular users.

Second, the present study was conducted to understand the participative behaviours of regular users. Expert users may behave diferently than regular users in the community (Thomas-Hunt et al., 2003). Expert users with high expertise are an important source of quality answers and helpful suggestions. They are able to solve software-related problems by themselves and thus seem to have no necessity to ask questions. Thus, their participation may lie more in knowledge contribution than in seeking knowledge. These users with high levels of contribution may be less interested in their self-image than in their influence on product design (Saleem, 1996) and peer influence (Chen et al., 2018). More efort should go into understanding their participative motivations and propensities.

Third, this research studied social networks that are based on friends’ relations. The feeling of friendship is mutual because both sides agree to establish the relationship. In other firm-hosted online communities (e.g., Chinese Tencent Cloud’s online Q&A community for solving technical software problems), users could establish relations through following behaviours to show their support or admiration of others. Oneway relationships of followers may give regular users a sense of distance from the “star” expert users they follow (Agarwal et al., 2012). Regular users may act diferently under expert users’ influence. More studies are needed to compare the social influence on users in diferent types of social networks.

Fourth, we focused only on questions and answers that were posted in the function-related topics. This is because function-related topics are the most popular topics in a community. Our focal users are regular users. They mainly report their usage problems of software products and seldom participate in discussions of other topics. In addition to software functions, topics in the community forum also include informal activities such as chitchat, picture sharing and club activities. Our data did not include adequate data of user participation in these topics. In a subsequent study we can collect more participation data on other topics and study how the antecedents influence user participation in multiple topics diferently.

Last, we used longitudinal data of user participation in this study, which helps to reduce the probability that covariance problems will occur. However, we only collected two periods of dependent variables (12/18- month lagged data). In future studies, more periods of data can be collected and panel data models can be used to control for the user-level fixed efects on timeinvariant variables. In addition, our study mainly relies on objective data that may not capture users self-evaluation of changes in reputation or their perceptions of their friends’ participative behaviours. In future research, we could use surveys or interviews to investigate the perceptions of users.

## 6. Conclusion

Because of the new role of customers as value cocreators of software firms, this paper focuses on their participation behaviours in online software communities. Firms’ agile software development process demands user participation such as defining software features, reviewing existing features and providing usage feedback to developers. It is necessary to understand how to motivate users’ participation in software communities. Moreover, users are embedded in socia relationships with other users. The dependencies and relationships among users may afect their participation to a large extent. However, researchers have paid limited attention to the efects of peers and network position.

Drawing upon social capital theory, we studied the efects of the quantity of friends’ participation, source credibility from expert friends’ participation, and network position on users’ knowledge seeking and knowledge contribution behaviours. Based on the data of 2,192 users of a firm-hosted software community, we conducted a longitudinal study. We found that the quantity of friends’ participation and the source credibility from expert friends’ participation are important antecedents of users’ participation. Most interestingly, we found that network centrality that depicts a user’s relative position in the network significantly moderates the peer efects. This study contributes to the literature of user participation by identifying peer influence and network influence. It also provides insights for software firms about how to improve users’ level of participation. With increased participation, the firm hopes to improve the software development and other product innovation process.
