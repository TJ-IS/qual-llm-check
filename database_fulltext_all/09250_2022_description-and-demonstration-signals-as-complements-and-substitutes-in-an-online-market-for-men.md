# DESCRIPTION AND DEMONSTRATION SIGNALS AS COMPLEMENTS AND SUBSTITUTES IN AN ONLINE MARKET FOR MENTAL HEALTH CARE $^{1}$

Junjie Zhou
School of Business, Guangdong-Taiwan Research Institute, Shantou University
Shantou, CHINA {jjzhou@stu.edu.cn}

Rajiv Kishore
Lee Business School, University of Nevada, Las Vegas
Las Vegas, NV, U.S.A. {rajiv.kishore@unlv.edu}

Laura Amo
Management Science & Systems, State University of New York at Buffalo
Buffalo, NY, U.S.A. {lccasey@buffalo.edu}

Cheng Ye
Wuhan Xiaozhou Shipping Information Technology Co., Ltd.
Wuhan, CHINA {yecheng@cug.edu.cn}

Online markets for mental health care (OMMH) allow clients to connect remotely with counselors to receive psychological therapy. Rooted in signaling theory and in the specific context of an OMMH, we theorize relative credibility of signals as the boundary condition that determines whether the demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions substitutes or complements the two description signals of professional qualifications and counseling style in predicting market demand for counselors from new clients in an OMMH. Based on a panel dataset of 823 observations from 309 counselors participating on YiXinLi, a leading OMMH in China, we tested our hypotheses using linguistic and sentiment analysis methods and zero-inflated negative binomial models. We found broad support for nine out of ten hypotheses. Findings are robust with respect to different measures of variables, potential endogeneity from the simultaneity of responsiveness and counselor demand, and potential selection bias from both observable and endogenous covariates. Our study extends the literature on signaling in online markets in the unique context of OMMH by showing that: (1) relative credibility of signals is the boundary condition that determines when a demonstration signal will complement and when it will substitute for a description signal, (2) previous clients' feedback on counselors' empathy and warmth was deemed not credible by new clients in the context of online counseling, and (3) responsiveness to client questions is the most influential predictor of market demand from new clients in an OMMH.

Keywords: Online markets, online counseling, signaling theory, description signal, demonstration signal, empathy and warmth, demand for counselor, professional qualifications, counseling style, responsiveness

## Introduction

Online healthcare markets served an estimated seven million patients in the United States in 2018 alone (Mahar et al., 2018). Although in high demand, these markets are dually prone to negative outcomes associated with information asymmetry, which exists when parties involved in a transaction have access to different information (Akerlof, 1970). Online healthcare markets combine features of online markets wherein sellers/providers maintain their advantage by having greater information than buyers/clients (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Dimoka et al., 2012) with features of traditional healthcare settings wherein providers have more medical training and expertise, relative to patients (Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019; Saifee et al., 2020). To understand how compounded information asymmetry affects patients and determine how signals can reduce uncertainty (Spence, 1973), scholars in information systems (IS) have begun exploring online healthcare markets (Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Saifee et al., 2020; Shukla et al., 2021) as an extension of work on signaling in broader online markets (Bockstedt & Goh, 2011; Feng & Xie, 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2017; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014).

Notably absent from this growing body of literature, however, is an exploration of the demand for providers in online markets for mental health care (OMMH) that are emerging as a promising means of improving access to psychological therapy for emotional wellness for people across the globe (e.g., Apolinário-Hagen et al., 2018). The mental health care context is distinct from general healthcare because people with mental illnesses experience significant psychological vulnerability during dyadic interactions that typically involve high affect, intimate personal distance, and extended duration (Price et al., 1995). Further, there are unique power dynamics within the counselor-client dyad, making counselor empathy and warmth integral for establishing a strong therapeutic alliance (Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019; Nienhuis et al., 2018). Therefore, signaling, as it has been studied extensively in the IS, marketing, and strategic management literatures, does not readily apply to an OMMH.

A second gap in the body of literature on signaling in online markets, and specifically in online healthcare markets, pertains to the joint effects of multiple signals on consumer demand. A single signal sent by a sender exerts some impact on its receivers (Connelly et al., 2011). However, the impact of this signal on receivers is moderated in the presence of other signals that are sent by the sender or third parties (Bapna, 2019; Higgins et al., 2011; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014). Multiple signals sent together create joint effects on receivers, and either complement or substitute for each other. Significant joint effects of signals have been found in various contexts including venture funding (Plummer et al., 2016), product launches (Price & Dawar, 2002), online labor markets (Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014), and online healthcare markets (Khurana et al., 2019). Despite the fact that signals tend to be presented alongside other signals, scholars generally agree that joint signaling effects are significantly underexplored and undertheorized in the literature (Bapna, 2019; Connelly et al., 2011; Price & Dawar, 2002). While different papers in the signaling domain theorize substitutive and/or complementary effects of multiple signals on receivers, the literature is relatively scant on the boundary condition that separates two signals as substitutes for each other from two signals as complements of each other in their effects on signal receivers. To our knowledge, there is no study that considers this issue in the context of description and demonstration signals, a notion we briefly discuss below and develop more fully in the following sections.

This study aims to address the above two gaps by asking: How do description and demonstration signals interact with each other in a complementary or substitutive way to affect the demand for counselors from new clients in an OMMH? We address this question by first conceptualizing description signals versus demonstration signals inspired by and based on the distinction between characteristic and action signals (Plummer et al., 2016) and between announcement and action signals (Prabhu & Stewart, 2001). Description signals allow observers to gain information about a provider and their products/services by observing images, reading statements/narratives, or viewing announcements/advertisements provided by a seller. Demonstration signals, on the other hand, enable observers to experience some aspects of a provider and their product/service through some demonstration by the seller (Boleslavsky et al., 2017; Plummer et al., 2016), and generally have higher credibility than description signals due to their higher costs and observability (Connelly et al., 2011; Price & Dawar, 2002; Spence, 1973). Accordingly, we conceptualize professional qualifications and counseling style as description signals about the provider and the service, respectively, and responsiveness to client questions as a demonstration signal of both the provider and the service. We also divide the two description signals into self-description and credible-description signals, as different description signals can also have different levels of credibility depending on their cost and observability.

Aligned with the extant literature, we hypothesize that each of these signals will have direct positive effects on counselor demand from new clients in the OMMH. Further, we hypothesize that the demonstration signal of responsiveness will either substitute for or complement the description signals based on the relative credibility of the two interacting signals (Price & Dawar, 2002, p. 166). We tested our hypotheses using a panel dataset of 823 observations of 309 mental health care providers in an OMMH (YiXinLi) in China. We found that each signal in our model is positively associated with counselor demand (H1a-b, H2a-b, H3). We also found support for the hypothesized substitution effects in that the positive effects of the two less credible self-description signals of professional qualifications and counseling style are significantly weaker when presented alongside the demonstration-based signal, responsiveness to client questions (H4a and H5a). The hypothesized complementary effects between credible-description signals and the demonstration signal, however, hold only for the credible-description signal of professional qualification (H4b). Contrary to our hypothesis, responsiveness is found to substitute for the credible-description counseling style signal (H5b). Our findings are robust with the respect to potential endogeneity from the simultaneity of responsiveness and demand and potential sample selection bias from both observable and endogenous covariates.

This paper makes a unique and significant contribution to the growing body of literature on signaling in online markets in general (e.g., Bockstedt & Goh, 2011; Feng & Xie, 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2017; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014) and in online healthcare markets in particular (e.g., Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019). In the unique and unexplored context of an OMMH, we advance our understanding of signaling in online markets in one major and two subsidiary ways. First, and most importantly, we posit and show relative credibility of signals (Price & Dawar, 2002, p. 166) as the boundary condition that determines whether the demonstration signal of responsiveness substitutes for or complements the two description signals of professional qualifications and counseling style in predicting market demand for counselors from new clients in an OMMH. Second, consistent with findings about online reviews of physicians in the context of chronic diseases (Saifee et al., 2020), our findings show that a counselor's new clients do not find previous client reviews on the counselor's empathy and warmth to be credible, and thereby add new insights to the signaling literature on healthcare services with high levels of credence aspects. Finally, our results show that responsiveness to client questions is the most influential predictor of market demand from new clients in an OMMH and adds not only to the literature on online healthcare markets (e.g., Khurana et al., 2019) but to the emerging literature on provider responsiveness in online markets as well (e.g., Chevalier et al., 2018).

## Theoretical Background

## Information Asymmetry and Signal Credibility in Signaling

Information asymmetry exists when different people involved in a transaction have access to different information (Akerlof, 1970). Signaling theory helps to explain how different signals sent by a seller reduce information asymmetry for the buyer, who typically has less information about some unobservable quality of the seller, and how they enable economic transactions to be completed in an efficient manner (Akerlof, 1970; Spence, 1973). Signals are, essentially, observable pieces of information that communicate unobservable, desirable characteristics of a provider and/or product/service relevant to an economic transaction (Connelly et al., 2011; Kirmani & Rao, 2000). The most effective signals have two basic characteristics—they are both observable and costly (Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973). Highly effectual signals are strong and visible, i.e., observable, and successfully convey not only the unobservable quality the signaler is attempting to convey but also the fact that the signaler has invested substantial effort, time, and cost to obtain the signaled quality or characteristic of the signaler and/or their products/services (Connelly et al., 2011; Pavlou et al., 2007). Signaling theory has been applied to understand issues in e-commerce (Aiken & Boush, 2006; Feng & Xie, 2012; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Wells et al., 2011) as well as phenomena in online healthcare markets (e.g., Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019; Saifee et al., 2020).

Signal credibility is defined as the combination of signaler honesty and signal fit, where signaler honesty is the “extent to which the signaler actually has the unobservable quality being signaled,” and signal fit is the “extent to which the signal is correlated with unobservable quality” being signaled (Connelly et al., 2011, p. 52). The observability of a signal, one of the two main characteristics of effective signals, provides clear and visible information on the unobservable aspect the signaler is trying to convey to outsiders and is, therefore, a necessary condition for establishing signal credibility and trust in sellers (Deeds et al., 1997; Doney & Cannon, 1997). Further, recipients perceive signals as more credible when they believe that the signalers made significant investment/effort in sending the signal and believe that the signalers’ investment will be lost if the signals are false (Wells et al., 2011). The signaling literature has also found that third-party signals are usually more credible than first-party signals because they come from an entity with relatively less interest/potential gain in the transaction (Plummer et al., 2016; Spence, 1973). Third-party online ratings of sellers and the provision of third-party payment services increase buyers' trust in the provider (Li et al., 2009). Similarly, third-party certifications and third-party objective source ratings are deemed credible by buyers and increase trust in the seller (Aiken & Boush, 2006; Bapna, 2019; Li et al., 2009; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014).

Further, the marketing literature has established that trust in a seller is directly related to seller credibility and that a seller achieves credibility by providing reliable information to customers (Aiken & Boush, 2006; Atefi et al., 2020; Doney & Cannon, 1997). Signal credibility and trust in the seller are, therefore, intricately related constructs. Signals that are credible are more believable and trustworthy and therefore engender trust in the seller. The central facets of signal credibility are signal cost and signal observability, as discussed above, and costlier and more observable signals build greater trust. Moreover, signal credibility and trustworthiness are associated with greater purchase intent (Li et al., 2009; Wells et al., 2011). In summary, costly and observable signals are associated with greater credibility and trust, which is associated with positive market outcomes including customer purchasing behavior.

## Signaling for New vs. Repeat Customers

The signaling literature has established the value of signals for both new and repeat customers for a variety of goods and services in diverse markets, especially for “complex experience goods” (Kopalle et al., 2017, p.116). However, customers need different types of signals to build new relationships versus continuing existing relationships with a service provider. New relationships entail a higher degree of information asymmetry between customers and the provider as compared to repeat relationships (Dagger & Sweeney, 2007). Information uncertainty for repeat customers diminishes through firsthand experience with the provider, particularly in the case of experience or credence goods, which include healthcare services (Dagger & Sweeney, 2007; Saifee et al., 2020). Accordingly, signals are relatively more important for new customers of a provider (Dagger & Sweeney, 2007). Although repeat customers also need signals because provider capabilities and their services change over time (Kirmani & Rao, 2000), signals are interpreted by existing customers based on their past experiences, making it a different phenomenon than signaling to new consumers (Ho & Rai, 2017). In this paper, we focus on the role of signaling for new clients, as they are likely to rely largely on counselor signals prior to purchasing a counselor’s service to fit their needs (Ho & Rai, 2017).

## Signals about Provider vs. Product/Service

## Signals about the Provider

As highlighted by other IS scholars (e.g., Li et al., 2009; Pavlou et al., 2007), information asymmetry in online markets pertains to both the provider and the products/services they sell. As such, signals intended to reduce information asymmetry relate to the provider as well as the good or service being sold. Provider-related information asymmetry could be reduced by both first-party and third-party signals. Typical first-party signals about providers include the provider's disposition (Uhas et al., 2008), experience and education (Shah et al., 2019), disclosure of self-information (Bockstedt & Goh, 2011), and follow-up attention to customers (Chevalier et al., 2018; Li et al., 2017). Third-party signals related to providers include certification (Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014), badges denoting position or rank (Hann et al., 2013), endorsement by celebrities (Knittel & Stango, 2014), quality seals or trust marks (Aiken & Boush, 2006), and reviews and related reputation and feedback mechanisms (Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014). These signals tend to be perceived as more reliable.

Healthcare providers also use a variety of signals. For example, signals related to providers' specialized training and experience communicate technical competency (Guo et al., 2017). These signals also appear in online dyadic healthcare markets, where providers give information on professional titles or positions (Deng et al., 2019; Shah et al., 2019). These characteristics denote technical competency and qualifications and build the credibility of the healthcare provider (Shah et al., 2019). Third-party signals of healthcare providers include records of malpractice, board actions or sanctions (Shah et al., 2019), existing patients' reviews and ratings (Deng et al., 2019), and letters of gratitude and gifts (Guo et al., 2017).

## Signals about the Product/Service

In addition to signals about providers, signals may also pertain to the functionality, features, performance, and overall value of a product or service. Providers send a number of product-focused signals such as diagnostic features (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012), pictures (Li et al., 2009), history reports (Dimoka et al., 2012), product-focused advertising (Kihlstrom & Riordan, 1984), new product announcements (Dehning et al., 2003), previewing product features (Hoxmeier, 2000), warranties (Chu & Chintagunta, 2011), and product pricing, which has been found to be correlated with product quality (Milgrom & Roberts, 1986). High visibility in the market (e.g., a highlighted auction) also signals high levels of product quality (Bockstedt & Goh, 2011).

A variety of signals about services are also sent by providers, including office cleanliness and organization (Lochman, 1983), parking (Uhas et al., 2008), and website features (Wells et al., 2011). Providers send signals about services by responding to online reviews (Chevalier et al., 2018) and answering questions from potential customers (Li et al., 2017). Providers also convey the ability to understand and adapt during meetings and interviews, which has been linked to clients experiencing “gut feelings” (i.e., instinctual sense) about the provider, leading to provider-client chemistry (Pemer & Skjølsvik, 2019, p.359). Providers also signal their customer orientation by going above and beyond consumer expectations, e.g., by gift-wrapping products for customers (Bockstedt & Goh, 2011). Third-party sources in these contexts include media coverage (Greenwood & Gopal, 2015), expert bloggers (Aggarwal et al., 2012), and social media endorsements (Qiu et al., 2018).

In healthcare markets where services are rendered in person, signals of service quality include friendly disposition (Uhas et al., 2008) and communication (Rimondini et al., 2019), as well as gaze and body orientation (Robinson, 1998). Most relevant to our OMMH context are signals sent by healthcare providers in dyadic settings in online healthcare markets. Providers in these contexts send signals related to their service by answering patients' questions in online forums or being responsive to client questions (Deng et al., 2019; Khurana et al., 2019). Existing patients' reviews and ratings (Deng et al., 2019) and letters of gratitude and gifts (Guo et al., 2017) are third-party signals of dyadic healthcare quality in these markets.

## Description Signals vs. Demonstration Signals

The signaling literature distinguishes between signals of signaler characteristics versus their actions (Plummer et al., 2016), and between action signals versus announcement signals (Prabhu & Stewart, 2001). Signals about signaler characteristics provide information to outsiders about “the quality of a firm’s unobservable resource stocks and capabilities that lesser firms do not have (Plummer et al., 2016, p. 1589). On the other hand, “there are also a number of actions a firm can take to demonstrate value to outside investors” (Plummer et al., 2016, p. 1590). Similarly, a firm’s actions and announcements convey information about its intentions and abilities (Prabhu & Stewart, 2001, p. 63). Inspired by these and similar distinctions in the literature, in a similar vein, we conceptualize two major types of signals in this paper: description signals and demonstration signals. Description signals from a provider convey information to outsiders including customers on certain unobservable aspects about themselves and their products/services by way of descriptions in various forms, which are disseminated through various media. Demonstration signals, on the other hand, are actions taken and demonstrations made by a provider from which outsiders including customers can experience certain unobservable aspects about the provider and their products/services. These two types of signals generally differ with regard to their cost and observability, which are key to establishing the credibility of a signal, as discussed earlier (Connelly et al., 2011). Further, both these types of signals are discussed in the signaling literature; we discuss them in more detail below.

## Description Signals

Description signals are provided to outsiders by way of descriptions about a provider and their products and services to allow observers to glean information about providers' capability and the features, functionality, etc. of their products/services. These signals can be provided through various media and in various forms including the provider website, advertisements on TV, newspapers, magazines, and social media, product brochures, etc. As discussed above, many of the signals about the provider and about the product/service are description signals. Examples of description signals about a provider include security and privacy statements, daily deal sales (Subramanian & Rao, 2016), ratings (Ba & Pavlou, 2002), offers of third-party payment or escrow services (Li et al., 2009), and third-party endorsements such as trustmarks and ratings from consumer agencies (Aiken & Boush, 2006). Examples of product/service description signals include product descriptions (Dimoka et al., 2012), product pictures (Li et al., 2009), inspection and history reports of products (Dimoka et al., 2012), and performance-based advertising (Feng & Xie, 2012). Description-based signals can also provide information about provider capability through new product announcements and by displaying providers' managerial experience (Plummer et al., 2016), by displaying certification of coding skills in an online marketplace for software development (Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014), and through the display of clinical and/or academic certifications and titles (Guo et al., 2017; Shah et al., 2019).

## Demonstration Signals

Signals may also be expressed by an action or demonstration, which enables observers to experience some aspects of a provider as well as that of the product/service being demonstrated, which thereby reduces information asymmetry between the buyer and seller (Boleslavsky et al., 2017). Examples of demonstration signals include a seller communicating with potential buyers to convey information about themselves and their products/services before forming a new relationship (Kozlenkova et al., 2017); giving free samples to potential customers to allow them to experience the product before they buy it so that they can form opinions about the seller's capabilities in making high-quality products (Bawa & Shoemaker, 2004); delivering short wait times prior to service, which signals both service quality as well as the concern of the provider for the customer (Buell & Norton, 2011); and providing different ways for patients to access their health information and schedule doctors' appointments, signaling provider concern for patients as well as the ease of accessing medical services (Uhas et al., 2008).

Demonstration signals always convey information to a buyer on some aspects of both the provider and the product/service being demonstrated. Products being demonstrated through various means (e.g., in a store display at Best Buy, through hands-on interaction with products in Apple Store, by taking a free sample home) provide consumers not only with information on some aspects of the product, such as product features and quality, but also provide information on some aspects of the seller, such as their capabilities and values (Boleslavsky et al., 2017; Plummer et al., 2016). For example, a manager responding to negative client reviews on an online platform, especially those with specific concerns, demonstrates not only the service provider's willingness to address client issues but also provides more information about the specific service in question (Chevalier et al., 2018). Other high-profile examples of demonstration signals that demonstrate both product features and the capabilities of the seller include those of Boeing and SpaceX. Boeing not only demonstrated its ability to design state-of-the-art aircraft by successfully flying the “787-9 Dreamliner” at an airshow but also demonstrated the features of the new aircraft (Stewart, 2016). Similarly, SpaceX also demonstrated not only its innovation ability in designing and flying a crewed rocket by providing videos of its “Crew Dragon’s Demo-2” launch and docking on its website but also demonstrated the features of the new crewed Dragon rocket (SpaceX, 2010).

## Credibility of Description vs. Demonstration Signals

Relative to description signals, demonstration signals are generally more observable and costly and, therefore, more credible. Demonstration signals strongly and more clearly display the attributes, features, and quality of the product/service and the capabilities and technological expertise of the provider, and can thereby evoke stronger perceptions about the provider among potential consumers (Atefi et al., 2020; Doney & Cannon, 1997). In terms of signaling theory, this suggests that demonstration signals are generally superior and more effectual than description signals. For example, the “Pepsi Challenge” is a classic example illustrating the positive impact of a demonstration signal on perceptions of not only product quality but also of the ability of the manufacturer to make a “better” product. After decades of advertising (a description signal), Pepsi was finally able to take a significant market share away from then industry leader Coke by inviting people in public places to participate in blind taste tests (a demonstration signal) and then state their preference for Pepsi vs. Coke (Bhasin, 2011). Another example comes from online markets where a seller’s active managerial response to negative client reviews sends consumers a more credible demonstration signal that the seller actually does care about “consumer voice” than a description signal sent by way of simply stating on a website that “we truly care about our customers and value consumer voice” (Chevalier et al., 2018).

However, as discussed above, providers' description signals can also have high credibility when they are strengthened using certifications, endorsements, and validations, etc., given by third parties who are deemed trustworthy themselves. This happens because the validation of a provider's first-party description signal comes from an entity with less interest/potential gain in the transaction (Plummer et al., 2016; Spence, 1973), and because of a trust transference process, whereby trust is “transferred from one trusted ‘proof source’ to another person or group with which the trustor has little or no direct experience” (Doney & Cannon, 1997, p. 37). In summary, while demonstration signals are usually deemed more credible than description signals due to their high cost and observability, description signals can also have high credibility when they are bolstered using third parties, as we discuss below.

## Self-Description vs. Credible-Description Signals

Beyond differentiating between description and demonstration signals, we also divide description signals into two types in this research based on the notion of credibility: (1) self-description signals, and (2) credible-description signals. Self-description signals are described by the provider, but their credibility may not only be difficult to assess by potential customers but may also be deemed lower because the provider, as a first party, has an inherent interest in embellishing their own credentials as well as that of their products/services. In essence, these signals without third-party validation are like “pedestrian signals” (Plummer et al., 2016, p. 1586) in that they are more readily observable but are weaker and less credible signals because they are also less costly. On the other hand, credible-description signals are also described by the provider, but their credibility is easier to assess and thus deemed to be higher by potential customers because these signals have some third-party certification, endorsement, validation, etc., that can “unlock the potential" of the otherwise pedestrian signals that self-description signals are (Plummer et al., 2016, p. 1585). As discussed above, this happens due to a trust transference process whereby trust in the third party validating a first party's self-description signal is transferred to the first party and its self-description signals become a credible-description signal (Doney & Cannon, 1997).

Examples of self-description signals include disclosure of self-information (Bockstedt & Goh, 2011), product descriptions (Dimoka et al., 2012), product pictures (Li et al., 2009), product-focused advertising (Kihlstrom & Riordan, 1984), and new product announcements (Dehning et al., 2003). Examples of credible-description signals include trustmarks and ratings from trusted third-parties and consumer agencies (Aiken & Boush, 2006; Kopalle et al., 2017), endorsement by celebrities (Knittel & Stango, 2014), third-party payment or escrow services (Li et al., 2009), and certifications of technical skills (Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014). We now turn our attention to the research model and hypotheses.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Based on the signaling literature discussed above, in our research model, we conceptualize three different signals that predict demand for a counselor from new clients. Of these, two are description signals, one for the provider (i.e., the counselor) and the other for the service they provide (i.e., counseling service). Accordingly, we conceptualize professional qualifications of the counselor as a description signal about the provider's competence, and counseling style as a description signal about the features of the counseling service the counselor provides. Further, we also conceptualize a demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions. As discussed above, demonstration signals always convey information about both some aspect of the provider, such as their capabilities, intentions, values, etc., as well as some aspect of the product/service being demonstrated, such as product/service attributes, features, and quality, etc. (e.g., the Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner flight example discussed above). This is particularly true about the demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions in the counseling context, which we discuss in detail below when we discuss responsiveness as a construct and its effect on counselor demand from new clients. Accordingly, Figure 1 shows a categorization of these three signals in the counseling context. These three signals are more critical for new clients than for repeat clients, as they provide crucial information to new clients about both the counselor and their services, both through descriptions and demonstration, information that repeat clients do not need because they have firsthand experience with the counselor and their service (e.g., Dagger & Sweeney, 2007; Ho & Rai, 2017; Saifee et al., 2020). We discuss the research constructs and hypotheses next.

## Main Effects of Description and Demonstration Signals

## Professional Qualifications (PQ)

Providers of healthcare services invest significant resources in career preparation and enhancement (e.g., education, certification, engagement in professional activities). Professional qualifications (PQ) signal a counselor's technical competence, and counselors list their various credentials including education and certifications as a way to convey information about themselves and their ability to provide effective counseling services. However, counselors may list many credentials as part of their professional qualifications on an OMMH website, but only some of them may be deemed credible based on the cost incurred in obtaining that credential, as well as the credibility of the third-party institution that provided that credential. Accordingly, we distinguish between a PQ self-description signal and a PQ credible-description signal in our model. A PQ signal is associated with positive outcomes in different markets (e.g., Bockstedt & Goh, 2011; Feng & Xie, 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2017; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014), including healthcare markets (e.g., Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019). This signal is especially important for new clients because they need to form opinions about the counselor's competence, a need repeat clients don't have due to their firsthand experience with the counselor. New clients generally prefer well-trained healthcare providers who are knowledgeable and informed about relevant healthcare issues and who can provide an accurate and prompt diagnosis and treatment plan that addresses patient needs (Lewis et al., 2000). Even in non-healthcare environments, providers who list degrees and educational profiles affiliated with prestigious institutions tend to experience positive outcomes such as price premiums (Columbo et al., 2019). Further, healthcare providers in online environments who self-describe/list more professional qualifications are more likely to be recommended by existing patients (Khurana et al., 2019). Receiving badges and awards from third parties also signals technical competence in the community and is associated with better recommendations (Hann et al., 2013; Shah et al., 2019). In other words, both PQ self-description and PQ credible-description signals lead to better recommendations. Accordingly, we hypothesize the effects of both a self-description signal of PQ (H1a) and a credible-description signal of PQ (H1b) on demand for a counselor from new clients:

## Counseling Style (CS)

Across markets in general, descriptions of products or services is a common way to mitigate information asymmetry (Dimoka et al., 2012; Li et al., 2009). Specific to the OMMH context, a counselor conveys their personal counseling style (CS) as a description signal of their future counseling service through proper selection of CS keywords. This description signal reduces information asymmetry regarding the way in which a counselor delivers counseling to clients. This signal is especially important for new clients, as they need to form opinions about the way a counselor provides counseling, a need repeat clients don't have due to their firsthand experience with the counselor. The counseling literature has identified empathy and warmth as the central pillars of a “therapeutic alliance” between a counselor and a client, finding that regardless of any other counseling style, success in counseling hinges upon the counselor's display of empathy and warmth during therapy (Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019; Nienhuis et al., 2018). While a counselor may select many keywords to describe their CS given their particular orientation, given that empathy and warmth are central to counseling success, only keywords that reflect empathy and warmth constitute the CS signal in our study, as these foundational CS elements are the most likely to impact counselor demand from new clients. We note that empathy and warmth-related keywords selected by a counselor capture and convey the time, effort, and cost expended in acquiring the competencies of an empathetic and warm CS by the counselor, the various facets of which are conveyed by the selected keywords. In addition, using more keywords to describe an empathetic and warm counseling style make the CS signal more intense, stronger, clearer, more visible, and thereby more observable (Connelly et al., 2011, p. 52) by covering the various facets of empathetic and warm competencies.

Similar to PQ, we distinguish between CS self-description and CS credible-description signals in our model. Mere self-description of counseling style by way of selected keywords, i.e., CS self-description, is a weak signal with low credibility because both the cost and observability of that signal are low. However, the credibility of this signal can be strengthened by incorporating in the self-description signal third-party client reviews about the counselor's display of empathy and warmth during therapy sessions, which invokes the CS credible-description signal. Given that empathy and warmth are key for success in the counseling domain, both the CS self-description signal (H2a) and the CS credible-description signal (H2b) are likely to lead to demand for the counselor from new clients. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H2a: A counselor's self-description signal of counseling style is positively associated with demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

H2a: A counselor's credible-description signal of counseling style is positively associated with demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

## Responsiveness to Client Questions

The last theoretical construct in our model, responsiveness to client questions, is a demonstration signal that pertains to both the provider and the service. Responding to patient questions in an online market such as an OMMH is a time-consuming and resource-intensive endeavor that takes significant effort (Deng et al., 2019; Khurana et al., 2019). Therefore, given its high observability and high cost, responsiveness to client questions is a high-credibility demonstration signal in an OMMH. Further, effort in a service context is viewed as displaying empathy and warmth (Leung et al., 2020). Potential new clients are thus likely to view a counselor with higher responsiveness as exhibiting higher empathy and warmth, which are the key ingredients for a successful counseling service, as noted above. This assessment of the empathy and warmth of a counselor through their responsiveness is critical for new clients but it is not as important for repeat clients who have experienced the counselor's empathy and warmth firsthand. Further, the counseling literature widely recognizes empathy and warmth as core competencies that counselors need to develop as part of their academic and professional training (Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019; Nienhuis et al., 2018). Finally, a recent study in the healthcare arena showed that “high-expertise doctors choose to write online responses, and low-expertise doctors choose not to write online responses” (Khurana et al., 2019, p. 875). Therefore, while demonstration signals always provide information on some aspects of both the provider and the product/service, the above findings provide further validation that responsiveness to client questions in an OMMH is a demonstration signal of both: (1) a counselor’s professional qualifications and competence as an empathetic and warm counselor, and (2) the counselor’s empathetic and warm counseling style. Given that empathy and warmth are fundamental to achieving good counseling outcomes (Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019; Nienhuis et al., 2018), highly responsive counselors are more likely to attract new clients. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H3: A counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions is positively associated with demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

## Joint Effects of Description and Demonstration Signals

Drawing upon the notion of the relative credibility of signals (Price & Dawar, 2002, p. 166), also referred to as the “relative effectiveness” of the seller’s attempt to signal trustworthiness (Aiken & Boush, 2006, p. 309), we posit that relative credibility is at the heart of the joint effect of a description and a demonstration signal and that it determines whether a joint effect would be complementary or substitutive. The signaling literature clearly notes that different signals have different levels of credibility due to their cost and levels of observability (Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973), and they are not always equally valuable or useful (Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014). Further, signal receivers give different weights to different signals based on their credibility when considering them together in their decision-making (Price & Dawar, 2002). Based on these notions, we posit that when two signals are present, the signal with higher credibility will substitute for the signal with lower credibility. On the other hand, the two signals will complement each other when both are highly credible signals.

Essentially, when a lower-credibility signal and a higher-credibility signal co-occur, signal receivers compare their relative credibility and accord a higher weight to the higher-credibility signal in their decision-making. As a result, the impact of the lower-credibility signal on the receiver's decision is weakened, leading to a substitutive joint effect. This logic regarding substitution effects has been discussed and investigated in prior empirical studies in the IS, economics, and marketing literatures (Higgins et al., 2011; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Price & Dawar, 2002). Accordingly, we expect a self-description signal, which has lower credibility, to be substituted by a demonstration signal, which has higher credibility, as discussed in earlier sections.

On the other hand, when a second highly credible signal co-occurs in the presence of the first highly credible signal, the newly appearing signal provides additional credible information and thereby further validates and enhances the credibility of the first signal. As a result, the two credible signals together substantially reduce the signal receiver's uncertainty in decision-making and thereby serve as complements in their effects on the receiver's decision. This logic for complementarity effects has been discussed and investigated in prior empirical studies in the IS, marketing, and strategic management literatures (Bapna, 2019; Plummer et al., 2016; Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014). Accordingly, we expect a credible-description signal, which has higher credibility, to be complemented by a demonstration signal, which also has higher credibility, as discussed in earlier sections. We now turn to specific joint effect hypotheses.

## Joint Effects of Professional Qualifications and Responsiveness

As noted before, professional qualifications listed as self-descriptions on a counselor's profile can include various credentials and professional titles (e.g., degrees, diplomas, certifications, and memberships of prestigious associations), which may have different levels of credibility. Some of these credentials and titles may incur more cost and effort to obtain than others, and some may be from more well-known third-party institutions and credentialing bodies than others. As a result, this self-description signal of PQ is a weaker and less credible signal of professional qualifications, and potential clients cannot readily determine with a high degree of certainty whether the stated technical competencies will translate into a successful counseling outcome or a strong counselor-client relationship. The demonstration signal of responsiveness, on the other hand, is more diagnostic and provides the opportunity for potential clients to directly observe counselors' actual performance in handling clients' issues as well as their conspicuous efforts and empathetic and warm behaviors (Khurana et al., 2019; Leung et al., 2020). As a result, potential clients will likely consider responsiveness to be a more credible signal of the technical competence of the counselor, which is rooted in empathy and warmth, than the self-descriptions of a counselor's PQ. Given the general discussion on joint effects above, potential clients are likely to thereby give responsiveness, which is a higher-credibility signal, more weight in their decision-making on purchasing the counselor's service than PQ self-description, which is a lower-credibility signal (Price & Dawar, 2002). Accordingly, consistent with other findings (Higgins et al., 2011; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Price & Dawar, 2002), we hypothesize that the responsiveness signal will substitute for the PQ self-description signal:

H4a: A counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions negatively moderates the positive relationship between the counselor's self-description of professional qualifications and the demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

However, the joint effects of PQ and responsiveness will be different if we consider only those credentials and titles that are deemed credible because they either incur higher effort and cost to obtain and/or they are from well-known third-party institutions and credentialing bodies. As discussed earlier, due to the phenomenon of trust transference, credentials and titles from credible and trusted third-party institutions are deemed more credible (Doney & Cannon, 1997). As a result, the credible demonstration signal of responsiveness provides additional evidence about the technical competence of a counselor, resulting in complementary effects that enhance the effects of each other in the decision-making process of potential clients (Price & Dawar, 2002). Accordingly, consistent with other findings (Bapna, 2019; Plummer et al., 2016; Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014), we hypothesize that the responsiveness signal will complement the PQ credible-description signal:

H4b: A counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions positively moderates the positive relationship between the counselor's credible description of professional qualifications and the demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

Joint effects of counseling style and responsiveness: Next, we consider how counselor responsiveness interacts with counseling style description to affect the demand for a counselor from new clients. Counseling style keywords selected by the counselors themselves that denote empathy and warmth, which are the central pillars of a successful therapeutic alliance, describe their approach to counseling. As discussed above, these CS keywords are important for potential clients because they convey important information about the counselor's counseling style in any potential future counseling service. However, self-describing oneself as warm and empathetic does not make one warm and empathetic, and CS self-description is therefore an inherently weak signal with low credibility because it has low cost and observability. But whether or not a counselor is empathetic and warm could be easily assessed from the counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions. As discussed before, responding to client questions involves significant time and effort (Deng et al., 2019; Khurana et al., 2019), and effort in a service context is viewed as displaying empathy and warmth (Leung et al., 2020). As a result, potential clients will consider responsiveness as a more credible signal of the counseling style of the counselor than the self-descriptions of the counseling style. Given the general discussion on joint effects above, potential clients are likely to thereby give responsiveness, which is a higher-credibility signal, more weight in their decision-making on purchasing the counselor's service than CS self-description, which is a lower-credibility signal (Price & Dawar, 2002). Accordingly, consistent with other findings (Higgins et al., 2011; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Price & Dawar, 2002), we hypothesize that the responsiveness signal will substitute for the CS self-description signal:

H5a: A counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions negatively moderates the positive relationship between the counselor's self-description of counseling style and the demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

Finally, the joint effects of CS and responsiveness are thus likely to be different if we consider a credible signal of counseling style that factors in client evaluations of the empathy and warmth actually displayed by a counselor during counseling sessions rather than considering a signal of counseling style that is based just on the self-described CS keywords denoting empathy and warmth. Prior studies have also shown that client feedback is usually more credible in e-commerce contexts (Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006) and health services contexts (Lu & Rui, 2018). As discussed earlier, due to the phenomenon of trust transference, incorporating actual client feedback about empathy and warmth in one's self-described counseling style will make the CS signal more credible (Doney & Cannon, 1997). In this case, the credible demonstration signal of responsiveness will provide additional evidence about the empathetic and warm counseling style of the counselor, resulting in complementary effects that enhance the effects of each other in the decision-making process of potential clients (Price & Dawar, 2002). Accordingly, consistent with other findings (Bapna, 2019; Plummer et al., 2016; Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014), we hypothesize that responsiveness will complement the CS credible-description signal:

H5b: A counselor's demonstration signal of responsiveness to client questions positively moderates the positive relationship between the counselor's credible-description of counseling style and the demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH.

## Methods

## Main Data Sample

We collected data from an OMMH in China, YiXinLi (www.xinli001.com). Established in July 2011, YiXinLi is a pioneer in China's online psychological counseling market. YiXinLi is an OMMH with more than 20 million registered members, as of 2019, and more than 6000 counselors listed, as of market entry in 2011. It is one of the biggest online counseling markets in China and has already received four rounds of venture capital investment. Mental health counselors apply to be listed on the YiXinLi OMMH, which conducts an elaborate and rigorous verification of each of their educational and professional credentials, professional experience, etc., prior to listing them on their website. Following successful verification of their credentials/experience and after they pass a professional interview, counselors can set up their profile webpage that displays their various professional information, counseling services, etc., and advertise their counseling services on this website (see Appendix for an example of counselor profile webpage on the YiXinLi website and https://www.xinli001.com/info/100352793). Using this information, clients can choose particular counselors to make appointments with them. Clients also have the option to post an open question to the forum (for anyone to answer, including other clients), as well as the option to directly ask questions of a specific counselor.

This OMMH automatically records for each counselor and displays on their individual profile webpages the total number of unique clients, the total number of completed client appointments, the total number of clients providing feedback on them, and the total number of client questions they have answered to date. Because these numerical values are automatically calculated by the OMMH, they represent third-party verified information. The textual content of recent client feedback and recent counselor responses to client questions are also available on counselors' profile webpages that clients and potential clients can view by clicking on related hyperlinks on counselor webpages. Clients can also request a paid appointment with a counselor via the online appointment system. Paid counseling services offered on YiXinLi include voice, video, and face-to-face (i.e., in person) counseling.

We wrote a web crawler program to scrape data from the OMMH and crawled the website during three periods—in September 2017, December 2017, and April 2018—with the official approval of the website. The time window between successive data collection periods was kept at four months based on information from the American Psychological

Association and from industrial practical experience in China regarding the average length of treatment for counseling. $^{2}$ We combined the three datasets using counselors' unique IDs on the website, which resulted in an unbalanced panel dataset consisting of a total of 827 observations on 309 unique counselors; 229 of those counselors had data in all the three time periods. In terms of specific time periods, our dataset consists of 230 counselors from September 2017, 289 counselors from December 2017, and 309 counselors from April 2018.

## Data Sample for Addressing Selection Bias

To address concerns of selection bias in our analyses, we also collected data from counselors who were not listed on the YiXinLi website but who were either listed on counseling websites other than YiXinLi or were affiliated with a professional association for psychology, the Henan Province Association for Psychology. We collected data via a survey (n = 158 counselors) and also collected data on counselors listed on websites other than YiXinLi (n = 108 counselors). We collected data from these counselors on gender (0 = male, 1 = female; $\bar{X} = 0.91$ , SD = 0.94), city (0 = small, 1 = medium, 2 = large; $\bar{X} = 0.84$ , SD = 0.37), and price ( $\bar{X} = 395.53$ , SD = 327.01). This second dataset thus consisted of 266 counselors and was used in our analyses to address selection bias, discussed below.

## Variables and Measurement

We operationalized 23 variables from data available on counselor profile webpages in the OMMH (see Appendix). Our dependent and independent variables reflect the perspective of two different parties with the independent variables capturing the perspective of counselors (signals sent by them about their capabilities and service quality), while the dependent variable captures the perspective of the clients served by the counselor. Accordingly, our data do not suffer from common method bias, which arises from the same respondent providing data on both independent and dependent variables (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

## Dependent Variable: Number of New Clients

Our dependent variable (DV) is the number of new clients of a mental health counselor, which is operationalized using the number of unique clients (UC) for the counselor to capture the demand for the counselor from new clients. This value is automatically calculated and updated by the OMMH website when a new unique client makes their first appointment with the counselor ( $\overline{X} = 113.40$ , SD = 256.94), and is shown on counselor list pages. The OMMH website lists 20 counselors on each page, along with some important information about each counselor, including their number of unique clients. $^{3}$

## Independent Variables: Counselor Signals

The three types of counselor signals of interest in this study are professional qualifications, counseling style, and responsiveness to client questions. Below, we describe each of these variables in detail and address the underlying notion of signal credibility.

Professional qualifications: Consistent with the methods used in literatures on online healthcare markets (Khurana et al., 2019), psychology and mental health (APA, 2011), and scientometrics (Judge et al., 2007), and in consonance with our research model, we measured two signals of professional qualifications (PQ)—the PQ self-description signal (PQ-SD) and the PQ credible-description signal (PQ-CD). First, we operationalized PQ-SD ( $\bar{X} = 1.54$ , $SD = 0.97$ ) by counting all the professional qualifications listed by a counselor as degrees, diplomas, certifications, memberships, fellowships, titles, etc. (credentials hereafter) on their profile page with equal weights. $^{4}$ While the OMMH website verified these credentials during the initial verification process described above, all the credentials do not have the same degree of credibility, as discussed in the theory section. We therefore operationalized PQ-CD ( $\bar{X} = 0.66$ , $SD = 0.72$ ), where we counted only those credentials that require significant cost and effort to obtain or that indicate memberships in reputable professional organizations. $^{5}$ Specifically, credentials included in PQ-CD pertain to educational degrees and diplomas, national counselor certification (Levels 1, 2 and 3), and membership in CAPA or DCAP. $^{6}$ Low-credibility credentials listed by counselors, such as senior counselor, columnist, etc., were counted for a new control variable PQ-others, which is essentially the difference between PQ-SD and PQ-CD.

Counseling style: Counselors on the YiXinLi website have the option to describe their counseling style (CS) using up to 20 keywords, such as warm, calm, patient, gentle, receptive, easygoing, firm, brisk, clear, concise, steady, etc. Similar to the PQ measure and consistent with our research model, we measured two CS signals—the CS self-description signal (CS-SD) and the CS credible-description signal (CS-CD). First, we operationalized CS-SD as the count of CS keywords that includes only empathy- and warmth-related keywords, given that these are the core pillars of counseling (Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019; Nienhuis et al., 2018). We used four reputed English-Chinese dictionaries/translators $^{7}$ to translate the terms empathy and warmth into Chinese words. We then used these Chinese terms for empathy and warmth as root words and identified all their synonyms from the HIT synonyms dictionary $^{8}$ and created a custom dictionary of Chinese keywords that include empathy and warmth, as well as all their synonyms found in the HIT dictionary. Using the Chinese version of LIWC 2015 (Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010), for each counselor, we then counted the number of CS keywords that matched the words in the custom Chinese empathy/warmth dictionary. This count represents CS-SD ( $\bar{X}=0.89$ , SD=0.83) as it is the count of a counselor’s self-described empathy and warmth CS keywords. However, these self-described CS empathy/warmth-related keywords are chosen directly by the counselors and lack any third-party verification or credibility. Therefore, we used the content of client feedback $^{9}$ on counselors to create a credible CS-CD measure.

Following existing work in the IS literature (e.g., Chen et al., 2019; Khurana et al., 2019), we combined text mining and sentiment analysis to analyze the content of client feedback using the above-mentioned empathy/warmth dictionary. We first identified client reviews with positive sentiment $^{10}$ using the Baidu Senta text analysis system (Gao et al., 2018) and then counted the total number of words in all the client reviews for a counselor that matched keywords in the empathy/warmth dictionary. The Baidu Senta system provides the probability of both the positive and negative sentiment of a given text, with the sum of the two probability scores equal to one. We characterized sentiment as a categorical variable with a value of 0 for negative sentiment (probability of negative sentiment $\geqslant 0.6$ ), a value of 1 for neutral sentiment (0.4 < probability of negative sentiment <

0.6), and a value of 2 for positive sentiment (probability of negative sentiment $\leqslant 0.4$ ). After adjusting for exposure (i.e., dividing the number of matched keywords by the number of days the counselor had been on the OMMH), we calculated the percentile rank for each counselor in that time period using the count of matched empathy/warmth keywords in client reviews. We then used this value to create a weighted count of CS keywords calculated as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Weighted Count of CS Keywords} \\ = \text {Count of CS keywords} ^ {*} (1 + \text {PercentileRank} _ {\text {CS match,t}}) \end{array}
$$

This weighted variable represents CS-CD ( $\bar{X} = 1.30$ , SD = 1.27) for a counselor, as it represents the degree to which empathy and warmth were reflected in all client feedback notes for that counselor with positive sentiment during a specific time period, relative to the degree of match of the warmth and empathy keywords of all other counselors in that time period. This results in a credible CS signal as it incorporates a third-party, client perspective on whether a counselor is truly empathetic and warm in therapy sessions.

Counselor responsiveness: As noted earlier, clients may ask questions in the OMMH and any counselor can choose to answer them. A counselor choosing to answer one or more client questions is expending significant effort in building relationships with current and future clients. The OMMH doesn't (yet) generate a response quality metric for counselors based on their responses to client questions. However, it keeps track of all responses to client questions and automatically quantifies and updates each counselor's profile webpages with their response quantity, i.e., the total number of client questions answered by them, which is readily visible at the top of their profile webpage. Further, the duration between a client question and a counselor's responses to that question is a crucial indicator of the counselor's timeliness and diligence in addressing client questions. Usually, a shorter duration indicates that a counselor not only devotes time and effort in responding to client questions but does so in a quick and timely manner; it thereby creates a strong perception of empathy and warmth on the part of the counselor (Leung et al., 2020). We therefore use the count of responses provided by a counselor in the first two days of a question being posted by a client as a measure of counselor responsiveness to client questions $^{11}$ (Zhang et al., 2022). Therefore, our objective measure of responsiveness in terms of response quantity in the first two days ( $\bar{X} = 397.53$ , SD = 1160.62) serves as a demonstration signal of both a counselor's capability of being empathetic and warm and their counseling style of extending empathy and warmth during therapy, which requires little interpretive effort by clients.

## Control Variables

We included 16 control variables in our analyses. The OMMH website provides nine types of counseling specialties, $^{12}$ and a counselor can choose up to three of them. To address potential homogeneity by specialty, we treat the nine specialty areas as control variables. Prior studies have shown that the price of online products influences transaction volumes (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Huang et al., 2014), that physical distance remains a psychological factor in online purchasing behavior (Edwards et al., 2009), and that people tend to have gender preference when selecting a health care counselor (Fennema et al., 1990). Therefore, we controlled for the effects of the counselor's type of city (2 = large-sized city; 1 = mid-sized city; 0 = small-sized city; based on the current classification criterion in ChinaDaily, 2017); gender (0 = male; 1 = female); and the average price of counseling services (across listed media of voice counseling, video services, and face-to-face services, price per session, usually lasting one hour). In addition, we controlled for the number of articles and the number of books published listed on the profile webpage, as these variables also serve as a reputation-enhancing signal. Following literature in the e-commerce (Lin et al., 2017) and online communities (Moqri et al., 2018) arenas, the number of days $^{13}$ to the data collection date (for a particular time period) since a counselor first became active and/or present on the OMMH website was used as an exposure variable in our count data models. Finally, we included a time period variable in our models to control for unobserved time effects in our panel data. Descriptive statistics and correlations are presented in Table 1.

## Analysis and Results

## Analytical Models

All analyses were performed using Stata version 16. Our DV was measured as the number of unique clients (UC) of a counselor, which is a count variable. Accordingly, we used count data models for our analysis, choosing the most appropriate count data model for our analyses after systematic inspection of the data. We started with a panel-data Poisson model, but the results showed that the DV was significantly conditionally overdispersed (overdispersion parameter $\alpha = 2.95$ , p = 0.00 for the self-description model; $\alpha = 2.85$ , p = 0.00 for the credible description model), indicating that a Poisson model is not appropriate and that a negative binomial model may be an appropriate model for our analysis (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013). However, conditional overdispersion is often due to an excess number of observations with a value of zero on the DV, which results from a separate data-generating process that leads to degenerate, structural zeros on the DV (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013). In such cases, the use of a zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) model is warranted, as it assesses and corrects for these excess zeros while predicting the outcome of interest (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013; Du et al., 2011). Further inspection of our data showed a high number of zeros on the DV with 103 of 823 observations, or 12.52% observations, being zero-valued. We posit that the activity of counselors may be a separate data-generating process, with inactivity on the part of the counselor leading to structural zero values, while signals from counselors constitute the main data-generating process for the outcome variable of interest and lead to other zero and non-zero values on appointments. Accordingly, we followed econometric modeling guidelines and estimated the number of unique clients as the DV using a ZINB model with the vce (cluster panel-id) option (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013). The general form of the ZINB model that we tested is shown below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\xi_ {j} ^ {\beta} = \pmb {x} _ {j} \pmb {\beta} + o f f s e t _ {j} ^ {\beta}; \xi_ {j} ^ {\gamma} = \pmb {z} _ {j} \pmb {\gamma} + o f f s e t _ {j} ^ {\gamma}; \mu_ {j}} \\ & {\qquad = \exp \big (\xi_ {j} ^ {\beta} \big); p _ {j} = \frac {1}{1 + \alpha \mu_ {j}}; m = \frac {1}{\alpha},} \end{array}
$$

where $\xi_{j}^{\beta}$ represents the DV for the main model for the $j^{th}$ observation; $\xi_{j}^{\gamma}$ represents the DV for the inflate model for the $j^{th}$ observation; $x_{j}$ represents a vector of predictors for the $j^{th}$ observation for the main model; $z_{j}$ represents a vector of predictors for the $j^{th}$ observation for the inflate model; $\beta$ represents a vector of parameters for the main model predictors; $\gamma$ represents a vector of parameters for the inflate model predictors; $\mu_{j}$ is the intensity parameter for the $j^{th}$ observation; and, $\alpha$ is the dispersion parameter and captures the over- and underdispersion in the DV.

For the ZINB model, we tested the first-stage inflation model using a logit model predicted by the number of books written by a counselor. This variable is an indicator of counselors' professional activity that likely influences whether or not a client would request an appointment from a counselor, and can accordingly constitute the data-generating process for structural zeros on the DV (Koocher & Keith-Spiegel, 2008). Results from the ZINB models are discussed next.

## Results of the ZINB Models

The main variables in our models are standardized scores such that values associated with the main and interaction effects are comparable; all other variables in the model are raw values. All results reflect robust standard errors. Results from our main analyses using the ZINB model for the self-description signals model and the credible-description signals model are provided under Models 1 and 4, respectively, in Table 2. The number of books written by a counselor is significant in the inflation model for both these models indicating that activity by a counselor is a separate process that predicts if the counselor will have any clients (i.e., structural zeros).

As shown in the self-description signals model (Model 1), the coefficients for the five hypothesized effects in the ZINB self-description model are significant at the 0.01 level supporting H1a, H2a, H3, H4a, and H5a. The IRRs for the various terms in the ZINB model indicate that the rate of new clients over the base rate of already acquired clients increases at 110.6%, 27.6%, and 119.8% for a 1σ increase in signals of professional qualifications, counseling style, and responsiveness, respectively. Further, a 1σ increase in the signal of responsiveness reduced the rate of getting new clients over the base rate by 38.2% and 25.5% for signals of professional qualifications and counseling style, respectively.

As shown in the credible-description signals model (Model 4), the coefficients for the five hypothesized effects in the ZINB credible-description model are all significant at the 0.01 level, but the direction of H5b is negative (shown in red) and opposite to the proposed positive moderating relationship, supporting H1b, H2b, H3, and H4b, but not H5b. The IRRs for the various terms in the ZINB model indicate that the rate of new clients over the base rate of already acquired clients increases at 73.3%, 47.2%, and 135.8% for a 1σ increase in signals of professional qualifications, counseling style, and responsiveness, respectively. Further, a 1σ increase in the signal of responsiveness increased and reduced the rate of getting new clients over the base rate by 36.7% and 47.4% for signals of professional qualifications and counseling style, respectively.

We plotted these two interaction effects in ZINB models using Stata's marginscontplot command as the moderator (responsiveness) is a continuous variable (Royston, 2013). We plotted the interaction effects for low and high responsiveness at z-score values of -0.25 and 1.00, respectively, and not with z-score values of -1.00 and +1.00, as the responsiveness variable was highly skewed and exhibited a Pareto type of distribution with a minimum raw value of 0 corresponding to a -0.35 z-score value. The plots shown in Figures 3a and 3b for the main self-description signals model (Model 1 in Table 2) confirm graphically that responsiveness negatively moderates the effects of professional qualifications and counseling style on the counselor's number of unique clients, respectively, as hypothesized. Further, the plot shown in Figure 3c for the main credible-description signals model (Model 4 in Table 2) graphically confirms that responsiveness positively moderates the effect of professional qualifications on the counselor's number of unique clients, as hypothesized. Finally, the plot shown in Figure 3d for the main credible-description signals model (Model 4 in Table 2) shows graphically that responsiveness negatively moderates the effect of counseling style on counselors' unique clients, which is contrary to hypothesis H5b but consistent with the results of Model 4 in Table 2.

## Robustness Tests

We performed several additional analyses to ensure that our results were robust across different measures $^{14}$ and after accounting for different sources of potential bias. Below we discuss two important robustness tests related to (1) the simultaneity of responsiveness and demand for counselors from new clients, and (2) selection bias.

## Addressing Simultaneity of Responsiveness and Demand for the Counselor

The issue of simultaneity may be a concern in our model because counselor responsiveness may be driving the demand from new clients at the same time that counselors in higher demand may be asked more questions and thus be more responsive. To address this concern, we ran one-period forward lead models for our DV using a ZINB model. ## Addressing Selection Bias

The data in our study are subject to issues related to endogenous switching, and we address two types of selection bias in this section: (1) potential selection bias from observable covariates using propensity score matching (PSM), and (2) potential selection bias from unobservable covariates using a two-stage method of the moments (TSM).

Addressing selection bias from observable covariates using propensity score matching (PSM): Using data collected from counselors not on the YiXinLi website $n = 266$ and Rosenbaum and Rubin's (1983) PSM approach that was also recently used in a healthcare-related study in the IS literature (Khurana et al., 2019), we identified counselors from our main YiXinLi sample (treatment group) who were comparable to counselors not on the YiXinLi platform (control group) (Table 3a).

We performed this matching with propensity scores calculated using gender, city, and price as covariates, and using a nearest-neighbor matching algorithm (with n=6 neighbors, equivalent to an n=2 neighbors approach $^{15}$ ) that included sampling with replacement and random ordering.

Addressing selection bias from unobservable covariates using a two-stage method of moments (TSM) approach: For addressing potential endogenous sample selection bias from unobservable covariates, we conducted an analysis following the approach of a two-stage method of moments (TSM) estimator, which is “a nonlinear least-square analog to the popular Heckman (1976, 1979) estimator” (Terza, 1998, p. 129). This approach is also recommended by other econometricians (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013) for addressing endogenous switching issues in count data models, including endogenous sample selection bias. This approach involves first estimating a probit equation for the endogenous switching variable and then applying a “correction term” similar to that of Heckman’s (1976, 1979) inverse Mills ratio in the estimation of the count outcome variable using a nonlinear least squares (NLS) equation. This correction term, $\psi(\theta,\alpha)$ , reduces to the equation shown below for the endogenous sample selection case, where outcome y is not observed when d=0 (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013; Terza, 1998):

$$
\psi (\theta , \alpha) = d \frac {\phi (\theta + z \alpha)}{\phi (z \alpha)},
$$

where d (binary switching variable) = 1 is observed iff $z\alpha + \nu > 0$ ; z is a $1xK_{2}$ vector of 0 otherwise observable exogenous variables in the participation equation; $\alpha$ is a corresponding $K_{2}x1$ vector of unknown parameters; v is a random term; $\phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function; $\theta$ is $\sigma\rho$ ; $\sigma$ is the SD of endogenous variable $\epsilon_{d}$ in the main equation; and $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient between endogenous variable $\epsilon_{d}$ and random error term v.

We chose city as the instrumental variable for the first-stage probit estimation as it meets both conditions for qualifying as a good instrumental variable: instrument exogeneity and instrument relevance (Wooldridge, 2016). First, city exhibits instrument exogeneity, as it is not expected to correlate with demand for a counselor from new clients, the DV in our study.

Clients living in larger cities but originally from smaller cities may in fact desire a counselor from a smaller city who shares a similar cultural and socioeconomic background as the client $^{16}$ (Kim et al., 2005). Further, clients' choice of a counselor is more likely influenced by the stigma associated with mental health problems and the appropriateness of the counselor (e.g., their specialties) rather than where a counselor lives (Sartorius, 2007). Our results also show no significant effects of city size on the demand for a counselor in any of our regression models, providing evidence that city indeed exhibits instrument exogeneity in our study. Second, city is also a good instrument from the perspective of instrument relevance. Counselors in larger cities throughout China have greater access to internet connections (Thomala, 2020) and thus greater access to an OMMH. Further, eHealth behavior is also associated with urban residence (Hong & Zhou, 2018), and patrons of online counseling are more likely to be from larger cities (CBNData, 2018), implying that counselors from larger cities are more likely to turn to online platforms and services for business. Our results from the two-stage model discussed next show strong correlation between city and the selection of the YiXinLi platform by counselors, providing evidence that city also exhibits instrument relevance in our study.

The results of this estimation are similar to our main results confirming that city did not affect our main results in any significant way. $$
E [ y | w, d ] = \delta (\beta^ {*}) \psi (\theta , \alpha)
$$

where y is the outcome variable (unique clients); w is a $1 \times K_{1}$ vector of x and z variables not including the switching d variable; x is $1 \times K_{3}$ vector of observable variables in the main outcome equation; $\delta$ is $exp(x\beta^{*})$ ; $\beta^{*}$ is the same as $\beta$ except for its first element which is shifted by $\sigma^{2}/2$ ; $\beta$ is a corresponding $K_{3} \times 1$ vector of unknown parameters; and z, d, and $\sigma$ are as defined above.

## Discussion

The results from this study provide generally strong and robust support for our hypotheses with 9 of 10 hypotheses finding full support across different models tested in this study. Only one of the hypotheses pertaining to the interaction between credible-description counseling style (CS-CD) and responsiveness (Resp) shows a significant substitution instead of the hypothesized complementary effect. A potential explanation for this anomalous finding is that potential new clients of a counselor do not find the feedback from prior clients of the counselor, which was used to create the CS-CD measure as discussed earlier, to be reliable that can lend credibility to the self-described empathetic and warm counseling style of the counselor. As a result, CS-CD remains a low-credibility signal which is then substituted by the high-credibility responsiveness signal in consonance with the fundamental notion that the substitution and complementarity of two signals is based on the relative credibility of those signals. This explanation is, in fact, consistent with recent research in the general healthcare and counseling domains. A recent study showed that online reviews of physicians do not reliably predict their patients' clinical outcomes and concluded that online reviews may not be helpful in the context of chronic diseases, which exhibit credence aspects in which “quality cannot be ascertained even after the purchase and direct personal experience” (Saifee et al., 2020, p. 1282).

On the other hand, a recent study of online ratings of cardiac surgeons showed that patient ratings indeed predicted physician quality measured in terms of clinical outcomes (Lu & Rui, 2018). Taken together, these findings suggest that while patient reviews and ratings may be useful for healthcare services where outcomes and quality are readily observable, such as cardiac surgery, they may not be reliable for mental health counseling services, which are like chronic diseases and exhibit a high level of credence aspects. Accordingly, our conjecture that client feedback does not create a credible signal of the counseling style of a counselor, resulting in the CS-CD signal also being substituted by the more credible responsiveness signal, is fully consistent with findings about patient online reviews in the context of chronic diseases (Saifee et al., 2020). We now turn to the contributions and implications of this study and then conclude by noting the limitations of this study.

## Theoretical Contributions

This paper makes a unique and significant contribution to the growing body of literature on signaling in online markets. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the IS literature that provides novel and nuanced findings about signaling in the context of an OMMH, which is significantly different from other online markets for general healthcare. Through our research, we shed light on how different description and demonstration signals with different levels of credibility about counselors' competence and their counseling services interact differently with each other and in turn affect new clients' choice of counselors in these unique markets. We thereby extend the literature on signaling in online markets in general (e.g., Bockstedt & Goh, 2011; Feng & Xie, 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2017; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014) and in online healthcare markets in particular (e.g., Guo et al., 2017; Khurana et al., 2019). We advance our understanding of signaling in online markets in one major and two subsidiary ways.

First, and most importantly, we posit and demonstrate the relative credibility of signals (Price & Dawar, 2002, p. 166) as the boundary condition that determines whether the demonstration signal of a counselor's responsiveness will substitute for or complement the two description signals of professional qualifications and counseling style in predicting market demand for the counselor from new clients in an OMMH. We first conceptualize description signals versus demonstration signals, based on the distinction between characteristic and action signals (Plummer et al., 2016) and between announcement and action signals (Prabhu & Stewart, 2001). Description signals allow observers to gain information about a provider and their products/services by observing images, reading statements/narratives, and viewing announcements/advertisements provided by a seller. Demonstration signals, on the other hand, enable observers to experience some aspects of a provider and their products/services and generally have higher credibility than description signals due to their higher costs and observability (Connelly et al., 2011; Price & Dawar, 2002; Spence, 1973). We also divide the description signals into self-description and credible-description signals because different description signals can also have different levels of credibility, depending on their cost and observability. Our results confirm that responsiveness substitutes for the impacts of the two self-description signals of professional qualifications and counseling style on demand for a counselor from new clients because consumers give more weight to the more credible signal of responsiveness in their decision-making, similar to other findings in the literature (Higgins et al., 2011; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Price & Dawar, 2002). Our results also show that responsiveness complements the credible-description signal of professional qualification, as both further validate and enhance the credibility of the other signal, similar to other findings in the literature (Bapna, 2019; Plummer et al., 2016; Price & Dawar, 2002; Stern et al., 2014).

Second, our findings show that the credible-description signal of counseling style (CS-CD) is substituted rather than complemented by the demonstration signal of responsiveness in its effect on demand from new clients for a counselor. As discussed above, the CS-CD signal was developed using client feedback on the extent to which a counselor exhibits empathy and warmth in their interaction with clients and, accordingly, deemed to be a high credibility signal. Therefore, this finding is contrary to the fundamental notion that two high-credibility signals will complement each other. However, as discussed above, this finding is consistent with recent literature which finds that patient online reviews about physicians do not reliably predict their patients' clinical outcomes in the context of chronic diseases, which exhibit credence aspects (Saifee et al., 2020), and adds to the insights about the value of client feedback for evaluating the quality of providers, especially in healthcare services with high levels of credence aspects.

Finally, our results show that responsiveness to client questions is the most influential predictor of market demand from new clients in an OMMH. Responsiveness demonstrably conveys provider cost and effort and thereby conveys provider empathy and warmth (Leung et al., 2020), which are not only central pillars in counseling but are also essential to other dyadic personal service contexts. Accordingly, this finding adds not only to the literature on online healthcare markets (e.g., Khurana et al., 2019) but to the emerging literature on provider responsiveness in online markets as well (e.g., Chevalier et al., 2018).

## Practical Implications

There are two practical implications stemming from our work. Related to the above point that responsiveness signals empathy and warmth, we consider this important for counselors (and healthcare providers in general) to take into consideration when developing a presence in online markets such as OMMHs. In addition, the website's decision to enumerate and publish this information seems to be an effective design choice for the OMMH because it makes it easier for clients to interpret and incorporate this signal into their decision-making.

Our findings also raise issues related to treatment efficacy in OMMHs. There is no doubt that these types of markets offer increased efficiency. However, many clients voice concerns about the quality of online counseling, and clients tend not to be informed about the quality standards of online counseling (Apolinário-Hagen et al., 2018). Our findings do confirm that counselor responsiveness in an OMMH drives demand to the point of diminishing the effects of the technical competence of counselors, leading to clients potentially choosing a technically inferior counselor. Clients should, therefore, be advised by OMMH managers to choose counselors carefully, considering not only their responsiveness but their technical competence and counseling style as well in order to receive the best possible counseling service.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Our investigation is not without limitations. First, Chinese culture fosters a large power distance and the interaction style between doctors and patients in China might be different from that in other countries. At the same time, regardless of country or culture, counseling dyads tend to give more power to the counselor, relative to the client, hence the importance of the “therapeutic alliance” (Nienhuis et al., 2018) between the counselor and client within the dyad. Further, our logic is based on empathy and warmth, which are considered central pillars of counseling and are not culture specific. Therefore, we consider our findings generalizable outside of China but nonetheless recommend future research to determine how OMMHs function in other countries.

Second, we employed sentiment and text analyses on the Chinese language in our analysis, which is still an emerging field of study. To mitigate potential limitations, we used two separate methods, both of which were created by highly reputable entities in China, including Baidu, which is a multinational company specializing in artificial intelligence, and the Harbin Institute of Technology (HIT), which is one of the leading educational institutions in China. We also performed robustness checks for both methods, using both the count and length of clients' positive feedback to weight counseling style, and results converged across both methods, as discussed in our findings. Accordingly, we believe that this limitation did not seriously affect our results.

Finally, we used response quantity (responses within a two-day window), and not response quality, as a measure of the counselor responsiveness signal in our study. It may be argued that a counselor reuses or recycles responses across different questions and therefore response quantity may not be a good measure of responsiveness. However, counselors are unlikely to recycle responses to client questions because doing so can backfire and actually create negative perceptions about the counselor among potential clients, thereby diminishing demand (Deng et al., 2019), particularly because counselor responses are viewable by the entire OMMH community and the recycling of responses would be apparent. Recent studies support this notion, as they show that “high-expertise doctors choose to write online responses, and low-expertise doctors choose not to write online responses” (Khurana et al., 2019, p.875). Finally, results from a separate analysis of actual response text content also provide support for this argument. Our findings indicate that a higher quantity of responses doesn’t necessarily mean a lower quality of responses in terms of recycled responses, and we thus do not believe that this limitation seriously affects our findings. $^{17}$ Nonetheless future studies should consider using response quality (which we used for TSM analysis) while addressing questions of demonstration signals in online markets.

In addition to the aforementioned limitations, we also suggest two future directions. First, counselors might practice many other counseling styles, either in conjunction with empathy and warmth or entirely independent of empathy and warmth. Future studies could theorize on different counseling styles that may impact counselor demand from new clients and then operationalize the dominant counseling style to study its impact on counselor demand from new clients.

Second, counselors might respond to client questions due to different motivations, including both high client demand and low client demand, among others. Future studies could theorize the different motivations behind why counselors respond to client questions in open forums, or those directed to them, and then theorize on the different types of responsiveness and their direct and moderating impacts on counselor demand from new clients.
